The Kingdom of Bohemia (Czech: České království; German: Königreich Böhmen) was a medieval and early modern monarchy in Central Europe, the core of the Lands of the Bohemian Crown. Centred on Prague, it was a constituent state of the Holy Roman Empire whose king ranked as one of the seven prince-electors, before becoming a crown land of the Habsburg monarchy and, after 1867, of Cisleithanian Austria until the monarchy's collapse in 1918.

History

Přemyslid kingdom

The Bohemian duchy of the Přemyslid dynasty was raised to a hereditary kingdom under Ottokar I, whose royal title was confirmed by the Golden Bull of Sicily (1212) issued by Frederick II. Under Ottokar II (r. 1253–1278) Bohemia briefly became the greatest power in the Empire, extending to the Adriatic, until his defeat and death at the Battle on the Marchfeld in 1278. Wenceslaus II extended Přemyslid rule over Poland and Hungary before the dynasty died out with the murder of Wenceslaus III in 1306.

Luxembourg golden age

Under the House of Luxembourg — John the Blind and especially Charles IV (r. 1346–1378), who became Holy Roman Emperor — Bohemia enjoyed a golden age. Prague became the imperial capital; Charles founded Charles University (1348), built the Charles Bridge, the New Town of Prague, and Karlštejn Castle, and raised the Bohemian king's electoral dignity permanently in the Golden Bull of 1356. The reign of Wenceslaus IV saw growing religious tension culminating in the execution of the reformer Jan Hus at Constance in 1415.

Hussite wars and estates monarchy

The Hussite Wars (1419–1434) saw radical Hussite armies under Jan Žižka and Prokop the Great repeatedly defeat imperial crusades. The Compacts of Basel (1436) legalised Utraquist communion in both kinds, making Bohemia a land of two faiths. The estates later elected the "national" king George of Poděbrady (1458–1471), and from 1471 Bohemia shared Jagiellonian kings with Poland and Hungary. After the death of Louis II at Mohács in 1526, the estates elected the Habsburg Ferdinand I.

Habsburg rule

Under the Habsburgs Bohemia remained a formally separate kingdom within the composite monarchy. Rudolf II made Prague his imperial residence (1583–1612). Bohemian Protestant estates' resistance — beginning with the Defenestration of Prague (1618) — ignited the Thirty Years' War; after the Battle of White Mountain (1620) the kingdom was subjected to forced re-Catholicisation, Germanisation of its administration, and the confiscation of much of the native nobility's land. The Renewed Land Ordinance (1627) made the crown hereditary in the Habsburg line and reduced the estates' power. In the nineteenth century Bohemia industrialised rapidly and became the economic heartland of the Austrian Empire, while the Czech National Revival and Czech–German national conflict dominated its politics. The kingdom ended with the collapse of Austria-Hungary in October 1918, when its lands formed the core of the new Czechoslovakia.
