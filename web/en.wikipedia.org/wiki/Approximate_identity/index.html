Approximate identity

In mathematics, particularly in functional analysis and harmonic analysis, an approximate identity is a net or sequence of functions that acts increasingly like an identity element under convolution, even when no true identity exists in the space. Convolving a function with an approximate identity yields functions converging to the original in norm or pointwise.

Definition

For functions on the real line, an approximate identity is a family of functions φδ with integral one, concentrated ever more tightly near the origin as δ → 0. Then for a continuous function f, the convolutions f * φδ converge to f. Standard examples include scaled Gaussian kernels and the Fejér kernel of Fourier analysis. Mollifiers used in the theory of partial differential equations are a special case: smooth approximate identities whose convolutions produce smooth approximations of rough functions.

Banach algebras

Abstractly, a (bounded) approximate identity in a Banach algebra is a net that reproduces elements in the limit, substituting for a multiplicative identity the algebra may lack. The concept underlies Cohen's factorization theorem and the theory of C*-algebras, where every C*-algebra possesses an approximate identity of self-adjoint elements bounded by one.
