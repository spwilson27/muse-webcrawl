Pyruvic acid


Structure of pyruvic acid, CH3COCOOH

Pyruvic acid (CH3COCOOH) is the simplest of the alpha-keto acids, with a carboxylic acid group and a ketone group. Its conjugate base, pyruvate, is a key intermediate in several metabolic pathways throughout the cell. Pyruvic acid can be made from glucose through glycolysis, converted back to carbohydrates via gluconeogenesis, or transformed into energy through the citric acid cycle.

Biochemistry

Pyruvate is the end product of glycolysis; each glucose molecule yields two pyruvate molecules and a net gain of two ATP and two NADH. Under aerobic conditions pyruvate is oxidatively decarboxylated to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Under anaerobic conditions it is fermented — reduced to lactate in animals and many bacteria, or decarboxylated to acetaldehyde and then ethanol in yeast. Pyruvate also feeds alanine synthesis by transamination and oxaloacetate synthesis by carboxylation.

History and uses

The acid was first obtained by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1835 by dry distillation of tartaric acid. Pyruvic acid is used in chemical synthesis, in cell culture media, and as a dietary supplement marketed with claims of fat-burning effects that are not well supported by evidence.
