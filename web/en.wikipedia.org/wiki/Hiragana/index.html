Hiragana

Hiragana (平仮名, ひらがな) is a Japanese syllabary, one component of the Japanese writing system alongside katakana, kanji and sometimes rōmaji. It consists of 46 basic characters, each representing a mora (a unit of sound timing), and is used to write native Japanese words for which there is no kanji, grammatical endings and particles, and — as furigana printed above characters — to show the pronunciation of kanji.

History

Hiragana developed during the Heian period from man'yōgana, Chinese characters used phonetically, written in a highly cursive grass script. Because it was at first used mainly by women, who were excluded from formal Chinese learning, it was called onnade, "women's hand"; classical works such as Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji were written largely in it. Many variant forms (hentaigana) coexisted until a standard set was fixed by the script reforms of 1900 and the post-war modern kana usage of 1946.

Basic characters

あ aい iう uえ eお o
か kaき kiく kuけ keこ ko
さ saし shiす suせ seそ so
た taち chiつ tsuて teと to
な naに niぬ nuね neの no

Further sounds are written with diacritics: the dakuten ( ゛) voices consonants (か → が), the handakuten ( ゜) turns h into p, and small versions of や, ゆ, よ create palatalised combinations such as きゃ (kya). The character ん represents a moraic nasal and never begins a word.
