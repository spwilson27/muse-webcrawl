Mahayana sutras

The Mahayana sutras are a large body of Buddhist scriptures composed in India, mainly in Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit, beginning around the 1st century BCE and continuing for over a millennium. They present themselves as discourses of the Buddha (buddhavacana, "the word of the Buddha") preserved and revealed over time, and they form the scriptural foundation of Mahayana Buddhism. Hundreds of these texts survive, preserved in Sanskrit, in Chinese translation (assembled in the Chinese Buddhist canon), and in Tibetan translation (in the Kangyur). Theravada Buddhism does not accept them as canonical.

Major sutras and groups

The earliest stratum is the Prajñāpāramitā (Perfection of Wisdom) corpus, including the Heart Sutra and Diamond Sutra, which expound the doctrine of emptiness. Other influential texts include the Lotus Sutra, which teaches the One Vehicle and the universal potential for Buddhahood; the Vimalakīrti Sutra, whose hero is a layman; the Pure Land sutras, centred on the Buddha Amitābha; the Avataṃsaka (Flower Garland) Sutra, source of the Huayan school; the Laṅkāvatāra Sutra, associated with Yogācāra and Chan; and the Tathāgatagarbha sutras, which teach Buddha-nature. The Diamond Sutra of 868 CE, printed in Tang China, is the world's earliest dated printed book.

Composition and transmission

Unlike the early discourses preserved in Pali, the Mahayana sutras appeared in writing relatively early and circulated as physical books whose copying and worship were themselves meritorious acts praised within the texts. Their transmission to Central and East Asia began in the 2nd century CE, with translators such as Lokakṣema and later Kumārajīva and Xuanzang producing the Chinese versions that shaped East Asian Buddhism. Many Sanskrit originals were lost in India and survive only in translation, though Sanskrit manuscripts from Nepal, Gilgit, and Central Asia preserve an important portion of the corpus.

Authority

Within Mahayana, the sutras' late composition was traditionally explained by legends that they had been hidden — for example, in the realm of the nāgas (serpent deities) — until the world was ready for them. Modern scholarship treats them as the products of visionary and literary communities within Indian Buddhism over many centuries.
