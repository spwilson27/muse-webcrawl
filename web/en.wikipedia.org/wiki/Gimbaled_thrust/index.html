Gimbaled thrust

A rocket engine mounted on gimbals can be swivelled to steer the vehicle

Gimbaled thrust is a system of thrust vectoring used in rockets and missiles: the exhaust nozzle of the rocket engine is mounted on gimbals so that it can be pivoted from side to side by actuators, deflecting the thrust direction and thereby steering the vehicle. It is the most common method of pitch and yaw control for large liquid-propellant rockets.

Operation

Hydraulic or electric actuators tilt the engine (or in some designs only the nozzle) through a few degrees in two perpendicular planes, in response to commands from the guidance system. Because the thrust line then no longer passes through the vehicle's centre of mass, it produces a torque that rotates the rocket onto the desired attitude. Roll control usually requires more than one engine, vernier thrusters, or reaction jets, since a single gimbaled engine cannot generate torque about its own thrust axis.

History and examples

Gimbaled engines were pioneered in the German V-2 programme's successors and were central to designs by Wernher von Braun's team; earlier rockets such as the V-2 itself used graphite jet vanes and fins instead. Engines using gimbals include the Saturn V's F-1 and J-2 (the four outer F-1s gimbaled), the Space Shuttle main engines, and most modern launch vehicle engines such as the Falcon 9's Merlin engines. An alternative for solid rocket motors is a flexibly jointed nozzle, as on the Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Boosters.
