Cell line

A cell line is a population of cells derived from a single cell or tissue sample that can be propagated repeatedly — in the case of immortalised cell lines, indefinitely — in laboratory culture. Establishing a cell line is a key step in cell culture, providing a consistent, renewable model system for research and biotechnology.

Types

Primary cultures are taken directly from tissue and have a finite lifespan. Continuous or immortalised cell lines have acquired (naturally, through cancer, or artificially, through viral transformation or telomerase expression) the ability to divide indefinitely. Well-known examples include HeLa (from a human cervical cancer, 1951), Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells used in biopharmaceutical production, and HEK 293 cells used widely in molecular biology.

Uses

Cell lines are used to study cell biology, test drug toxicity and efficacy, produce vaccines and therapeutic proteins, and model disease. Resources such as the Cellosaurus knowledgebase catalogue them, and repositories such as ATCC distribute authenticated stocks.

Problems

Cell lines can become contaminated by other lines (notably HeLa contamination) or by mycoplasma, and can drift genetically over many passages, undermining reproducibility. Authentication by DNA profiling is now widely recommended.
