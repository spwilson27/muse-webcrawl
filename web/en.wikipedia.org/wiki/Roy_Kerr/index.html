Roy Kerr

Roy Patrick Kerr (born 16 May 1934) is a New Zealand mathematician best known for discovering the Kerr geometry, an exact solution of the Einstein field equations of general relativity. Published in 1963, the Kerr metric describes the spacetime around a rotating, uncharged black hole and made possible the modern theoretical understanding and eventual astrophysical modelling of rotating black holes. Kerr is an emeritus professor at the University of Canterbury in Christchurch.

Life and career

Kerr was born in Kurow, in the Waitaki District of New Zealand, and educated at St Andrew's College, Christchurch, and Canterbury University College, where he completed a master's degree in mathematics. He worked briefly at the New Zealand DSIR before winning a scholarship to Cambridge, where he received his PhD in 1959 under Philip Hall. After a postdoctoral period at Syracuse University with Peter Bergmann, and a position at the University of Texas at Austin, he returned to New Zealand in 1971 as professor of mathematics at the University of Canterbury, a chair he held until retiring in 1993.

Kerr was also a notable bridge player, representing New Zealand internationally — an unusual distinction for a research mathematician.

The Kerr solution

At Austin in 1963, Kerr found a new exact solution of Einstein's vacuum equations using the theory of algebraically special spacetimes and null congruences. Published in Physical Review Letters as "Gravitational Field of a Spinning Mass as an Example of Algebraically Special Metrics", the solution generalises the Schwarzschild metric to rotating masses. Its later interpretation by Brandon Carter and others showed it possesses an outer event horizon and inner Cauchy horizon surrounding a ring-shaped singularity, and an ergoregion — the ergosphere — within which no observer can remain stationary and from which energy can in principle be extracted by the Penrose process.

The Kerr solution turned out to be uniquely significant: uniqueness theorems subsequently established that a stationary, asymptotically flat black hole in vacuum general relativity must be described by the Kerr (or, with charge, Kerr–Newman) metric. Since astrophysical black holes are expected to rotate, often rapidly, the Kerr geometry underpins the interpretation of observations from X-ray binaries to the gravitational-wave detections of merging black holes by LIGO and the imaging of black hole shadows by the Event Horizon Telescope.

Recognition

Kerr was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 2019 (having been a Fellow of the Royal Society of New Zealand since 1975) and received the Hughes Medal of the Royal Society in 1984, the Rutherford Medal in 1993, the Albert Einstein Medal in 2013, and the Crafoord Prize in Astronomy in 2016, shared with Roger Blandford, "for fundamental work concerning rotating black holes and their astrophysical consequences".
