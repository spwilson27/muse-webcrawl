Constitution of Turkey

The Constitution of the Republic of Turkey (Turkish: Türkiye Cumhuriyeti Anayasası), also known as the Constitution of 1982, is Turkey's fundamental law. It was drafted by the military junta following the 1980 coup d'état (the Constituent Assembly and the National Security Council), ratified in a referendum on 7 November 1982, and replaced the Constitution of 1961. It has been amended many times, most significantly in 2017, when a referendum replaced the parliamentary system with a presidential system.

Principles

The constitution defines Turkey as a democratic, secular and social state governed by the rule of law (Article 2). The first three articles, covering the form of the state, its characteristics, and its integrity, official language, flag, anthem and capital, are entrenched: Article 4 provides that they may not be amended nor their amendment proposed. The official language is Turkish and the capital is Ankara.

History of amendments

  • 1987–2004: a series of amendment packages, especially those of 2001 and 2004 connected with Turkey's European Union accession process, expanded fundamental rights, abolished the death penalty, and reduced the military's institutional role, including changes to the National Security Council.
  • 2007: following a crisis over the presidential election, amendments provided for the direct popular election of the president.
  • 2010: a referendum package restructured the Constitutional Court and the Supreme Board of Judges and Prosecutors.
  • 2017: approved by a narrow margin in referendum, the amendments abolished the office of prime minister, made the president head of the executive with power to issue decrees, increased the number of deputies in the Grand National Assembly from 550 to 600, and lowered the age of candidacy to 18. The changes took full effect after the 2018 elections.

Institutions

Legislative power is vested in the Grand National Assembly of Turkey. Executive power is exercised by the President of the Republic. Judicial power is exercised by independent courts, with the Constitutional Court empowered to review the constitutionality of laws and to try certain high officials; since 2012 individuals may apply to it directly for alleged violations of fundamental rights.

Criticism

The 1982 constitution has long been criticised for the tutelary role it originally reserved to the military, for restrictions on freedom of expression and association, and for its treatment of minority rights. Successive governments have debated drafting an entirely new, civilian constitution, though no replacement has been adopted.
