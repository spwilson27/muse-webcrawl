Human sacrifice in Inca culture

Human sacrifice in Inca culture was practised by the Inca Empire of the Andes, most notably in the capacocha (Quechua: qhapaq hucha, "royal duty" or "royal crime") ceremonies, in which children were offered at sacred shrines, often on high mountain summits, on important occasions such as the death of an emperor or in response to natural disasters.

Capacocha ceremonies

Children chosen for capacocha were typically selected for their beauty and perfection, brought to Cusco to be blessed, and then sent on long processions to provincial shrines. They were given coca leaves and chicha (maize beer) before being killed by strangulation, a blow to the head, or exposure, and buried with fine textiles, figurines of gold and silver, and pottery.

Archaeological evidence

The frozen mummies of children found on peaks such as Llullaillaco and Ampato — including the famous "Juanita" mummy discovered in 1995 — provide unusually well-preserved evidence. Hair analysis has shown changes in diet and coca and alcohol consumption in the months before death.
