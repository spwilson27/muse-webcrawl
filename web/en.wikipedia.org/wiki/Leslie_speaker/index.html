The Leslie speaker is a combined amplifier and loudspeaker system that projects sound through a rotating system, creating a distinctive chorus- and tremolo-like effect through the Doppler effect and amplitude modulation. Invented by Donald Leslie in the late 1930s and first marketed in 1941, it became inseparably associated with the Hammond organ, and its swirling sound is a hallmark of gospel, jazz, blues, rock, and soul recordings.

Design

In the classic models, such as the Leslie 122 and 147, the amplifier drives two rotating elements. The treble signal is fed into a compression driver mounted above a rotating horn — actually a single horn paired with a dummy counterweight arm — that spins inside the cabinet. The bass signal drives a downward-firing woofer beneath a rotating drum with a scoop that deflects the sound. Both rotors can be switched between two speeds: a slow "chorale" speed of roughly 40 rpm and a fast "tremolo" speed of roughly 400 rpm. The gradual acceleration and deceleration of the heavy rotors as they change speed is an important part of the effect.

The changing rotation produces a combination of pitch shift (from the Doppler effect as the sound source moves toward and away from the listener), amplitude modulation, and shifting reflections within the cabinet, resulting in a rich, three-dimensional sound that is difficult to reproduce electronically, though many digital simulations now exist.

History and use

Don Leslie originally offered his invention to the Hammond Clock Company, which rejected it; Hammond even redesigned its speaker connectors in an attempt to exclude Leslie cabinets, a move Leslie countered with adapters. Despite this, organists overwhelmingly preferred the Leslie sound. Although created for organs, the Leslie was adopted by guitarists and vocalists as well: it can be heard on recordings by the Beatles, Pink Floyd, the Beach Boys, and many others. The cabinet's manufacturer changed hands several times, and modern versions are produced by Hammond Suzuki.
