Trap–neuter–return

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trap–neuter–return (TNR), also known as trap–neuter–release, is a humane method of managing feral cat populations. Feral cats are humanely trapped, spayed or neutered (and usually vaccinated against rabies and ear-tipped for identification), then returned to their outdoor home. Kittens young enough to be socialized and friendly adults are typically removed for adoption. Related programs include trap–neuter–vaccinate–return (TNVR).

How it works

Volunteers or animal-control staff use box traps baited with food to catch unsocialized cats. At a clinic the cats are anaesthetised, sterilised, vaccinated, and given an "ear tip" — the universal sign of a sterilised outdoor cat, in which the tip of one ear is surgically squared off. After recovery, the cats are released where they were trapped, where volunteer caregivers often continue to provide food and shelter and monitor the colony for newcomers.

Rationale

Advocates argue that removal-based control fails because of the "vacuum effect": when cats are taken away, surviving and immigrating cats breed up to the carrying capacity set by food and shelter. Sterilised resident cats, by contrast, hold their territory, prevent intact newcomers from settling, and cannot reproduce, so the colony shrinks over time through natural attrition. Neutering also reduces nuisance behaviours such as yowling, spraying, and fighting. Long-running programmes, such as those documented in Rome and in studies of managed colonies in Florida and elsewhere, have reported substantial population declines where sterilisation coverage is high and abandonment is controlled.

Criticism

TNR is opposed by many wildlife and bird-conservation organisations, which note that free-ranging cats kill large numbers of birds and small mammals, and argue that returning cats outdoors perpetuates predation and disease risks and may constitute abandonment. Some veterinarians' and public-health groups raise concerns about rabies and toxoplasmosis. Critics contend that population reductions require sterilisation rates rarely achieved and that alternatives — adoption, sanctuaries, and enforcement against abandonment — are preferable. Supporters respond that lethal control has repeatedly failed to reduce feral cat numbers and that managed TNR, combined with adoption of socialised cats, is the only approach with sustained public support.

Legal status

Hundreds of municipalities in the United States and elsewhere have adopted TNR ordinances or policies, while some jurisdictions discourage or prohibit the release of cats. TNR is promoted by organisations such as Alley Cat Allies and supported in modified form by several large humane societies.
