Azulejo (Portuguese and Spanish for "small polished stone") is a form of Iberian painted tin-glazed ceramic tilework. Azulejos are found on the interior and exterior of churches, palaces, houses, schools, restaurants and railway stations in Portugal and Spain, and throughout their former colonial empires, including Brazil, Mexico, Goa and Macau. The word derives from the Arabic al-zulayj, "polished stone", reflecting the technique's Moorish origins.

History

Glazed tilework was introduced to Iberia under Muslim rule, with major early centres at Málaga and later Seville, where Italian potters such as Francisco Niculoso Pisano brought Renaissance maiolica techniques in the early 16th century. Portugal adopted the art enthusiastically; King Manuel I ordered tiles for the National Palace of Sintra after visiting Seville in 1498. In the 17th century large figurative panels influenced by Flemish engravings became fashionable, and in the 18th century the classic blue-and-white palette — influenced by Chinese porcelain imported by the Dutch East India Company — dominated monumental narrative cycles in churches and palaces. Noted workshops included those of António de Oliveira Bernardes in Lisbon.

19th century and modern use

After a decline, industrial production revived the azulejo in the 19th century, covering whole building facades in Lisbon and Porto with patterned tiles (azulejos de fachada). In the 20th century, artists such as Maria Keil, who designed tiles for Lisbon's Metro stations, renewed the medium. Lisbon's Museu Nacional do Azulejo (National Tile Museum), founded in 1965, preserves one of the world's major collections. Azulejos also serve practical functions: they protect walls from damp, reflect heat, and are easy to clean.
