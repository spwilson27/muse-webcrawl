Darwin's finches


Image placeholder: Darwin's finches

Darwin's finches (also known as the Galápagos finches) are a group of about 18 species of passerine birds in the tanager family, found mainly on the Galápagos Islands. They are famous for their role in inspiring Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection.

Darwin's observations

During the voyage of HMS Beagle in 1835, Darwin collected specimens on the Galápagos but did not fully appreciate their significance until ornithologist John Gould identified them as a closely related group of distinct species. The variation in beak size and shape among species, adapted to different foods — seeds, insects, cactus flowers, and even blood — suggested descent with modification from a common ancestor.

Research

The finches became a classic example of adaptive radiation. From the 1970s, Peter and Rosemary Grant's long-term field study on Daphne Major documented evolution in action, showing beak traits changing in response to drought-driven shifts in seed availability.
