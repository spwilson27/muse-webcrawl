Lord of the World is a dystopian science fiction novel by the English Catholic priest and author Robert Hugh Benson, published in 1907. The novel depicts a future world, set in the early twenty-first century, in which secular humanism and materialism have triumphed, the Catholic Church survives only as a persecuted remnant, and a charismatic figure named Julian Felsenburgh rises to become the ruler of the world and is identified by the faithful as the Antichrist.

The novel is frequently cited as one of the earliest modern dystopian novels and has been praised by Popes Benedict XVI and Francis, the latter of whom recommended it publicly in 2013 as a depiction of "the spirit of the world" and ideological colonisation.

Plot summary

The novel opens in London in the early 2000s. The world is divided between a Western bloc dominated by a secularised Europe and America, and an Eastern confederation. Euthanasia is legal and organised through "euthanasia houses", Esperanto-like universal language and rapid transit have flattened national differences, and religion has largely been replaced by a vague humanitarian creed. Two English priests, Percy Franklin and Father Francis, represent divergent responses to the age: Franklin remains steadfast, while Francis eventually apostatises.

A mysterious senator from Vermont, Julian Felsenburgh, emerges as a miraculous peacemaker who averts war between East and West. His uncanny physical resemblance to Percy Franklin is repeatedly noted. Felsenburgh is acclaimed President of Europe and then effectively ruler of the world, and a cult of worship is established around him, with liturgies modelled on Catholic ritual but directed to Man. When a plot by surviving Catholics is followed by an attack on Felsenburgh, persecution becomes total: Rome is destroyed by aerial bombardment after a conspiracy is uncovered, and the Pope and Franklin — elected Pope Silvester III after his predecessor's death — retreat to Nazareth. The novel ends with Felsenburgh's forces approaching for the final assault as the world passes away in an apocalyptic conclusion, closing with the words of the liturgy sung as destruction descends.

Themes

Benson wrote the novel as a Catholic counterblast to the optimistic socialist futures of authors such as H. G. Wells. Its central themes include the conflict between faith and secular humanism, the seductiveness of a humanitarianism that demands worship, the marginalisation and persecution of believers, and the eschatological confrontation between Christ and Antichrist. The novel imagines technological developments including volors (aerial craft), pneumatic transit, and instant global communication.

Reception and influence

Lord of the World has remained in print and is regarded as a classic of Catholic fiction. Pope Benedict XVI referred to it in a 1992 speech as depicting a "unified anti-Christian culture". Pope Francis recommended it in a homily in November 2013 and again in a 2015 interview aboard a papal flight, saying it shows "what happens when man wants to become God". The novel influenced later dystopian and apocalyptic fiction and is often compared with the later works of Benson's contemporaries and successors in the genre.
