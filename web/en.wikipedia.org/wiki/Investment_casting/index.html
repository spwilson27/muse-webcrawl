Investment casting

Investment casting
Also calledLost-wax casting, precision casting
MaterialsSteel, aluminium, bronze, superalloys, titanium
ApplicationsTurbine blades, aerospace parts, jewellery, dental work, sculpture

Investment casting is an industrial process based on lost-wax casting, one of the oldest known metal-forming techniques, in use for over 5,000 years. A pattern made of wax (or, increasingly, 3D-printed polymer) is coated — "invested" — with a refractory ceramic material to form a mould. The wax is then melted out, molten metal is poured into the cavity, and the ceramic shell is broken away to reveal the casting.

Process

Wax patterns are usually injection-moulded and assembled onto a central wax sprue to form a "tree." The assembly is repeatedly dipped in ceramic slurry and stuccoed with sand to build a shell, which is dried, dewaxed in an autoclave or furnace, and fired. Metal is poured — sometimes under vacuum or centrifugal assistance — and after solidification the shell is removed by vibration, blasting or chemical dissolution. The process achieves excellent surface finish and dimensional accuracy, and can reproduce fine detail and thin walls, though it is slower and more costly per part than sand casting or die casting.

Applications

Investment casting is used where precision and high-temperature performance matter: single-crystal turbine blades for jet engines, aerospace and power-generation components in nickel superalloys, firearm and golf-club parts, dental crowns, jewellery, and fine-art bronze sculpture. Historical examples of the lost-wax method include bronze statues of ancient Greece and the Benin Bronzes.
