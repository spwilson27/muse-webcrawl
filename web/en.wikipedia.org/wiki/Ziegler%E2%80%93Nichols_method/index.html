Ziegler–Nichols method

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ziegler–Nichols method is a heuristic method of tuning a PID controller. It was developed by John G. Ziegler and Nathaniel B. Nichols and presented in their 1942 paper "Optimum Settings for Automatic Controllers". The method provides simple rules for choosing the proportional, integral and derivative gains of a controller based on the observed response of the controlled process.

Ultimate gain method

In the best-known version, the closed-loop or ultimate gain method, the integral and derivative actions are first disabled. The proportional gain Kp is then increased from zero until the output of the loop exhibits sustained oscillations of constant amplitude. The gain at which this occurs is the ultimate gain Ku, and the period of the oscillation is the ultimate period Tu. Controller parameters are then set from Ku and Tu: for a classic PID controller, Kp = 0.6 Ku, the integral time Ti = Tu/2, and the derivative time Td = Tu/8.

Process reaction curve method

Ziegler and Nichols also described an open-loop method based on the process reaction curve: the step response of the plant is approximated by a delay L and a time constant T, and tuning rules are given in terms of these quantities and the process gain.

Criticism and variants

The Ziegler–Nichols rules aim for a quarter-amplitude decay response, which is often too oscillatory and aggressive for process control applications. Numerous modifications — such as the Tyreus–Luyben rules and the "some overshoot" and "no overshoot" variants — have been proposed to give less aggressive tuning. Despite its limitations, the method remains a common starting point for PID tuning in practice and teaching.
