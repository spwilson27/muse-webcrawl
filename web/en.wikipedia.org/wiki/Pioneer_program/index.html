Pioneer program

The Pioneer program was a series of United States space missions, begun in 1958, that explored the Moon, the interplanetary medium, Venus, Jupiter, and Saturn. The early Pioneers were among the first American spacecraft; the later Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11 became the first probes to visit the outer planets, and the Pioneer Venus missions mapped Venus in the late 1970s.

Early missions

The first Pioneers (1958–1960), launched by the Air Force and later NASA, attempted lunar flybys and orbits amid many failures; Pioneer 4 (1959) became the first US craft to escape Earth's gravity and enter solar orbit. Pioneer 5 (1960) mapped interplanetary magnetic fields. Pioneers 6–9, launched 1965–1968, formed an early space-weather monitoring network in solar orbit, and some operated for decades.

Outer planet missions

Pioneers 10 and 11, managed by NASA's Ames Research Center, were designed as pathfinders for the outer Solar System: Pioneer 10 flew by Jupiter in 1973; Pioneer 11 flew by Jupiter in 1974 and Saturn in 1979. Both carried the Pioneer plaque and are now on escape trajectories from the Sun.

Pioneer Venus

In 1978 the program concluded with Pioneer Venus 1 (an orbiter that radar-mapped the surface through the clouds for over a decade) and Pioneer Venus 2 (a multiprobe that released four atmospheric entry probes, one of which survived briefly on the surface).

Legacy

The program pioneered deep-space navigation, radiation-hardened design, and spin-stabilized spacecraft, and its name was revived in NASA's Pioneer (Discovery) concepts. Data from Pioneers 10 and 11 produced the "Pioneer anomaly," resolved as thermal recoil, a cautionary tale in precision tracking.
