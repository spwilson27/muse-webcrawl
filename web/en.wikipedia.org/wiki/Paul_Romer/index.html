Paul Michael Romer (born November 6, 1955) is an American economist who received the 2018 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for integrating technological innovation into long-run macroeconomic analysis. He was chief economist and senior vice president of the World Bank from 2016 to 2018, and is a pioneer of endogenous growth theory.

Early life and education

Romer was born in Denver, Colorado, the son of former Colorado governor Roy Romer. He studied at the University of Chicago, earning a bachelor's degree in mathematics in 1977 and a PhD in economics in 1983. He taught at the University of Rochester, the University of Chicago, and the University of California, Berkeley, before joining Stanford's Graduate School of Business in 1996.

Research

Romer's landmark 1990 paper "Endogenous Technological Change" built a model in which intentional investment in research and development by profit-seeking firms generates new designs and sustained growth, treating ideas — nonrival goods that can be shared without limit — as the central engine of rising living standards. His earlier 1986 paper had already helped launch the modern "new growth theory" literature. He also founded the Aplia educational software company. From 2010 he taught at New York University's Stern School of Business and directed NYU's Marron Institute of Urban Management, where he promoted the concept of "charter cities": new urban jurisdictions built on reform-friendly rules in developing countries.

World Bank and controversy

As World Bank chief economist, Romer drew attention for criticising the Bank's Doing Business rankings after methodological changes appeared to have moved Chile's ranking in ways correlated with its politics; the Bank subsequently reviewed its procedures. He resigned in January 2018 and returned to NYU. In October 2018 he shared the Nobel Prize with William Nordhaus.
