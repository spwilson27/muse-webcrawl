Anterograde amnesia

Anterograde amnesia is a loss of the ability to create new memories after the event that caused amnesia, leading to a partial or complete inability to recall recent events, while long-term memories from before the event remain intact. This is in contrast to retrograde amnesia, in which memories created prior to the event are lost while new memories can still be created.

Causes

The condition typically results from damage to the hippocampus and surrounding medial temporal lobe, or to the diencephalon. Causes include stroke, encephalitis, surgical removal of both hippocampi, chronic alcohol abuse leading to Korsakoff syndrome, and traumatic brain injury. Certain benzodiazepines can produce temporary anterograde amnesia.

Famous cases

The most famous case is that of Henry Molaison (patient H.M.), who after bilateral hippocampal surgery in 1953 for epilepsy could no longer form new declarative memories yet retained procedural learning — a distinction that reshaped the science of memory. Clive Wearing, a British musician who developed dense amnesia after viral encephalitis, is another well-known case.
