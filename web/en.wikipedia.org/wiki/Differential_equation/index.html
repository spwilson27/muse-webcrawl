Differential equation

[Image: a notebook page of handwritten differential equations with a plotted family of solution curves]

In mathematics, a differential equation is an equation that relates one or more unknown functions and their derivatives. In applications, the functions generally represent physical quantities, the derivatives represent their rates of change, and the equation defines a relationship between the two — making differential equations the principal mathematical language of change, used throughout physics, engineering, biology, chemistry and economics.

Ordinary differential equations (ODEs) involve a single independent variable; partial differential equations (PDEs) involve several, and include such famous examples as the heat equation, the wave equation and the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid dynamics.

Solving and significance

Some differential equations have exact, closed-form solutions — exponential growth and decay, harmonic oscillation, Newton's law of cooling — but most do not, and are solved approximately by numerical methods on computers. The theory of existence, uniqueness and stability of solutions, founded in the nineteenth century, tells us when such solutions can be trusted.

Qualitative theory, from Poincaré onward, studies the geometry of solution spaces and gave rise to dynamical systems and chaos theory: even simple nonlinear equations (such as the logistic map or Lorenz system) can produce unpredictable, chaotic behaviour.

Famous equations

Newton's second law, written as a differential equation, founded classical mechanics. Maxwell's equations govern electromagnetism as PDEs; the Schrödinger equation does the same for quantum mechanics; the Black–Scholes equation prices financial options; and epidemiological models such as SIR are systems of ODEs.

The Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem — whether solutions of the equations of fluid flow can blow up — is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a million-dollar prize.
