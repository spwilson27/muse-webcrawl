Reactive arthritis

SpecialtyRheumatology
SymptomsJoint pain and swelling, conjunctivitis, painful urination
Onset1–4 weeks after an infection
TreatmentNSAIDs, corticosteroids, antibiotics for active infection

Reactive arthritis is a form of inflammatory arthritis that develops in response to an infection elsewhere in the body, most often in the intestines or the genitourinary tract. It belongs to the family of spondyloarthropathies and is strongly associated with the HLA-B27 genetic marker. The condition was formerly known as Reiter's syndrome, after the German physician Hans Reiter.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically appear one to four weeks after the triggering infection and may include an asymmetric arthritis affecting the knees, ankles, and feet; conjunctivitis; urethritis; enthesitis (inflammation where tendons attach to bone); dactylitis ("sausage digits"); and skin changes such as keratoderma blennorrhagicum. The classic triad of arthritis, conjunctivitis, and urethritis occurs in only a minority of patients.

Causes

Common triggering organisms include Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, and Chlamydia trachomatis. The arthritis is "reactive" because viable organisms are generally not cultured from the joint itself; instead, the inflammation is thought to result from an abnormal immune response to microbial antigens.

Treatment

Management focuses on treating any ongoing infection and controlling inflammation with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroid injections, and, in chronic cases, disease-modifying agents such as sulfasalazine. Most cases resolve within six to twelve months, though a proportion become chronic or relapse.
