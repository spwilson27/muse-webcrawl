Ultramontanism

Ultramontanism
Meaning"Beyond the mountains" (i.e., the Alps)
ContextRoman Catholic Church, esp. 18th–19th centuries
Core claimSupreme authority of the pope over national churches and states
Opposed toGallicanism, Febronianism, conciliarism
Placeholder of St Peter's, Rome.

Ultramontanism is a clerical political conception within the Catholic Church that places strong emphasis on the prerogatives and powers of the pope. In particular, ultramontanism asserts the supremacy of the pope over the temporal powers of national states and over the local bishops and national churches. The word derives from the Latin ultra montes, "beyond the mountains" — from the perspective of northern Europe, looking toward Rome beyond the Alps.

History

The term arose in the medieval and early modern disputes over the extent of papal jurisdiction. In France it was opposed by Gallicanism, which defended the independence of the French church and crown; in the Holy Roman Empire by Febronianism and Josephinism, which subordinated the church to enlightened absolutist states. Ultramontane thinkers such as Joseph de Maistre, writing after the French Revolution, portrayed papal authority as the sole bulwark of social order.

The nineteenth century

Under Pope Pius IX (1846–1878), ultramontanism became the dominant movement in the Church. The definitions of the Immaculate Conception (1854, defined on papal authority alone) and the Syllabus of Errors (1864), which condemned liberalism and the separation of church and state, were key ultramontane victories. Supporters such as Louis Veuillot in France championed Rome against liberal Catholic attempts at compromise.

First Vatican Council

The movement reached its high point at the First Vatican Council (1869–70), whose constitution Pastor aeternus defined papal infallibility and the pope's universal immediate jurisdiction. A minority of "inopportunist" bishops opposed the definition's timing; some dissenters in Germany and Austria formed the Old Catholic churches.

Legacy

After 1870, ultramontanism in its classic form faded as papal authority came to be taken for granted within Catholicism, though tensions with states — Bismarck's Kulturkampf in Germany, laïcité in France — continued. The balance struck by the Second Vatican Council (1962–65), emphasizing collegiality of bishops, is often read as a partial correction to ultramontane centralization.
