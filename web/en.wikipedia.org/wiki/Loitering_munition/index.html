A loitering munition, also known as a suicide drone or kamikaze drone, is an aerial weapon system in which the munition itself loiters—flies in a holding pattern—around a target area for an extended period, searching for targets, and attacks only once a target is located and identified. Unlike a conventional missile, a loitering munition can be controlled by an operator in real time, can abort or be redirected, and combines the roles of reconnaissance drone and precision weapon in a single expendable airframe.

History

The concept emerged in the 1980s in the suppression of enemy air defences (SEAD) role: Israel's IAI Harpy (which entered service in the 1990s) autonomously homes on radar emissions, and its successor, the Harop, adds an electro-optical seeker and an operator in the loop. Small, man-portable systems such as the American Switchblade, carried by infantry, brought loitering munitions to the tactical level in the 2010s. The 2020 Nagorno-Karabakh War showcased Harop and other loitering munitions in the destruction of Armenian armour and air defences, and the Russo-Ukrainian War from 2022 saw mass use of systems including Russia's Lancet and the Iranian-designed Shahed-136 (used by Russia as the Geran-2), which is often classed as a one-way attack drone. Cheap first-person-view (FPV) racing drones adapted to carry warheads have further blurred the category.

Characteristics and debate

Loitering munitions are generally slow but persistent, with endurance from tens of minutes to many hours, and warheads ranging from small anti-personnel charges to shaped charges able to kill tanks. Their low cost relative to the targets they destroy has made them strategically disruptive. Their growing autonomy—particularly systems that can select targets without human approval—has drawn sustained criticism from campaigners against "killer robots" and debate in international humanitarian law about meaningful human control.
