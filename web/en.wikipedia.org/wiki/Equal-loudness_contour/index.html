Equal-loudness contour

An equal-loudness contour is a measure of sound pressure level, over the frequency spectrum, for which a listener perceives a constant loudness when presented with pure steady tones. The unit of measurement for loudness levels is the phon, defined by reference to the sound pressure in decibels of a 1 kHz tone judged equally loud. The contours demonstrate that human hearing is not equally sensitive to all frequencies: the ear is most sensitive in the region of roughly 2–5 kHz and requires far greater sound pressure at low and very high frequencies for the same perceived loudness.

Fletcher–Munson curves

The first contours were published in 1933 by Harvey Fletcher and Wilden A. Munson at Bell Labs, based on measurements with headphones. Known as the Fletcher–Munson curves, they showed that at low listening levels the ear strongly discounts bass frequencies, which is why music played quietly sounds thin — an effect compensated by the "loudness" switch on older amplifiers. Fletcher and Munson's values were later superseded by the Robinson–Dadson measurements of the 1950s, which formed the basis of the original ISO standard.

ISO 226

The current international standard, ISO 226, presents revised contours derived from a 2003 synthesis of studies from several countries. The modern curves differ noticeably from the older ones, particularly at low frequencies, reflecting improved measurement technique and free-field rather than headphone presentation. Contours are drawn for loudness levels from the threshold of hearing (the lowest curve) up to about 90 phons at intervals; at high levels the contours flatten, meaning loudness perception becomes more nearly independent of frequency.

Applications

Equal-loudness contours underpin the frequency weightings used in sound level meters: A-weighting approximates the ear's response at modest levels and is used in environmental and occupational noise measurement, while C-weighting represents the response at high levels. The contours also inform audio engineering, hearing-aid fitting, and perceptual audio coding, which exploits the ear's frequency-dependent sensitivity and masking to discard inaudible information.
