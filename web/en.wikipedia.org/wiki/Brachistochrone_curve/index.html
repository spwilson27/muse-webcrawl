Brachistochrone curve
Name fromGreek brachistos (shortest) + chronos (time)
Problem posed1696, by Johann Bernoulli
SolutionAn arc of a cycloid
RelatedTautochrone curve; calculus of variations

The brachistochrone curve (from Ancient Greek βράχιστος χρόνος, "shortest time") is the curve of fastest descent between two points: the path along which a bead sliding frictionlessly under gravity reaches the lower point in the least time. Counter-intuitively, it is neither the straight line between the points nor the steepest possible drop, but an arc of a cycloid dipping below the direct line to gain speed early.

History

Galileo had studied the problem in 1638 and correctly guessed an arc of a circle was faster than a straight descent, though his candidate was not optimal. In June 1696 Johann Bernoulli posed the problem as a challenge in the Acta Eruditorum. Solutions came from Leibniz, Jakob Bernoulli, l'Hôpital, Tschirnhaus — and from Isaac Newton, who, according to his own account, solved it in a single night after a day at the Mint and published anonymously; Bernoulli is said to have recognised the author "as the lion by his claw". Jakob Bernoulli's technique of varying the curve laid foundations of the calculus of variations.

Solution

Using conservation of energy, the speed at depth y is v = √(2gy). Minimising the travel time ∫ ds/v leads — by an argument Bernoulli modelled on Snell's law of refraction through layers of increasing speed — to the differential equation of the cycloid:

x = r(θ − sin θ),   y = r(1 − cos θ).

The same cycloid is the tautochrone curve: a bead reaches the bottom in the same time no matter where it starts. Johann Bernoulli was delighted to find the two curves identical, "the curve of quickest descent is the same as the curve of equal descent".
