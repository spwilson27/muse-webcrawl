Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race

Iditarod

First held: 1973

Route: Anchorage (ceremonial start) / Willow to Nome, Alaska

Distance: about 1,000 miles (1,600 km)

The Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race, more commonly known as The Iditarod, is an annual long-distance sled dog race held in Alaska, United States, in early March. Mushers and their teams of sled dogs travel roughly 1,000 miles from the Anchorage area to Nome on the Bering Sea coast, through some of the harshest winter terrain in North America. It is often called "The Last Great Race on Earth."

History

The race was founded by Joe Redington Sr. and others, with the first full-length race run in 1973. It commemorates the 1925 serum run to Nome, in which relay dog teams carried diphtheria antitoxin to the stricken town — an event remembered through the dog Balto — and the historic Iditarod Trail, a gold-rush-era supply route.

The race

Teams typically begin with 12 to 14 dogs and must finish with at least five in harness. The route alternates between a northern and southern path in alternating years, passing through checkpoints where mushers rest, feed their dogs and take mandatory layovers, including a 24-hour rest. Winning times have fallen from over 20 days in the 1970s to around 8–9 days; Mitch Seavey set a record of 8 days, 3 hours in 2017. The ceremonial start is held in Anchorage, with the competitive restart from Willow.

Notable mushers

Rick Swenson won a record five times; Susan Butcher and Martin Buser each won four. Libby Riddles became the first woman to win in 1985, and the race has a long-standing tradition recognising the last finisher with the Red Lantern award.
