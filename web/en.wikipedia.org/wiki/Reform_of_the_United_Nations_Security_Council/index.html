Reform of the United Nations Security Council

Security Council reform
Current membership15: five permanent (P5) with veto, ten elected for two-year terms

Reform of the United Nations Security Council encompasses proposals to change the membership, working methods and veto powers of the Security Council, the UN organ with primary responsibility for international peace and security. Critics argue that its composition — five permanent, veto-wielding members (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States) chosen in 1945, plus ten elected members — no longer reflects global power or population, with Africa, Latin America and most of Asia lacking permanent representation. Reform has been debated since the 1990s without a breakthrough.

Main camps

The G4 nations — Brazil, Germany, India and Japan — support each other's bids for new permanent seats, usually with additional permanent seats for Africa. The Uniting for Consensus group, including Italy, Pakistan, South Korea, Mexico and Argentina — often regional rivals of the G4 — opposes new permanent members and instead proposes more elected or longer-term renewable seats. The African Union's Ezulwini Consensus (2005) demands at least two permanent African seats with the veto, so long as the veto exists. Other proposals address the veto itself: France and Mexico have proposed voluntary veto restraint in cases of mass atrocities, and the ACT group campaigns for improved transparency and accountability in working methods.

Procedural history

The Council was enlarged only once, in 1965, from 11 to 15 members as UN membership grew. In 2005, Secretary-General Kofi Annan's In Larger Freedom report and the High-Level Panel proposed two models (new permanent seats, or new longer-term renewable seats), but the 2005 World Summit produced no agreement. Since 2009 negotiations have proceeded in the Intergovernmental Negotiations (IGN) framework in the General Assembly, where disputes persist over categories of membership, the veto, regional representation, Council size and working methods.

Obstacles

Any change to permanent membership requires amending the UN Charter, which under Article 108 needs adoption by two-thirds of the General Assembly and ratification by two-thirds of member states including all five permanent members — giving each P5 state a veto over reform itself. Rivalries between aspirants and their neighbours, and disagreement over whether new permanent members should have the veto, have so far prevented any model from commanding the necessary support.
