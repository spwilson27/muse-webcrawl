Stock character

A stock character is a stereotypical fictional character in a work of art such as a novel, play, or film, whom audiences recognize from frequent recurrences in a particular literary tradition. Stock characters are archetypal characters distinguished by their simplification and flatness; as a result, they are easy for an audience to recognize and are often used because they advance the plot quickly without the need for extensive character development.

History

The use of stock characters dates to ancient Greek and Roman theatre. The comedies of Plautus and Terence relied on recognizable types such as the clever slave, the braggart soldier (miles gloriosus), the miserly old man, and the young lovers. The tradition continued in the Italian commedia dell'arte, whose masked characters — Pantalone, Il Dottore, Il Capitano, Arlecchino, and the Innamorati — improvised around fixed personalities. English Renaissance drama inherited many of these types, and they persist in modern sitcoms, genre fiction, and popular film.

Examples

Common stock characters include the damsel in distress, the femme fatale, the mad scientist, the absent-minded professor, the hard-boiled detective, the wise old mentor, the comic sidekick, the villain with a tragic past, the girl next door, and the final girl of horror films. In pantomime and melodrama, stock characters were signaled by costume and mannerism so that audiences could instantly identify hero, heroine, and villain.

Criticism and use

Critics sometimes use "stock character" pejoratively, implying lazy writing or reliance on cliché. Defenders note that stock characters provide a shared shorthand between author and audience, can be subverted for comic or dramatic effect, and serve as building blocks that may be deepened into fully rounded characters. E. M. Forster's distinction between "flat" and "round" characters in Aspects of the Novel (1927) is frequently invoked in discussions of the technique.
