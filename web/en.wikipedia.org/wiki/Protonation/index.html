Protonation

In chemistry, protonation (or hydronation) is the adding of a proton (or hydron, H+) to an atom, molecule, or ion, forming a conjugate acid. It is the fundamental step in most Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reactions: an acid donates a proton to a base, and the base is thereby protonated.

Examples

  • Protonation of ammonia by water or an acid gives the ammonium ion, NH4+.
  • Protonation of water gives the hydronium ion, H3O+.
  • Protonation of alkenes initiates many acid-catalysed reactions, generating carbocations.
  • In mass spectrometry, protonation of analyte molecules (as in electrospray ionisation) is the usual route to the ions being measured.

Protonation and deprotonation

The reverse process — removal of a proton from a (conjugate) acid — is deprotonation. For a given species, the equilibrium between protonated and deprotonated forms in solution is governed by its pKa and the pH of the medium, as described by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. Protonation changes many properties of a molecule: it adds a positive charge, typically makes the species more hydrophilic, and often alters its reactivity and spectroscopic signature.

Rates

Proton transfers to and from electronegative atoms such as oxygen and nitrogen are usually extremely fast, often diffusion-controlled. Protonation at carbon is typically much slower because it requires greater electronic reorganisation — one reason carbon acids can show measurable kinetic acidity effects. Protonation steps are central to enzyme catalysis, where amino-acid side chains in active sites act as proton shuttles.
