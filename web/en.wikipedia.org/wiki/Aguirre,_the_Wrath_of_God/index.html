Aguirre, the Wrath of God (German: Aguirre, der Zorn Gottes) is a 1972 West German epic historical drama film written and directed by Werner Herzog. Klaus Kinski stars as the Spanish conquistador Lope de Aguirre, who leads a doomed expedition down the Amazon River in search of the legendary city of gold, El Dorado. The film is widely regarded as one of the greatest films ever made and a foundational work of the New German Cinema.

Plot

In 1560, a large Spanish expedition under Gonzalo Pizarro descends from the Andes into the Amazon rainforest in search of El Dorado. When progress stalls, a scouting party of forty men is sent ahead on rafts under Don Pedro de Ursúa, with Lope de Aguirre as his second-in-command. Aguirre, consumed by ambition, mutinies, installs a puppet emperor, and drives the shrinking party ever deeper into the jungle, where starvation, disease, hostile attacks and his own escalating madness destroy them one by one. In the final scene, Aguirre drifts alone on a raft overrun with monkeys, ranting about founding a dynasty.

Production

Herzog shot the film on location in the Peruvian rainforest on a very small budget, using a camera he famously stole from the Munich film school. The production was marked by the turbulent relationship between Herzog and the volatile Kinski, later dramatised in Herzog's documentary My Best Fiend (1999). The hypnotic electronic score was composed by the German band Popol Vuh.

Reception and legacy

The film was a major critical success on its release in the United States in 1977 and has appeared on numerous critics' lists of the greatest films. Its account of megalomania in the wilderness influenced Francis Ford Coppola's Apocalypse Now (1979), and its final shot — Aguirre alone on the raft — is among the most famous images in cinema.
