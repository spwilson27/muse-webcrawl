Social construct

Money, borders and marriage are frequently cited examples of social constructs

Key thinkersPeter L. Berger, Thomas Luckmann, John Searle, Ian Hacking
Common examplesMoney, citizenship, gender roles, race categories, deviance

A social construct is a concept, category or institution that exists because people collectively agree to behave as if it exists, or agree to follow certain conventional rules, rather than because it exists independently in the physical world. Money is the classic example: a banknote has value only because a community treats it as valuable. Other commonly cited social constructs include national borders, property rights, marriage, social class, gender roles, and categories of race.

Overview

To call something a social construct is not necessarily to say that it is unreal, arbitrary or unimportant. Constructed institutions have real, sometimes life-and-death, consequences: a person without money cannot buy food, and crossing a border without permission has material effects. The claim is rather about how the thing exists — its existence depends on collective human agreement, practices and institutions, and it could in principle be arranged differently, as comparison across cultures and historical periods shows.

History of the idea

The idea has roots in the work of Giambattista Vico, who held that humans can know the civil world because they made it, and in German idealism and phenomenology. Its modern form comes largely from Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann's The Social Construction of Reality (1966), which described how human activity creates institutions that then confront their creators as objective facts. Philosopher John Searle later analysed "institutional facts" created by constitutive rules of the form "X counts as Y in context C" — for example, a piece of paper counts as money. Ian Hacking's The Social Construction of What? (1999) surveyed the many different things the label has been applied to and urged asking what purpose each construction claim serves.

Debate

Constructionist claims are contentious in areas such as race, gender and mental illness. Critics argue that the label is sometimes applied too loosely, eliding the biological or material constraints on a category; defenders respond that recognising construction leaves room for such constraints while highlighting the contingent, changeable elements. Philosophers such as Sally Haslanger have developed "ameliorative" analyses that ask not only how categories are constructed but how they should be reconstructed to serve justice.
