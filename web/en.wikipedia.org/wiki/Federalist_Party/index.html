The Federalist Party was a political party in the United States active from the late 1780s until the mid-1820s. It was the first political party in the United States, formed by supporters of Alexander Hamilton, the first Secretary of the Treasury, who favored a strong national government, a national bank, close commercial ties with Britain, and policies promoting commerce and manufacturing. Its principal rival was the Democratic-Republican Party of Thomas Jefferson and James Madison.

History

The party coalesced during George Washington's first administration (1789–1793) around Hamilton's financial program: federal assumption of state debts, funding of the national debt, a national bank, and excise taxes. Washington himself remained formally nonpartisan but generally leaned toward Hamiltonian policies. John Adams, the party's second leader, served as the only Federalist president (1797–1801).

Policies

Federalists supported a strong central government with implied powers under the Constitution, a standing army and navy, the Bank of the United States, protective tariffs, and commercial alignment with Great Britain over revolutionary France. During the Quasi-War crisis of 1798, a Federalist Congress passed the controversial Alien and Sedition Acts, which damaged the party's popularity.

Decline

The Federalists lost the presidency and Congress in the election of 1800 and never regained national power, though they remained influential in New England and dominated the federal judiciary through Chief Justice John Marshall. Opposition to the War of 1812 culminated in the Hartford Convention (1814–1815), whose perceived disloyalty destroyed the party's reputation. It effectively dissolved by the mid-1820s, its voters absorbed into the National Republican and later Whig parties.

Legacy

Federalist ideas—a broad construction of the Constitution, a national financial system, and judicial review—shaped the early republic. The Federalist Papers (1787–1788), essays by Hamilton, Madison, and Jay written to promote ratification of the Constitution, remain a foundational text of American political thought, though they predated the party itself.
