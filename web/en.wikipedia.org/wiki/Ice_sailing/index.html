Ice sailing

Ice sailing, also called ice yachting or ice boating, is the sport of sailing and racing iceboats, also called ice yachts. Iceboats evolved from ordinary sailing ships and boats that were adapted for travel over ice, and later became specialized racing craft supported on steel runners (skates) that glide over frozen lakes and rivers.

The craft

A typical iceboat has a fuselage-like hull mounted on a triangular frame of three runners: one steering runner at the bow (or, in stern-steerers, at the stern) and two side runners on a cross-plank. Because friction with the ice is minimal, iceboats can sail several times faster than the true wind speed; racing DN-class boats reach 80 km/h and large yachts have exceeded 140 km/h.

History

The sport originated in the Netherlands in the 17th and 18th centuries, where iceboats carried goods and passengers over frozen canals, and in North America on the Hudson River, where large stern-steering yachts raced in the 19th century — sometimes against trains. The International DN class, designed in 1937 from a newspaper competition, is now the most popular iceboat class in the world.

Racing

Races are held on large frozen lakes in northern Europe, Russia, Canada and the United States when ice conditions permit. World and continental championships are organised under the International DN Ice Yacht Racing Association and national ice sailing associations.
