Moroccan architecture

Moroccan architecture reflects Morocco's diverse geography and long history, marked by successive waves of settlers through both migration and military conquest. This architectural heritage includes ancient Roman sites, historic Islamic architecture, historic colonial-era buildings, and modern architecture from the 20th century onwards.

Islamic period

From the Idrisid dynasty onward, Morocco developed a distinctive western Islamic style related to that of al-Andalus, sometimes grouped as Hispano-Moorish or Maghrebi architecture. Hallmarks include horseshoe arches, riad courtyards, intricate carved plaster (tadelakt and stucco), carved cedar wood, and zellige — geometric mosaic tilework. Monumental examples include the Kutubiyya Mosque and the Marinid madrasas of Fes, such as the Bou Inania Madrasa, and the Saadian Tombs in Marrakesh.

Vernacular architecture

In the Atlas Mountains and pre-Saharan valleys, Amazigh (Berber) communities built fortified villages (ksar) and collective granary-fortresses (kasbah) of rammed earth (pisé) and mud brick, exemplified by Aït Benhaddou. Coastal cities feature whitewashed medinas with Portuguese and Spanish influences.

Colonial and modern

During the French Protectorate (1912–1956), colonial authorities under Resident General Lyautey and urban planner Henri Prost built new European-style quarters (villes nouvelles) beside preserved historic medinas, mixing Art Deco, Neo-Mauresque and modernist styles, most notably in Casablanca. Post-independence architecture has continued to combine modern construction with traditional ornament.
