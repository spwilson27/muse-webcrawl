The diatonic scale of C major, consisting only of the white keys of the piano.

In music theory, a diatonic scale is any heptatonic (seven-note) scale that includes five whole steps (whole tones) and two half steps (semitones) in each octave, in which the two half steps are separated from each other by either two or three whole steps, depending on their position in the scale. This pattern ensures that, in a diatonic scale spanning more than one octave, all the half steps are maximally separated from each other by two or three whole steps.

The seven pitches of any diatonic scale can also be obtained by using a chain of six perfect fifths. For instance, the seven natural pitches that form the C-major scale can be obtained from a stack of perfect fifths starting from F: F–C–G–D–A–E–B.

History

Western music from the Middle Ages through the common practice period is built on diatonic scales and their associated modes, originally organised around the church modes. The major scale and natural minor scale are the diatonic modes known as Ionian and Aeolian. Medieval theorists derived the diatonic genus from ancient Greek music theory, in which the diatonic tetrachord was one of three genera alongside the chromatic and enharmonic.

Modes

Starting the same collection of seven pitches on each successive degree yields the seven diatonic modes:

  • Ionian — the major scale (from C: C D E F G A B)
  • Dorian — from D
  • Phrygian — from E
  • Lydian — from F
  • Mixolydian — from G
  • Aeolian — the natural minor scale, from A
  • Locrian — from B

Properties

The diatonic scale is a maximally even scale: its seven notes are distributed as evenly as possible within the octave. It contains exactly one tritone, between the fourth and seventh degrees of the major scale, which gives tonal music much of its directed tension and resolution. Every diatonic collection has a unique key signature in standard notation.
