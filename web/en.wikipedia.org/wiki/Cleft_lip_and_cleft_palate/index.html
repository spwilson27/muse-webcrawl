Cleft lip and cleft palate

Cleft lip and cleft palate
Specialty: Oral and maxillofacial surgery, paediatrics
Incidence: About 1 in 700 live births (varies by population)
Causes: Genetic and environmental factors
Treatment: Reconstructive surgery, speech therapy

Cleft lip and cleft palate are variations of a type of clefting congenital deformity caused by abnormal facial development during gestation. A cleft is a fissure or opening — a gap — in the upper lip, the roof of the mouth (palate), or both. They are among the most common birth defects worldwide.

Signs and symptoms

A cleft lip may be unilateral or bilateral, and ranges from a small notch to a complete split extending into the nose. A cleft palate can involve the soft palate alone or extend through the hard palate. Affected children may have difficulties feeding, frequent ear infections and hearing problems, dental abnormalities, and speech difficulties.

Causes

In most cases the cause is a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental influences during the first weeks of pregnancy, when the facial structures fuse. Risk factors include maternal smoking, alcohol use, diabetes, obesity and deficiency of folic acid. Clefts also occur as part of more than 300 syndromes, such as van der Woude syndrome.

Treatment

Treatment is provided by a multidisciplinary team, typically involving surgeons, orthodontists, speech therapists, audiologists and psychologists. Cleft lip repair is usually performed within the first months of life, and palate repair before 18 months to support speech development. Outcomes in well-resourced settings are generally excellent. Charities such as Smile Train and Operation Smile fund repairs in low-income countries, where untreated clefts can cause severe stigma.
