Monte Testaccio

LocationTestaccio, Rome, Italy
Heightabout 35 m (115 ft)
Circumferenceabout 1 km
Created1st – mid-3rd century AD

Monte Testaccio (sometimes spelled Monte Testaceo) is an artificial mound in Rome, composed almost entirely of testae — fragments of broken Roman amphorae dating from the time of the Roman Empire. It is one of the largest spoil heaps of the ancient world, with a volume of roughly 550,000 cubic metres, and gives its name to Rome's Testaccio rione (district), the 20th district of the city.

Origin and composition

The hill grew between the 1st century AD and the middle of the 3rd century AD as a purpose-managed dump for the broken oil amphorae unloaded at Rome's nearby Tiber port, the Emporium. The great majority of the vessels are Dressel 20 amphorae, large globular jars of about 70 litres capacity that carried olive oil from Baetica (the Guadalquivir valley in modern Andalusia, Spain) to Rome under a state-organised supply system. It is estimated that the mound contains the remains of some 53 million amphorae, which carried roughly 6 billion litres of olive oil. The amphorae were broken deliberately, stacked in terraces, and sprinkled with lime to neutralise the rancid oil.

Inscriptions and research

Many sherds bear painted inscriptions (tituli picti) and stamps recording the oil's weight, the names of exporters, merchants, and customs officials, and consular dates — an unmatched archive for the economic history of the Roman Empire. Excavations and surveys, notably by Heinrich Dressel in the 19th century and by Spanish teams since the 1980s, have used these inscriptions to reconstruct the administration of Rome's food supply.

Later history

In the Middle Ages and Renaissance the hill became a site of popular festivals and jousts, and its cool storage caves (grottini), cut into the mound, were used to store wine; the district around it later became a working-class quarter of slaughterhouses and workshops. Today the hill is a protected archaeological site, visitable by guided tour.
