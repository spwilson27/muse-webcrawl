Bilateria

KingdomAnimalia
SubkingdomEumetazoa
CladeParaHoxozoa
CladeBilateria (Hatschek, 1888)
Major groupsProtostomia, Deuterostomia, Xenacoelomorpha
Fossil rangeEdiacaran – present

Bilateria is a large clade of animals characterised by bilateral symmetry — a body plan with left and right sides that are mirror images — during embryonic development. The group includes the great majority of animal phyla, including arthropods, molluscs, annelids, echinoderms, and chordates (including humans). Animals outside Bilateria include sponges, cnidarians, ctenophores, and placozoans.

Body plan

Bilaterians typically have a front (anterior) end with sense organs concentrated in a head region — a tendency called cephalisation — and a through-gut with a mouth and anus. They are triploblastic, developing from three germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm. Most possess a coelom or a reduced body cavity, though some groups have secondarily lost it. Some bilaterians, notably adult echinoderms, have secondarily evolved radial symmetry while retaining bilaterally symmetric larvae.

Evolution

The earliest widely accepted bilaterian fossil is Kimberella from the Ediacaran period, about 555 million years ago; burrows possibly made by bilaterians occur somewhat earlier. Molecular clock estimates often place the origin of Bilateria deeper in the Neoproterozoic. The last common ancestor of all living bilaterians, sometimes called Urbilateria, is thought to have been a small, simple worm-like animal living at the end of the Precambrian.

Classification

Bilateria is divided principally into the Protostomia (including the spiralian and ecdysozoan clades) and the Deuterostomia (chordates, echinoderms, hemichordates, and allies). The simple marine worms of Xenacoelomorpha are now generally placed as the sister group to the remaining bilaterians (Nephrozoa). This phylogenetic framework, built largely from molecular data, has substantially revised older morphology-based classifications.
