Spaghetti-tree hoax

The spaghetti-tree hoax was a three-minute April Fools' Day hoax segment broadcast on the BBC current affairs programme Panorama on 1 April 1957. It showed a family in the canton of Ticino in southern Switzerland gathering a bumper spaghetti harvest, with strands of pasta being pulled from trees and laid out to dry in the sun.

The broadcast

The segment was narrated by the respected broadcaster Richard Dimbleby, whose authoritative delivery lent the report credibility. He explained that the mild winter had nearly wiped out the spaghetti weevil and that Swiss growers were enjoying an exceptional crop. Footage showed workers harvesting spaghetti from trees and a traditional "harvest festival" meal. Dimbleby concluded by advising home growers to "place a sprig of spaghetti in a tin of tomato sauce and hope for the best."

Reaction

An estimated eight million people watched the programme. Hundreds phoned the BBC asking how to grow their own spaghetti trees; the BBC reportedly replied, deadpan, with the advice given in the segment. At the time, spaghetti was still an exotic food for many Britons, sold mainly in tins in tomato sauce, and few viewers knew how pasta was made. The idea for the hoax came from cameraman Charles de Jaeger, and the segment cost about £100 to produce. It is regularly cited as one of the most successful and beloved media hoaxes of all time, and Panorama's editor Michael Peacock approved it for broadcast on April Fools' Day.
