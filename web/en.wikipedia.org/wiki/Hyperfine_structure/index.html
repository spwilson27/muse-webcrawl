Hyperfine structure

Hyperfine splitting of the hydrogen ground state gives rise to the 21 cm line.

In atomic physics, hyperfine structure is a small perturbation of the energy levels of atoms, molecules and ions, due to the interaction between the nucleus and the electrons. It is a small effect compared to the fine structure, arising from the coupling of the electron's angular momentum with the magnetic dipole moment and electric quadrupole moment of the nucleus.

Causes

The dominant contribution is magnetic: the nuclear spin creates a magnetic field that interacts with the magnetic field produced by the electrons. A second contribution comes from the electric quadrupole moment of nuclei with spin greater than 1/2, which interacts with the electric field gradient at the nucleus. The resulting splittings are typically in the megahertz to gigahertz range of frequencies, roughly a thousand times smaller than fine-structure splittings.

The 21 cm line

The most famous hyperfine transition is the spin-flip transition of neutral hydrogen, between the parallel and antiparallel alignments of the electron and proton spins. Its energy corresponds to a photon of wavelength 21 cm and frequency 1420.4 MHz. Predicted by Hendrik van de Hulst in 1944 and detected in 1951, the hydrogen line lets radio astronomers map neutral hydrogen throughout the Milky Way and other galaxies, since it passes almost unimpeded through interstellar dust.

Applications

Hyperfine transitions provide the basis of the most precise clocks: the caesium standard defines the second using the hyperfine transition of caesium-133 at exactly 9,192,631,770 Hz, and hydrogen masers exploit the 21 cm transition. Hyperfine interactions are also measured in Mössbauer spectroscopy and electron paramagnetic resonance.
