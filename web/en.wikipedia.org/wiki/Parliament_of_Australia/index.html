The Parliament of Australia (also called the Federal Parliament) is the federal legislature of Australia. It consists of three elements: the monarch (represented by the governor-general), the Senate, and the House of Representatives. The combination of two elected chambers, in which the members of the upper house represent the states and territories on a roughly equal basis while members of the lower house represent electoral divisions according to population, follows the model of the United States Congress, adapted to the Westminster system of responsible government.

History

The Parliament was established on 1 January 1901 by the Constitution of Australia following the federation of the six Australian colonies. It first met in Melbourne, and moved to Canberra in 1927, sitting in the Provisional Parliament House (now Old Parliament House) until the new Parliament House opened on 9 May 1988.

Composition

The Senate has 76 members: 12 from each of the six states, and two each from the mainland territories. Senators are elected by proportional representation for six-year terms, with half facing election every three years. The House of Representatives has 151 members elected from single-member divisions using preferential voting, generally for three-year terms.

Functions

The Parliament makes and amends federal law, represents the people, scrutinises the executive through question time and committees, and authorises the raising and spending of public money. The government is formed by the party or coalition holding a majority in the House of Representatives, and the prime minister sits in that chamber.
