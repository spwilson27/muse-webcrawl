Peak bagging

Peak bagging or hill bagging is an activity in which hikers, climbers and mountaineers attempt to reach the summits of a collection of peaks, usually those above a certain height or prominence, or those on a defined list. The goal is typically recorded on a scorecard or in a logbook, and completers of famous lists are often formally recognised by the club or organisation that maintains the list.

A peak bagger at a summit cairn in the mountains.

History

The activity grew out of 19th- and early-20th-century mountaineering culture in Britain and the United States. In Scotland, Sir Hugh Munro's 1891 list of mountains over 3,000 feet — the Munros — inspired generations of "Munro baggers"; the first recorded completion, or "compleation", was by the Reverend A. E. Robertson in 1901. In the United States, Robert Marshall and his companions completed the 46 High Peaks of the Adirondacks in the 1920s–30s, founding the Adirondack Forty-Sixers club.

Notable lists

  • Munros — 282 Scottish peaks over 3,000 ft (914.4 m), with related lists of Corbetts, Grahams and Donalds.
  • Wainwrights — 214 fells in England's Lake District described by Alfred Wainwright.
  • Adirondack High Peaks — 46 peaks in New York traditionally listed at over 4,000 ft.
  • Colorado fourteeners — 50+ peaks over 14,000 ft (4,267 m) in Colorado.
  • New Hampshire 48 — the 48 White Mountain peaks over 4,000 ft.
  • Seven Summits — the highest mountain on each continent.
  • Eight-thousanders — the 14 peaks over 8,000 m, all in the Himalaya and Karakoram.

Peak bagging and the environment

The popularity of list-based hiking has been criticised for concentrating foot traffic and erosion on famous peaks while less celebrated hills go unnoticed, though defenders argue it draws people outdoors and spreads visits across entire ranges rather than single honeypot summits.
