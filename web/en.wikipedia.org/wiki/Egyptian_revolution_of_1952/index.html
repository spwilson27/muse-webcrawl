Egyptian revolution of 1952

Crowds in Cairo during the July 1952 revolution

The Egyptian revolution of 1952, also known as the 23 July Revolution, was a period of profound political, economic and societal change in Egypt. On 23 July 1952, the Free Officers Movement, led by Muhammad Naguib and Gamal Abdel Nasser, overthrew King Farouk in a coup d'état, ending the Muhammad Ali dynasty and, a year later, the monarchy itself.

Background

Discontent had built over corruption, the humiliation of the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, continued British occupation of the Suez Canal Zone, and the widely resented 1952 Cairo fire. The Free Officers, a clandestine group of young army officers, seized key installations in Cairo on the night of 22–23 July with almost no bloodshed; Farouk abdicated on 26 July and went into exile.

Aftermath

The Revolutionary Command Council dissolved political parties, enacted land reform breaking up large estates, and in June 1953 abolished the monarchy and proclaimed a republic, with Naguib as president. After a power struggle, Nasser became president in 1956, nationalized the Suez Canal — provoking the Suez Crisis — and pursued Arab socialist policies and pan-Arab leadership. The revolution reshaped Egypt into a republic dominated by the military, a pattern that persisted for decades, and inspired nationalist movements across the Arab world.
