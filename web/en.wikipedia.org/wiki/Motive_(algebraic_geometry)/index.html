Motive (algebraic geometry)

In algebraic geometry, a motive (or sometimes motif, the French original) is a conjectural universal cohomology object that underlies the various cohomology theories of algebraic varieties — Betti, de Rham, ℓ-adic, and crystalline — in the same way that an abelian variety's structure unifies its different realizations. The theory was envisioned by Alexander Grothendieck in the 1960s.

Motivation

The Weil conjectures, proved through étale cohomology, suggested that all Weil cohomology theories of a smooth projective variety arise from a single object. Grothendieck proposed that this object should be constructed by cutting varieties into pieces using algebraic correspondences: idempotent correspondences split a variety's "motive" into simpler summands, such as the Picard and Albanese parts of a curve.

Construction and developments

The category of pure motives is built from smooth projective varieties with morphisms given by correspondences modulo an adequate equivalence relation (rational, homological, or numerical). Yuri Manin and others developed the formalism; the finest well-behaved theory uses numerical equivalence, while full conjectural properties — the standard conjectures on algebraic cycles — remain unproved. For non-proper or non-smooth varieties, mixed motives were later developed, notably by Vladimir Voevodsky, whose triangulated categories of motives earned a Fields Medal and underlie modern motivic homotopy theory and motivic cohomology.
