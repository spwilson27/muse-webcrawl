Soldado de cuera

The soldados de cuera ("leather soldiers") were frontier cavalrymen of the Spanish Empire in northern New Spain during the 18th and early 19th centuries. They garrisoned the line of presidios stretching across what is now the southwestern United States and northern Mexico, from Texas to Alta California, defending missions, settlements, and supply routes against Apache, Comanche, and other Indigenous raiders.

Equipment

Their name derives from the cuera, a heavy sleeveless coat of up to seven layers of buckskin that could turn arrows and even stop some musket balls at range. A soldado typically carried a lance, a short sword (espada ancha), a carbine or escopeta, pistols, and an oval or heart-shaped leather shield (adarga) of Moorish-derived design. Each man was required to maintain several horses and a mule so that remounts were available on long patrols.

Service

Regulations issued in 1729 and especially the Royal Regulations of 1772 standardised their armament, uniform (a blue short jacket with red cuffs and collar), and duties. Their ranks were filled largely by mestizo and frontier-born recruits, and service was arduous and poorly paid. After Mexican independence in 1821, the presidial companies declined for lack of funding, though some continued into the 1830s and 1840s.
