Electromagnetism

[Image: a diagram of the electromagnetic spectrum — a rainbow band labelled from radio waves to gamma rays]

In physics, electromagnetism is an interaction that occurs between particles with electric charge. It is one of the four fundamental interactions of nature, alongside gravity and the strong and weak nuclear interactions, and it is described by the electromagnetic field and Maxwell's equations. Electromagnetic forces are responsible for the structure of atoms and molecules, and hence for chemistry and almost all everyday phenomena except gravity.

The electromagnetic spectrum — radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays — is radiation of this single field at different frequencies.

Unification

Electricity and magnetism were long studied separately: Ørsted showed in 1820 that an electric current deflects a compass needle, Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction (1831), and James Clerk Maxwell unified the phenomena mathematically in the 1860s, predicting electromagnetic waves travelling at the speed of light — and thereby identifying light itself as such a wave. Heinrich Hertz demonstrated radio waves experimentally in 1887–88.

In the twentieth century, quantum electrodynamics (QED), developed by Feynman, Schwinger and Tomonaga, described electromagnetism at the quantum level with extraordinary precision, and the electroweak theory unified it with the weak interaction.
