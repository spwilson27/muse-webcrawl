Levothyroxine, also known as L-thyroxine, is a synthetic form of the thyroid hormone thyroxine (T4), used to treat thyroid hormone deficiency (hypothyroidism), including its severe form, myxedema coma. It is also used to treat and prevent certain types of thyroid tumours and goitres. It is not indicated for weight loss. Levothyroxine is taken by mouth or given by injection into a vein. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is consistently one of the most commonly prescribed medications in the United States and the United Kingdom.

Medical uses

Levothyroxine replaces the hormone a healthy thyroid would produce. Most hypothyroidism results from Hashimoto's thyroiditis, thyroid surgery, or radioiodine treatment. A typical full replacement dose in a young healthy adult is about 1.6 micrograms per kilogram of body weight per day, taken once daily on an empty stomach; older patients and those with heart disease start lower and increase gradually. Dosing is monitored with TSH blood tests, usually checked six to eight weeks after any change because of the drug's long half-life of about seven days. Levothyroxine is also given to newborns with congenital hypothyroidism, where prompt treatment prevents intellectual disability, and at suppressive doses after treatment for certain thyroid cancers.

Side effects and interactions

Side effects are generally those of excessive thyroid hormone — effectively symptoms of hyperthyroidism: weight loss, tremor, headache, heat intolerance, anxiety, insomnia, rapid or irregular heartbeat, and, with long-term overtreatment, reduced bone density and atrial fibrillation. Absorption from the gut is impaired by food, coffee, calcium and iron supplements, and several medications, so consistent timing matters; many other drugs, including antacids and bile-acid sequestrants, interact. Because products have a narrow therapeutic index, regulators require tight potency specifications, and switching between brands or generics can shift hormone levels in some patients.

History

Thyroxine was first isolated in 1914 by Edward Kendall at the Mayo Clinic and its structure determined and synthesis achieved in the 1920s by Charles Harington and George Barger. Before synthetic levothyroxine became standard in the 1960s, hypothyroidism was treated with desiccated animal thyroid extract, in use since the 1890s. Synthetic levothyroxine gradually displaced it because of its consistent potency and long half-life, though combination T4/T3 therapy remains a topic of debate for the minority of patients who feel unwell on T4 alone.
