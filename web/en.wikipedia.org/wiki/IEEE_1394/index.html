IEEE 1394

TypeSerial bus
DesignedLate 1980s–1990s (Apple-led)
Speed100–800 Mbit/s (up to 3.2 Gbit/s in later revisions)
Also known asFireWire, i.LINK

IEEE 1394 is an interface standard for a serial bus for high-speed communications and isochronous real-time data transfer. It was developed in the late 1980s and 1990s by Apple, which trademarked the name FireWire, working with other companies including Sony, which marketed it as i.LINK, and Texas Instruments.

Features and history

The standard supports peer-to-peer communication without a host computer, hot plugging, bus power, and guaranteed bandwidth for isochronous streams, which made it popular for digital video camcorders (DV), external hard drives, professional audio interfaces and some early iPods. FireWire 400 (1394a) runs at 100–400 Mbit/s; FireWire 800 (1394b, 2003) doubled this and changed the connector. Later revisions raised speeds to 1.6 and 3.2 Gbit/s, though these saw little adoption.

Decline

Licensing disputes slowed early adoption against USB; after USB 2.0 (2000) and especially USB 3.0 and Thunderbolt, which absorbed its advantages, FireWire disappeared from consumer computers during the 2010s, surviving mainly in industrial, military and legacy audio/video equipment.
