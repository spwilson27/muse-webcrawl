Plausible deniability

Plausible deniability depends on keeping senior decision-makers insulated

Plausible deniability is the ability of a person or organisation, typically a senior official or a state, to deny knowledge of or responsibility for wrongdoing, because the evidence linking them to it is deliberately kept weak or absent. The concept is usually achieved by structuring chains of command and communication so that sensitive instructions are given informally, without records, allowing superiors to claim credibly that subordinates acted on their own initiative.

Origin

The phrase is associated with the United States intelligence community during the Cold War. A 1948 National Security Council directive on covert operations specified that such activities should be planned and executed so that the US government could "plausibly disclaim any responsibility" for them. The term entered wider public use during investigations of the CIA, and again during the Iran–Contra affair of the 1980s, when officials described arrangements designed to insulate the President from knowledge of arms sales and funding transfers.

In other fields

In cryptography, deniable encryption allows a sender to plausibly claim that encrypted data contains something other than its real content. The concept also appears in discussions of corporate governance, political campaigning and military covert action, where it is often criticised as a mechanism for evading accountability.
