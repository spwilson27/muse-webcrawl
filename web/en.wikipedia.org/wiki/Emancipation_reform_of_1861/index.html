The Emancipation reform of 1861 in Russia ended serfdom and freed more than 23 million serfs from personal bondage. Announced by Tsar Alexander II in his Emancipation Manifesto of 19 February 1861 (3 March in the New Style), it was the single most important of the Great Reforms undertaken during his reign.

Background

Serfdom had bound Russian peasants to the land and their landlords for centuries. Defeat in the Crimean War (1853–1856) exposed Russia's backwardness, and Alexander II famously told Moscow nobles in 1856 that it was better to abolish serfdom from above than to wait for it to be abolished from below. Secret committees and, from 1859, provincial editing commissions drafted the settlement.

Terms

Serfs gained personal freedom and could marry, own property, and trade without their landlord's permission. Household serfs were freed without land. Field serfs received allotments of land, but these were held through the village commune (mir) and peasants had to buy them through "redemption payments" spread over 49 years, with the state advancing 80 percent of the price to landlords. The resulting debt burdened peasants until the payments were cancelled in 1907.

Consequences

The reform transformed Russian society but left many grievances: allotments were often smaller and poorer than the land peasants had previously farmed, and communal controls restricted mobility. The shortcomings of the settlement contributed to rural unrest and, ultimately, to the pressures behind the Stolypin reforms and the Russian Revolutions of 1917.
