Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus

LocationCapitoline Hill, Rome
Dedicated509 BC (traditionally)
DeitiesJupiter, Juno, Minerva (Capitoline Triad)
StatusDestroyed; foundations survive

The Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, also known as the Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus, was the most important temple in Ancient Rome. It stood on the Capitoline Hill and was dedicated to the Capitoline Triad: Jupiter, his wife Juno, and Jupiter's daughter Minerva. The temple was the centre of Roman state religion and the destination of triumphal processions.

History

According to Roman tradition, construction began under the kings of Rome: Lucius Tarquinius Priscus vowed the temple, and his son or grandson, Tarquinius Superbus, raised much of the structure with Etruscan craftsmen, including the sculptor Vulca of Veii, who made the great terracotta cult statue of Jupiter and the quadriga on the roof. The temple was dedicated in 509 BC, the first year of the Roman Republic, by the consul Marcus Horatius Pulvillus.

The temple burned down three times — in 83 BC, in AD 69 during the civil wars of the Year of the Four Emperors, and again in AD 80 — and was rebuilt each time on the same plan, with increasing magnificence. The fourth and final version, rebuilt by Domitian, stood until the early Middle Ages, when it was progressively robbed of marble, gilding, and bronze.

Architecture

The temple rested on a high podium nearly 62 by 53 metres, one of the largest temple platforms in the Roman world. It followed the Etruscan temple plan: a deep porch with three rows of columns and three cellae side by side, the central and widest belonging to Jupiter, flanked by those of Juno and Minerva. Behind the temple, in the area known as the Area Capitolina, stood temples, altars, triumphal statues, and the records of treaties Rome had made, which were inscribed on bronze tablets and stored in the temple precinct.

Significance

The Capitoline temple was the symbolic heart of the Republic and Empire. Consuls took their vows there on entering office, triumphing generals ended their processions at its doors, and the Sibylline Books were kept beneath Jupiter's statue. Temples to Jupiter Optimus Maximus in provincial capitals across the empire were modelled on it, spreading the Capitoline plan throughout the Roman world. Substantial remains of the podium survive today, preserved partly within the Capitoline Museums.
