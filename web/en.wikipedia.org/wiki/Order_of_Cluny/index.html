Order of Cluny

The Order of Cluny (the Cluniac Order) was a Benedictine monastic order founded in 910 when Duke William I of Aquitaine donated land at Cluny in Burgundy for a monastery placed directly under the authority of the Pope, free from lay and episcopal interference. The order became the centre of the Cluniac reforms, a movement to restore strict observance of the Rule of Saint Benedict, elaborate liturgy and prayer for the dead throughout Western Christendom.

Organisation

Unlike earlier Benedictine practice, in which every abbey was autonomous, Cluny created a centralised order: all daughter houses were priories subject to the Abbot of Cluny, who appointed their priors. Under abbots such as Berno, Odo, Odilo and Hugh of Semur, the Cluniac network grew to more than a thousand houses across France, Spain, Italy, England and the Holy Roman Empire by the twelfth century.

Influence and decline

Cluny III, consecrated in 1095, was the largest church in Christendom until the rebuilding of St Peter's in Rome. The Cluniacs promoted the Peace and Truce of God movements, pilgrimage (notably to Santiago de Compostela) and Gregorian reform, and were important patrons of Romanesque architecture and chant. From the twelfth century the order faced criticism from new orders such as the Cistercians for its wealth and liturgical opulence, and its influence gradually waned, though the abbey survived until its suppression during the French Revolution.
