The Catcher in the Rye

The Catcher in the Rye is a novel by American author J. D. Salinger, published on 16 July 1951 by Little, Brown and Company. Narrated by the teenage Holden Caulfield from a rest home in California, it recounts a few days in New York City after his expulsion from prep school, in a voice that became the defining portrait of teenage alienation and rebellion in American literature. The novel has sold more than 65 million copies and remains both widely taught and frequently challenged in schools.

Plot

Holden Caulfield, sixteen, has been expelled from Pencey Prep in Pennsylvania for failing his subjects. Rather than go home early, he takes a train to New York, checks into the shabby Edmont Hotel, and wanders the city for two days: he goes to nightclubs, hires and then recoils from a prostitute, meets an old girlfriend, gets drunk, and visits his beloved younger sister Phoebe. Haunted by the death of his brother Allie and obsessed with the "phoniness" of adults, Holden dreams of being "the catcher in the rye" — standing at the edge of a cliff in a field of rye, catching children before they fall. His breakdown deepens until, watching Phoebe on a carousel, he accepts that children must be allowed to reach for the gold ring and risk falling. The frame narrative implies he is recovering in a sanatorium.

Themes and style

Themes include innocence and its loss, alienation, grief, identity, and the corruption of adulthood. Salinger's first-person vernacular — slang, repetition, digression — was revolutionary for its capture of adolescent speech and interiority and influenced generations of writers.

Reception and controversy

The novel was an immediate success and a Book-of-the-Month Club selection; it has been among the most taught American novels and also, because of its profanity and themes, among the most frequently banned or challenged in US schools. It acquired a darker notoriety through its association with several violent men who fixated on it, most notoriously Mark David Chapman, who murdered John Lennon in 1980 and was carrying a copy. Salinger, who never sold the film rights and guarded the book jealously, published nothing after 1965.
