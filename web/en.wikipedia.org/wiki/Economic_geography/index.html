Economic geography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Economic geography studies how economic activity is distributed across space

Economic geography is the subfield of human geography that studies economic activity and the factors that influence it — the location, distribution and spatial organisation of production, trade and consumption. It examines why industries cluster, how regions develop unevenly, and how transport, resources, labour and markets shape economic landscapes at scales from the local to the global.

Approaches

Early theory focused on optimal location: Johann Heinrich von Thünen modelled agricultural land use around a market town (1826), Alfred Weber developed least-cost industrial location theory (1909), and Walter Christaller's central place theory (1933) explained hierarchies of settlements. Twentieth-century work added regional science and neoclassical models; later Marxist and critical geographers stressed uneven development and the spatial divisions of labour, while the "new economic geography" associated with Paul Krugman formalised how increasing returns and transport costs produce core–periphery patterns. Contemporary economic geography also draws on evolutionary and institutional perspectives, studying industrial clusters and global production networks.

Topics
