Lloyd Allayre Loar (January 9, 1886 – September 14, 1943) was an American musician, instrument designer and acoustical engineer. He is best known for his work with the Gibson Mandolin-Guitar Company, where in the early 1920s he designed the company's celebrated "Master Model" instruments, including the F-5 mandolin and the L-5 archtop guitar. Instruments signed by Loar during his tenure at Gibson are among the most prized and valuable stringed instruments ever made.

Early life and career

Loar grew up in Illinois and showed early musical talent, performing as a mandolinist and later studying music theory and composition. He performed professionally in vaudeville and concert ensembles, developing a keen interest in the acoustics of stringed instruments. He joined Gibson in Kalamazoo, Michigan in 1919 as an acoustical engineer, at a time when the company was the leading maker of mandolin-family instruments in the United States.

Gibson Master Models

As Gibson's chief acoustical engineer, Loar applied principles he called "tap tuning" — voicing the carved top and back plates of instruments to specific notes — along with violin-style f-holes in place of round or oval soundholes, longer necks, and refined graduations. The Master Model line introduced in 1922–1923 comprised the F-5 mandolin, H-5 mandola, K-5 mandocello, A-5 mandola variants, and the L-5 guitar, the first modern archtop guitar and an ancestor of the jazz guitar. Each instrument bore a label with Loar's signature and a serial number; F-5 mandolins signed by Loar, particularly those from February 1923 and the batch signed February 18, 1924, are the most collectible mandolins in existence and are closely associated with bluegrass founder Bill Monroe.

Later work

Loar left Gibson in 1924 and pursued experiments in electric instruments, founding the Vivi-Tone company in the 1930s with Lewis A. Williams, which produced some of the earliest electrically amplified instruments, including an electric double bass and amplified keyboards. He also taught at Northwestern University. Though his electric ventures were commercially unsuccessful, they anticipated later developments in amplified instruments.

Legacy

Loar-signed Gibson instruments regularly sell for six-figure sums, and his designs remain the template for contemporary mandolin and archtop guitar building.
