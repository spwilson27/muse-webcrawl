Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic respiratory disease characterised by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation that is not fully reversible. The most common symptoms are breathlessness, cough and sputum production. Most people with COPD have both emphysema and chronic bronchitis, though the relative contribution of each varies. COPD is a progressive disease that typically worsens over time and can limit the ability to carry out routine activities.

Causes

The primary cause of COPD is tobacco smoking, which accounts for the majority of cases in high-income countries. Other risk factors include long-term exposure to lung irritants such as smoke from biomass fuels used for cooking and heating in poorly ventilated homes, occupational dusts and chemicals, outdoor air pollution, and genetic factors — most notably alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. Repeated respiratory infections in childhood may also contribute.

Pathophysiology

In COPD, chronic inflammation causes structural changes in the airways and lung parenchyma. In chronic bronchitis, inflammation and scarring narrow the small airways and increase mucus production. In emphysema, the walls of the alveoli are destroyed, reducing the surface area available for gas exchange and causing loss of elastic recoil, which leads to air trapping and hyperinflation. Airflow limitation is measured by spirometry: a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio of less than 0.70 confirms persistent airflow limitation.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually develop slowly and may not appear until significant lung damage has occurred. They include:

  • Shortness of breath, especially during physical activity
  • Wheezing and chest tightness
  • A chronic cough that may produce sputum
  • Frequent respiratory infections
  • Fatigue and, in advanced disease, weight loss and ankle swelling

People with COPD are prone to exacerbations — episodes of acute worsening, often triggered by infection or air pollution, that accelerate the decline in lung function.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on symptoms, exposure history and spirometry. The severity of airflow limitation is graded by the GOLD (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease) classification, from GOLD 1 (mild) to GOLD 4 (very severe), based on the predicted FEV1. Chest imaging and arterial blood gas measurements may be used to assess complications.

Management

There is no cure for COPD, but treatment can relieve symptoms and reduce exacerbations. Smoking cessation is the single most effective intervention to slow progression. Pharmacological therapy centres on inhaled bronchodilators — short- and long-acting beta-2 agonists and muscarinic antagonists — with inhaled corticosteroids added for patients with frequent exacerbations. Pulmonary rehabilitation improves exercise capacity and quality of life. Long-term oxygen therapy benefits patients with severe resting hypoxaemia, and vaccination against influenza, pneumococcus and COVID-19 is recommended. In selected patients with severe emphysema, lung volume reduction surgery or endobronchial valves may be considered, and lung transplantation is an option for a small minority.

Epidemiology

COPD affects roughly 200 million people worldwide and was the third leading cause of death globally in 2019, causing about 3.3 million deaths. Prevalence increases with age, and historically rates were higher in men, though in many high-income countries rates have equalised as smoking patterns have changed.
