Kurt Lewin

Kurt Lewin (9 September 1890 – 12 February 1947) was a German-American psychologist, known as one of the modern pioneers of social, organizational, and applied psychology in the United States. Exiled from the land of his birth, he made a new life for himself, in which he defined himself and his contributions within three lenses of analysis: applied research, action research, and group communication were his major offerings to the field of communication.

Life and career

Lewin was born in Mogilno, then in the Prussian province of Posen, and studied at Freiburg, Munich, and Berlin, receiving his doctorate under Carl Stumpf in 1916. Associated with the Gestalt school, he developed his "field theory", summarized in the famous formula B = f(P, E): behaviour is a function of the person and their psychological environment, the "life space". In 1933 he emigrated to the United States, working at Cornell, the University of Iowa (where he founded the Iowa Child Welfare Research Station group), MIT — where in 1945 he founded the Research Center for Group Dynamics — and Stanford. He died in 1947, shortly after founding the Commission on Community Interrelations and co-founding the journal Human Relations.

Contributions

Lewin coined the term "group dynamics" and, with Ronald Lippitt and Ralph White, conducted the classic 1939 studies of autocratic, democratic, and laissez-faire leadership climates. His change-management model of unfreezing–change–refreezing, sensitivity training (T-groups), and force field analysis became foundational in organizational development. He is frequently described as "the founder of modern social psychology", and his students included Leon Festinger, Stanley Schachter, and Dorwin Cartwright.
