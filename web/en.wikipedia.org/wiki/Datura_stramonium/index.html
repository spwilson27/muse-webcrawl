Datura stramonium

KingdomPlantae
OrderSolanales
FamilySolanaceae
GenusDatura
SpeciesD. stramonium

Datura stramonium, known by the common names thornapple, jimsonweed (jimson weed), devil's snare, or devil's trumpet, is a species of flowering plant in the nightshade family Solanaceae. Its likely origin is in Central America, and it has been introduced in many world regions as an invasive weed. It is an aggressive invader of disturbed soils and waste ground, and all parts of the plant are toxic when ingested.

Description

Datura stramonium is an erect annual herb forming a bush up to 60–150 cm (2–5 ft) tall. The root is long, thick, and fibrous. The stem is stout, smooth, and pale yellow-green, forking repeatedly. The leaves are soft, irregularly undulate-toothed, and covered with fine hairs. The fragrant flowers are trumpet-shaped, white to creamy or violet, 6–9 cm long, opening only in the evening and lasting a single night; they are pollinated by hawk-moths. The fruit is a walnut-sized, spiny capsule that splits into four chambers, each releasing numerous small black seeds.

Toxicity

All parts of the plant contain dangerous levels of tropane alkaloids — chiefly atropine, hyoscyamine, and scopolamine — which are potent anticholinergics. Ingestion can produce delirium, hyperthermia, tachycardia, photophobia, and amnesia, and may be fatal. The common name "jimsonweed" derives from Jamestown, Virginia, where in 1676 soldiers who ate the plant in a salad suffered mass poisoning, an episode recorded by Robert Beverley. Despite its toxicity, the plant has a long history in traditional medicine and ritual use, and its alkaloids remain pharmacologically important.

Cultivation and uses

The species has been used historically as a medicinal plant for asthma (smoked in cigarettes), as an analgesic, and in shamanic practices in both the Old and New Worlds. Commercial cultivation supplies atropine and scopolamine for pharmaceutical manufacture. Because of the risks and the variability of alkaloid content, self-medication is considered dangerous, and recreational use frequently results in hospitalisation.
