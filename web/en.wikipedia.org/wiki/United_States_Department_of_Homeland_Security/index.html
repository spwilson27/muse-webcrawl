The United States Department of Homeland Security (DHS) is the U.S. federal executive department responsible for public security, including counterterrorism, border security, immigration enforcement, cybersecurity, and disaster prevention and management. It was created in response to the September 11 attacks in the largest reorganization of the federal government since the creation of the Department of Defense in 1947.

History

President George W. Bush established the Office of Homeland Security in October 2001 under Tom Ridge. The Homeland Security Act of 2002 created the department, which on 1 March 2003 absorbed 22 agencies and roughly 180,000 employees from across the government, including the Coast Guard, the Secret Service, the Customs Service, the Immigration and Naturalization Service's enforcement functions, the Transportation Security Administration, and the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA).

Component agencies

Major components include U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), the Transportation Security Administration (TSA), the U.S. Coast Guard, the U.S. Secret Service, FEMA, and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA, established 2018). The department also operates the Federal Law Enforcement Training Centers and maintains the National Terrorism Advisory System.

Criticism

DHS has faced persistent criticism over mission sprawl, management challenges—it remained on the GAO's high-risk list for management integration for two decades—immigration enforcement practices, and civil-liberties concerns about surveillance and data collection, as well as proposals from across the political spectrum to restructure or abolish particular components.
