Oral rehydration therapy

Oral rehydration therapy (ORT) is a type of fluid replacement used to prevent and treat dehydration, especially due to diarrhea. It involves drinking water with modest amounts of sugar and salts, specifically sodium and potassium. Oral rehydration therapy can also be given by a nasogastric tube. Therapy should routinely include the use of zinc supplements. Use of oral rehydration therapy has been estimated to decrease the risk of death from diarrhea by up to 93%.

Side effects may include vomiting, high sodium, or high potassium. If vomiting occurs, it is recommended that use be paused for 10 minutes and then gradually restarted. The recommended formulation includes sodium chloride, sodium citrate, potassium chloride, and glucose. Glucose may be replaced by sucrose and sodium citrate may be replaced by sodium bicarbonate, if not available. It works as glucose increases the uptake of sodium and thus water by the intestines. A number of other formulations are also available including versions made at home. The use of homemade solutions, however, has not been well studied.

History

Oral rehydration therapy was developed in the 1940s using electrolyte solutions with or without glucose on an empirical basis chiefly for mild or convalescent patients, but did not come into common use for rehydration and maintenance therapy until after the discovery that glucose promotes sodium and water absorption during cholera in the 1960s. Key work was carried out in Dhaka, East Pakistan (now Bangladesh), and Calcutta by researchers including Dilip Mahalanabis, who famously applied the therapy on a large scale during the Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971, when cholera broke out among refugees. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Between 2006 and 2011, an estimated 1.5 billion ORS packets were distributed in developing countries. The therapy is credited with saving the lives of more than 50 million children worldwide since its introduction. The British medical journal The Lancet has called oral rehydration therapy "potentially the most important medical advance of the 20th century."

Preparation

The World Health Organization specifies an oral rehydration solution with reduced osmolarity containing 75 mmol/L sodium, 75 mmol/L glucose, 65 mmol/L chloride, 20 mmol/L potassium, and 10 mmol/L citrate, with a total osmolarity of 245 mOsm/L. The solution is typically supplied as a powder in sachets to be dissolved in one litre of clean water. A basic homemade solution can be prepared with six teaspoons of sugar and half a teaspoon of salt dissolved in one litre of clean water.

Mechanism

ORT exploits the sodium-glucose cotransport mechanism (SGLT1) in the small intestine. When glucose and sodium are present together in the intestinal lumen, the cotransporter carries both into the enterocyte, and water follows osmotically. Crucially, this mechanism remains intact during secretory diarrheas such as cholera, in which toxin-mediated secretion continues but absorption via the cotransporter is preserved.

Use in cholera and diarrhea

In cholera, rapid and large-volume stool losses can lead to hypovolemic shock and death within hours. Oral rehydration solution can replace most of these losses in all but the most severely dehydrated patients, who receive intravenous fluids first and transition to ORS as soon as they can drink. Zinc supplementation in children shortens the duration of diarrheal episodes, and continued feeding is recommended alongside therapy.
