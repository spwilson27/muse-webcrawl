A driveshaft (also called a propeller shaft or prop shaft) is a component for transmitting mechanical power, torque, and rotation, usually used to connect other components of a drivetrain that cannot be connected directly because of distance or the need to allow for relative movement between them. As torque carriers, driveshafts are subject to torsion and shear stress, equivalent to the difference between the input torque and the load.

Automotive driveshafts

In a front-engine, rear-wheel-drive vehicle, a long driveshaft runs the length of the car from the transmission to the differential, often through a raised tunnel in the floorpan. Because the rear axle moves with the suspension while the transmission is fixed to the body, the shaft incorporates universal joints (U-joints) and a slip yoke or splined section to accommodate changes in angle and length. Longer vehicles may use a two-piece shaft with a centre support bearing. Hotchkiss drive, the dominant open-shaft arrangement of the twentieth century, replaced the earlier torque tube, which enclosed the shaft in a rigid tube that also located the axle.

Construction

Shafts must be light yet strong, and are usually tubular: welded steel tube is standard, with aluminium and carbon-fibre shafts used in performance applications to cut rotating mass and raise the critical speed at which the shaft begins to whip. Yokes at each end carry needle-roller universal joints; front-wheel-drive and independent-rear-suspension cars instead use shorter half-shafts with constant-velocity (CV) joints, which transmit power smoothly through larger, continuously varying angles.

Other uses

Driveshafts appear in marine propulsion (the propeller shaft driven through a thrust bearing), in motorcycles with shaft drive (such as many BMW and Moto Guzzi models, where the shaft runs inside the swingarm), in locomotives, and in industrial machinery. The term also describes the human-powered shaft drives once fitted to bicycles as an alternative to chains.

Failure and maintenance

Worn universal joints typically announce themselves with clunking on gear changes, vibration at speed, or chirping sounds; loss of a shaft at speed can be destructive, so racing rules often require driveshaft safety loops. CV joint boots that split and lose grease are the common failure point of half-shafts.
