Corporate social responsibility

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) or corporate social impact is a form of international private business self-regulation which aims to contribute to societal goals of a philanthropic, activist, or charitable nature by engaging in, with, or supporting professional service volunteering through pro bono programs, community development, administering monetary grants to non-profit organizations for the public benefit, or to conduct ethically oriented business and managerial processes. A business model that helps a company be socially accountable to itself, its stakeholders, and the public, CSR allows companies to be conscious of the kind of impact they are having on all aspects of society, including economic, social, and environmental.

History

The modern discussion of CSR is often traced to Howard Bowen's 1953 book Social Responsibilities of the Businessman. In 1970, economist Milton Friedman published an influential critique arguing that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits within the rules of the game. In 1979 Archie Carroll proposed a four-part definition of CSR encompassing economic, legal, ethical, and philanthropic responsibilities, later presented as a "pyramid of CSR".

Approaches

Common approaches include corporate philanthropy, employee volunteering, environmentally conscious operations, ethical labour practices, and community development. CSR strategies encourage companies to make a positive impact on the environment and stakeholders including consumers, employees, investors, communities, and others. Related frameworks include the triple bottom line ("people, planet, profit"), benefit corporations, and environmental, social, and governance (ESG) criteria used by investors.

Regulation and standards

ISO 26000 provides guidance on social responsibility. Some jurisdictions mandate CSR reporting or spending: India's Companies Act 2013 requires certain companies to spend at least 2% of average net profits on CSR activities. In the European Union, directives on non-financial and sustainability reporting require large companies to disclose environmental and social information.

Criticism

Critics argue that CSR can function as "greenwashing" — public-relations activity that distracts from harmful core practices — and that voluntary self-regulation is no substitute for law. Others contend that CSR beyond legal compliance misuses shareholders' funds. Supporters respond that CSR can align long-term business interests with social welfare and manage reputational risk.
