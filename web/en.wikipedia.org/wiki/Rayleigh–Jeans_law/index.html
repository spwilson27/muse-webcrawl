Rayleigh–Jeans law

The Rayleigh–Jeans law is an approximation to the spectral radiance of electromagnetic radiation as a function of wavelength from a black body at a given temperature through classical arguments. It was derived by Lord Rayleigh in 1900, with corrections by James Jeans in 1905. The law agrees with experimental results at large wavelengths (low frequencies) but strongly disagrees at short wavelengths — a failure known as the ultraviolet catastrophe. Its breakdown was one of the problems that led Max Planck to formulate Planck's law and helped launch quantum mechanics.

The Rayleigh–Jeans law (red) diverges at short wavelengths, unlike Planck's law, which matches experiment.

Formulation

For wavelength λ, the law gives the spectral radiance of a black body at absolute temperature T as

Bλ(T) = 2ckBT / λ4

where c is the speed of light and kB is the Boltzmann constant. In terms of frequency ν, the equivalent form is

Bν(T) = 2ν2kBT / c2

Derivation

The classical derivation counts the standing electromagnetic waves (modes) inside a cavity; the number of modes per unit volume grows as ν². By the equipartition theorem of classical statistical mechanics, each mode carries an average energy of kBT regardless of frequency. Combining the mode density with this average energy yields the Rayleigh–Jeans law.

The ultraviolet catastrophe

Because the predicted energy density rises without bound as frequency increases, integrating the law over all frequencies gives an infinite total energy — an obviously unphysical result dubbed the "ultraviolet catastrophe" by Paul Ehrenfest in 1911. Planck resolved the problem in 1900 by postulating that energy is exchanged in discrete quanta, E = hν, which suppresses the high-frequency modes. The Rayleigh–Jeans law is recovered from Planck's law in the limit hν ≪ kBT, i.e. at long wavelengths.
