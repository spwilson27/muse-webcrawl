Jalal al-Din Mangburni (Persian: جلال‌الدین منگبرنی), also rendered Mingburnu or Manguberdi, was the last ruler of the Khwarazmian Empire, reigning from 1220 to 1231. The eldest son of Shah Muhammad II, he became legendary for his defiance of the Mongol invasion: after his father's empire collapsed before Genghis Khan, Jalal al-Din rallied resistance in Afghanistan, defeated Mongol forces at the Battle of Parwan (1221) — the Mongols' first major reverse — and escaped Genghis Khan himself by leaping on horseback into the Indus River.

Mongol invasion and Parwan

When the Mongols invaded in 1219, Muhammad II dispersed his forces among his cities, which fell one by one; he died in flight on an island in the Caspian Sea in late 1220, naming Jalal al-Din his successor. At Urgench, Jalal al-Din was undermined by his brothers' supporters and withdrew through Khorasan to Ghazni, gathering a large army of Khwarazmians, Afghans and Turks. In the spring of 1221 he destroyed a Mongol detachment near Parwan, north of Kabul — often reckoned the first substantial defeat inflicted on the Mongols during the western campaigns. The victory dissolved in quarrels over booty between his commanders, and when Genghis Khan advanced in person, the Khwarazmian army was crushed at the Battle of the Indus in November 1221. Jalal al-Din escaped by riding his horse off the high bank into the river and swimming across under a hail of arrows; Genghis Khan, watching, is said to have praised him to his sons as a model of a warrior.

Exile in India and return

Jalal al-Din spent about three years in the Indus valley and the Punjab, skirmishing with local rulers and the Delhi Sultanate of Iltutmish, who prudently refused him alliance against the Mongols. In 1224 he returned to Persia, re-established a kingdom centred on Azerbaijan and western Iran, fought the Seljuks of Rum and the Ayyubids, and twice captured Tbilisi from the Kingdom of Georgia, where his regime is remembered for harsh rule. His campaigns against fellow Muslim powers, including a war with the Abbasid Caliph, dissipated his strength while a new Mongol army under Chormaqan advanced west.

Death and legacy

Defeated by a Seljuk–Ayyubid coalition at the Battle of Yassıçemen in 1230 and then driven from Azerbaijan by the Mongols, Jalal al-Din fled into the mountains near Diyarbakır, where he was killed on 15 August 1231 — by a Kurdish assassin, according to most accounts. His exploits were recorded by his secretary and biographer al-Nasawi, and he became a heroic figure in Persian and Turkic tradition; the Uzbek national hero Jaloliddin Manguberdi lends his name to streets, a metro station in Tashkent and an order of merit in Uzbekistan.
