Industrial design

Industrial design is a professional service of creating and developing concepts and specifications that optimise the function, value and appearance of products and systems for the mutual benefit of both user and manufacturer. Industrial designers work on mass-produced goods — from automobiles, appliances and furniture to consumer electronics and medical devices — integrating ergonomics, materials, manufacturing processes, branding and aesthetics. The profession is legally recognised in many countries, and designs may be protected by design patents and registered designs.

History

The division between designing and making emerged with industrialisation in the 18th and 19th centuries: manufacturers such as Wedgwood employed artists to design goods for factory production. Reform movements — the Arts and Crafts movement, Art Nouveau and the German Werkbund (founded 1907, with Peter Behrens designing products and graphics for AEG) — sought to reconcile art and industry. The Bauhaus (1919–1933) became the most influential school of the new discipline.

In the United States, industrial design became a distinct profession in the 1920s–30s, when consultants such as Raymond Loewy (the streamlined PRR S1 locomotive, Studebaker cars), Henry Dreyfuss (the Western Electric Model 302 telephone) and Walter Dorwin Teague restyled consumer products, helping manufacturers compete on appearance as well as price.

Post-war and contemporary design

Post-war design was shaped by Scandinavian modernism, Italian firms such as Olivetti, and the German Ulm School (HfG Ulm), whose rationalist approach informed Dieter Rams's celebrated work for Braun and his "ten principles of good design". From the 1980s, computer-aided design transformed the workflow; in the 2000s, designers such as Jony Ive at Apple made industrial design central to the value of consumer electronics. Contemporary practice increasingly includes service design, interaction design and sustainability (design for disassembly and the circular economy).

Process

A typical process comprises research into users and markets, concept generation through sketching, development in CAD, prototyping (from foam models to 3D prints), testing, and refinement for manufacture with engineers. Designers balance the classic triad of form, function and production cost, and consider regulatory safety and accessibility standards.
