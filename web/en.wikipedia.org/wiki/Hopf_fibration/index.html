Hopf fibration

Hopf fibration
Discovered byHeinz Hopf (1931)
Total space3-sphere S³
Base space2-sphere S²
FibreCircle S¹
SignificanceFirst example of a nontrivial higher homotopy group, π₃(S²) = Z

The Hopf fibration (or Hopf bundle) is a map p : S³ → S² from the 3-sphere to the 2-sphere, discovered by Heinz Hopf in 1931, in which the preimage of each point is a circle. It describes the 3-sphere as a disjoint union of circles (fibres) over the 2-sphere, and it is the archetypal nontrivial fibre bundle: unlike the product S² × S¹, the total space is genuinely twisted, a fact reflected in the linking of any two fibres — each pair of distinct fibres has linking number one.

Construction

Identify S³ with the set of pairs (z₁, z₂) of complex numbers with |z₁|² + |z₂|² = 1. The map sends (z₁, z₂) to the point z₁/z₂ (or (2z₁z̄₂, |z₁|² − |z₂|²)) of the Riemann sphere S². Multiplication of both coordinates by a common unit complex number eiθ leaves the image unchanged, so each fibre is the orbit circle of this action. Equivalently, viewing S³ as the unit quaternions, the Hopf map sends a unit quaternion q to q i q⁻¹ on the sphere of imaginary unit quaternions.

Significance

Before Hopf's work it was not known whether the higher homotopy groups of spheres could be nontrivial beyond the sphere's own dimension. The fibration's long exact sequence shows π₃(S²) ≅ Z, generated by the Hopf map itself; its generator has Hopf invariant one. Generalisations — the real, complex, quaternionic and octonionic Hopf constructions — correspond to the four normed division algebras and yield fibrations S¹→S¹→S¹, S³→S²→S¹-family analogues, S⁷ → S⁴ and S¹⁵ → S⁸. The Hopf invariant one problem, asking which spheres admit maps of Hopf invariant one, was solved by J. Frank Adams (1960) using K-theory.

Geometry and visualisations

Under stereographic projection to R³, the fibres (other than one line) become circles lying on nested tori; rotating a fibre generates a torus foliated by Villarceau-like circles. The picture connects the Hopf fibration to the geometry of the Clifford torus and has analogues in physics, including magnetic monopoles, the Bloch sphere description of a qubit (where S³ is the space of normalised two-component spinors and S² the Bloch sphere), and liquid crystals.
