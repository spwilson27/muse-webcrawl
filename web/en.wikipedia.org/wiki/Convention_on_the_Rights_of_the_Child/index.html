Convention on the Rights of the Child

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) is an international human rights treaty which sets out the civil, political, economic, social, health and cultural rights of children (defined as persons under 18, unless majority is attained earlier). Adopted in 1989 and in force from 1990, it is the most widely ratified human-rights treaty in history — every UN member state is a party except the United States, which signed in 1995 but has never ratified (federalism, parental-rights and juvenile-justice objections recurring in its Senate debates).

Content

The Convention's four guiding principles are non-discrimination; the best interests of the child as a primary consideration; the right to life, survival and development; and respect for the child's views in matters affecting them (Article 12's participation right, its most innovative provision). Substantive articles cover identity, family life and alternative care, health, education, protection from violence, exploitation and armed conflict, and juvenile justice; optional protocols address children in armed conflict, the sale of children, and individual complaints. The Committee on the Rights of the Child monitors implementation through periodic reports. Debates — over child marriage ages, corporal punishment (banned in a growing list of states under CRC pressure), digital rights and children's climate litigation standing — show the Convention operating as both legal instrument and moral vocabulary of childhood worldwide.
