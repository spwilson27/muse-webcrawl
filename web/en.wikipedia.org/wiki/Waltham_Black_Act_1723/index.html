Waltham Black Act 1723

The Waltham Black Act 1723 (9 Geo. 1. c. 22) was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain that created a multitude of capital offences, aimed at the armed poaching gangs known as the "Blacks"—named for the practice of blackening their faces as camouflage. It is regarded as one of the harshest statutes of the "Bloody Code".

Background and provisions

The Act responded to organised poaching around Waltham Chase in Hampshire and Windsor Forest, where gangs ("Waltham Blacks" and "Windsor Blacks") clashed with keepers. It made it a capital felony, among other things, to appear armed in a forest, chase, or park with a blackened or otherwise disguised face; to hunt, wound, or steal deer; to poach fish; or to damage trees, cattle, or fishponds. Over time amendments expanded the number of offences under the so-called Black Act to some fifty capital crimes.

Later history

Historian E. P. Thompson analysed the Act in his 1975 book Whigs and Hunters as an instrument of the ruling class enforcing new property relations in the forests. Most of the Act was repealed in the criminal law reforms of the 1820s, and its remaining provisions were progressively abolished during the 19th century.
