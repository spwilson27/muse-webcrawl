Public domain

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The public domain consists of all creative works and knowledge to which no exclusive Intellectual property rights apply — because copyright has expired (the usual case), because the work was never eligible (facts, ideas, US federal government works), or because rights were waived (as by CC0 dedications). Public-domain material may be used by anyone for any purpose: it is the raw stock from which new works are built, from editions of Shakespeare to samples of old recordings.

Copyright terms (life plus 70 years in most of Europe and, since 1998, the United States) mean the public domain grows in annual waves — works entering it are celebrated on Public Domain Day (1 January). Critics of term extension (including the economists' brief in Eldred v. Ashcroft, 2003, which upheld the US extension) argue that delayed entry impoverishes culture without materially incentivising creation; defenders of strong rights emphasise investment in restoration and distribution. The concept also names what was never enclosed — the “intellectual commons” — and anchors movements for open access, free software and free culture that use licences (copyleft, Creative Commons) to keep works in circulation even where rights subsist.
