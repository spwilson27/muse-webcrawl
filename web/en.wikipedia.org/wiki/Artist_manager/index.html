Artist manager

An artist manager at work backstage

An artist manager (also called a talent manager or band manager) is an individual or company that guides the professional career of artists in the entertainment industry. The manager's responsibility is to oversee the day-to-day business affairs of an artist; advise and counsel them concerning professional matters, long-term plans and personal decisions that may affect their career; and assemble the team — agents, lawyers, publicists, accountants — around them.

Role

The manager is usually the artist's closest professional adviser and often the hub of their career. Duties commonly include negotiating contracts (or supervising the lawyers and agents who do), planning releases and tours with record labels and booking agents, coordinating marketing and media, managing budgets, hiring session and touring personnel, and long-term career strategy. Day-to-day tour logistics are usually handled by a separate tour manager.

Managers are typically paid by commission, traditionally around 15–20% of the artist's gross or net earnings in their field, a structure that has been debated as revenue sources have shifted from record sales to touring, merchandise and brand deals.

History and notable managers

The modern music manager emerged with the pop boom of the 1950s and 1960s. Celebrated — and sometimes controversial — examples include Colonel Tom Parker (Elvis Presley), Brian Epstein (the Beatles), Andrew Loog Oldham (the Rolling Stones), Peter Grant (Led Zeppelin), Malcolm McLaren (the Sex Pistols) and Scooter Braun. Famous management failures and disputes over money, including the cases of Billy Joel, the Backstreet Boys and *NSYNC with Lou Pearlstein, have driven demands for independent legal advice for artists before signing management agreements.

Distinction from agents

In many jurisdictions, notably California, talent agents are licensed and legally permitted to procure employment for artists, whereas managers are not; in practice the roles overlap, and the distinction has generated recurring legal disputes, including under the California Talent Agencies Act.
