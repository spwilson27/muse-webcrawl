The Wars of the Diadochi (Greek: Πόλεμοι των Διαδόχων, Pólemoi tōn Diadóchōn), or Wars of Alexander's Successors, were a series of conflicts fought between the generals of Alexander the Great — known as the Diadochi, or "Successors" — over who would rule his vast empire after his death in 323 BC. The wars ended in 281 BC with the empire permanently divided into several Hellenistic kingdoms.

Background

Alexander died in Babylon in June 323 BC leaving no competent adult heir: his half-brother Philip III Arrhidaeus was mentally unfit and his son Alexander IV was born after his death. At the Partition of Babylon the generals divided the satrapies among themselves under a regent, Perdiccas. Rivalries quickly produced open war: Perdiccas was murdered while invading Egypt in 320 BC, and a new settlement followed at Triparadisus under the regent Antipater.

The wars

Over four decades the Successors — among them Antigonus I Monophthalmus and his son Demetrius Poliorcetes, Ptolemy I Soter in Egypt, Seleucus I Nicator in the east, Lysimachus in Thrace and Cassander in Macedon — fought a bewildering sequence of wars and alliances across the eastern Mediterranean and Near East. Antigonus came nearest to reassembling the empire before being defeated and killed at the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC by a coalition of Seleucus, Lysimachus, Ptolemy and Cassander's forces. Alexander's surviving family was eliminated in the process: Alexander IV and Roxana were murdered around 309 BC, ending the Argead dynasty.

Conclusion and legacy

The last surviving Successors, Seleucus and Lysimachus, fought at Corupedium in 281 BC, where Lysimachus was killed; Seleucus himself was assassinated shortly afterwards. By 281 BC three great kingdoms had crystallised — the Seleucid Empire in Asia, Ptolemaic Egypt, and Antigonid Macedon — whose rivalries dominated the Hellenistic world until the Roman conquests of the following two centuries.
