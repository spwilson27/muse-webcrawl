Sweatt v. Painter, 339 U.S. 629 (1950), was a pivotal U.S. Supreme Court case that challenged the "separate but equal" doctrine first established in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). The Court held that the separate law school created for Black students in Texas was not equal to the University of Texas Law School reserved for white students, and ordered that Heman Marion Sweatt be admitted.

Background

In 1946, Heman Marion Sweatt, a Black postal worker from Houston, applied for admission to the University of Texas School of Law, then the state's only law school. His application was rejected solely because of his race, as Texas law prohibited integrated education. With support from the NAACP, Sweatt filed suit against Theophilus Painter, the university's president. The state court deferred ruling to give Texas time to create a separate law school for Black students, and in 1947 the state opened a law school at what became Texas Southern University.

Decision

The Supreme Court found that the hastily assembled separate school was grossly unequal: it lacked a comparable faculty, library, alumni network, and — crucially — the intangible advantages of prestige and interaction with the white majority, who would comprise most of the practicing bar. The Court ordered Sweatt's admission but stopped short of overturning Plessy outright. The decision, together with the companion case McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents decided the same day, undermined the legal foundation of segregation in graduate education and paved the way for Brown v. Board of Education (1954).

Legacy

Sweatt enrolled at UT Law in the fall of 1950 amid considerable hostility and left without graduating, but his case stands as a landmark in the long legal campaign against Jim Crow education. The former university admissions building in Austin was renamed the Heman Marion Sweatt Center for Black Males in his honor.
