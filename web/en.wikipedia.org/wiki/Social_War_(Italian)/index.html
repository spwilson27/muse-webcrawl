Social War (Italian)

The Social War (from Latin bellum sociale, "war of the allies"), also called the Italian War or the Marsic War, was fought from 91 to 87 BC between the Roman Republic and its Italian allies, the socii, who demanded Roman citizenship and a share in the political power their military service supported.

Causes

Italian allies supplied much of Rome's armies but lacked the franchise and were disadvantaged in land disputes. The assassination in 91 BC of the tribune Marcus Livius Drusus, who had proposed extending citizenship, convinced many allies that peaceful reform was impossible. The revolt began at Asculum and spread through central and southern Italy; the rebels set up a rival state, Italia, with its capital at Corfinium, renamed Italica, and minted their own coinage.

Course of the war

Roman commanders including Gaius Marius, Lucius Cornelius Sulla and Gnaeus Pompeius Strabo campaigned against the rebels. Rome combined military pressure with political concessions: the Lex Julia of 90 BC granted citizenship to loyal and surrendering communities, and the Lex Plautia Papiria extended it further, largely draining the rebellion of support. Fighting continued against diehard Samnite and Lucanian rebels until about 87 BC.

Consequences

The war cost perhaps 300,000 lives but achieved the rebels' central aim: virtually all free inhabitants of Italy south of the Po received Roman citizenship, unifying the peninsula politically. Registering the new citizens, however, became a contentious issue that fed the civil conflicts of the following decades.
