Pyrrhus (Greek: Πύρρος; 319–272 BC) was a Greek general and statesman of the Hellenistic period who became king of Epirus (306–302, 297–272 BC) and of Macedon (288–285, 274–272 BC). A second cousin of Alexander the Great, he was one of the most celebrated military commanders of antiquity, and the term "Pyrrhic victory" — a victory at such cost that it amounts to a defeat — originates from his campaigns against Rome.

Early life

Pyrrhus was the son of Aeacides, king of Epirus, and Phthia of Thessaly. After his father's deposition and death in battle when Pyrrhus was an infant, the child was carried off to the court of Glaucias, king of the Taulantii, who raised him. He regained the Epirote throne in 306 BC at the age of thirteen, lost it again, and was restored in 297 BC with the support of Ptolemy I Soter of Egypt.

Wars in Italy and Sicily

In 281 BC Pyrrhus answered the appeal of the Greek city of Tarentum against Rome, landing in Italy in 280 with a professional army including war elephants. He defeated the Romans at Heraclea (280 BC) and at Asculum (279 BC), but suffered heavy, irreplaceable losses among his veteran troops. According to Plutarch, after Asculum he remarked that another such victory would undo him. In Sicily (278–275 BC) he drove back the Carthaginians but alienated the Greek cities, and returning to Italy he was defeated by the Romans at Beneventum in 275 BC, after which he abandoned the western enterprise.

Later campaigns and death

Back in Greece, Pyrrhus seized the Macedonian throne in 274 BC but lost it to Antigonus II Gonatas. Invading the Peloponnese in 272 BC, he attacked Sparta and then Argos, where during street fighting he was struck on the head by a roof tile thrown by an old woman, stunned, and killed by an Argive soldier.

Legacy

Pyrrhus is remembered as one of the great tacticians of antiquity; Hannibal reportedly ranked him second only to Alexander. His name survives in "Pyrrhic victory".
