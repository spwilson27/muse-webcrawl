Illuminated manuscript

An illuminated page with a historiated initial and gold-leaf decoration.

An illuminated manuscript is a handwritten book (codex) in which the text is supplemented with such decoration as initials, borders and miniature illustrations. The term comes from the use of gold or silver leaf, which made the pages appear to glow or be "illuminated" by candlelight. Illuminated manuscripts were produced in Western Europe from late antiquity through the Middle Ages, and parallel traditions flourished in the Byzantine, Islamic and other manuscript cultures.

Production

Manuscripts were written on parchment (prepared animal skin) or vellum. Scribes copied the text, leaving spaces for decoration executed by illuminators: first a rough underdrawing, then gesso and gold leaf, then painting in mineral and plant pigments bound with egg white or gum, finished with burnishing of the gold and penwork detail. Major religious houses maintained scriptoria, and from the 13th century lay workshops in cities such as Paris and Bruges took over much production for wealthy patrons.

Types and famous examples

Illumination adorned Gospel books, psalters, books of hours, bestiaries, chronicles and legal texts. Celebrated examples include the Insular Book of Kells and Lindisfarne Gospels, the Carolingian Utrecht Psalter, the Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry by the Limbourg brothers (c. 1412–1416), and the lavish prayer books of Renaissance courts. Historiated initials, carpet pages and marginal drolleries are hallmarks of the craft.

Decline

Printed books from the mid-15th century gradually replaced manuscripts, though hand illumination continued on luxury printed books for a time. Many manuscripts were destroyed in the Reformation, the dissolution of the monasteries and later conflicts; surviving examples are prized holdings of national libraries and museums.
