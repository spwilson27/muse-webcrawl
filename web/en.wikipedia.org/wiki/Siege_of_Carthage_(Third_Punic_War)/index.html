The siege of Carthage was the main engagement of the Third Punic War, fought between the Roman Republic and the Punic city-state of Carthage in 149–146 BC. After nearly three years of blockade and assault, Roman forces under Scipio Aemilianus stormed the city in the spring of 146 BC, destroyed it systematically, and enslaved its surviving population, ending Carthaginian civilisation and annexing its territory as the Roman province of Africa.

Ancient Carthage occupied a fortified peninsula on the Gulf of Tunis, with formidable triple land walls.

Background

Despite its defeats in the first two Punic Wars, Carthage had recovered economically by the mid-2nd century BC, provoking fear and envy in Rome; Cato the Elder famously ended his speeches with the demand that "Carthage must be destroyed" (Carthago delenda est). When Carthage defended itself against the Numidian king Masinissa without Roman permission, Rome declared war in 149 BC. The Carthaginians surrendered their weapons and hostages but refused the final Roman demand — that they abandon their city and resettle inland — and resolved to resist.

The siege

Initial Roman assaults failed against Carthage's massive defences, and two years of incompetent command brought little progress. In 147 BC the young Scipio Aemilianus took command, restored discipline, completed the blockade by land and sea, and starved the city. In spring 146 BC the Romans breached the harbour defences, fought street by street for six days up to the Byrsa citadel, and accepted the surrender of the last defenders. The city was then burned and demolished; the later story that the Romans sowed the ruins with salt is a modern legend not found in ancient sources. Carthage was refounded as a Roman colony a century later under Julius Caesar and Augustus.
