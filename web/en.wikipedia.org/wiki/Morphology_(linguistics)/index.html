Morphology (linguistics)

In linguistics, morphology is the study of words, including the principles by which they are formed and their relationship to other words in the same language. Most approaches to morphology treat words as being made up of morphemes, the smallest units of meaning or grammatical function — for example, the word unbreakable consists of the morphemes un-, break, and -able. Morphology, together with phonology, syntax, and semantics, is one of the core components of grammar.

Basic concepts

Morphemes may be free (able to stand alone, like break) or bound (requiring attachment, like prefixes and suffixes). Variant pronunciations of a morpheme in different contexts are called allomorphs; English plural -s, for instance, is realised as /s/, /z/, or /ɨz/. The study of how morphemes combine is shaped by processes including affixation, reduplication, compounding, and internal changes such as ablaut (sing/sang).

Inflection and derivation

Inflectional morphology modifies a word to express grammatical categories such as tense, number, case, or agreement without changing its core meaning or word class: walk, walks, walked, walking. Derivational morphology creates new words, often of a different class: happy → happiness; teach → teacher. Languages differ greatly in how much inflection they use, from highly inflected languages like Latin or Finnish to largely isolating languages like Vietnamese.

Morphological typology

Languages are traditionally classified as isolating, agglutinative, fusional, or polysynthetic according to how morphemes combine. Turkish is a classic agglutinative example, chaining transparent suffixes; Inuit languages are polysynthetic, building very long words expressing what other languages need whole sentences to say.
