Millard Fillmore (January 7, 1800 – March 8, 1874) was the 13th president of the United States, serving from 1850 to 1853. A member of the Whig Party, he had been the 12th vice president under President Zachary Taylor, whose death he succeeded. Fillmore was the last Whig to hold the presidency.

Early life and career

Fillmore was born in a log cabin in Cayuga County, New York, and grew up in poverty. Apprenticed as a clothier, he largely educated himself, studied law, and was admitted to the bar in 1823. He built a successful legal practice in the Buffalo area, entered state politics as an Anti-Masonic candidate, and served in the U.S. House of Representatives and as New York State Comptroller. In Congress he chaired the Ways and Means Committee and helped write the Tariff of 1842.

Presidency

Elected vice president on the Whig ticket with the slaveholding general Zachary Taylor in 1848, Fillmore was elevated to the presidency on Taylor's sudden death in July 1850. His presidency is dominated by the Compromise of 1850: Fillmore supported and signed the package of measures — admitting California as a free state, organising the Utah and New Mexico territories, settling Texas's boundary, abolishing the slave trade in the District of Columbia, and enacting a stringent new Fugitive Slave Act — that temporarily defused the sectional crisis over slavery. His enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act, however, enraged Northern opinion and damaged his party. In foreign affairs, his administration dispatched Commodore Matthew Perry's expedition to Japan, opening the way to trade.

Denied the Whig nomination in 1852, Fillmore ran for president in 1856 as the candidate of the nativist American (Know Nothing) Party, carrying only Maryland. He retired to Buffalo, where he was a civic leader and the first chancellor of the University of Buffalo.

Assessment

Historians generally rank Fillmore in the lower tier of presidents, criticising his support for and enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act and his later association with the Know Nothings, though some credit his role in passing the Compromise of 1850 with postponing civil war by a decade.
