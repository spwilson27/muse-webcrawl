The Isle of Mull (Scottish Gaelic: Muile) is the second-largest island of the Inner Hebrides, off the west coast of Scotland in the council area of Argyll and Bute. Its colourful capital, Tobermory, founded as a fishing port in 1788, is the island's main settlement; Mull is a gateway to the islands of Iona and Staffa.

Geography and geology

Mull has a deeply indented coastline of some 480 km. Its rocks are largely Palaeogene lavas of an ancient volcano whose caldera formed the ring of hills around Ben More (966 m), the island's highest point and the only Munro on a Hebridean island other than Skye. Sea lochs such as Loch na Keal cut into the west coast.

History

The island has Bronze Age stone circles and standing stones, Iron Age brochs and duns, and early Christian sites. In the medieval period Mull belonged to the Lordship of the Isles and later to clan chiefs including the MacLeans of Duart Castle. A Spanish Armada galleon, wrecked in Tobermory Bay in 1588, is the subject of enduring treasure legends. The nineteenth century brought clearances and emigration, reducing the population from a peak of over 10,000.

Wildlife and economy

Mull is celebrated for wildlife tourism: white-tailed sea eagles were reintroduced from 1975 and now breed alongside golden eagles, otters, red deer and, offshore, minke whales and basking sharks. Farming, forestry, fishing, whisky distilling at Tobermory, and tourism are the main economic activities. Ferries from Oban serve Craignure, with smaller crossings from Lochaline and Kilchoan.
