Modal realism

Modal realism
FieldMetaphysics, philosophical logic
ProponentDavid Lewis
Key workOn the Plurality of Worlds (1986)

Modal realism is the view propounded by the philosopher David Lewis that all possible worlds are real in the same way as the actual world. It holds that "possible worlds" are not merely ways things might have been in the abstract, but concrete, spatiotemporally organised universes, causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another. On this view, the actual world is just one among indefinitely many, and "actual" is an indexical term — like "here" or "now" — picking out whichever world the speaker inhabits. Modal realism contrasts with actualism, the view that only the actual world (and its contents) genuinely exists.

Main theses

Lewis's theory, developed in Counterfactuals (1973) and given its fullest statement in On the Plurality of Worlds (1986), comprises several claims: (1) possible worlds exist, and each is a maximal mereological sum of spatiotemporally interrelated things; (2) every way a world could be is a way some world is, and every way a part of a world could be is a way some part of some world is (the "principle of recombination"); (3) worlds are causally and spatiotemporally isolated — nothing in one world causes anything in another; (4) no world is privileged — actuality is indexical.

Modality is thereby analysed without primitive modal notions: "possibly p" is true just in case p is true at some possible world, and "necessarily p" just in case p holds at every world. Counterpart theory supplies the logic of de re modality: an individual exists in exactly one world, but has "counterparts" — suitably similar individuals — in others, so that Humphrey might have won the election means that a counterpart of Humphrey wins in some world.

Arguments for and objections

Lewis's central argument is theoretical utility: modal realism, he claimed, provides a powerful, unified reduction of modality, counterfactuals, propositions, properties, and content, and "the price is right" compared with the primitive modality or abstract ersatz worlds of rival theories. He famously adopted a tone of qualified acceptance toward the "incredulous stare" — the widespread objection that the doctrine is simply unbelievable. Other objections include the epistemic problem of how we can know about causally isolated worlds (Lewis replied that we know them as we know mathematics, by their theoretical role), worries about recombination generating worlds with alien properties, and concerns that counterpart theory misrepresents de re modal claims. Despite the controversy, modal realism remains one of the most influential — and most discussed — positions in contemporary analytic metaphysics.
