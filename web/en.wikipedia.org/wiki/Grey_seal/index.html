Grey seal

Grey seal
Halichoerus grypus
Family: Phocidae
Conservation status: Least Concern

The grey seal (Halichoerus grypus) is a large seal of the family Phocidae, the "true seals." It is the only species of the genus Halichoerus and is found on both shores of the North Atlantic Ocean. Its scientific name means "hook-nosed sea pig."

Description

Males are considerably larger than females, reaching 2.3–3.3 m and 170–310 kg, with darker, thicker skin and a heavier, more convex "Roman" nose; females reach about 1.6–2.0 m and are paler and more finely spotted. Pups are born with a white lanugo coat that they shed after two to four weeks ashore.

Distribution and populations

Three populations are recognized: the western Atlantic (centred on Sable Island and the Gulf of St. Lawrence in Canada, with colonies in New England), the eastern Atlantic (Iceland, the Faroes, the British Isles — which host roughly 40% of the world population — and the French and Scandinavian coasts), and a distinct Baltic Sea subspecies, H. g. macrorhynchus. Total numbers were estimated around 600,000 in the 2020s, having recovered strongly under legal protection.

Ecology

Grey seals feed at sea on sand eels, cod, flatfish, and other fish and cephalopods, diving to depths of several hundred metres. They haul out on remote beaches and islands to breed in autumn (Atlantic) or late winter (Baltic), and face predators including killer whales, sharks, and — on land — conflicts with fisheries over fish stocks.
