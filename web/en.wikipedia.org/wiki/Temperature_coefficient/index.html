Temperature coefficient

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A temperature coefficient describes the relative change of a physical property associated with a given change in temperature. For a property R that changes by a factor when the temperature changes by an amount, the coefficient α is defined by the fractional change per degree: α = (1/R)·(dR/dT), typically quoted in parts per million per kelvin (ppm/K) or per degree Celsius.

Electrical resistance

The temperature coefficient of resistance measures how the electrical resistivity of a material changes with temperature. Most pure metals have a positive coefficient near +0.003–0.004 per °C (resistance rises with temperature), whereas semiconductors and carbon have negative coefficients. Precision resistors use alloys such as manganin or constantan with near-zero coefficients. Resistance thermometers (e.g., the Pt100 platinum sensor) exploit the predictable positive coefficient of platinum.

Other coefficients

Temperature coefficients are also defined for reactivity in nuclear reactors (where a negative fuel temperature coefficient contributes to safety), for the voltage of semiconductor junctions and batteries, for capacitance, and for the resonant frequency of oscillators. The Q10 coefficient in biology describes the factor by which a reaction rate changes with a 10 °C temperature rise.
