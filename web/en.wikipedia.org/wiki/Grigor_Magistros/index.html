Grigor Magistros (Armenian: Գրիգոր Մագիստրոս; c. 990–1058) was an Armenian prince, military commander, scholar, and writer, a member of the Pahlavuni family. He is one of the most celebrated figures of medieval Armenian culture, renowned for his encyclopedic learning at a time of political upheaval following the Byzantine annexation of the Armenian kingdoms.

Life

Grigor was the son of Vasak Pahlavuni, commander of the Armenian forces at Ani, and descended from the Kamsarakan family. After the fall of the Bagratuni kingdom and the surrender of Ani to the Byzantines in 1045, Grigor entered imperial service, receiving the title of magistros and serving as governor (doux) of Mesopotamia. He founded the monastery and school of Kecharis, which became an important center of learning.

Works

Grigor's surviving oeuvre includes 88 letters addressed to leading political and ecclesiastical figures of his day, which are a rich source for the social, political, and intellectual life of 11th-century Armenia and Byzantium. He translated Euclid's Elements and Plato's Timaeus and Phaedo from Greek into Armenian. He also composed a long poem, A Thousand Lines to Manuche, summarizing the contents of the Bible in verse for a Muslim emir, demonstrating his engagement in interreligious dialogue.

Legacy

Grigor Magistros is remembered as a central figure of the Armenian cultural renaissance of the 10th–11th centuries. His son, Vahram Pahlavuni, and grandson Grigor II Martyrophile continued the family's scholarly and ecclesiastical prominence; the latter became Catholicos of All Armenians.
