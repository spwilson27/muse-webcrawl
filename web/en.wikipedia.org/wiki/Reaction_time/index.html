Reaction time

Reaction time (RT) is the interval between the presentation of a stimulus and the initiation of a response to it. It is a basic measure in experimental psychology and neuroscience, used to study the speed of mental processing. Simple reaction time — responding to a single expected stimulus — averages roughly 150–200 milliseconds for auditory stimuli and 200–250 milliseconds for visual stimuli in healthy young adults.

Types

Simple RT involves one stimulus and one response. Choice RT requires selecting among several responses according to the stimulus presented; Hick's law describes how choice RT increases with the logarithm of the number of alternatives. Recognition (go/no-go) RT requires responding to one stimulus type while withholding a response to others.

Factors

Reaction time is affected by arousal, fatigue, practice, age, alcohol and other drugs, stimulus intensity, and the compatibility between stimulus and response. It lengthens across adulthood and is slowed in many neurological and psychiatric conditions, making it a useful, if non-specific, clinical and research measure. In sport, sprint rules treat a start within 0.1 seconds of the gun as a false start, on the assumption that faster reactions are physiologically implausible.

History

The systematic study of reaction time began with astronomers' observations of the "personal equation" and with Franciscus Donders's subtraction method in the 1860s, which attempted to time individual mental operations by comparing simple and choice tasks.
