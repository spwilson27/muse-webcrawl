Grand Unified Theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram showing the three gauge couplings of the Standard Model converging at high energy]
The running couplings nearly meet near 10¹⁶ GeV — motivation for grand unification.

In particle physics, a Grand Unified Theory (GUT) is a model in which, at high energies, the three gauge interactions of the Standard Model — electromagnetic, weak and strong — merge into a single force characterised by one larger gauge symmetry and one coupling constant. The unification scale is around 10¹⁶ GeV, far beyond any collider.

The first GUT was the SU(5) model of Howard Georgi and Sheldon Glashow (1974); Abdus Salam and Jogesh Pati's model, embedding quarks and leptons in common multiplets, appeared the same year. GUTs naturally explain the quantisation of electric charge, relate quark and lepton masses, and typically predict that the proton decays with a lifetime above ~10³⁴ years — a prediction whose non-observation (notably by Super-Kamiokande) has ruled out the simplest models.

Evidence and status

The running of the three couplings with energy — the strong coupling falling, the others rising — brings them close together near 10¹⁶ GeV, meeting almost exactly if Supersymmetry exists at the TeV scale. Gauge coupling unification, the seesaw explanation of tiny neutrino masses in SO(10) models, and the needs of cosmology (baryogenesis, inflation) keep GUTs central to thinking beyond the Standard Model, even as proton-decay searches and the LHC's failure so far to find superpartners constrain the simplest versions.
