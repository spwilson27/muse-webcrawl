Cedrus libani

A cedar of Lebanon in the Cedars of God forest, Lebanon.

Cedrus libani, commonly known as the cedar of Lebanon or Lebanon cedar, is a species of true cedar native to the mountains of the Eastern Mediterranean basin, including Lebanon, Syria, and Turkey. It is a large evergreen conifer growing up to about 40 metres tall, with massive trunks and broad, tiered layers of foliage in mature trees.

Description

Young trees are conical, but with age the crown flattens into the characteristic layered silhouette. The needles are 15–35 mm long, borne in clusters on short shoots, and range from green to blue-green. The seed cones are barrel-shaped, 8–12 cm long, maturing every second year.

History and significance

Cedar of Lebanon timber was prized throughout the ancient world for its durability, fragrance, and resistance to decay. It was used in shipbuilding, palaces, and temples — most famously, in tradition, Solomon's Temple in Jerusalem — and was exported to Egypt and Mesopotamia. The Epic of Gilgamesh describes a cedar forest guarded by the monster Humbaba. The tree is the national emblem of Lebanon and appears on its flag.

Distribution and conservation

Millennia of logging drastically reduced the original forests. In Lebanon the best-known remnant is the Cedars of God (Arz ar-Rabb) near Bsharri, a UNESCO World Heritage site. Reforestation programmes in Lebanon and Turkey have planted large areas, and the species is widely grown as an ornamental tree in parks and large gardens in temperate climates.
