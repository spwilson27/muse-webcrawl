The Bloodhound is a large scent hound, originally bred for hunting deer and wild boar and, since the Middle Ages, for tracking people. Renowned for its extraordinary sense of smell — its nose has roughly 300 million scent receptors, more than any other breed — the Bloodhound can follow scent trails several days old over long distances. Its trailing evidence has been admitted in courts of law in the United States.

History

The breed descends from hounds kept by the monks of the Abbey of St Hubert in the Ardennes, and is known in French as the chien de Saint-Hubert. Bloodhounds were brought to England, traditionally by William the Conqueror, and were refined there. The name derives not from blood-tracking but from their status as the "blooded hound" — the aristocratic hound of noble breeding. Bloodhounds were used to track poachers, escaped prisoners and lost children, and in the American South, controversially, to pursue escaped slaves.

Characteristics

Bloodhounds have long, drooping ears, loose wrinkled skin, and a solemn expression. They are gentle and affectionate but famously stubborn and single-minded when following a scent. Their baying voice carries over long distances. Because they are driven by smell, they require secure fencing and consistent training. The breed is prone to gastric dilatation-volvulus (bloat) and to ear infections.
