Assyrian homeland

The Nineveh Plains, heartland of the Assyrian homeland

The Assyrian homeland is the region of northern Mesopotamia — today divided between northern Iraq, south-eastern Turkey, north-western Iran and north-eastern Syria — that has been inhabited for millennia by the Assyrians, an indigenous Christian people speaking Neo-Aramaic languages. Its historical core lies in the triangle between the ancient cities of Nineveh (opposite modern Mosul), Arbil and Kirkuk, together with the Nineveh Plains east of the Tigris.

Geography

The heartland includes the Nineveh Plains with towns such as Alqosh, Tel Keppe, Karamlesh and Qaraqosh (Bakhdida); the Dohuk region; and, further afield, the Hakkari mountains of Turkey, Tur Abdin around Midyat, and the Urmia plain in Iran. These districts correspond broadly to the centre of the ancient Assyrian empire, whose capitals at Assur, Nimrud and Nineveh gave the people their name and identity.

History

Assyrian communities survived the fall of their empire in the 7th century BC and adopted Christianity in the first centuries AD, producing the Church of the East and Syriac Orthodox traditions. Persecutions sharply reduced their presence in the homeland: the Sayfo (Assyrian genocide) in the Ottoman Empire during the First World War, the Simele massacre in Iraq in 1933, and the flight and displacement following the 2003 Iraq War and the 2014 ISIS offensive on the Nineveh Plains, which emptied many Christian towns. A large diaspora now lives in Europe, North America and Australia, and many Assyrians advocate an autonomous administrative region in the Nineveh Plains.
