The Hungarian Revolution of 1848 was one of the many European revolutions of 1848 and was closely linked to other Revolutions of 1848 in the Habsburg areas. The revolution in the Kingdom of Hungary grew into a war for independence from the Austrian Empire, ruled by the Habsburg dynasty.

After a number of serious reverses, the revolution was crushed by the combined forces of Austria and the Russian Empire, whose intervention in the summer of 1849 proved decisive. Many of the revolution's leaders were executed or driven into exile; the reprisals under General Julius Jacob von Haynau earned him the nickname "the Hyena of Brescia". Full independence would only be addressed decades later in the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867.

Background

Hungary had long chafed under Habsburg rule while retaining its own Diet and constitution. Reformers of the so-called Reform Era, including Count István Széchenyi and the fiery orator Lajos Kossuth, demanded abolition of serfdom, equality before the law, freedom of the press and responsible government. News of revolution in Paris and Vienna in early 1848 galvanised the reformers in Pest.

Course of the revolution

On 15 March 1848, revolutionary youth led by the poet Sándor Petőfi gathered in Pest; the crowd adopted the Twelve Points and Petőfi recited his National Song. The Diet at Pressburg (Pozsony) responded by passing the April Laws, which created a responsible Hungarian ministry under Count Lajos Batthyány and abolished serfdom and noble tax exemption, while maintaining the personal union with the Emperor.

Relations with Vienna deteriorated when the Croatian Ban Josip Jelačić invaded Hungary with imperial backing. Hungarian forces defeated Jelačić at Pákozd in September 1848, but the imperial counteroffensive under Prince Windisch-Grätz retook Pest in January 1849. The Hungarian army under Artúr Görgei counterattacked in the successful Spring Campaign, and on 14 April 1849 the Diet at Debrecen declared the dethronement of the Habsburgs and full independence, with Kossuth as Governor-President.

Russian intervention and surrender

Emperor Franz Joseph appealed to Tsar Nicholas I of Russia. Some 200,000 Russian troops under Ivan Paskevich entered Hungary in June 1849. Facing overwhelming force, Kossuth resigned and transferred power to Görgei, who surrendered to the Russians at Világos on 13 August 1849. Thirteen Hungarian generals — the Martyrs of Arad — were executed on 6 October 1849, and former Prime Minister Batthyány was shot in Pest the same day.

Aftermath

Hungary was placed under a harsh neo-absolutist regime, its historic counties reorganised and German made the language of administration. Yet the April Laws' social achievements, notably the emancipation of the serfs, could not be undone. Passive resistance eventually forced Vienna to negotiate, producing the Compromise of 1867 and the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary.
