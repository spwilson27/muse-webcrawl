Nitrile rubber

Nitrile rubber, also known as nitrile butadiene rubber (NBR) or Buna-N, is a synthetic rubber copolymer of acrylonitrile (ACN) and butadiene. It is the most widely used elastomer for applications requiring resistance to oil, fuel, and chemicals, and is familiar as the material of most disposable medical and laboratory gloves.

Properties

The acrylonitrile content of commercial grades ranges from about 18% to 50%; higher ACN content gives greater resistance to petroleum oils and fuels and lower swelling, but poorer low-temperature flexibility. NBR has good tensile strength, abrasion and compression-set resistance, and a typical service temperature range of −40 to +108 °C. It is, however, degraded by ozone, sunlight, ketones, and oxidising agents, and has poor flame resistance. Hydrogenated nitrile rubber (HNBR), produced by hydrogenating the butadiene segments, offers improved heat, ozone, and chemical resistance.

Production and history

NBR is produced by emulsion polymerisation of butadiene and acrylonitrile. It was developed in Germany in the 1930s by Bayer chemists (patented 1934) under the names Buna N and Perbunan, and was manufactured in the United States during the Second World War as GR-N when natural rubber supplies were cut off.

Applications

Typical uses include automotive and industrial seals, O-rings, gaskets, fuel and oil hoses, V-belts, conveyor belts, and cable jacketing. Because it contains no latex proteins, nitrile is the standard material for disposable examination gloves used by people with latex allergy. Powder-free nitrile gloves are ubiquitous in healthcare, laboratories, food handling, and tattooing.
