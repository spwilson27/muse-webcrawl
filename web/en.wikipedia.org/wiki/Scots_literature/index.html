Scots literature

Scots literature is literature written in the Scots language, one of the two indigenous languages of lowland Scotland (the other being Scottish English, and alongside Scottish Gaelic in the Highlands). It spans from the medieval court poetry of the Makars to the work of Robert Burns and the modern Scots-language revival known as the Scottish Renaissance.

Middle Scots and the Makars

Scots — descended from the Northumbrian dialect of Old English — emerged as the language of the Scottish court and administration in the later Middle Ages. Its greatest literary flowering came in the 15th and early 16th centuries with the Makars: Robert Henryson, whose Testament of Cresseid and Morall Fabillis are landmarks; William Dunbar, author of The Dance of the Sevin Deidly Synnis and Lament for the Makaris; Gavin Douglas, whose Eneados (1513) was the first complete translation of Virgil's Aeneid into any Anglic language; and King James I, credited with The Kingis Quair. John Barbour's epic The Brus (c. 1375), celebrating Robert the Bruce, is the earliest major surviving work in Scots.

Decline and vernacular revival

After the Union of the Crowns (1603) and the Union of Parliaments (1707), Scots lost status as a language of state and print, with standard English dominating formal writing. Scots nonetheless persisted in speech and song, and the 18th century saw a vernacular revival: Allan Ramsay's The Gentle Shepherd (1725), Robert Fergusson's city poetry, and above all Robert Burns (1759–1796), whose poems and songs — "Tam o' Shanter", "A Man's a Man for A' That", "Auld Lang Syne" — made Scots world-famous.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

In the 19th century Scots flourished in popular poetry, newspaper verse and the "kailyard" fiction associated with J. M. Barrie, though critics later attacked kailyard sentimentality. Walter Scott preserved Scots speech in the dialogue of his novels. In the 20th century, Hugh MacDiarmid led a deliberate renaissance of literary Scots, synthesising vocabulary from dialects and older texts ("synthetic Scots") in works such as A Drunk Man Looks at the Thistle (1926). Later writers including Robert Garioch, Sydney Goodsir Smith, Tom Leonard (in Glaswegian demotic), Liz Lochhead and James Kelman extended Scots' range in poetry, drama and the novel.
