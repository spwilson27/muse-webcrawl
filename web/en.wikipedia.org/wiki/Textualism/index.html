Textualism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Textualism is a formalist theory of statutory interpretation holding that the ordinary meaning of a legal text — primarily the words of the statute itself — should govern. A textualist judge seeks the meaning that a reasonable reader would have given the text at the time it was enacted, and generally declines to rely on legislative history, such as committee reports and sponsor statements, which textualists view as unreliable and manipulable.

Principles

Textualism emphasizes canons of construction such as the ordinary-meaning canon, the whole-act rule, and the surplusage canon. Its leading modern exponent was U.S. Supreme Court Justice Antonin Scalia, co-author (with Bryan A. Garner) of Reading Law: The Interpretation of Legal Texts (2012). Applied to constitutional interpretation, the related theory is originalism, specifically original public meaning.

Criticism

Critics, including purposivists and proponents of "living" interpretation, argue that rigid textualism can frustrate legislative intent in cases of drafting error or unforeseen circumstances, and that ordinary meaning is often indeterminate. Supporters respond that textualism constrains judicial discretion, respects the constitutionally prescribed lawmaking process, and gives fair notice of legal obligations.
