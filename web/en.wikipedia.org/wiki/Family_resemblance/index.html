Family resemblance

Family resemblance (German: Familienähnlichkeit) is a philosophical idea made popular by Ludwig Wittgenstein, particularly in his posthumous work Philosophical Investigations (1953). It argues that things which could be thought to be connected by one essential common feature may in fact be connected by a series of overlapping similarities, where no single feature is common to all of them.

Wittgenstein's example

Wittgenstein's famous example is games: board games, card games, ball games, and Olympic games share no one defining property — some involve winning and losing, others skill, luck, competition, or amusement — but a network of overlapping similarities (like the resemblances among members of a family: build, eye colour, temperament, "which no one member necessarily shares with all the others"). The concept challenged the classical, essentialist view of definitions and the search for necessary and sufficient conditions.

Influence

Family resemblance influenced Eleanor Rosch's prototype theory of categorization in psychology and cognitive linguistics, in which category membership is graded around central exemplars. It has also been applied in natural-language semantics, biological taxonomy, and discussions of concepts such as art and religion, where attempts at single definitions often founder on borderline cases.
