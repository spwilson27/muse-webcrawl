Cockney

Cockney is a dialect of the English language traditionally spoken by working-class Londoners, particularly in the East End, and also the name for a native Londoner. Historically a true Cockney was said to be born within earshot of the bells of St Mary-le-Bow church in the City of London. The dialect is famous for its rhyming slang and distinctive pronunciation, though the traditional form has declined as East End communities have dispersed.

History of the term

The word cockney derives from Middle English cokenay, "cock's egg"—a misshapen egg, later used derisively for a pampered townsman ignorant of country ways. By the 17th century it denoted a native Londoner generally, and later specifically the working-class Londoner and their speech.

Pronunciation

Characteristic features include th-fronting (pronouncing think with an initial f-sound), the glottal stop replacing t between vowels (as in "bu'er" for butter), h-dropping, and vowel shifts affecting diphthongs, so that face may sound close to standard price. Many features once stigmatized have spread into the general speech of south-eastern England known as Estuary English.

Rhyming slang

Decline and legacy

Post-war slum clearance, new towns, and immigration reshaped the East End. Linguists note younger Londoners increasingly speak Multicultural London English, which incorporates some Cockney features. Cockney speech survives in popular culture through figures such as Eliza Doolittle in Pygmalion and My Fair Lady, and through Pearly Kings and Queens, market costermongers in mother-of-pearl-buttoned suits.
