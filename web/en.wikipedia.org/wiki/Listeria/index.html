Listeria is a genus of bacteria that comprises roughly 28 recognised species (the count has grown steadily as new species are described). They are Gram-positive, rod-shaped, facultatively anaerobic bacteria found in soil, water, vegetation and the intestines of animals. The genus is named after the British surgeon and antisepsis pioneer Joseph Lister.

One species, Listeria monocytogenes, is an important foodborne human pathogen: it causes listeriosis, an infection with a high fatality rate in pregnant women, newborns, the elderly and the immunocompromised. L. ivanovii mainly infects ruminants and only rarely humans; most other species, such as the commonly isolated L. innocua, are considered harmless.

Characteristics

Listeria cells are short rods, 0.4–0.5 × 0.5–2 µm, Gram-positive and non-spore-forming. They are motile by peritrichous flagella at room temperature (20–25 °C), showing a characteristic "tumbling" motility and forming an umbrella-shaped growth in semisolid medium, but are largely non-motile at 37 °C. They are catalase-positive and oxidase-negative.

Unusually among foodborne pathogens, L. monocytogenes grows at refrigeration temperatures (down to about 0 °C) and tolerates high salt concentrations and a wide pH range, which makes it a persistent contaminant of food-processing plants. Risky foods include soft cheeses, unpasteurised milk, ready-to-eat deli meats and refrigerated smoked seafood.

L. monocytogenes as an intracellular pathogen

L. monocytogenes invades host cells using surface proteins (internalins) that bind receptors such as E-cadherin. Inside the cell it escapes the phagosome using the pore-forming toxin listeriolysin O, then polymerises host actin via the ActA protein to form "comet tails" that propel it into neighbouring cells — allowing it to spread without exposure to antibodies. Its cell biology has made it a model organism for studying intracellular parasitism.

Selected species

  • Listeria monocytogenes — the main human pathogen
  • Listeria ivanovii — animal pathogen, rare human cases
  • Listeria innocua — widespread and non-pathogenic; used as an indicator organism
  • Listeria seeligeri, Listeria welshimeri, Listeria grayi — the classical "sensu lato" group
  • Listeria marthii, Listeria rocourtiae, Listeria fleischmannii — among many species described since 2010

History

The type species was described in 1926 by Everitt Murray and colleagues as Bacterium monocytogenes, after an epizootic in laboratory rabbits and guinea pigs in Cambridge; the genus name Listeria was proposed by Harvey Pirie in 1940. Although sporadic human cases were known earlier, foodborne transmission was firmly established only after a 1981 outbreak in Canada traced to contaminated coleslaw.
