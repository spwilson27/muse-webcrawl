Edward Goodrich Acheson

Edward Goodrich Acheson (March 9, 1856 – July 6, 1931) was an American chemist and inventor. He is best known for inventing silicon carbide (which he named carborundum) and for developing a process to manufacture high-quality graphite, marketed as Acheson graphite.

Career

Born in Washington, Pennsylvania, Acheson worked for Thomas Edison's laboratory at Menlo Park and helped install electric lighting systems in Europe. In 1891, experimenting in Monongahela, Pennsylvania with an electric furnace in an attempt to make artificial diamonds, he fused clay and carbon and produced brilliant crystals of silicon carbide — one of the hardest known substances and a major industrial abrasive. He founded the Carborundum Company, and later, at Niagara Falls, developed his graphite process, in which amorphous carbon is converted to graphite in an electric furnace; the method is still known as the Acheson process.

Honours

Acheson received numerous patents and honours and was awarded the Perkin Medal. The Electrochemical Society's Acheson Award is named for him.
