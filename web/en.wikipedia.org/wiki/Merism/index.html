Merism

"Heaven and earth" — a classic merism meaning everything.

A merism is a figure of speech in which a whole is referred to by naming some of its parts, most characteristically two contrasting or extreme parts. The word derives from the Greek merismos, "a dividing or partition". "Heaven and earth" for the entire cosmos, "young and old" for everybody, "high and low" for all ranks of society, and "night and day" for all the time are all merisms.

Biblical usage

Merisms are especially characteristic of ancient Hebrew poetry and prose, and recognising them is important for biblical interpretation. Genesis opens with God creating "the heavens and the earth" (the totality of creation); "the knowledge of good and evil" denotes all knowledge; Psalm 139's "you know when I sit and when I rise" signifies complete knowledge of the psalmist's life. In the New Testament, "the Alpha and the Omega" and "the first and the last" function similarly.

In law and other fields

Legal drafting has long used meristic pairings to achieve completeness — "cease and desist", the transfer of property "to have and to hold". In biology, the term merism has a separate technical meaning relating to the repetition of homologous parts (as in the segmentation of animals and the arrangement of floral whorls), from the same Greek root.

Related figures

Merism is related to, but distinct from, hendiadys (one idea expressed through two words joined by "and", as in "nice and warm") and synecdoche (a part standing for the whole, as in "sails" for ships). A three-part merism covering a whole domain is sometimes called a meristic triad.
