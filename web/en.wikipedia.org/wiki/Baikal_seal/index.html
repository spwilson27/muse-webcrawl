The Baikal seal or nerpa (Pusa sibirica) is a species of earless seal endemic to Lake Baikal in Siberia. It is one of the smallest pinnipeds and the only exclusively freshwater seal species. How the seals reached the landlocked lake, thousands of kilometres from the ocean, remains a zoological puzzle; they are thought to have arrived from the Arctic via ancient river systems, possibly during glacial periods, and are closely related to the ringed and Caspian seals.

Description

Adults measure about 1.3 m and weigh around 70 kg on average, though large individuals reach 130 kg. They have large forward-facing eyes adapted for seeing in the lake's exceptionally clear water, strong claws on their foreflippers for scratching breathing holes in ice, and a uniformly grey to brownish coat, lighter below; newborn pups are covered in white lanugo.

Behaviour and ecology

Baikal seals are mostly solitary. In winter, when the lake freezes over, each seal maintains breathing holes in the ice, and females give birth to a single pup (occasionally twins) in snow dens on the ice in late February–March. Their diet consists mainly of golomyanka (oilfish) and other endemic fish, supplemented by amphipods. In summer they haul out on rocky shores and islands, notably the Ushkany Islands.

Population and conservation

Estimates place the population at roughly 80,000–100,000 individuals. The species was hunted commercially for fur, meat and oil into the late 20th century; regulated hunting continues at modest levels. An epizootic of canine distemper virus killed several thousand seals in 1987–88. The species is listed as Least Concern, but pollution, warming of the lake and reduced ice cover are monitored threats.
