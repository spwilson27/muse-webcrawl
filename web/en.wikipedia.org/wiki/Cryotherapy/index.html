Cryotherapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A whole-body cryotherapy chamber exposes users to very cold air for a few minutes.

Cryotherapy is the medical use of low temperatures in therapy. The term encompasses several distinct practices, from applying an ice pack to an injured joint to the surgical destruction of tissue with extreme cold (cryosurgery) and exposure of the whole body to very cold air in a chamber. Local cooling reduces blood flow, swelling, and pain perception, which is why cold treatment is a standard first response to sprains and strains.

Forms

Local cryotherapy and ice therapy

Ice packs, cold compresses, ice massage, and cold-water immersion are used to limit inflammation and relieve pain after acute injuries, and are a component of the RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation) protocol. Ice baths are also popular among athletes for post-exercise recovery, although evidence for their benefit is mixed and they may blunt some training adaptations.

Cryosurgery

Cryosurgery (cryoablation) uses extreme cold, usually liquid nitrogen or a cryoprobe, to destroy abnormal tissue. Dermatologists commonly freeze warts, actinic keratoses, and some small skin cancers. Cryoablation is also used internally, guided by imaging, to treat tumours of the prostate, kidney, liver, and other organs, and to ablate abnormal electrical pathways in the heart in some arrhythmias.

Whole-body cryotherapy

Whole-body cryotherapy involves standing in a chamber cooled with nitrogen vapour to temperatures around −110 °C to −140 °C for two to four minutes. It originated in Japan in the late 1970s for rheumatoid arthritis and was adopted by European clinics and professional sports teams. Proponents claim benefits for muscle soreness, inflammation, pain, and mood. Regulators such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration have cautioned that whole-body cryotherapy lacks proven medical benefits and carries risks including frostbite, burns, and asphyxiation from nitrogen displacement.

Risks

Improper use can cause frostbite, cold burns, and nerve injury; ice should not be applied directly to skin for prolonged periods. Cryosurgery can cause blistering, scarring, and pigment changes at the treated site.
