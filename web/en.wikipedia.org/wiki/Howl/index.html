"Howl", also known as "Howl for Carl Solomon", is a poem written by Allen Ginsberg in 1954–55 and published in his 1956 collection Howl and Other Poems. The poem is dedicated to Carl Solomon, whom Ginsberg had met in a psychiatric institution, and is considered to be one of the great works of American poetry of the Beat Generation.

The poem begins: "I saw the best minds of my generation destroyed by madness, starving hysterical naked, / dragging themselves through the negro streets at dawn looking for an angry fix..." Its three parts catalogue the destruction of Ginsberg's contemporaries by addiction, mental illness, and a conformist America personified as Moloch, and close with the redemptive "Footnote to Howl" ("Holy! Holy! Holy!"). Ginsberg's long, breath-based lines drew on jazz, Whitman, and biblical cadence.

Reception and obscenity trial

Ginsberg first performed the poem at the Six Gallery reading in San Francisco on 7 October 1955, an event often taken as the public debut of the Beat Generation. Published by Lawrence Ferlinghetti's City Lights, Howl was seized by customs and San Francisco police in 1957 and Ferlinghetti tried for obscenity; Judge Clayton W. Horn ruled the poem had redeeming social importance, a landmark decision for American free-speech law. The trial made the poem famous, and it remains one of the most widely read and anthologised American poems of the 20th century.
