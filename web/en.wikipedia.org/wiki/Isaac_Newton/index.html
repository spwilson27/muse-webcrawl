Isaac Newton

Sir Isaac Newton (1643–1727) was an English polymath — a mathematician, physicist, astronomer, alchemist, theologian and author — recognised as one of the most influential scientists and mathematicians of all time. His book Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687) formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation that completed the synthesis of the Scientific Revolution and provided the basis of classical mechanics, dominating scientists' view of the physical universe for the next three centuries.

Early life and work

Born prematurely at Woolsthorpe Manor, Newton attended Trinity College, Cambridge. During the university's closure for the Great Plague in 1665–67 he worked at home in his "years of wonder", developing early calculus ("fluxions"), optics and the law of gravitation. The popular story that a falling apple in the Woolsthorpe garden suggested universal gravitation comes from Newton's own later telling to acquaintances.

Principia and optics

Prompted by Edmond Halley's question about planetary orbits, Newton wrote the Principia, proving that the same gravity that pulls an apple also holds the Moon and planets, and deriving Kepler's laws. His Opticks (1704) reported experiments splitting sunlight with prisms into a spectrum and recombining it, arguing light was corpuscular. He built the first practical reflecting telescope in 1668.

Later life and legacy

Newton became Warden then Master of the Royal Mint, pursued counterfeiters vigorously, presided over the Royal Society from 1703, and was knighted in 1705. He feuded famously with Leibniz over calculus priority and with Robert Hooke. He wrote extensively, and mostly privately, on theology and alchemy — work that led John Maynard Keynes to call him "the last of the magicians". Buried in Westminster Abbey, he is commemorated by the unit of force, the newton, and by institutions from the Royal Society (royalsociety.org) onward.

Newtonian mechanics remained unchallenged until Albert Einstein's relativity and quantum mechanics reframed its limits in the 20th century; it remains the working framework for most engineering and for much of spaceflight.
