Aridification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aridification is the process by which a region becomes progressively drier over a long period of time, shifting toward an arid climate. It is measured by the decline of available moisture — typically the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration — and differs from drought, which is a temporary anomaly, and from desertification, which specifically denotes land degradation in drylands driven partly by human activity.

Causes

Natural drivers include long-term changes in atmospheric circulation, such as shifts in the Hadley cells and storm tracks, changes in sea-surface temperatures, and tectonic and orbital forcing over geological timescales. A classic geological example is the drying of Central Asia as the uplift of the Tibetan Plateau intensified the continental interior's rain shadow. In the present climate, anthropogenic global warming is increasing potential evapotranspiration and vapour-pressure deficit, so many subtropical and Mediterranean-type regions are aridifying even where rainfall totals change little.

Effects

Aridification reduces soil moisture, streamflow, lake levels, and groundwater recharge; stresses rain-fed agriculture and ecosystems; and increases wildfire risk, dust emissions, and water scarcity. Examples of marked recent aridification include parts of the southwestern United States and Mexico, the Mediterranean basin, southern Africa, and Central Asia, where the Aral Sea has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s (in that case mainly through irrigation diversions compounding climatic drying).
