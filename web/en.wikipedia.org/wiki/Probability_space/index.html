Probability space

A probability space consists of a sample space, a collection of events, and a probability measure.

In probability theory, a probability space or probability triple (Ω, F, P) is a mathematical construct that provides a formal model of a random phenomenon or experiment. It consists of three elements:

  • A sample space Ω, which is the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment.
  • An event space F, a σ-algebra of subsets of Ω, whose elements are called events.
  • A probability measure P that assigns to each event a probability — a real number between zero and one, with P(Ω) = 1.

Interpretation

An outcome is a result of a single execution of the model, and an event is a set of outcomes to which a probability can be assigned. Random variables are then defined as measurable functions from the sample space to another space, most commonly the real numbers.

Example

For the toss of a fair coin, the sample space is Ω = {Heads, Tails}, the event space is the power set of Ω, and the probability measure assigns P({Heads}) = P({Tails}) = 1/2. More complex experiments — such as repeated tosses or continuous measurements — require richer event spaces and the machinery of measure theory.

History

The modern axiomatic formulation of probability spaces was given by Andrey Kolmogorov in his 1933 work Foundations of the Theory of Probability, which placed probability on the rigorous footing of measure theory and remains the standard framework today.
