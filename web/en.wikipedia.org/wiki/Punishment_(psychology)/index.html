Punishment (psychology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Punishment, in operant conditioning, is any change in a human or other animal's surroundings that occurs after a given behaviour or response and reduces the likelihood that the behaviour will recur in the future. As with reinforcement, it is not the animal's subjective experience but its effect on future behaviour that defines a consequence as punishment. A consequence that increases future behaviour is reinforcing; one that decreases it is punishing.

Types

B. F. Skinner and later behaviour analysts distinguish two forms:

  • Positive punishment — the presentation of an aversive stimulus following behaviour (e.g., a fine, a reprimand, an electric shock in a laboratory experiment).
  • Negative punishment (also called penalty or response cost) — the removal of an appetitive stimulus following behaviour (e.g., losing privileges, a time-out, losing tokens in a token economy).

The positive/negative distinction refers to adding or subtracting a stimulus, not to good or bad. Punishment is contrasted with extinction (withholding reinforcement) and with negative reinforcement, which strengthens behaviour by removing an aversive stimulus.

Effectiveness and side effects

Research on punishment finds that it works best when it is immediate, consistent, contingent on the specific behaviour, intense enough to be noticed, and accompanied by explanation and reinforcement of alternative behaviour. Its suppression is often temporary, however. Documented side effects include escape and avoidance, aggression (as in the classic experiments on pain-induced aggression), generalised suppression of behaviour, modelling of aversive control by the recipient, and damage to the relationship with the punishing agent. These findings led many behaviour analysts and professional bodies to recommend using punishment only sparingly, in combination with reinforcement-based procedures, and with safeguards — an ethical debate particularly prominent in applied behavior analysis and in the use of aversive procedures for people with developmental disabilities.

Applications

Punishment principles underlie fines and other criminal sanctions (see deterrence theory in criminology), school discipline, and clinical behaviour-change techniques such as time-out and response cost. Debates about corporal punishment of children draw heavily on this psychological literature.
