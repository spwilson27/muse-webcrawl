Polar front

In meteorology, the polar front is the weather front boundary between the polar air cell and the Ferrel cell at around 60° latitude in both hemispheres. The polar front is a nearly continuous, semi-permanent boundary separating cold polar air from warmer mid-latitude air, and it is one of the most important features in the weather of the middle latitudes.

Formation

At this boundary, a sharp temperature and moisture gradient forms between the two air masses, each of different densities. The thermal wind balance and the steep gradient generate the polar front jet stream in the upper troposphere above the front. The front arises naturally from the general circulation of the atmosphere: air in the polar cell subsides over the poles and flows toward the equator at the surface, while air in the Ferrel cell flows poleward, and the two meet near 60° latitude.

Frontal cyclones

The polar front theory, developed by Vilhelm Bjerknes and the Bergen School of meteorology in the years around the First World War, explained how mid-latitude (extratropical) cyclones form, develop and decay along the polar front. Waves develop along the stationary front; as a wave amplifies, a low-pressure centre forms, warm air advances poleward ahead of it as a warm front, and cold air pushes equatorward behind it as a cold front. Over several days the cold front typically overtakes the warm front, producing an occluded front, after which the cyclone dissipates.

Seasonal variation

The polar front shifts with the seasons, moving equatorward in winter — when the temperature contrast between pole and equator is greatest and the front is strongest — and poleward in summer, when it weakens. Its position strongly influences storm tracks and the weather of North America, Europe and the Southern Ocean.
