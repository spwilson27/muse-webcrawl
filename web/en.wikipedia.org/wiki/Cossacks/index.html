The Cossacks are a predominantly East Slavic Orthodox Christian people originating in the steppe frontiers of Ukraine and southern Russia. From the late medieval period they formed freebooting, semi-military communities — organised in hosts (voiska) under elected leaders called atamans or hetmans — who combined farming, raiding, and frontier defence. In return for military service, first Polish and later Russian rulers granted them land and self-government.

Origins

The name derives from a Turkic word for a free man or adventurer. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, runaway serfs, hunters, and adventurers gathered beyond the reach of Polish-Lithuanian and Muscovite authority in the "wild fields" along the Dnieper, Don, and Volga rivers. The Zaporozhian Cossacks below the Dnieper rapids and the Don Cossacks became the largest and most famous hosts.

Service and rebellion

Cossacks fought as light cavalry in the armies of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth and the Tsardom of Russia, and played the leading role in the Russian conquest of Siberia. Yet they also led the great frontier revolts of the era: the Khmelnytsky Uprising of 1648 against Poland, which reshaped Ukraine, and the rebellions of Stenka Razin (1670–71) and Yemelyan Pugachev (1773–75) in Russia. As the frontier closed, the tsars progressively converted the hosts into a privileged military estate, and the Zaporozhian Sich was destroyed in 1775.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

In the Russian Empire the Cossack hosts — Don, Kuban, Terek, Orenburg, Ural, and Siberian among them — served as cavalry and as mounted police, gaining a reputation both as elite soldiers and as agents of repression. In the Russian Civil War they were divided between Reds and Whites; the Soviet policy of decossackization brought repression, famine, and the abolition of the hosts. Since the late 1980s there has been a Cossack revival in both Russia and Ukraine, and registered Cossack organizations again perform state service in Russia.
