Homotopy theory

Homotopy theory
FieldAlgebraic topology
Central objectsHomotopy groups, fibrations, spectra
Key figuresHeinz Hopf, Lev Pontryagin, Jean-Pierre Serre, Daniel Quillen

Homotopy theory is a branch of algebraic topology concerned with the properties of spaces and maps that are preserved under homotopy — continuous deformations. Two maps f, g : X → Y are homotopic if one can be continuously deformed into the other; spaces are homotopy equivalent if there are maps between them whose composites are homotopic to the identities. Homotopy theory studies spaces "up to" such weak equivalences, which retain fundamental shape information while ignoring fine geometric detail.

Basic concepts

The fundamental invariant is the fundamental group π₁(X), recording loops up to homotopy. Higher homotopy groups πₙ(X) capture n-dimensional "holes": π₂ of the 2-sphere, for example, is infinite cyclic, generated by the identity map. A celebrated result is the Hopf invariant and Hopf fibration (1931), exhibiting a nontrivial element of π₃(S²). Unlike homology, higher homotopy groups are notoriously hard to compute; even the homotopy groups of spheres remain only partially known and exhibit rich periodic patterns (Serre's finiteness theorems, the image of the J-homomorphism, and chromatic homotopy theory).

Fibrations and cofibrations

Key structural tools include fibrations — maps with the homotopy lifting property, exemplified by Serre fibrations — which give rise to long exact sequences relating the homotopy groups of fibre, total space, and base. Dual cofibrations organise cell attachments as in CW complexes. The Puppe sequence, suspension and loop-space adjunction (the loop–suspension adjunction giving Freudenthal's suspension theorem and the entry to stable homotopy theory), and fibre/cofibre sequences form the everyday machinery of the subject.

Spectrum and stable homotopy

Stabilising by repeated suspension leads to spectra, the objects of stable homotopy theory, and to generalised cohomology theories such as topological K-theory, cobordism, and Morava K-theories arranged by the chromatic filtration. Landmarks include Adams's solution of the Hopf invariant one problem and the vector-fields-on-spheres problem using K-theory, and the nilpotence and periodicity theorems of Devinatz–Hopkins–Smith.

Abstract homotopy theory

Daniel Quillen's model categories (1967) axiomatised homotopy theory, making it applicable to simplicial sets, chain complexes, and algebraic geometry. This abstraction led to higher category theory and ∞-categories, and homotopy type theory in foundations of mathematics.
