The Kasubi Tombs in Kampala, Uganda, are the burial place of four kings (kabakas) of Buganda and a major spiritual and cultural site for the Baganda people. The site, on Kasubi Hill, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001 as an outstanding example of traditional African architecture built almost entirely from organic materials — wood, thatch, reed and wattle-and-daub.

History

The site began in 1882 as a palace built by Kabaka Muteesa I on Kasubi Hill. After his death in 1884 it became a royal burial ground, following Buganda tradition that a king's palace becomes his tomb. Four kabakas are buried in the main building (Muzibu Azaala Mpanga): Muteesa I, Mwanga II, Daudi Cwa II and Mutesa II. Behind the main tomb, a courtyard contains the graves of other royals, and the site is tended by descendants of the kabakas' widows and traditional caretakers.

The 2010 fire and restoration

On 16 March 2010 the main tomb building was almost completely destroyed by fire, in circumstances that remained officially unresolved and caused deep public grief and unrest in Kampala. UNESCO placed the site on the List of World Heritage in Danger. A major reconstruction, led by the Buganda Kingdom with support from UNESCO and the Japanese government, rebuilt the tomb in traditional form using traditional materials and crafts; the completed restoration led to the site's removal from the danger list in 2023.

Significance

Kasubi remains an active religious site: Baganda spiritual practices, including those associated with the balubaale (deified ancestors), continue there alongside its funerary function, and it is one of Uganda's most important cultural landmarks.
