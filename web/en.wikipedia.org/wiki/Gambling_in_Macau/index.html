Gambling in Macau has been legal since the 1850s, when the Portuguese government legalised the activity in its colony. Since then, gaming has become the pillar of Macau's economy, and the city has been known as the "Monte Carlo of the Orient" and the "Las Vegas of Asia". Macau surpassed Las Vegas in gaming revenue in 2007 and by 2013 generated about seven times the Las Vegas Strip's revenue, making it the world's largest gambling centre.

History

The Portuguese legalised gambling in 1849 to raise revenue, starting with fantan houses. In 1962 the government granted a monopoly to the Sociedade de Turismo e Diversões de Macau (STDM), led by Stanley Ho, which modernised the industry with the Casino Lisboa and ferry links to Hong Kong. After Macau's return to China in 1999, the monopoly ended in 2002 and foreign operators (Las Vegas Sands, Wynn, MGM) entered, building mega-resorts on the Cotai Strip, an area of reclaimed land joining Taipa and Coloane.

The VIP and junket era, and its end

High-roller play, channelled by junket promoters who extended credit and brought VIP clients mainly from mainland China, accounted for over half of revenue at its peak. An anti-corruption campaign from 2014 cut VIP revenue sharply; COVID-19 border closures collapsed revenue in 2020–2022; and new concessions awarded for 2023–2032, alongside a revised gaming law, effectively ended the junket model and imposed requirements for investment in non-gaming attractions.

Economy and regulation

Gaming taxes (an effective rate around 39–40% of gross gaming revenue) have historically supplied most of the Macau SAR government's budget. Casinos are regulated by the Gaming Inspection and Coordination Bureau (DICJ); the entry age is 21, advertising of gambling is restricted, and problem-gambling and money-laundering controls have tightened under both Macanese and Beijing oversight.
