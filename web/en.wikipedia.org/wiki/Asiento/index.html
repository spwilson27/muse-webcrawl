Asiento

The asiento (from Spanish asentar, "to settle") was a contract granted by the Spanish Crown to an individual or company, giving a monopoly or license for a particular trade or service in the Spanish Empire. The term is most commonly associated with the asiento de negros, the contracts by which the Crown licensed the importation of enslaved Africans into Spanish America.

The slave trade asiento

Spain's American colonies required labour, and the Spanish Crown, which had no African trading posts of its own, licensed foreign merchants to supply enslaved people. From the 16th century onward, asientos were granted successively to Portuguese, Dutch, French and British contractors. The asiento typically specified the number of enslaved Africans (measured in piezas de Indias) to be delivered over a fixed term, the ports of entry, and the duties payable to the Crown.

The most famous asiento was that granted to Britain under the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), which ended the War of the Spanish Succession. The contract, held by the South Sea Company, gave Britain a 30-year monopoly to supply 4,800 enslaved people per year to Spanish America, together with the right to send one annual trading ship (the navío de permiso). Disputes over the asiento and contraband contributed to the outbreak of the War of Jenkins' Ear in 1739. Britain renounced the asiento in the Treaty of Madrid (1750) in exchange for compensation.

Other asientos

The asiento system was used more broadly in Spanish colonial administration for contracts covering mercury supply for silver refining, tax farming, food supply to garrisons, and other services, reflecting the Crown's practice of administering its empire through negotiated contracts with private parties.
