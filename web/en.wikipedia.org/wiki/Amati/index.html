Amati

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Amati family of Cremona, Italy, were the founders of the Cremonese school of violin making and among the most celebrated luthiers in history. Between the mid-sixteenth and the early eighteenth century, across five generations, they established the basic form, proportions, and construction methods of the modern violin family that later makers — Guarneri and Stradivari among them — refined to perfection.

Andrea Amati and the birth of the violin

Andrea Amati (c. 1505–1577) is credited with creating the violin in its recognizable modern form in Cremona around the middle of the sixteenth century. His earliest surviving instruments, dating from the 1560s, include violins made for the court of Charles IX of France, some retaining their painted royal decoration. Andrea also produced violas and cellos and set the pattern of Cremonese workmanship: carved scrolls, elegant f-holes, and fine varnish.

Later generations

Andrea's sons Antonio (c. 1540–1607) and Girolamo (1561–1630), the "Brothers Amati", worked together and separately, slightly enlarging the violin outline. Girolamo's son Nicolò Amati (1596–1684) is generally considered the family's greatest maker. Surviving the plague of 1630 that killed most rival Cremonese craftsmen, Nicolò ran the dominant workshop of the century and taught — according to tradition and strong circumstantial evidence — both Andrea Guarneri and Antonio Stradivari, the two great successors of the Cremonese tradition. His grandson Girolamo II (1649–1740) was the last significant Amati luthier.

Legacy

Surviving Amati instruments — several hundred violins, violas, and cellos — are prized by soloists, collectors, and museums for their sweet, refined tone. Original Amati instruments by Nicolò regularly rank among the world's most valuable string instruments, and the family's models remain a standard reference for contemporary makers.
