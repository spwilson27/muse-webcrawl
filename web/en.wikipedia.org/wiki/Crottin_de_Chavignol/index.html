Crottin de Chavignol is a French goat's-milk cheese produced in and around the village of Chavignol, near Sancerre in the Loire Valley, in the former province of Berry. Small, cylindrical, and weighing about 60 grams, it is the most famous of the Loire's goat cheeses and has been protected by an appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC) since 1976. Its name is said to derive from the local word crot, a small oil lamp whose shape the moulded cheese resembles.

Production

Crottin de Chavignol is made from raw (or, under modern rules, sometimes pasteurized) whole goat's milk, lightly renneted and lactic-coagulated, ladled into small moulds, drained, salted, and matured. Goat farming around Chavignol grew with the phylloxera crisis of the late nineteenth century, when ruined vineyards were replaced by goats and the women of winegrowing families developed the cheese trade; production expanded again after the Second World War as the AOC system formalized it.

Stages of maturity

The crottin is sold at a celebrated range of maturities, changing character completely as it ages:

  • Mi-sec (semi-dry, around 8–15 days) — white, soft, mild and lactic under a thin natural rind.
  • Sec (dry) — firmer and denser, with a developing goaty tang and a bloomy, sometimes cream-coloured rind.
  • Très sec / affiné — small, hard, and shrunken, intensely flavoured, eaten grated or crumbled.
  • Bleu — very mature crottins develop blue-grey moulds and a powerful, pungent flavour esteemed by connoisseurs.

Serving

Young-to-mid-maturity crottins are classically served warm: grilled or baked on toast (crottin grillé) over a green salad — the salade de chèvre chaud of bistro menus. The cheese's home is Sancerre country, and its canonical pairing is a crisp local Sauvignon blanc — Sancerre or Pouilly-Fumé — an archetypal example of the "what grows together goes together" principle in French food and wine culture (see Valençay cheese, its great Loire rival).
