Incoterms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Incoterms allocate the costs and risks of shipping goods between seller and buyer.

The Incoterms (International Commercial Terms) are a set of pre-defined commercial terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) relating to international commercial law. First published in 1936 and revised periodically — most recently as Incoterms 2020 — they are widely used in international commercial transactions to define exactly the tasks, costs, and risks borne by buyers and sellers in the delivery of goods.

The eleven rules

Seven rules apply to any mode of transport: EXW (Ex Works), FCA (Free Carrier), CPT (Carriage Paid To), CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To), DAP (Delivered at Place), DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded, renamed from DAT in the 2020 edition), and DDP (Delivered Duty Paid). Four rules apply only to sea and inland waterway transport: FAS (Free Alongside Ship), FOB (Free on Board), CFR (Cost and Freight), and CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight).

Points of delivery and risk

Each rule specifies where the seller "delivers" the goods — the point at which risk of loss passes to the buyer — and which party arranges and pays for transport, insurance, and export and import clearance. Under EXW the buyer bears almost everything from the seller's premises; under DDP the seller bears maximum obligation, delivering the goods cleared for import at destination.

Use and legal status

Incoterms are not law in themselves but are incorporated by reference into contracts of sale; in some jurisdictions, such as the United States under the Uniform Commercial Code's trade-usage doctrine, they carry legal weight when used by merchants. Correct citation includes the rule, a named place, and the edition, e.g. "FOB Shanghai, Incoterms 2020."
