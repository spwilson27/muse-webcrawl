Sitcom

A typical multi-camera sitcom set: the living room.

A sitcom (short for situation comedy or situational comedy) is a genre of comedy centred on a fixed set of characters who mostly carry over from episode to episode in a common environment such as a home, workplace, or community. The situation, as opposed to the characters' actions, traditionally remains stable, and the humour arises from the characters' reactions to unusual events and from witty dialogue and visual gags.

History

The sitcom format developed on radio in the 1920s and 1930s; Amos 'n' Andy became one of radio's most popular early series. On television, I Love Lucy (1951–1957), starring Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz, pioneered the multi-camera studio format filmed before a live audience and became one of the most influential programmes of all time. In Britain, series such as Steptoe and Son and Fawlty Towers developed a darker, more farcical tradition.

American sitcoms of the 1970s — All in the Family, M*A*S*H, The Mary Tyler Moore Show — took on social issues while remaining comedic. The 1980s and 1990s saw family and ensemble hits such as The Cosby Show, Cheers, Seinfeld, and Friends. From the 2000s, single-camera shows without laugh tracks, including The Office, 30 Rock, and Arrested Development, became prominent, often blending the sitcom with mockumentary and cringe-comedy styles.

Formats

Multi-camera

Traditional sitcoms are filmed with multiple cameras before a live studio audience on standing sets, with jokes cued by audience laughter or an added laugh track. Episodes are typically 22 minutes long and resolve their plots within the episode.

Single-camera

Single-camera sitcoms are shot like films, without a studio audience, and generally use no laugh track. Location shooting and visual comedy are more common, and story arcs may extend across episodes or seasons.

Animated

Animated sitcoms such as The Simpsons, Family Guy, and South Park use the sitcom structure for both family and satirical comedy, unconstrained by physical sets or actors aging.

Conventions

Common elements include a recurring ensemble cast, a limited number of sets, a "status quo" to which the story returns by the end of each episode, catchphrases, and bottle episodes. In many series the comedy derives from a contrast between an eccentric character and a comparatively level-headed foil.
