In ecology, primary production is the synthesis of organic compounds from atmospheric or aqueous carbon dioxide. It principally occurs through the process of photosynthesis, which uses light as its source of energy, but it also occurs through chemosynthesis, which uses the oxidation or reduction of inorganic chemical compounds as its source of energy. The organisms responsible for primary production are known as primary producers or autotrophs, and form the base of the food chain. Almost all life on Earth relies directly or indirectly on primary production.

Gross and net primary production

Gross primary production (GPP) is the total amount of chemical energy as biomass that primary producers create in a given length of time. A substantial fraction of this energy is used by the producers themselves for cellular respiration and maintenance; the remainder is net primary production (NPP):

NPP = GPP − Ra

where Ra is respiration by autotrophs. NPP is the biomass available to be eaten by herbivores and decomposers, and it is usually reported in units of mass per unit area per unit time, e.g. grams of carbon per square metre per year.

Global patterns

Global NPP is estimated at roughly 105 billion tonnes (petagrams) of carbon per year, divided almost evenly between the land surface and the oceans. On land, productivity is highest in tropical rainforests and lowest in deserts and polar regions, limited mainly by water and temperature. In the ocean, tiny phytoplankton account for nearly all production, limited principally by nutrient availability (nitrogen, phosphorus, iron) and light; coastal upwelling zones and continental shelves are far more productive per unit area than the open-ocean gyres.

Measurement

Methods include harvesting and weighing biomass in terrestrial plots, gas-exchange measurements using infrared gas analysers and eddy covariance flux towers, and, in aquatic systems, measuring oxygen evolution in paired light and dark bottles or using radiocarbon (14C) uptake. Satellite remote sensing of chlorophyll and vegetation indices (such as MODIS products) now provides estimates of productivity at global scales.

Human appropriation

Humanity appropriates a large share of Earth's primary production — studies estimate that roughly a quarter of terrestrial NPP is co-opted by humans through agriculture, forestry, grazing and land conversion (the "human appropriation of net primary production", HANPP) — with significant consequences for biodiversity and the carbon cycle.
