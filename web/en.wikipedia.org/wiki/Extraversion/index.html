Extraversion

Extraversion (also spelled extroversion) is the state of primarily gaining gratification from outside oneself, and a personality trait characterized by outgoingness, talkativeness, and high energy. Extraverts tend to enjoy human interaction, be enthusiastic and assertive, and seek stimulation in the company of others. It is one pole of the extraversion–introversion dimension, popularized by Carl Jung and central to the Big Five personality traits model, Hans Eysenck's theory of personality, and the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator.

Traits

Extraverts are often described as talkative, assertive, and gregarious. They enjoy activities involving large social gatherings, such as parties, community activities, and group sports. In the Big Five model, extraversion comprises facets including warmth, gregariousness, assertiveness, activity, excitement-seeking, and positive emotions. Introverts, by contrast, are quieter, more reserved, and require less external stimulation; they often prefer solitary activities or small groups. Most people fall somewhere in the middle and are sometimes called ambiverts.

Explanations

Eysenck proposed that extraversion is linked to levels of cortical arousal: introverts have higher baseline arousal and therefore avoid stimulation, while extraverts have lower baseline arousal and seek it out. Later research connected extraversion to the dopaminergic reward system and sensitivity to positive affect. Twin studies indicate extraversion is substantially heritable, with estimates around 40–60%.

Behaviour and outcomes

Extraversion is associated with leadership emergence, job performance in sales and social occupations, larger social networks, and higher subjective well-being, though it can also correlate with impulsivity and risk-taking.
