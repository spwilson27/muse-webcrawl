Euler equations (fluid dynamics)

In fluid dynamics, the Euler equations are a set of partial differential equations governing adiabatic, inviscid flow. They are named after Leonhard Euler, who derived them in 1755. The equations express conservation of mass, momentum, and energy for a fluid with no viscosity or thermal conduction; adding viscosity yields the more general Navier–Stokes equations.

Formulation

In conservation form, with density ρ, velocity u, pressure p, and total energy E, the momentum equation reads:

∂(ρu)/∂t + ∇·(ρu⊗u + pI) = ρg

Unsteady, compressible flow problems closed by an equation of state (for an ideal gas, p = (γ−1)ρe, with γ the adiabatic index). For incompressible flow of constant density the system simplifies to ∇·u = 0 together with the momentum balance.

Properties and applications

Solutions can develop discontinuities — shock waves in compressible flow — which must satisfy the Rankine–Hugoniot jump conditions. Because the equations neglect friction, they permit d'Alembert's paradox (zero drag on a body in steady flow) but are highly successful for aerodynamics at high Reynolds number away from boundary layers, for astrophysical flows, and as the backbone of many computational fluid dynamics codes.
