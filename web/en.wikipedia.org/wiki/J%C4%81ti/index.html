Jāti

Jāti (from Sanskrit जाति, "birth") is a term used for the thousands of endogamous hereditary social groups — often translated as "castes" — that structure much of traditional South Asian society. Whereas varṇa refers to the fourfold theoretical classification (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra), jāti refers to the concrete, localised groups into which people are actually born, marry, and often inherit an occupation.

Characteristics

Jātis are traditionally endogamous, with marriage restricted to members of the group, and have historically been associated with particular occupations, dietary rules, and ritual status. Estimates of their number range from about 2,000 to over 3,000 across India. Relations among jātis were traditionally governed by ideas of purity and pollution and by systems of reciprocal service, such as the jajmani system.

Modern developments

Colonial censuses, especially under British rule, attempted to rank and enumerate jātis, hardening boundaries that had often been more flexible. In independent India, caste-based reservation policies in education and government employment, and caste-based political mobilisation, have kept jāti identity socially and politically significant even as urbanisation weakens some traditional features.
