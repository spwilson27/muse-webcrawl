Cilice

A cilice (pronounced /ˈsɪlɪs/) is a garment or device of haircloth or rough material, traditionally made of coarse animal hair or wire, worn close to the skin as an instrument of voluntary penance and mortification of the flesh in the Christian tradition.

Original meaning

The word derives from the Latin cilicium, a cloth made from the hair of goats from Cilicia in Asia Minor. In its earliest sense a cilice was a hairshirt: an undergarment of coarse haircloth worn by ascetics, monks and penitents to cause discomfort and humble the wearer, in imitation of the sackcloth and hair garments of biblical figures such as John the Baptist.

Modern form

In modern Catholic usage the term more commonly refers to a spiked metal chain or band worn around the thigh, the small inward-pointing spikes of which press into the flesh. This form of the cilice is used by some members of religious orders and by numeraries of Opus Dei, typically for limited periods of about two hours a day as a practice of corporal mortification. Its use became widely known to the general public through the character of Silas in Dan Brown's novel and film The Da Vinci Code, though the depiction there is more extreme than real practice.

History and practice

Hairshirts were worn by many saints and founders of religious orders, including Saint Francis of Assisi and Saint Thomas More, who wore one beneath his court clothing. Rules governing the practice varied by order; some communities prescribed the hairshirt or chain on particular days of the week or seasons such as Lent. Since the Second Vatican Council the practice has declined in most orders, though it continues in some contemplative communities and personal piety.
