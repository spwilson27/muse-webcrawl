Haemoglobin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Haemoglobin (hemoglobin) is the iron-containing oxygen-transport protein in the red blood cells of almost all vertebrates. Each molecule has four subunits, each holding a haem group with an iron atom that binds one oxygen molecule reversibly — loading in the lungs where oxygen is plentiful, unloading in tissues where it is scarce. Cooperative binding (each oxygen bound makes the next easier) and the Bohr effect (acidity and CO₂ promoting release) tune delivery to need.

Haemoglobin gives blood its red colour — bright scarlet when oxygenated, darker red when not; the blue tint of veins is an optical effect of skin, not blue blood. It is the textbook example of protein structure–function, among the first proteins whose structure was solved by X-ray crystallography (Perutz, 1960).

Variants and disease

Hundreds of haemoglobin variants are known; the important ones cause disease — haemoglobin S in sickle cell disease, and the thalassaemias from reduced globin production. Fetal haemoglobin (HbF, with higher oxygen affinity) draws oxygen across the placenta and is replaced by adult forms after birth — a switch modern gene therapy reactivates to treat blood disorders. Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin ~200 times more strongly than oxygen, the basis of its toxicity.

Measurement

Haemoglobin concentration is the standard measure of anaemia; glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) gives the 2–3-month average of blood glucose used in Diabetes care.
