Inquisition

The Inquisition was a group of institutions within the Catholic Church whose aim was to combat heresy, conducting trials of suspected heretics through "inquisitorial" procedure, in which judges actively investigated accusations rather than waiting for a formal accuser. Beginning in the 12th century, distinct inquisitions arose in different eras and regions: the Medieval Inquisition (1184 – c. 1500s), the Spanish Inquisition (1478–1834), the Portuguese Inquisition (1536–1821), and the Roman Inquisition (1542 onward), whose governing congregation survives today as the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith.

Medieval Inquisition

Pope Lucius III's decretal Ad abolendam (1184) required bishops to seek out heretics; in 1231 Pope Gregory IX placed the task largely in the hands of papally appointed inquisitors, often Dominicans and Franciscans. The medieval inquisitions targeted movements such as the Cathars of southern France (also fought in the Albigensian Crusade) and the Waldensians. Procedures allowed secret denunciations, withheld the names of witnesses from the accused, and — from 1252, with Innocent IV's Ad extirpanda — permitted limited torture. Penalties ranged from penances, pilgrimages and wearing crosses, to imprisonment; unrepentant or relapsed heretics were "relaxed to the secular arm", which could impose burning at the stake.

Spanish Inquisition

Established in 1478 by Ferdinand II and Isabella I, the Spanish Inquisition was controlled by the Crown rather than the papacy. Under its first Grand Inquisitor, Tomás de Torquemada, it focused on conversos — Jews who had converted to Christianity — suspected of secretly practising Judaism; later targets included Moriscos, Protestants, and offences such as bigamy and blasphemy. Modern historians estimate it tried well over 100,000 people across its 356 years, executing on the order of 3,000–5,000. Its public sentencing ritual, the auto-da-fé, became emblematic. It was finally abolished in 1834.

Portuguese and Roman Inquisitions

The Portuguese Inquisition (1536) operated in Portugal and its empire, including the notorious Goa Inquisition in India, and targeted converts from Judaism and Hinduism. The Roman Inquisition, formally the Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Roman and Universal Inquisition (1542), policed doctrine in Italy and for the whole Church; it is best known for the trials of Giordano Bruno (executed 1600) and Galileo Galilei (condemned 1633), and for the Index of Forbidden Books.

Historiography

Protestant and Enlightenment polemic created the "Black Legend" of the Inquisition as an engine of mass slaughter; modern archival scholarship has greatly reduced estimated execution figures while documenting the real fear, denunciation culture and destruction of communities the tribunals caused. The subject remains a byword for intolerance — a reputation reinforced by cultural works from Dostoyevsky's "Grand Inquisitor" to Monty Python's famous sketch.
