Nucleophilic addition

In organic chemistry, a nucleophilic addition (AN) reaction is an addition reaction where a chemical compound with an electrophilic double or triple bond reacts with a nucleophile, such that the double or triple bond is broken. Nucleophilic additions differ from electrophilic additions in that the former involve the group to which atoms are added accepting electron pairs, whereas the latter involve the group losing electron pairs.

Addition to carbon–heteroatom double bonds

The most important examples involve the carbonyl group of aldehydes and ketones. The nucleophile attacks the planar, electron-poor carbonyl carbon, forming a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate which is then protonated. Reactions of this class include:

  • Addition of Grignard reagents and organolithiums, giving alcohols after work-up.
  • Hydration, and formation of hemiacetals and acetals with alcohols.
  • Addition of hydrogen cyanide to give cyanohydrins.
  • Formation of imines and enamines with primary and secondary amines.
  • Reduction with hydride reagents such as NaBH4 and LiAlH4.

Addition to carbon–carbon multiple bonds

Ordinary alkenes are electron-rich and not susceptible to nucleophilic attack, but electron-poor alkenes conjugated with electron-withdrawing groups undergo conjugate (Michael) addition. Activated alkynes similarly accept nucleophiles. Stereochemistry at the newly formed tetrahedral centre is governed by steric approach and models such as Felkin–Anh for chiral carbonyl compounds.
