Precipitation (chemistry)

Precipitation is the process of transformation of a dissolved substance into an insoluble solid from a supersaturated solution. The solid formed is called the precipitate. In inorganic chemistry, precipitation often occurs when the concentration of an ionic compound exceeds its solubility — for example when two solutions containing soluble salts are mixed and an insoluble salt forms, as when silver nitrate and sodium chloride solutions produce a white precipitate of silver chloride.

Formation

When a solution becomes supersaturated — containing more dissolved solid than equilibrium allows — nucleation occurs: tiny clusters of the solid phase form, either spontaneously or on seed particles, and grow into visible particles. The rate of nucleation relative to particle growth determines the particle size; high supersaturation favours many small particles, which can produce colloidal suspensions that are difficult to filter.

Applications

Precipitation is used in gravimetric analysis, where a weighed precipitate determines the amount of an analyte; in water treatment, to remove hardness and contaminants by lime softening and coagulation; in metallurgy and pigment manufacture; and in protein purification, for example by ammonium sulfate precipitation ("salting out").

Related concepts

Because solubility usually falls with temperature, cooling a saturated solution can trigger precipitation, as can adding an antisolvent. The reverse process is dissolution. Precipitation differs from crystallisation mainly in speed and in the usual size and regularity of the particles produced.
