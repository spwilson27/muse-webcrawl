Watersprout

A watersprout is an energetic shoot that arises from the trunk or an older branch of a tree or shrub, typically from a dormant or adventitious bud. Watersprouts are characterised by rapid, upright (vertical) growth, long internodes, and large leaves, and they usually arise in response to stress, heavy pruning, damage, or a sudden increase in light reaching the interior of the crown.

Characteristics

Because they grow from older wood rather than from the tree's normal extension shoots, watersprouts are usually weakly attached to the branch from which they emerge, having only superficial bark attachment rather than interlocking wood. They rarely flower or fruit well in their first years and, in fruit trees, they shade the interior of the canopy, spoil the tree's structure and divert nutrients from productive wood, so orchardists generally remove most of them during pruning.

Watersprouts differ from suckers, which arise from the roots or the base of the trunk below the graft union; shoots arising from a grafted tree's rootstock can overtake the scion variety if left unchecked.

Management

Arborists recommend removing watersprouts while young, pruning in summer rather than in the dormant season to reduce regrowth, and avoiding overly severe heading cuts, which stimulate the tree to produce more watersprouts. A small number of watersprouts may be retained and trained to replace a damaged limb or to rejuvenate an old tree.
