Pacific decadal oscillation

The Pacific decadal oscillation (PDO) is a robust, recurring pattern of ocean–atmosphere climate variability centred over the mid-latitude Pacific basin. It is detected as warm or cool surface waters in the Pacific Ocean north of 20°N, and it shifts between "warm" and "cool" phases lasting roughly 20 to 30 years. The PDO was named by Steven Hare in 1997, who noticed it while studying connections between Alaska salmon production and Pacific climate.

Characteristics

During a warm (positive) phase, the west Pacific becomes cooler and part of the eastern ocean warms; during a cool (negative) phase the pattern reverses. PDO phases correlate with patterns of temperature and precipitation around the Pacific Rim: warm phases have been associated with wetter conditions in the southwestern United States and drought in the Pacific Northwest, while cool phases show the opposite tendency. The PDO also modulates the effects of El Niño and La Niña events, amplifying their impacts when the two are in phase.

Regime shifts and mechanisms

Clear shifts in the PDO occurred in 1925, 1947, 1977, and around 1998–2014. The causes are not fully understood and are thought to combine several processes: stochastic atmospheric forcing of the ocean, re-emergence of subsurface temperature anomalies, tropical Pacific influences transmitted through "atmospheric bridges," and oceanic Rossby waves. Unlike ENSO, the PDO has no single dominant mechanism, and researchers continue to debate whether it is a distinct physical mode or an emergent pattern of several processes.

Effects

Beyond regional climate, PDO phases have been linked to multi-decadal fluctuations in fish stocks, including Pacific salmon, sardines, and anchovies, as well as to streamflow, snowpack, and wildfire frequency in western North America.
