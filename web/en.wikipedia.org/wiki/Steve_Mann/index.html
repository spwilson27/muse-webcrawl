Steve Mann

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Steven Mann (born 1962) is a Canadian engineer and inventor widely regarded as a pioneer of wearable computing and a founder of the field of augmented reality. A professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the University of Toronto and chief scientist at the Creative Destruction Lab's wearable/AI stream, Mann has worn computerized vision systems — evolving through generations of his "EyeTap" devices — in daily life since the late 1970s.

Wearable computing

Mann began experimenting as a teenager with backpack-mounted computers and helmet cameras that fed his eye a digitally processed view of the world. At the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where he earned his PhD from the MIT Media Lab in 1997, his "WearComp" and "WearCam" systems became reference designs for wearable computing. His EyeTap device intercepts light entering the eye so that a computer can alter the wearer's visual perception in real time, enabling what he calls "mediated reality." He has worn such apparatus continuously for decades and is sometimes described as the world's first cyborg.

Concepts and work

Mann coined the terms sousveillance (watching from below, the inverse of surveillance) and veillance, and co-developed the concept of equiveillance. He also founded the field he calls hydraulikos, the study of water-based instruments and interfaces, and established a hydraulophone research group. His writings include Cyborg: Digital Destiny and Human Possibility in the Age of the Wearable Computer (2001). In 2012, an incident in a Paris McDonald's, where employees allegedly assaulted him over his Digital Eye Glass, attracted international attention to the social acceptance of wearable cameras — preceding by a year the public debates around Google Glass.

Recognition

Mann was inducted into the McMaster University Alumni Hall of Fame, and his early wearable systems are held in museum collections. He continues to research wearable AI, imaging (including high-dynamic-range imaging, of which he is an early pioneer), and the ethics of surveillance and sousveillance.
