Plea bargain

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A plea bargain (also plea agreement, plea deal) is an agreement in a criminal case between the prosecutor and defendant whereby the defendant agrees to plead guilty (or nolo contendere) to a particular charge in return for concessions — charge reductions, dropped counts, a sentence recommendation or an agreed sentence. In the United States the overwhelming majority of criminal convictions (commonly estimated around 90–95 per cent) result from guilty pleas rather than trials, making bargaining the system's central institution.

Operation and criticism

Defendants trade the uncertainty and exposure of trial — including the "trial penalty", the often dramatic differential between the bargained sentence and the post-trial sentence — for certainty and leniency; prosecutors secure convictions without trial's cost and risk. US courts accepted the practice constitutionally only in 1970–71 (Brady, Santobello) and require pleas to be knowing and voluntary, with counsel's competence reviewable after Lafler and Frye (2012). Critics argue the plea machine coerces the innocent (a documented share of later-exonerated defendants had pleaded guilty), hides police and prosecutorial conduct from testing, and discriminates by resources; defenders answer that abolition would collapse court capacity and often harm defendants. Many civil-law systems long resisted bargaining but have adopted negotiated-justice procedures under caseload pressure (Germany, Italy's patteggiamento, France's CRPC), blending traditions.
