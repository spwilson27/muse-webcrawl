School of Antioch

RegionAntioch, Roman Syria
Era3rd–5th centuries AD
Key figuresLucian of Antioch, Diodore of Tarsus, Theodore of Mopsuestia, John Chrysostom, Nestorius
ApproachLiteral-historical exegesis; dyophysite Christology

The School of Antioch was one of the two major centres of biblical hermeneutics and theology in early Christianity, alongside the School of Alexandria. Based in Antioch on the Orontes (in modern-day Turkey), the Antiochene tradition is characterised by an emphasis on the literal and historical interpretation of Scripture, in contrast to the allegorical method favoured at Alexandria.

Origins

The school is traditionally traced to Lucian of Antioch (c. 240–312), a presbyter and martyr who produced a widely used recension of the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament. Whether Lucian founded a formal institution is debated, but Antioch developed a recognisable exegetical tradition by the fourth century.

Method and theology

Antiochene exegetes such as Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia insisted on the historia of the biblical text — its plain, contextual sense — while still allowing a higher contemplative meaning (theoria) grounded in the literal sense. They resisted allegorical readings that, in their view, dissolved the reality of the biblical narrative.

In Christology, the Antiochenes stressed the full humanity of Christ and the distinction between his two natures, an approach later called dyophysitism. This emphasis brought the school into conflict with the Alexandrian tradition in the controversies surrounding Nestorius, Patriarch of Constantinople (an Antiochene product), whose teaching was condemned at the Council of Ephesus in 431. Theodore of Mopsuestia was posthumously condemned at the Second Council of Constantinople in 553.

Notable figures

  • Lucian of Antioch — textual scholar and martyr
  • Diodore of Tarsus — bishop and exegete
  • Theodore of Mopsuestia — the school's most systematic theologian
  • John Chrysostom — preacher and Archbishop of Constantinople, famed for his homilies
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus — bishop, historian and exegete
  • Nestorius — Patriarch of Constantinople, condemned in 431

Legacy

Antiochene exegesis influenced later literal-historical interpretation and the theology of the Church of the East. Modern scholarship has rehabilitated much of the school's reputation, recognising its contributions to biblical studies and its concern to preserve the integrity of Christ's humanity.
