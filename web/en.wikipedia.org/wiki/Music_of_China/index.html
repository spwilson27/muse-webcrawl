Music of China

The music of China encompasses a long and varied tradition stretching back thousands of years, from the ritual music of the Zhou dynasty court to the regional operas, folk traditions, and popular music of today. Chinese music has developed largely independently of Western traditions, with its own systems of tuning, notation, instruments, and aesthetics, and it has profoundly influenced the music of neighbouring East and Southeast Asian cultures.

A traditional ensemble: the silk-and-bamboo combination of strings and winds is characteristic of Chinese chamber music.

History

Archaeological finds include the Jiahu bone flutes, dated to around 7000 BC, among the oldest playable instruments in the world. Confucian philosophy placed music (yue) at the heart of ritual and social order, and the court maintained elaborate yayue (elegant music) for ceremonies. Under the Han and Tang dynasties, imperial music bureaus collected folk songs and absorbed influences from Central Asia along the Silk Road — the pipa, for example, derives from the Persian barbat. Song-dynasty narrative singing and Yuan-dynasty drama laid the foundations of Chinese opera, which flowered regionally into forms such as Peking opera and Cantonese opera.

Instruments and theory

Traditional instruments are classically grouped by material into eight categories — the "eight sounds": silk (strings such as the guqin, erhu, and pipa), bamboo (flutes such as the dizi and xiao), wood, stone (chimes), metal (bells and gongs), clay, gourd, and skin (drums). The traditional scale system is based on the pentatonic scale generated by the cycle of fifths, codified in the pitch pipes (lü) series.

Modern era

In the 20th century, Chinese music absorbed Western harmony, notation, and instruments; conservatories taught both traditions, and the modern Chinese orchestra — modelled on the Western symphony orchestra but using Chinese instruments — emerged. Revolutionary model operas dominated during the Cultural Revolution. From the 1980s, Mandopop and Cantopop became commercial powerhouses, and contemporary China has active rock, hip-hop, electronic, and indie scenes alongside state-supported traditional performance.
