Most favoured nation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Most favoured nation (MFN) is a status or level of treatment accorded by one state to another in international trade relations: a country that has been accorded MFN status may not be treated less advantageously than any other country with MFN status by the promising state. In the GATT/WTO system, MFN is the foundational non-discrimination rule (GATT Article I): any advantage granted to a product of any country must be extended immediately and unconditionally to like products of all members.

Despite the name, MFN confers not special favour but equal treatment — the United States renamed its version “permanent normal trade relations” (PNTR) in 1998. Principal exceptions include customs unions and free-trade areas (GATT Article XXIV), preferences for developing countries under the Enabling Clause, and measures for national security. MFN clauses are older than the multilateral system: they spread through nineteenth-century bilateral treaties after the Cobden–Chevalier Treaty of 1860 between Britain and France. In investment and other treaties the principle similarly guarantees the best treatment given to any comparable foreign party.
