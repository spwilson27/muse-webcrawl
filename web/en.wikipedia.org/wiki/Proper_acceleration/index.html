Proper acceleration

In relativity theory, proper acceleration is the physical acceleration — the acceleration relative to a free-fall (inertial) frame — that an object experiences and that can be measured by an accelerometer carried with the object. It is "proper" in the sense of being measured in the object's own momentary rest frame. A person standing on Earth experiences a proper acceleration of about 1 g (9.8 m/s²) directed upward, because the ground prevents free fall; an object in free fall or in orbit has zero proper acceleration despite being subject to gravity.

Definition

Proper acceleration is the three-vector part of the four-acceleration evaluated in the instantaneous rest frame. For motion along a straight line in special relativity, the proper acceleration α and the coordinate acceleration a (as measured by an inertial observer) are related by α = γ³a, where γ is the Lorentz factor. Unlike coordinate velocity, which is bounded by the speed of light, proper acceleration is unbounded and can remain constant indefinitely: an object under constant proper acceleration follows hyperbolic motion, its velocity asymptotically approaching c while its proper time accumulates ever more slowly relative to the inertial frame.

Constant proper acceleration and travel

Motion at a constant proper acceleration of 1 g is of special interest for hypothetical interstellar travel, since the crew would feel Earth-like gravity. Under such acceleration (with a flip and deceleration at the midpoint), travellers could reach distant stars within a human lifetime of ship time — the Andromeda galaxy in about 28.7 years of proper time — even though millions of years pass in the home frame, an extreme consequence of time dilation.

In curved spacetime

In general relativity, an object's four-acceleration is the covariant derivative of its four-velocity along its world line. Objects following geodesics (free fall) have zero proper acceleration; a non-zero proper acceleration indicates a non-gravitational force acting. The equivalence principle is expressed in the fact that proper acceleration, unlike "gravity," is an absolute, measurable quantity — it is what makes the situations of an accelerating rocket and a stationary observer in a gravitational field locally indistinguishable.
