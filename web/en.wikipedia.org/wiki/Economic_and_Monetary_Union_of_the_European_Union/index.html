Economic and Monetary Union of the European Union

The Economic and Monetary Union (EMU) is an umbrella term for the group of policies aimed at converging the economies of member states of the European Union in three stages, culminating for participating states in adoption of the euro as a single currency and a single monetary policy under the European Central Bank. Twenty EU member states form the euro area (eurozone).

Stages

The Delors Report of 1989 proposed three stages: (1) liberalisation of capital movements and closer economic coordination from 1990; (2) creation of the European Monetary Institute and convergence programmes from 1994; and (3) irrevocably fixed exchange rates, the introduction of the euro (1999 for electronic transactions, 2002 for notes and coins), and a single monetary policy. The Maastricht Treaty of 1992 gave EMU its legal basis and set the convergence criteria on inflation, public finances, exchange-rate stability and interest rates.

Governance

Monetary policy for the euro area is set by the ECB and the Eurosystem of national central banks. Fiscal policy remains national but is coordinated through the Stability and Growth Pact. The euro area sovereign debt crisis from 2009 led to new instruments, including the European Stability Mechanism, banking union with single supervision of banks, and stricter fiscal rules.

Opt-outs and non-members

Denmark negotiated a formal opt-out; other non-euro member states are obliged in principle to join once they meet the criteria, though Sweden has remained outside by not joining the exchange-rate mechanism.
