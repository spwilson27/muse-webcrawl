Quercus coccifera, the kermes oak, is an oak bush in the Eastern Mediterranean and North African region, in the beech family Fagaceae. It is native to the Mediterranean Basin, occurring from Morocco and the Iberian Peninsula in the west through France, Italy, and the Balkans to Turkey, Syria, Lebanon, and Israel in the east.

The species is closely related to the Palestine oak (Quercus calliprinos) of the eastern Mediterranean, and the two are sometimes treated as a single species.

Description

The kermes oak is a shrub or small tree, typically 1–5 metres tall, though it can reach 8 metres. Its leaves are small, leathery, and glossy green, 1.5–4 cm long and 1–3 cm broad, with spiny-toothed margins well adapted to resist browsing animals in dry Mediterranean scrubland (maquis and garrigue).

The acorns are 2–3 cm long and 1.5–2 cm in diameter, maturing in the second year. The tree is monoecious, with wind-pollinated catkins appearing in spring.

Ecology and uses

Quercus coccifera is a characteristic component of Mediterranean maquis shrubland. It is an important food plant for the kermes scale insect (Kermes vermilio), from which the crimson dye kermes — the source of the colour "crimson" — was historically harvested. The dye was one of the most valuable red dyestuffs of the ancient and medieval world before it was supplanted by cochineal from the Americas.

The wood is hard and dense, traditionally used for charcoal. The tannin-rich bark and galls have also been used in leather tanning and traditional medicine.

Taxonomy

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in Species Plantarum (1753). The specific epithet coccifera refers to the berry-like kermes insects ("cocci") it hosts. Some authors merge it with Quercus calliprinos, while others keep both distinct.
