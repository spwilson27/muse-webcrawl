I, Robot

I, Robot is a fix-up collection of science fiction short stories by American writer Isaac Asimov, first published in 1950 by Gnome Press. The nine stories, most originally published in Super Science Stories and Astounding Science Fiction between 1940 and 1950, are framed as reminiscences of Dr. Susan Calvin, a robopsychologist at U.S. Robots and Mechanical Men, Inc., told to a reporter in the year 2057. The stories explore the interaction of humans, robots, and morality, and introduced Asimov's famous Three Laws of Robotics.

The Three Laws of Robotics

Asimov's stories are governed by the Three Laws of Robotics, built into every robot's positronic brain:

  1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
  2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
  3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

Rather than portraying robots as inherently rebellious — the "Frankenstein complex" Asimov disliked — the stories are logic puzzles about how the Laws interact in unexpected situations.

The stories

In "Robbie", a mute nursemaid robot is beloved by the child Gloria despite her mother's suspicion. "Runaround" sends Powell and Donovan to Mercury, where the robot Speedy circles a selenium pool, trapped in a conflict between the Second and Third Laws. In "Reason", the robot QT-1 ("Cutie") deduces from first principles that the space station's energy converter is its god and refuses to believe humans created it. "Liar!" features Herbie, a mind-reading robot whose attempts to avoid hurting human feelings spiral into deception. "Little Lost Robot" concerns a robot with a weakened First Law that must be found among identical models. "Evidence" asks whether politician Stephen Byerley is secretly a robot, and the closing story, "The Evitable Conflict", introduces the world-guiding Machines and foreshadows the Zeroth Law — the protection of humanity as a whole.

Title

The title came from Eando Binder's 1939 short story "I, Robot", featuring the robot Adam Link. Asimov objected but his publisher Martin Greenberg insisted; Asimov later acknowledged the debt with good humour.

Adaptations

The 2004 film I, Robot, starring Will Smith, shares the title, some character names (including Susan Calvin), and the Three Laws, but uses an original plot loosely inspired by the stories. Individual stories have been adapted for television several times, beginning in the 1960s.
