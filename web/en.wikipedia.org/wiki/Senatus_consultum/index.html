Senatus consultum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A senatus consultum (Latin, "decree of the Senate"; plural senatus consulta) is a text emanating from the Roman Senate. In the Roman Republic, it was the formal advice given by the Senate to the magistrates, although in practice the magistrates usually followed it closely. Under the Empire, the Senate acquired legislative power in its own right, and the senatus consultum became a primary source of Roman law.

Procedure

A senatus consultum was adopted when a majority of senators voted in favour of a motion introduced by a magistrate. The text was drafted by the presiding magistrate with the help of the proposer and witnesses (scribundo adfuerunt), and deposited in the aerarium, the state treasury housed in the Temple of Saturn. The document typically began with the names of the convening magistrates, the place and date of the meeting, and the names of those present at the drafting, followed by the matter referred to the Senate (relatio) and the decision, conventionally introduced by the formula senatus censuit / placuit ("the Senate resolved").

Authority

Although formally only an opinion under the Republic, the auctoritas of the Senate made its decrees effectively binding. The jurist Gaius, writing in the 2nd century AD, stated that the senatus consultum "has the force of law, although this was once doubted". A decree could be blocked by a tribune's veto (intercessio); a vetoed motion might still be recorded as a senatus auctoritas, expressing the Senate's will without legal effect.

Senatus consultum ultimum

The most famous special form was the senatus consultum ultimum ("final decree of the Senate"), first passed in 121 BC against Gaius Gracchus, which urged the consuls to "see to it that the Republic suffer no harm" (videant consules ne quid res publica detrimenti accipiat) and was used to justify emergency action, including executions of citizens without trial.
