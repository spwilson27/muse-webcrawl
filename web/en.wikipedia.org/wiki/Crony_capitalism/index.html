Crony capitalism

Crony capitalism describes success in business driven by close ties to government.

Crony capitalism, sometimes called cronyism, is a term describing an economic system in which businesses thrive not as a result of free enterprise, but rather as a return on money amassed through a nexus between a business class and the political class. This is often achieved by manipulation of relationships with state power by business interests — for example through favourable tax breaks, government grants, permits, licences and other forms of state intervention — rather than by competition in an open market.

Concept

The term has been applied to a wide range of countries and periods: the chaebol of South Korea and zaibatsu of post-war Japan, the business oligarchs who emerged in Russia after the fall of the Soviet Union, Malaysia under Mahathir Mohamad, and the Philippines under Ferdinand Marcos, where the term was popularised. Economists critical of such systems argue that they distort markets, encourage rent-seeking, and concentrate wealth among the politically connected. The Economist publishes a "crony-capitalism index" ranking countries by the share of billionaire wealth estimated to arise from crony sectors.

Criticism and debate

The concept is used across the political spectrum, though its targets differ: free-market critics use it to attack subsidies, bailouts and protectionism, while critics on the left use it to describe the capture of the state by corporate interests. Some commentators argue the term is imprecise, since the systems described are departures from capitalism rather than forms of it.
