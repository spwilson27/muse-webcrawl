Taurine

Formula: C2H7NO3S

Molar mass: 125.15 g/mol

IUPAC name: 2-aminoethane-1-sulfonic acid

Appearance: Colourless/white crystalline solid

Taurine, or 2-aminoethanesulfonic acid, is an organic compound widely distributed in animal tissues. It is a major constituent of bile and accounts for up to about 0.1% of total human body weight. Taurine is named after the Latin taurus (bull) because it was first isolated from ox bile by Friedrich Tiedemann and Leopold Gmelin in 1827.

Chemistry and biosynthesis

Taurine is unusual among biological amino compounds in being a sulfonic acid rather than a carboxylic acid, and it is not incorporated into proteins. Humans synthesize modest amounts in the liver from cysteine and methionine through cysteine sulfinic acid, with the enzyme cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase; infants have limited synthetic capacity, so taurine is conditionally essential in early life. Dietary sources are principally meat, fish, and dairy; energy drinks supply synthetic taurine.

Biological roles

Taurine has many physiological functions: it conjugates bile acids (forming taurocholic acid), acts as an osmolyte in cell-volume regulation, modulates calcium signalling and neurotransmission (as an agonist at glycine and GABA receptors), stabilizes cell membranes, and acts as an antioxidant. It is highly concentrated in the retina, heart, and developing brain. Cats cannot synthesize enough taurine and require it in their diet; deficiency causes retinal degeneration and dilated cardiomyopathy.

Uses

Taurine is a common ingredient in energy drinks (typically about 1 g per serving) and infant formula, and is used in some pharmaceutical and contact-lens products. While often marketed for performance effects, evidence for benefits in healthy adults from supplemental taurine is limited; it is generally regarded as safe at customary doses.
