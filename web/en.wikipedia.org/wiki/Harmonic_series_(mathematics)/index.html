In mathematics, the harmonic series is the infinite series formed by summing all positive unit fractions:

1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + 1/5 + ⋯ = Σn=1∞ 1/n.

The first n terms of the series sum to approximately ln n + γ, where ln is the natural logarithm and γ ≈ 0.577 is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Because the logarithm has arbitrarily large values, the harmonic series does not converge but instead diverges, albeit extremely slowly: the partial sums require exponentially many terms to reach a given size, so that, for example, more than 1043 terms are needed for the sum to exceed 100.

History and divergence proofs

The divergence of the harmonic series was first proved in the 14th century by Nicole Oresme, whose argument grouped terms in blocks each exceeding 1/2. Other proofs use the integral test — comparing the series with the integral of 1/x — or the Cauchy condensation test. The sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges even more slowly, a result proved by Euler.

Partial sums

The nth partial sum Hn, called the nth harmonic number, satisfies Hn = ln n + γ + εn, where εn approaches 0 as n grows. Harmonic numbers appear in contexts such as the coupon collector's problem (whose expected solution is nHn), the analysis of the quicksort algorithm, and the book-stacking (leaning tower of Lire) problem.

Related series

The alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯ converges conditionally to ln 2. Replacing the denominators with their squares gives the Basel problem series, converging to π²/6. The generalised harmonic series Σ 1/np converges exactly when p > 1.
