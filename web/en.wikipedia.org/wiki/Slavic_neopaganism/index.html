Slavic neopaganism

Slavic neopaganism, commonly known as Rodnovery (from Slavic Rodnaya Vera, "Native Faith"), is a modern pagan religion and new religious movement that reconstructs and revives the pre-Christian beliefs and rituals of the Slavic peoples. Rodnovers venerate the gods of the Slavic pantheon — such as Rod, Perun, Veles, Svarog, Mokosh, and Dazhbog — and emphasize kinship, ancestry, nature worship, and the cyclical calendar of folk festivals.

History

Attempts to revive Slavic religion began in the 19th and early 20th centuries amid Romantic nationalism. Organized Rodnover groups appeared in the 1930s in Poland (the Zadruga movement) and continued semi-clandestinely in the Soviet Union, where dissidents such as Alexey Dobrovolsky ("Dobroslav") promoted Slavic paganism from the 1970s. The movement expanded rapidly after the fall of communism in 1989–1991, with communities forming across Russia, Ukraine, Poland, Belarus, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Serbia, and other Slavic countries, as well as diaspora communities.

Beliefs and practices

Rodnovery is generally polytheistic and pantheistic, viewing divinity as manifest in nature and ancestry. Theology in many groups derives from the concept of Rod, the primordial progenitor god, with the universe ordered between Prav (right/order), Yav (manifest reality), and Nav (the unseen world of the dead) — a framework popularized by the controversial Book of Veles, a text most scholars regard as a 20th-century forgery but which many believers hold sacred. Rituals include seasonal festivals (Kupala night, Maslenitsa-derived rites, solstice and equinox celebrations), weddings, name-givings, and funerary rites, often held at open-air temples (kapishche) or sacred groves.

Demographics and scholarship

Adherents number in the tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands across Slavic Europe, with surveys suggesting small but growing minorities in Russia and Ukraine. Scholars of religion study Rodnovery as part of the wider phenomenon of modern paganism and post-communist religious revival, noting internal diversity between reconstructionist groups seeking historical fidelity and more eclectic or nationalist currents.
