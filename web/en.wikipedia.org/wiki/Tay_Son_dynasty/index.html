Tay Son dynasty

The Tay Son dynasty (Vietnamese: Nhà Tây Sơn, 1778–1802) was a Vietnamese dynasty founded by the three Tay Son brothers — Nguyễn Nhạc, Nguyễn Huệ and Nguyễn Lữ — who rose from a rebellion in the village of Tây Sơn in Bình Định province to overthrow the ruling Nguyễn and Trịnh lords and briefly reunify Vietnam after two centuries of division.

Rise

The brothers' revolt began in 1771 amid heavy taxation and corruption under the Nguyễn lords of the south. Their forces took Quy Nhơn, then in 1777 stormed Saigon and destroyed the Nguyễn ruling family — all but the young Nguyễn Ánh, who escaped. Turning north, Nguyễn Huệ defeated the Trịnh in 1786, entering Thăng Long (Hanoi). In 1788 he proclaimed himself Emperor Quang Trung and, in the Tết offensive of early 1789, crushed a Qing Chinese invasion at the battles of Ngọc Hồi and Đống Đa, one of the most celebrated victories in Vietnamese history.

Rule and reforms

Quang Trung planned sweeping reforms: promoting chữ Nôm over classical Chinese in administration and examinations, land redistribution, encouraging trade, and reorganising the army and administration. His early death in 1792 left the throne to his young son Quang Toản (Cảnh Thịnh), while factional conflict and the rivalry of his brother Nhạc's court weakened the dynasty. Nguyễn Ánh, backed by French advisers such as Pigneau de Béhaine, advanced steadily; he took Saigon, then Quy Nhơn, and finally Thăng Long in 1802, founding the Nguyễn dynasty as Emperor Gia Long and executing the last Tay Son ruler.

Legacy

Despite its brevity the Tay Son era is remembered in Vietnam as a heroic age of national reunification and resistance to foreign invasion; Quang Trung is honoured with temples, statues and museums, notably at the Quang Trung Museum in his home district of Tây Sơn.
