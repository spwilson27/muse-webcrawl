Climate fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Climate fiction imagines worlds transformed by climate change

Climate fiction (often abbreviated cli-fi) is a genre of literature, film, and other media that deals with climate change and global warming. Not necessarily science fiction, the genre encompasses dystopian, utopian, and realist works that explore the social, political, and psychological consequences of a changing climate.

History

Early precursors include J. G. Ballard's The Drowned World (1962) and The Burning World (1964). The term "cli-fi" was popularised by journalist Dan Bloom in the late 2000s. The genre expanded rapidly in the 2010s alongside growing public awareness of climate change.

Notable works

  • Kim Stanley Robinson — New York 2140 (2017), The Ministry for the Future (2020)
  • Margaret Atwood — the MaddAddam trilogy (2003–2013)
  • Barbara Kingsolver — Flight Behavior (2012)
  • Nathaniel Rich — Odds Against Tomorrow (2013)
  • Film: The Day After Tomorrow (2004), Interstellar (2014), Don't Look Up (2021)

Themes

Common themes include sea-level rise, extreme weather, climate migration, collapse of governance, geoengineering dilemmas, and intergenerational ethics. Critics debate whether cli-fi raises awareness or risks normalising catastrophe.
