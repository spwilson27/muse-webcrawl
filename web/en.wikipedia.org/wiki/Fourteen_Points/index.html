Fourteen Points

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: President Woodrow Wilson addressing Congress, January 1918]
President Woodrow Wilson addressing Congress, January 1918

The Fourteen Points were a statement of principles for peace proposed by US President Woodrow Wilson in an address to Congress on 8 January 1918, outlining his vision for ending the First World War and preventing future wars. They demanded open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, disarmament, the settlement of colonial claims, the restoration of occupied territories, national self-determination in Europe and, finally, a League of Nations.

Content

The first five points were general principles; points six to thirteen redrew Europe — Russia's evacuation and self-government, Belgium and France restored (with Alsace-Lorraine), Italy's frontiers 'along clearly recognisable lines of nationality', autonomy for Austria-Hungary's peoples, Balkan settlements, Turkish sovereignty with autonomy for Ottoman peoples and open Dardanelles, and an independent Poland with access to the sea. The fourteenth proposed a 'general association of nations' guaranteeing political independence and territorial integrity to all states.

Reception and Paris

The Points were translated and dropped as propaganda over enemy lines; Germany appealed for an armistice on their basis in October 1918 — a fact that later fuelled the sense of betrayal when the Treaty of Versailles (1919) imposed reparations and territorial losses many Germans saw as violating the pre-armistice agreement. At Paris, Wilson traded away much of the programme (freedom of the seas, reparations restraint, colonial self-determination) to secure the League Covenant, and then the US Senate refused ratification, keeping America out of its own president's League.

Legacy

The Fourteen Points canonised self-determination as the organising norm of international order — applied selectively to Europe, denied to the colonised world, whose petitioners (from Ho Chi Minh to Korean and Irish delegations) invoked Wilson in vain in 1919, a disappointment with century-long consequences.
