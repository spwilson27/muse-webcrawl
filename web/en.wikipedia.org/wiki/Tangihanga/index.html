Tangihanga

Tangihanga, or more simply tangi, is the traditional Māori funeral rite practised in New Zealand. Held on a marae, a tangihanga typically lasts several days (commonly three), during which the body (tūpāpaku) lies in state — traditionally in an open coffin — while family (whānau) and visitors gather to mourn, speak to the deceased, sing waiata, and observe customs governed by tikanga (protocol).

Customs

The deceased is seldom left alone: close female relatives (ruahine) sit beside the coffin, wearing wreaths of leaves (pare kawakawa) and leading the lamentation. Speeches (whaikōrero) address the dead directly, reviewing genealogy and deeds; visitors arrive in groups and are welcomed with pōwhiri. Catholic and other Christian elements are frequently combined with traditional practice. After the funeral service the body is taken to the urupā (burial ground), and a hākari (feast) lifts the tapu of the occasion. Historically, practices included the preservation of tattooed heads (mokomokai) and secondary burial rites (hahunga) in which bones were exhumed, cleaned, and re-interred.

Modern practice

Employers in New Zealand commonly grant tangihanga leave, and disputes occasionally arise over releasing bodies promptly to whānau, autopsies, and burial timing; New Zealand law and case law (such as the Takamore v Clarke litigation over burial place) reflect the cultural weight of the rite. During the COVID-19 pandemic, gathering restrictions forced adaptations — smaller tangi, livestreaming, and delayed burials — prompting debate over tapu, safety, and cultural rights.
