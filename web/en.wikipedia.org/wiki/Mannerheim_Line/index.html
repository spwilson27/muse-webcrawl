The Mannerheim Line (Finnish: Mannerheim-linja) was a defensive fortification line on the Karelian Isthmus built by Finland to defend against a possible Soviet invasion. Stretching some 130 kilometres from the Gulf of Finland to Lake Ladoga, it crossed the terrain of lakes, forests and swamps that narrowed the approaches to Viipuri (Vyborg) and southern Finland. The line takes its name from Field Marshal Carl Gustaf Emil Mannerheim, Finland's commander-in-chief, although he neither designed nor originally ordered the fortifications.

Construction

The line was built in two main phases. In the 1920s, under the guidance of foreign and Finnish engineers, about a hundred small single-storey concrete firing bunkers were constructed. In the 1930s, and especially in 1938–1939 with the help of thousands of volunteers, larger and stronger bunkers — the so-called "million-forts" (miljoonalinnake), each costing about a million Finnish marks — were added, along with kilometres of anti-tank obstacles ("dragon's teeth" and granite boulder rows), trenches, and wire entanglements. In total the line comprised around 157 concrete structures, far weaker in depth and strength than the French Maginot Line with which foreign journalists flatteringly compared it; its real strength lay in the terrain and the determination of its defenders.

Winter War

When the Soviet Union attacked Finland on 30 November 1939, the Mannerheim Line bore the brunt of the Red Army's assaults on the Isthmus. The main defensive position held through December and January against repeated mass attacks, inflicting heavy Soviet losses, especially around Summa and Taipale. In February 1940, after a massive artillery preparation, Soviet forces finally breached the line at Summa, and the Finns withdrew to an intermediate position and then to a rear line (the VKT-line) before the Moscow Peace Treaty of 13 March 1940 ended the war. Finland ceded the entire Isthmus, including the line, to the Soviet Union.

Aftermath

During the Continuation War (1941–1944), Finnish forces re-occupied the Isthmus but the old line, largely demolished by the Soviets, played no significant defensive role; the decisive Soviet offensive of June 1944 bypassed and overran the area. Today the surviving bunkers lie in Russian territory, some preserved as memorials, and the name "Mannerheim Line" remains a symbol of Finnish resistance.
