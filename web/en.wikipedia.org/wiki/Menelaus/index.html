In Greek mythology, Menelaus (/ˌmɛnɪˈleɪəs/; Greek: Μενέλαος) was king of Sparta, the younger son of Atreus and Aerope, and the younger brother of Agamemnon. He was the husband of Helen, whose abduction by the Trojan prince Paris provoked the Trojan War.

Mythology

Marriage to Helen

Helen's father Tyndareus had all her suitors swear an oath to defend her marriage against any wrongdoer. Menelaus won her hand and inherited the Spartan throne. During Menelaus's absence, Paris — who had judged Aphrodite fairest of the goddesses and been promised the most beautiful woman in the world — visited Sparta and carried Helen off to Troy, violating the laws of hospitality (xenia) and triggering the oath.

The Trojan War

In Homer's Iliad, Menelaus fights a duel with Paris for Helen and victory in the war; he wins the combat, but Aphrodite rescues Paris from the field. Menelaus is wounded by Pandarus, whose arrow breaks the truce, and he distinguishes himself in the fighting over the body of Patroclus in Book 17. After Troy's fall, Menelaus recovered Helen — in some versions raising his sword to kill her before being disarmed by her beauty.

Homecoming

In the Odyssey, Menelaus's return voyage was blown off course to Egypt, where he wrestled the sea god Proteus to learn the way home — and the fate of his brother. Telemachus visits him at Sparta in Book 4, finding him restored to wealth and domestic peace with Helen. Menelaus was granted an exceptional fate: because he was Zeus's son-in-law, he would not die but pass to the Elysian Fields.

Cult and legacy

Menelaus and Helen were worshipped at the Menelaion, a shrine on a hill across the Eurotas from Sparta. His figure — the wronged husband and steadfast warrior — recurs in classical tragedy, notably Euripides' Helen and Andromache, where he is portrayed less favourably.
