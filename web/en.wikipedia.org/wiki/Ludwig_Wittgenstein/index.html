Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein (1889–1951) was an Austrian-British philosopher who worked primarily in logic, the philosophy of mathematics, the philosophy of mind and the philosophy of language. Uniquely, he produced two opposed masterpieces: the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921), which pictures language as mirroring facts and ends 'Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent', and the posthumous Philosophical Investigations (1953), which replaces that picture with language as a motley of 'language-games' embedded in forms of life.

Life

Born into one of Europe's richest families, Wittgenstein studied engineering in Berlin and Manchester, then philosophy at Cambridge under Bertrand Russell. He served in the First World War (writing the Tractatus in the trenches), gave away his fortune, and worked as a schoolteacher, gardener's assistant and architect before returning to Cambridge in 1929. His charismatic, austere teaching shaped a generation; he died in Cambridge in 1951, saying 'Tell them I've had a wonderful life.'
