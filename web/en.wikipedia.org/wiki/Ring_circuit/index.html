Ring circuit

In electrical wiring, a ring circuit (often called a ring final circuit or informally a ring main) is a technique used in the United Kingdom and other countries that follow British wiring practice. The circuit is wired as a loop: conductors leave the consumer unit, run from socket to socket around the area served, and return to the same terminals in the consumer unit, forming a ring.

Design

A typical British ring final circuit is protected by a 30 A or 32 A fuse or circuit breaker, and is wired with 2.5 mm² cable even though the radial rating of such cable is only about 20 A. This is safe because current can flow to each load by two paths around the ring, sharing the load between them. Ring circuits are conventionally permitted to serve a floor area of up to 100 m². Spurs may be taken from the ring to individual sockets. The arrangement works together with the fused plugs of the BS 1363 standard, which provide individual protection for appliances and their flexible cords.

History

The ring final circuit was developed in Britain during the Second World War and adopted in the post-war rebuilding programme, partly to save copper: for a given capacity it uses less conductor than equivalent radial circuits.

Criticism

Critics note that a break in the ring can go unnoticed while leaving the circuit apparently functional but overloaded on one leg, and that the system's safety depends on correct installation and on plugs being individually fused. Ring circuits are unusual outside countries influenced by British standards, where radial circuits predominate.
