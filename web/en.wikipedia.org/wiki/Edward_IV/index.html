Edward IV

Edward IV (28 April 1442 – 9 April 1483) was King of England from 4 March 1461 to 3 October 1470, then again from 11 April 1471 until his death in 1483. He was a central figure in the Wars of the Roses, a series of civil wars in England fought between the Lancastrian and Yorkist factions between 1455 and 1487.

Accession

Edward was the eldest surviving son of Richard Plantagenet, Duke of York, and Cecily Neville. After his father's death at the Battle of Wakefield in December 1460, Edward inherited the Yorkist claim to the throne. With the support of his cousin Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick ("Warwick the Kingmaker"), he defeated the Lancastrians at the Battle of Towton on 29 March 1461 — reputedly the bloodiest battle ever fought on English soil — and was crowned in London, deposing the mentally ill Henry VI.

First reign and exile

Edward's secret marriage in 1464 to the widow Elizabeth Woodville, rather than a diplomatic French match arranged by Warwick, fatally strained their relationship. Edward's promotion of the Woodville family and his brother-in-law's growing influence alienated Warwick, who defected to the Lancastrians, restored Henry VI in 1470 (the "Readeption"), and drove Edward into exile in Burgundy.

Restoration

Edward returned in 1471 with Burgundian support, defeated and killed Warwick at the Battle of Barnet (14 April 1471), then crushed the remaining Lancastrian army at the Battle of Tewkesbury (4 May 1471), where Henry VI's only son, Edward of Westminster, was killed. Henry VI died in the Tower of London shortly afterwards, almost certainly murdered. Edward ruled unchallenged for the rest of his reign.

Later reign and death

Edward's second reign saw a revival of royal finances, trade promotion, and patronage of printing (William Caxton dedicated works to him). In 1475 he invaded France but accepted the lucrative Treaty of Picquigny. His brother George, Duke of Clarence, was executed for treason in 1478. Edward died unexpectedly on 9 April 1483, aged 40, possibly from pneumonia or overindulgence. His twelve-year-old son succeeded as Edward V, but was deposed within months by Edward's younger brother, who became Richard III; Edward V and his brother (the "Princes in the Tower") disappeared.
