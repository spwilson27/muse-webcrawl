Airport 1975 (also styled Airport '75) is a 1974 American disaster film directed by Jack Smight and produced by Jennings Lang for Universal Pictures. It is the second film in the Airport franchise, following the 1970 hit Airport and preceding Airport '77 and The Concorde ... Airport '79. The film is remembered for its mid-air collision premise and for its later role as a principal target of the parody film Airplane! (1980).

Plot

Columbia Airlines Flight 409, a Boeing 747 from Washington Dulles to Los Angeles, is struck head-on at cruising altitude by a small private plane whose pilot has suffered a fatal heart attack. The collision tears a hole in the flight deck, killing the flight engineer, blinding the co-pilot, and incapacitating the captain, who is sucked partway out before being pulled back. Chief stewardess Nancy Pryor takes the controls of the damaged jumbo jet under radio guidance while chief instructor Al Murdock — her former lover — is lowered into the cockpit by helicopter sling through the hole in mid-flight, surviving a first failed attempt in which another pilot is lost. Murdock lands the 747 at Salt Lake City, where terrain and the crippled aircraft's speed make the approach perilous.

Cast and production

Charlton Heston stars as Murdock and Karen Black as Pryor; George Kennedy returns as Joe Patroni, promoted within the airline from the first film. Gloria Swanson appears in her final film role, playing herself; the cast also includes Helen Reddy as a singing nun, Linda Blair as a young kidney-transplant patient, Efrem Zimbalist Jr., Susan Clark, and Myrna Loy. A real American Airlines Boeing 747-100 was leased and painted in the fictional Columbia livery for aerial filming.

Reception and legacy

Though contemporary critics were cool, the film was a strong box-office success, earning about $47 million against modest costs and confirming the 1970s disaster-film cycle. Its earnest melodrama, singing nun, and stewardess-lands-the-plane scenario made it a prime source for Airplane!, which parodied several of its characters and sequences nearly scene-for-scene.
