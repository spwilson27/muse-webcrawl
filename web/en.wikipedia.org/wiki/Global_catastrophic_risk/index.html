Global catastrophic risk

A global catastrophic risk is a hypothetical future event that could damage human well-being on a global scale, crippling or destroying modern civilisation. Events with still greater impact — those that would permanently destroy humanity or drastically curtail its potential — are often classified as existential risks. The study and mitigation of such risks became a distinct field in the early 21st century, associated with researchers such as Nick Bostrom, whose 2002 paper "Existential Risks" and 2008 edited volume Global Catastrophic Risks (with Milan Ćirković) helped define it.

Classification of risks

Natural risks include asteroid and comet impacts, supervolcanic eruptions (such as the Toba event some 74,000 years ago), extreme climate change, geomagnetic storms, and nearby supernovae or gamma-ray bursts. Background rates for many natural extinction-level events are estimated to be low — on the order of less than 1 in 10,000 per century for some categories — because humanity has survived them for millennia.

Anthropogenic risks arise from human activity and technology: nuclear war and subsequent nuclear winter, engineered pandemics and other biotechnology accidents or misuse, runaway climate change, and misaligned artificial general intelligence. Researchers such as Toby Ord, in The Precipice (2020), argue that anthropogenic risks now exceed natural ones by orders of magnitude, because they are new, growing with technological capability, and not bounded by the historical record.

Estimation and challenges

Quantifying such risks is difficult: they involve rare or unprecedented events, deep uncertainty, and cognitive biases including scope neglect and the tendency to dismiss scenarios that have never occurred (the "anthropic shadow" created by the fact that observers, by definition, survive to look back). Analysts use expert elicitation, scenario planning and base-rate comparisons from geology and astronomy; estimates vary widely and are frequently contested by specialists in the relevant domains.

Mitigation and institutions

Proposed mitigations range from asteroid deflection (demonstrated by NASA's DART mission in 2022) and pandemic surveillance to nuclear arms control, food-system resilience (including alternative foods and refuges), and AI safety research. Dedicated organisations include the Future of Humanity Institute (Oxford, until its closure in 2024), the Centre for the Study of Existential Risk (Cambridge), the Global Challenges Foundation, and the Future of Life Institute, whose 2023 open letter called for a pause on giant AI experiments. Governments have also begun formal assessments, such as the UK's National Risk Register and the United States' planetary defence programme under NASA's Planetary Defense Coordination Office.
