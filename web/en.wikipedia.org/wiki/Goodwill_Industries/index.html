Goodwill Industries

Goodwill Industries International Inc., often shortened in speech to Goodwill, is an American non-profit organisation that provides job training, employment placement services and other community-based programmes for people with barriers to employment. It is funded largely by a network of more than 3,200 retail thrift stores that sell donated clothing and household goods; Goodwill operates as a federation of independent, community-based member organisations in the United States and Canada, with a presence in about a dozen other countries.

History

Goodwill was founded in Boston in 1902 by the Reverend Edgar J. Helms, a Methodist minister. Helms collected used household items from wealthier areas of the city, hired people who were poor or disabled to repair them, and resold or gave the goods to the people who had repaired them — a model he summarised as "a hand up, not a hand out". The organisation took the name Morgan Memorial Goodwill Industries and was officially incorporated in 1919.

The network grew rapidly in the 20th century; Goodwill Industries International now coordinates more than 150 autonomous local organisations. Its familiar "smiling G" logo, designed in 1968, is one of the best-known non-profit marks in North America.

Operations

Local Goodwills run thrift stores, donation centres, job-placement and training programmes, career centres, and contract services for business and government (janitorial, assembly, document management and similar work). Revenue, running to several billion dollars a year across the network, funds employment services for people with disabilities, people lacking education or work experience, and others facing challenges to finding employment. Goodwill also diverts enormous quantities of used goods from landfills through resale, recycling and salvage.

Criticism

Goodwill has faced criticism over executive compensation at some local organisations and over its use, in some chapters, of Section 14(c) certificates under the Fair Labor Standards Act permitting sub-minimum wages for some workers with severe disabilities — a practice several chapters have ended voluntarily and which has been the subject of state-level bans.
