Covered passages of Paris

The covered passages of Paris (French: passages couverts de Paris) are an early form of shopping arcade built in Paris, France, principally during the first half of the 19th century. They are roofed-over passageways between buildings, glazed on top, lined with shops, restaurants and, originally, apartments above.

The Galerie Vivienne, opened in 1823.

By the 1850s there were about 150 covered passages in Paris. All but about 18 of them were demolished during the Haussmann renovation of Paris under the Second Empire, as wider streets, department stores and railway stations changed the geography of Parisian retail. The surviving passages mostly lie on the Right Bank, in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 8th and 9th arrondissements.

Characteristics

The passages offered pedestrians shelter from weather, mud and horse-drawn traffic before the age of wide pavements and sewers. They were lit first by gaslight and later by electricity, allowing shopping and strolling in the evening; they are often cited as ancestors of the modern shopping mall and department store. Walter Benjamin made the Paris arcades the centrepiece of his unfinished Arcades Project (Passagenwerk), treating them as emblematic of 19th-century consumer capitalism and the figure of the flâneur.

Notable passages

  • Passage des Panoramas (opened 1799) — the oldest surviving passage, known for restaurants and stamp dealers.
  • Galerie Vivienne (1823) — elegant arcade near the Palais-Royal, with mosaic floors and wine merchants.
  • Passage du Caire (1798) — the oldest covered passage in Paris, decorated in an Egyptian style following Napoleon's campaign in Egypt.
  • Passage Brady (1828) — known for its Indian and Pakistani restaurants.
  • Passage Jouffroy (1845) — houses the entrance to the Musée Grévin wax museum.
  • Galerie Véro-Dodat (1826) — neoclassical arcade near the Louvre.
  • Passage des Princes (1860) — the last passage built in the classic period.

Decline and revival

From the 1860s the opening of large department stores such as Le Bon Marché and Printemps, combined with Haussmann's demolitions, caused the passages to decline; many fell into disrepair. From the 1970s onward, several were restored and classified as historic monuments, and they again became fashionable locations for boutiques, tea rooms and antiquarian booksellers.
