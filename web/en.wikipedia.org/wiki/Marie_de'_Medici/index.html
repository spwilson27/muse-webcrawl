Marie de' Medici (French: Marie de Médicis; 26 April 1575 – 3 July 1642) was Queen of France as the second wife of Henry IV, and regent of the kingdom for her son Louis XIII after her husband's assassination in 1610. A member of the powerful Medici family of Florence, she was one of the most politically influential women of seventeenth-century Europe, though her regency and later intrigues against Cardinal Richelieu ended in exile.

Marriage

Marie was the daughter of Francesco I de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany, and Joanna of Austria. Her marriage to Henry IV was arranged in part so that France could settle the crown's debts to the Medici bank; she brought an enormous dowry of 600,000 crowns. They were married by proxy in Florence in October 1600 and in person in Lyon in December. The marriage produced six children, including Louis XIII, Gaston, Duke of Orléans, and Henrietta Maria, queen consort of Charles I of England. Relations between Marie and Henry were strained by his numerous mistresses, notably Gabrielle d'Estrées's successors.

Regency

Marie was crowned Queen of France at Saint-Denis on 13 May 1610; the next day Henry IV was assassinated by François Ravaillac. As regent for the eight-year-old Louis XIII, she reversed Henry's anti-Habsburg policies, pursuing alliance with Spain, sealed by the double marriage of Louis XIII to Anne of Austria and of her daughter Elisabeth to the future Philip IV. Her reliance on Italian favourites, especially Concino Concini and his wife Leonora Galigaï, provoked resentment among the French nobility, who rose in revolt in 1614–1616. In 1617 Louis XIII asserted his authority: Concini was assassinated and Marie was exiled to Blois.

Rebellion and exile

Marie escaped from Blois in 1619 and twice raised rebellions against her son before reconciliations brokered by Richelieu, then a rising cleric in her service. After Louis made Richelieu his chief minister in 1624, relations deteriorated again; on the "Day of the Dupes" (November 1630), Marie's attempt to force Richelieu's dismissal backfired, and she fled France in 1631, never to return. She spent her last years in exile in Brussels, Amsterdam — where she was famously received in 1638 — and Cologne, where she died in poverty in 1642.

Patronage

Marie was a major patron of the arts. She built the Luxembourg Palace in Paris, modelled on the Pitti Palace of her childhood, and commissioned from Peter Paul Rubens the celebrated cycle of 24 monumental paintings depicting her life, now in the Louvre. She also expanded the Cours la Reine and the Luxembourg Gardens.
