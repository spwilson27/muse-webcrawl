Formation flying

Formation flying is the flight of multiple aircraft in a coordinated arrangement, in which each pilot maintains a set position relative to a designated leader. Used by military units since the First World War, formation flying serves purposes of mutual defence, concentrated force, efficient navigation, and — in aerobatic display teams — spectacle. Birds such as geese also fly in formations, notably the V formation, which improves aerodynamic efficiency for the flock.

Principles

Only the formation leader navigates and communicates for the group; the other pilots ("wingmen") fly fixed positions relative to the leader, continuously adjusting power and attitude to hold station. Small relative movements are constant because of turbulence and the leader's manoeuvres, which are therefore flown smoothly and announced in advance. Formations must also manage wake turbulence and the upwash and downwash from neighbouring aircraft — effects that trailing aircraft in a V can exploit to reduce induced drag, as migrating birds do.

Common formations

  • Vic (V) formation — three or more aircraft in a V, a standard of the interwar and early Second World War period.
  • Echelon — aircraft stepped diagonally to one side.
  • Finger-four — four aircraft positioned like the fingertips of an outstretched hand; developed from combat experience and standard in the Second World War.
  • Diamond — four aircraft in a close rhombus, favoured by display teams.
  • Line abreast / line astern — side-by-side or single-file arrangements.
  • Combat spread and fluid four — looser modern tactical formations maximising lookout and manoeuvrability.

Military use

Formation tactics evolved rapidly in the First World War as aircraft began operating in units. Between the wars, tight parade formations were emphasised; combat over Europe and the Pacific showed that loose, mutually supporting formations (such as the Luftwaffe's Schwarm and the US Navy's Thach Weave) were more effective. Modern combat aircraft typically operate in pairs or foursomes with wide spacing, relying on sensors and datalinks.

Display flying

Aerobatic display teams — such as the RAF Red Arrows, the US Navy Blue Angels, the USAF Thunderbirds, and the Patrouille de France — fly extremely close formations with smoke, performing manoeuvres that demand precise station-keeping. Civilian formation flying is also practised for airshows, commemorative flypasts, and the "missing man" formation honouring fallen aviators.

Birds

Large birds migrate in V and J formations. Research shows each bird positions itself in the upwash of the one ahead, reducing energy expenditure, and times its wingbeats to capture the benefit — measured savings that let flocks fly much farther than solitary birds.
