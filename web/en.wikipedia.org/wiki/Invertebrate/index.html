Invertebrate

Invertebrates are animals that neither develop nor retain a vertebral column (commonly known as a backbone or spine), derived from the notochord. This is a grouping that includes all animals excluding the subphylum Vertebrata. Familiar examples of invertebrates include arthropods, mollusks, annelid worms, echinoderms, flatworms, cnidarians, and sponges.

The majority of animal species are invertebrates; one estimate puts the figure at 97%. Many individual invertebrate taxa have a greater number and variety of species than the entire subphylum of Vertebrata.

Characteristics

The trait shared by all invertebrates is the absence of a vertebral column. Beyond this, invertebrates are extraordinarily diverse: some are supported by hydrostatic skeletons, others by hard exoskeletons of chitin or shells of calcium carbonate. Many undergo metamorphosis, and most are small, though the colossal squid may reach over ten metres in length.

Classification

The term "invertebrate" is not a formal taxon but a convenient paraphyletic grouping. The principal invertebrate phyla include:

  • Porifera — sponges
  • Cnidaria — jellyfish, corals, sea anemones
  • Mollusca — snails, clams, octopuses, squid
  • Annelida — segmented worms
  • Arthropoda — insects, spiders, crustaceans (the largest phylum)
  • Echinodermata — starfish, sea urchins
  • Platyhelminthes — flatworms
  • Nematoda — roundworms

History of the term

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck popularised the division of animals into vertebrates and invertebrates in the early nineteenth century, at a time when invertebrate zoology was only beginning to be studied systematically. Charles Darwin's work, and later studies of embryology and molecular phylogenetics, greatly refined understanding of invertebrate relationships.

Importance

Invertebrates underpin most ecosystems: they pollinate crops, decompose organic matter, aerate soil, and form the base of many food webs. Many are also of direct economic importance, as food (shellfish), as pests, and as vectors of disease.
