Metasomatism

Skarn, a rock formed by metasomatism at a granite contact

Metasomatism (from Greek meta, "change", and soma, "body") is the chemical alteration of a rock by hydrothermal and other fluids, in which chemical components are added or removed while the rock remains in a solid state. It differs from metamorphism in that it involves a significant change in the bulk chemical composition of the rock, not merely recrystallization under heat and pressure. Metasomatism occurs by diffusion through pore fluids or by infiltration of fluids along fractures and grain boundaries.

Types

Important metasomatic processes include skarn formation, where hot magmatic fluids alter carbonate rocks to produce calc-silicate assemblages often hosting ore deposits of tungsten, copper, iron, and zinc; greisenization, the alteration of granite by fluorine-rich fluids, associated with tin and tungsten mineralization; fenitization around alkaline intrusions; serpentinization of ultramafic rocks; and alkali metasomatism producing albitites. Regional metasomatism can affect large volumes of the crust and mantle, and metasomatized mantle is important in the generation of some magmas and diamond-bearing kimberlites.

Processes and products

Metasomatism requires a fluid phase, a chemical potential gradient between the fluid and the rock, and pathways for fluid flow. Replacement proceeds by coupled dissolution–precipitation at the mineral scale, and can preserve textures—including fossils—in remarkable detail, as in some silicified woods and carbonate-replaced fossils. The products are called metasomatites.
