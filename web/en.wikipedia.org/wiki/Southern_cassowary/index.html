The southern cassowary (Casuarius casuarius), also known as the double-wattled cassowary or helmeted cassowary, is a large flightless bird native to the rainforests of northeastern Australia and New Guinea. It is one of three living species of cassowary and among the largest birds in the world.

Description

The southern cassowary stands 155–170 cm tall, with females larger and more brightly coloured than males. It has coarse black plumage, a bare blue-and-red neck with hanging wattles, and a prominent keratin casque on its head. Its powerful legs bear dagger-like claws up to 12 cm long, and the bird can run at up to 50 km/h and jump 1.5 m.

Diet and ecology

Cassowaries are primarily frugivorous, eating the fallen fruit of hundreds of rainforest species, supplemented by fungi, invertebrates, and small vertebrates. Because they disperse the seeds of many large-fruited trees, they are considered a keystone species in Australian rainforests.

Behaviour

Southern cassowaries are solitary and shy. Males incubate the eggs and raise the striped chicks for around nine months. Although generally retiring, they can be dangerous if cornered and have been called one of the world's most dangerous birds.

Conservation

The species is listed as Least Concern globally by the IUCN, though the Australian population is threatened by habitat loss, vehicle strikes, and dog attacks, and is protected under national law.
