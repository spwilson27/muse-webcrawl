Semisimple module

In mathematics, especially in ring theory and representation theory, a semisimple module is a module that can be written as a direct sum of simple modules — modules that have no nontrivial submodules. Semisimple modules are among the best understood objects in algebra because they decompose completely into indecomposable building blocks.

Characterizations

Several equivalent conditions define semisimplicity. A module is semisimple if every submodule has a complement (a submodule with which it forms a direct sum giving the whole module); equivalently, every short exact sequence of modules into it splits. Over a field, every vector space is semisimple, since every subspace has a complement. The integers as a module over themselves are not semisimple, because the submodule of even integers has no complement.

Semisimple rings

A ring is called semisimple if it is semisimple as a module over itself. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem classifies these completely: a semisimple ring is a finite product of matrix rings over division rings. A fundamental application is Maschke's theorem in representation theory, which states that the group algebra of a finite group over a field whose characteristic does not divide the group order is semisimple — so every representation decomposes into irreducible representations.
