Siphonophores (order Siphonophorae) are highly polymorphic and complex hydrozoans in the phylum Cnidaria. Although a siphonophore appears to be a single organism, each specimen is in fact a colony composed of many small, highly specialised zooids that are physiologically integrated and cannot survive independently. Siphonophores are predators that drift or swim in the open ocean, capturing small crustaceans and fish with stinging tentacles.

Structure

A siphonophore colony is organised around a central stem bearing different kinds of zooids: nectophores (swimming bells) that propel the colony, gastrozooids that digest food, palpons, defensive tentilla armed with nematocysts, and reproductive gonophores. The best-known siphonophore is the Portuguese man o' war (Physalia physalis), which floats at the surface with a gas-filled sail. Other species, such as the giant siphonophore Praya dubia, can reach lengths of 40 metres or more, making them among the longest animals in the world.

Distribution and feeding

Siphonophores live in all of the world's oceans, from the surface to great depths. Many are bioluminescent; some, such as species of Erenna, produce red light — rare among deep-sea animals — to lure prey. They feed by trailing curtains of stinging tentacles through the water and digesting captured prey in shared gastrozooids, distributing nutrients throughout the colony.

Classification

The order is divided into suborders including Cystonectae (such as the Portuguese man o' war), Physonectae and Calycophorae. About 175 species have been described.
