Locative case

The locative case is a grammatical case that indicates a location: the place where something is situated or where an action takes place. It corresponds roughly to the English prepositions "in", "on", and "at". It is found in many inflected languages, including Sanskrit, Latin, the Slavic languages, the Baltic languages, Finnish, Hungarian, and Turkish.

In Indo-European languages

Proto-Indo-European had a distinct locative case. In Sanskrit it remained a full member of the eight-case system. In Latin the locative survived only in limited contexts — chiefly names of towns and small islands, and a few common nouns such as domī "at home" and rurī "in the countryside" — elsewhere its functions were taken over by the ablative with prepositions. In Russian and most other Slavic languages the corresponding case is usually called the prepositional case, because it is used only after certain prepositions, though its meaning remains largely locative.

In Uralic languages

Uralic languages elaborate location into a system of local cases: Finnish, for example, has the inessive ("inside"), elative ("out of"), illative ("into"), adessive ("on/at"), ablative ("off/from"), and allative ("onto/to"), of which the inessive and adessive most closely match the classic locative.

Usage

Beyond simple physical location, locative forms are used in various languages for time expressions ("in the morning"), situations, and after certain verbs. Where a language lacks a separate locative, adpositions or other cases perform the same role.
