Royal and noble ranks

Traditional royal and noble ranks are the hierarchical titles held by monarchs and members of the nobility in various societies. In the European tradition, the ranks of royalty and high nobility, in descending order of precedence, are generally: emperor (or empress), king (or queen), grand duke, prince, duke, marquess (marquis), count (earl), viscount, and baron, followed by lesser and untitled nobility.

Coronets in heraldry indicate the rank of their bearer

In the British peerage, the ranks are duke, marquess, earl, viscount, and baron; below them stand baronets and knights, who are not peers. Forms of address reflect rank: monarchs are styled "Majesty", royal princes "Royal Highness", and dukes "Grace".

Other traditions

Comparable hierarchies exist worldwide. The Ottoman court used sultan, pasha, bey, and agha; imperial China graded its nobility into ranks such as wang (prince/king), gong (duke), hou (marquess), bo (count), zi (viscount), and nan (baron); and Japan's court had its own system of court ranks alongside the feudal titles of the daimyo. In Ethiopia, titles such as ras and dejazmach ordered the imperial nobility.

Precedence

Orders of precedence governing who ranks above whom at court and in ceremonial processions were matters of intense concern historically, and continue to be formally regulated in surviving monarchies. Rank could derive from the antiquity of a title, the size and sovereignty of the territory attached to it, and the holder's relationship to the reigning monarch.
