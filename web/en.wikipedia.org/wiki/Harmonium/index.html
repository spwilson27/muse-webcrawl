Harmonium

The harmonium, also called a pump organ or reed organ, is a keyboard instrument that produces sound by pumping air through metal reeds. It belongs to the family of free-reed aerophones. Developed in Europe in the early nineteenth century, it became a common instrument in churches, chapels, schools, and homes, and in a hand-pumped form became one of the most important instruments of Indian music.

History

The French instrument maker Alexandre Debain patented the harmonium in Paris in 1842, building on earlier reed organs and on the Chinese sheng's free-reed principle, transmitted to Europe in the late eighteenth century. French makers such as Mustel added expressive features, while in the United States firms including Mason & Hamlin and Estey produced large numbers of suction-bellows "American organs" for homes and small churches.

Mechanism

In most Western harmoniums and reed organs, the player pumps foot treadles that work bellows; springs and a reservoir keep the air pressure steady. Pressing a key opens a pallet admitting air to the corresponding reed, which vibrates to produce the tone. Stops control which ranks of reeds sound and operate devices such as the octave coupler and the "expression" control of dynamics. The Indian harmonium differs in being pumped by one hand at the back while the other plays the keys, and in having drone stops and, often, a scale-changing mechanism.

Repertoire and use

Composers including Rossini, Franck, Liszt, and Karg-Elert wrote works for or featuring the harmonium, and it served widely as a substitute for the pipe organ in small venues. In India, where it arrived in the nineteenth century, the harmonium became central to Hindustani and devotional music, accompanying khayal vocalists, qawwali ensembles, bhajan and kirtan singing, and ghazal performance; its fixed tuning once provoked controversy and a temporary ban from All India Radio, but it remains ubiquitous.
