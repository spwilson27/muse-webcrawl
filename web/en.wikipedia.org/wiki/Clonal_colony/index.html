Clonal colony

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pando, a clonal colony of quaking aspen in Utah — a single genetic individual

A clonal colony or genet is a group of genetically identical individuals, such as plants, fungi, algae, or certain animals, that have grown in a given location, all originating asexually from a single ancestor. In plants, the individual members of the colony are called ramets. Clonal colonies are common in many plant species, spreading by rhizomes, stolons, or root suckers.

Notable examples

  • Pando ("The Trembling Giant"): a colony of quaking aspen (Populus tremuloides) in Utah covering about 43 hectares with some 47,000 stems from one root system; often cited as among the largest and oldest living organisms, possibly up to 80,000 years old (estimates vary).
  • King Clone: a creosote bush ring in the Mojave Desert, estimated at over 11,000 years old.
  • Armillaria ostoyae: a fungus in Oregon spanning several square kilometres, among the largest known organisms.
  • Posidonia oceanica: seagrass meadows in the Mediterranean that may be tens of thousands of years old.

Ecology

Clonal growth lets plants colonise habitat without relying on seed, share resources among connected ramets, and persist for extraordinary spans of time. The trade-off is low genetic diversity within the colony, which can leave it vulnerable to disease and environmental change.
