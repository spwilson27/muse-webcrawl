World

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. This article is about the concept of the world as a whole. For the planet, see Earth.

The world is the totality of entities, the whole of reality, or everything that exists. The nature of the world has been conceptualised differently in different fields. Some conceptions see the world as unique while others talk of a "plurality of worlds". In philosophy, "world" may refer to the universe as the physical sum of all matter and space, to a realm of human experience and history, or to a logical space of possibilities, as in discussion of possible worlds.

Etymology

The English word world comes from the Old English weorold, a compound of wer ("man") and eald ("age"), literally "age of man". Similar "human realm" senses appear in many languages, contrasted with words for the physical earth (from Proto-Indo-European roots giving Greek kosmos, "order", and Latin mundus).

In philosophy

Philosophers have long debated the world's fundamental structure. In metaphysics, questions include whether the world is material or mental in nature, whether it is one or many, and whether its existence is contingent or necessary. Leibniz defined a world in terms of compossible substances, and in the 20th century "possible worlds" became a central tool of modal logic through the work of Kripke and David Lewis, who defended modal realism — the view that all possible worlds are as real as the actual one. Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus opens with the claim that "the world is all that is the case", identifying the world with the totality of facts rather than things. Heidegger treated "being-in-the-world" (In-der-Welt-sein) as fundamental to human existence.

In religion and cosmology

Religions offer accounts of the world's origin, structure, and destiny. Many creation narratives describe the world emerging from chaos or from the act of a creator deity; Hindu cosmology envisions cyclical worlds through kalpas of creation and dissolution; Buddhism treats the world as impermanent and conditioned. Eschatologies in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam describe an end or transformation of the present world. Medieval European cosmology pictured a finite spherical cosmos of nested spheres centred on Earth, a picture displaced by the Copernican revolution.

Human and social senses

In everyday usage, "the world" often means the human realm: the sum of human civilisation, society, and experience ("world history", "world affairs", "the world's population"). Related senses include a sphere of activity (the art world, the business world), a large region or grouping (the Western world, the Arab world, the developing world), and a person's environment or outlook (worldview, German Weltanschauung).

The world as planet Earth

By metonymy, "the world" commonly denotes the planet Earth, especially in political and economic contexts — "world trade", "world leaders", "the largest city in the world". As human knowledge extended beyond Earth, the term also generalised: astronomers speak of other worlds (planets and moons) and of "world-building" in fiction.
