Bushfires in Australia

Bushfires in Australia are a widespread and regular occurrence that have contributed significantly to shaping the continent's ecology over millions of years. Parts of Australia, particularly the southeast, are among the most fire-prone regions on Earth. Fire is a natural part of the Australian landscape: many native plants, especially eucalypts, are adapted to fire, and Aboriginal Australians used fire deliberately in land management for tens of thousands of years.

Causes and conditions

Bushfires require fuel, low humidity, high temperatures, and wind. The fire season in northern Australia occurs during the dry winter months, while in the south it peaks in summer and autumn, often driven by hot, dry northerly winds ahead of cold fronts. Drought and heatwaves intensify risk; climate indices such as the Forest Fire Danger Index combine these factors. Ignitions come from lightning (the largest natural source), accidents, agricultural escapes, and arson.

Major fire events

Several disasters are remembered by the day or season on which they occurred:

  • Black Thursday (1851, Victoria) — vast fires during a heatwave, among the earliest catastrophic fires recorded by European settlers.
  • Black Friday (1939, Victoria) — fires burned about two million hectares and killed 71 people.
  • Ash Wednesday (1983, South Australia and Victoria) — 75 deaths.
  • Black Tuesday (1967, Tasmania) — fires around Hobart killed 62 people.
  • Black Saturday (2009, Victoria) — Australia's deadliest bushfires, killing 173 people.
  • Black Summer (2019–20, national) — an unprecedented season that burned an estimated 18 million hectares or more, destroyed thousands of homes, killed 33 people directly, and killed or displaced billions of animals; smoke affected cities across the country and reached South America.

Management and response

Firefighting is primarily a responsibility of the states and territories, delivered largely by volunteer services such as the New South Wales Rural Fire Service — one of the world's largest volunteer firefighting organisations — and Victoria's Country Fire Authority. Management techniques include hazard-reduction (prescribed) burning, firebreaks, aerial water-bombing, and community warning systems. Indigenous cultural burning practices have attracted renewed interest since Black Summer for their potential to reduce fuel loads with cooler, patchier burns.

Climate change

Australian climate assessments, including the royal commission into the Black Summer fires, have concluded that climate change is lengthening the fire season and increasing the frequency of dangerous fire weather, trends projected to continue.
