Siege of Leith

The siege of Leith took place from April to July 1560 during the Scottish Reformation. French troops defending the regency of Mary of Guise, mother of Mary, Queen of Scots, held the fortified port of Leith, Edinburgh's harbour. They were besieged by Scottish Protestant forces of the Lords of the Congregation, assisted by an English army and fleet sent by Elizabeth I under the Treaty of Berwick. The siege ended not by storm but by diplomacy: after Mary of Guise's death, the Treaty of Edinburgh (July 1560) required both French and English forces to leave Scotland, a landmark in the triumph of the Scottish Reformation.

Background

Since 1548 Scotland had been closely tied to France, and the regent Mary of Guise governed with French troops and officials, fortifying Leith with modern artillery defences including massive earthworks and bastions. In 1559 the Protestant Lords of the Congregation rebelled, and Elizabeth's England — fearing a French-dominated Scotland — intervened under the Treaty of Berwick (February 1560).

The siege

An English fleet under Admiral Winter blockaded the Firth of Forth, while an army under Lord Grey marched north and joined the Scots before Leith in April. The besiegers built batteries on the surrounding heights and bombarded the town; a major assault on 7 May was repulsed with significant losses. The French garrison, though short of supplies, defended the powerful walls skilfully, and neither side could force a decision. In June Mary of Guise, ill and sheltering in Edinburgh Castle, died (11 June 1560), removing the political keystone of the French position.

Treaty of Edinburgh

Negotiators including William Cecil concluded the Treaty of Edinburgh on 6 July 1560: French and English troops alike were to withdraw, Leith's fortifications were to be demolished, and France recognized Elizabeth's title. The settlement cleared the way for the Reformation Parliament of August 1560, which abolished papal authority in Scotland.
