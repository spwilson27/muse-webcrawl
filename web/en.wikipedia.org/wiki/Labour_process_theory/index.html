Labour process theory


The workplace as a contested terrain

Labour process theory (LPT) is a body of sociological and management theory, rooted in the work of Karl Marx, that analyses work organisation as a site of conflict between capital's drive to extract maximum surplus value and workers' resistance to managerial control. The modern field is usually dated from Harry Braverman's Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974), which argued that capitalist management progressively deskills craft work through the separation of conception from execution.

Origins

Marx described the labour process in general terms in the first volume of Capital and, in unpublished manuscripts, examined how machinery and the factory system subordinated workers to capital. Braverman revived this analysis for the twentieth-century corporation, contending that Taylorism (scientific management) systematically transferred workers' knowledge to management, reducing skilled crafts to routine, closely monitored tasks.

Development

Later scholars complicated Braverman's deskilling thesis. Michael Burawoy studied how consent is manufactured on the shop floor through workplace "games" and internal labour markets. Richard Edwards described successive systems of control — simple, technical and bureaucratic — while Andrew Friedman distinguished between "direct control" and "responsible autonomy" as alternative managerial strategies. Feminist and poststructuralist writers extended the analysis to gendered work, emotional labour and service employment.

Contemporary research

Today labour process theory informs studies of call centres, platform and gig work, algorithmic management, and global supply chains, while journals such as Work, Employment and Society host ongoing debates about the theory's core concepts of control, resistance and skill.
