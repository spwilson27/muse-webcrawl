Angle bisector theorem

In geometry, the angle bisector theorem states that the angle bisector of a triangle divides the opposite side into two segments that are proportional to the other two sides of the triangle.

Statement

If a line through vertex A of triangle ABC bisects the angle at A and meets side BC at point D, then BD/DC = AB/AC. In other words, the segments of the divided side are in the same ratio as the adjacent sides of the triangle.

Proof

A standard proof extends the bisector to meet a line drawn through C parallel to the bisector, forming an isosceles triangle; similar triangles then give the required proportion. The theorem also follows readily from the law of sines applied to the two subtriangles ABD and ACD.

Generalisations

The converse of the theorem is also true: a point dividing the opposite side in the ratio of the adjacent sides lies on the angle bisector. The external angle bisector divides the opposite side externally in the same ratio. The length of the internal bisector can be computed from the side lengths, and the theorem extends to the concept of the Apollonius circle.
