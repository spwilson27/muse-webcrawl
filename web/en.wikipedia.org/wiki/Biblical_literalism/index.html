Biblical literalism, also called biblicism, is the interpretation of the Bible's texts in their explicit, primary sense — that is, reading the words of Scripture as literally true where the genre permits, rather than as allegory or myth. In contemporary usage the term is closely associated with Christian fundamentalist and conservative evangelical traditions that affirm the Bible's complete truthfulness, often expressed by the doctrine of biblical inerrancy: the belief that the Bible, in its original manuscripts, is without error in all that it affirms, including history and science.

History

Largely literal readings of Scripture were common in pre-modern Christianity, though theologians from Origen to Augustine also employed allegorical interpretation extensively. The Protestant Reformation renewed emphasis on the "literal sense" (sensus literalis) as the plain meaning intended by the text. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the rise of higher criticism, Darwinian evolution, and theological liberalism prompted conservative Christians to articulate inerrancy more explicitly, notably in The Fundamentals (1910–15). The 1978 Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy codified the position for many evangelical institutions.

Views and practice

Literalist readings underpin beliefs such as a historical Adam and Eve, a global flood, and young-Earth creationism in some communities, as well as detailed expectations about prophecy and end-times events. Critics argue that a strictly literal hermeneutic overlooks genre, literary devices, and the historical context of the texts, and can produce readings (for example, of poetry or apocalyptic literature) that the original audiences would not have taken literally. Defenders respond that literalism properly understood reads each passage according to its genre, and that it safeguards the authority of Scripture.
