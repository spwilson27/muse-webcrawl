Non-directional beacon

Non-directional beacon (NDB)
Frequency: 190–1750 kHz (LF/MF)
Identification: Morse code, 1–3 letters
Receiver: Automatic direction finder (ADF)

A non-directional beacon (NDB) is a radio beacon at a known location that transmits a radio signal in all directions, used as an aviation and marine navigational aid. Unlike a VOR station, an NDB does not include inherent directional information; the bearing to the station is determined by the receiver.

Operation

NDBs operate in the low- and medium-frequency bands, typically between 190 kHz and 535 kHz in most regions (with some up to 1750 kHz). Each beacon continuously transmits a brief Morse code identifier. The airborne receiver, the automatic direction finder (ADF), points a needle toward the station, giving the pilot the relative bearing.

Uses and limitations

NDBs were among the earliest radio navigation aids and were used for en-route navigation, non-precision instrument approaches, and as locator beacons marking the outer marker of instrument landing approaches. Their signals are subject to errors from night effect (skywave reflections), terrain, thunderstorms — whose electrical discharges the ADF needle tends to point toward — and coastal refraction. With the spread of GPS-based navigation, many aviation authorities have decommissioned NDBs, though they remain common in remote regions and as a backup system.
