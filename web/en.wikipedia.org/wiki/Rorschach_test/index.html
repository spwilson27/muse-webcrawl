Rorschach test

The Rorschach test is a psychological test in which subjects are shown ten standard inkblots and asked what each one looks like; their responses are recorded and analysed for what they reveal about perception, thought processes and personality. It was created by the Swiss psychiatrist Hermann Rorschach, who published the test in his monograph Psychodiagnostics in 1921, shortly before his early death.

TypeProjective psychological test
CreatorHermann Rorschach (1921)
Materials10 inkblot cards (5 black, 2 red-black, 3 multicoloured)
Scoring systemsExner Comprehensive System; R-PAS

Method

Five blots are black and grey, two add red, and three are multicoloured pastels. The examiner records verbatim what the subject sees, then in an inquiry phase asks where and why. Responses are coded for location, determinants (form, colour, texture, shading), content and popularity, and interpreted against normative data — most famously under John Exner's Comprehensive System (1974) and its successor, the Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS).

Criticism and status

The test has been one of the most controversial instruments in psychology. Critics charge that older scoring lacked reliability and validity, that norms over-pathologized normal subjects, and that results can be misused in forensic settings. Defenders and meta-analyses find acceptable validity for some variables, such as detecting thought disorder, and the test remains widely taught and used, especially in clinical and forensic practice, while its iconic inkblots have become a cultural symbol of psychoanalysis.
