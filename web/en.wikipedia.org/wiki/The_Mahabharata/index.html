The Mahabharata (Sanskrit: महाभारतम्) is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, the other being the Ramayana. Traditionally attributed to the sage Vyasa, it narrates the struggle between two groups of cousins, the Kauravas and the Pandavas, for the throne of Hastinapura, culminating in the Kurukshetra War. With roughly 1.8 million words, it is one of the longest epic poems ever written—about ten times the length of the Iliad and the Odyssey combined.

Structure

The epic is organised into eighteen books (parvas), from the Adi Parva, which traces the ancestry of the Bharata dynasty, to the Svargarohana Parva, describing the heroes' ascent to heaven. A supplement, the Harivamsha, recounts the life of Krishna. The text interweaves the central war narrative with hundreds of sub-tales, fables, and discourses on law, ethics, statecraft, and philosophy.

Story summary

The blind king Dhritarashtra's sons, led by Duryodhana, refuse to share the kingdom with their Pandava cousins. After a rigged dice game, the Pandavas endure thirteen years of exile. When negotiations—undertaken by Krishna as envoy—fail, the cousins meet at Kurukshetra in an eighteen-day battle that destroys a generation of warriors. On the battlefield, Krishna delivers to the reluctant archer Arjuna the Bhagavad Gita, a dialogue on duty, action, and devotion that has become one of the most influential texts in Hindu philosophy. The Pandavas win but at devastating cost; Yudhishthira rules for decades before the brothers renounce the world.

Textual history

The epic grew over centuries, roughly between 400 BCE and 400 CE, by accretion around an older core war ballad. The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute produced a critical edition (1919–1966) collating over a thousand manuscripts. Major English translations include those of Kisari Mohan Ganguli (1883–1896) and the unfinished University of Chicago project begun by J. A. B. van Buitenen.

Influence

The Mahabharata has shaped South and Southeast Asian culture for two millennia, retold in regional literatures, dance-drama, shadow puppetry, and modern media, including B. R. Chopra's 1988–1990 television serial and Peter Brook's stage and film adaptations. Its maxim—that dharma is subtle—reflects the epic's refusal of simple moral resolution.
