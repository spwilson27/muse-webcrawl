The Percidae are a family of freshwater ray-finned fishes, collectively known as the perches, found mainly in the Northern Hemisphere. The family includes the true perches (genus Perca), the walleye, sauger and zander (genus Sander), the ruffe (Gymnocephalus), and the great diversity of North American darters (Etheostoma, Percina, and relatives). Depending on classification, the family contains around 200–250 species in about 11 genera, making it one of the larger families of freshwater fishes in North America because of the darter radiation.

Description

Percids have two dorsal fins — the first spiny and the second soft-rayed — which may be separate or joined; a lateral line that extends onto the caudal fin in many species; ctenoid scales; and pelvic fins in a thoracic position with one spine and five soft rays. They are generally small to medium-sized fishes: most darters are only 5–10 cm long, while the zander and walleye can exceed 90 cm and 10 kg.

Distribution and habitat

The family is restricted almost entirely to fresh waters of the Northern Hemisphere. Perca (the European perch and the yellow perch) and Sander occur in both Eurasia and North America, while the darters are exclusively North American and the logperches and ruffes have their own ranges. Percids inhabit lakes, rivers, and streams; darters are benthic specialists that have lost or greatly reduced the swim bladder and use their pectoral fins to hold station on the bottom in riffles and runs.

Importance to humans

Several percids are valued food and game fish: the yellow perch, European perch, walleye, sauger, and zander support commercial and recreational fisheries, and yellow perch and walleye are regional delicacies around the Great Lakes. The ruffe, introduced into the Great Lakes in ballast water in the 1980s, became an invasive species of concern. Many darter species have restricted ranges and are listed as threatened or endangered.

Classification

Subfamilies commonly recognized are the Percinae (perches), Luciopercinae (pikeperches and relatives), and Etheostomatinae (darters). Molecular studies have revised the boundaries of some genera, particularly within the darters, splitting the large genus Etheostoma in some treatments.
