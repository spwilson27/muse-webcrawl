Blood quantum laws

An early twentieth-century enrollment record (placeholder).

Blood quantum laws describe legislation in the United States that defines membership in Native American tribes and eligibility for related benefits by the degree of ancestry — the fraction of one's ancestors documented as belonging to an Indigenous people. "Blood quantum" is typically expressed as a fraction such as one-half or one-quarter. Many tribes today use a minimum blood quantum, often one-quarter, as a criterion for tribal enrollment, a practice with roots in federal policy.

Origins

The concept entered federal practice through treaties and allotment-era legislation. The Dawes Act of 1887 and subsequent allotment rolls recorded the stated degree of "Indian blood" of allottees. The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 defined "Indian" for its purposes to include persons of one-half or more Indian blood, reinforcing the measure's administrative importance. Blood quantum was also used in Virginia's Racial Integrity Act of 1924 and in laws governing other groups.

Modern enrollment

Tribal nations, as sovereign governments, set their own citizenship criteria. Some require a minimum quantum of tribal-specific blood; others, such as the Cherokee Nation, use lineal descent from an ancestor on a historical roll regardless of quantum. Federal recognition and certain federal benefits may also impose their own standards. A Certificate of Degree of Indian Blood (CDIB) is issued by the Bureau of Indian Affairs documenting an individual's total quantum.

Criticism

Critics argue that blood quantum is a colonial imposition alien to Indigenous kinship systems, and that fixed minimums mathematically guarantee a declining enrolled population over generations — sometimes called "statistical extermination". Supporters within tribal governments respond that quantum rules protect scarce resources and preserve a distinct political community. The debate continues in the context of disenrollment controversies and freedmen citizenship cases.
