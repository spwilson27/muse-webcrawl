Motor control

Motor control is the regulation of movement in organisms that possess a nervous system. It encompasses both reflexive actions and directed, voluntary movements, and concerns how the central nervous system (CNS) organizes muscles, joints, and sensory information to produce coordinated behaviour. Motor control is studied in neuroscience, psychology, biomechanics, and robotics.

Neural basis

Voluntary movement originates largely in the primary motor cortex, whose signals descend through the corticospinal tract to motor neurons in the spinal cord. The basal ganglia help select and initiate movements, while the cerebellum compares intended with actual movement and issues corrections, enabling fine coordination and motor learning. The spinal cord itself contains pattern-generating circuits and mediates reflexes such as the stretch reflex. Nikolai Bernstein, a foundational figure in the field, framed motor control as the problem of mastering the body's many redundant "degrees of freedom."

Theories

Competing frameworks explain how movements are planned and executed. Motor program theory posits stored commands for classes of movement. Equilibrium-point and mass-spring models treat movement as shifts in the equilibrium posture of the neuromuscular system. Contemporary optimal control and internal-model theories hold that the brain builds forward models predicting the sensory consequences of motor commands and inverse models computing the commands needed for desired outcomes, resolving redundancy by minimizing costs such as effort or variability. Feedback from proprioception, vision, and the vestibular system continuously updates ongoing movement.

Clinical significance

Damage to motor pathways produces disorders including stroke-related hemiparesis, Parkinson's disease, cerebellar ataxia, and motor neuron diseases. Rehabilitation and brain–computer interfaces draw directly on principles of motor learning and neuroplasticity.
