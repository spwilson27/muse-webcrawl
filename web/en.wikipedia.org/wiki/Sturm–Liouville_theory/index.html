Sturm–Liouville theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics and its applications, a Sturm–Liouville problem is a class of second-order linear ordinary differential equations with boundary conditions, named after the Swiss mathematician Jacques Charles François Sturm (1803–1855) and the French mathematician Joseph Liouville (1809–1882), who developed the theory in the 1830s.

The problem

A classical Sturm–Liouville equation takes the form

−d/dx [ p(x) dy/dx ] + q(x) y = λ w(x) y

on an interval [a, b], where p, q, and the weight function w are given functions and λ is a parameter. Together with separated boundary conditions, one seeks the values of λ (the eigenvalues) for which non-trivial solutions (the eigenfunctions) exist.

Main results

For a regular Sturm–Liouville problem, the theory guarantees a countable sequence of real eigenvalues λ1 < λ2 < … tending to infinity, with corresponding eigenfunctions that are orthogonal with respect to the weight function w and that form a complete basis: a wide class of functions can be expanded in a generalised Fourier series of these eigenfunctions. Familiar special cases include the Legendre, Bessel, Hermite, and Laguerre equations, whose polynomial solutions underpin much of mathematical physics.

Applications

Sturm–Liouville problems arise whenever the method of separation of variables is applied to partial differential equations such as the heat, wave, and Schrödinger equations on bounded domains. The eigenfunction expansion then yields the normal modes of vibration, the temperature distribution, or the stationary states of a quantum system.
