Yōkai (妖怪, "strange apparition") are a class of supernatural entities and spirits in Japanese folklore. They range from the malevolent to the mischievous to the merely strange, and include animals with magical powers, transformed objects, ogres, ghosts and phenomena given form. The word is sometimes translated "monster", "demon" or "goblin", though yōkai are not necessarily evil; many are simply uncanny or playful.

Types

Well-known yōkai include the kappa, a water imp with a dish of water on its head; the long-nosed, winged tengu; the horned ogre-like oni; shape-shifting foxes (kitsune) and raccoon dogs (tanuki); the yūrei, pale ghosts of the dead bound by strong emotion; and tsukumogami, household tools that come alive after a hundred years. Some yōkai embody natural phenomena or wordplay — Sekien's catalogues include creatures invented purely as visual puns.

History

Belief in such beings is ancient in Japan, but yōkai culture flourished in the Edo period (1603–1868), when printed picture books made them entertainment. The artist Toriyama Sekien's illustrated bestiaries (beginning with Gazu Hyakki Yagyō, 1776) catalogued hundreds of yōkai, many of his own invention, and the parlour game Hyaku Monogatari Kaidankai popularised ghost-storytelling. In the Meiji period folklorists such as Inoue Enryō and later Yanagita Kunio studied and classified yōkai lore.

Modern popularity

Shigeru Mizuki's manga GeGeGe no Kitarō (1960s onward) revived yōkai for modern audiences, and the town of Sakaiminato honours him with bronze yōkai statues. Yōkai permeate contemporary Japanese media — from the films of Studio Ghibli to Pokémon and the video game and anime franchise Yo-kai Watch — and folklorist Michael Dylan Foster has traced their role as a flexible vocabulary for explaining the uncanny.
