Stylistic originsSwing, Kansas City jazz
Cultural originsEarly 1940s, New York City, United States
Derivative formsHard bop, cool jazz, post-bop

Bebop or bop is a style of jazz developed in the early to mid-1940s in the United States. It is characterised by a fast tempo, complex chord progressions, numerous key changes, intricate melodies and a focus on individual virtuosity and improvisation rather than danceable big-band arrangements.

History

Bebop emerged in after-hours sessions at clubs such as Minton's Playhouse in Harlem. Its pioneers included alto saxophonist Charlie Parker, trumpeter Dizzy Gillespie, pianists Thelonious Monk and Bud Powell, drummer Kenny Clarke and guitarist Charlie Christian. Parker and Gillespie's recordings of 1945, including "Ko-Ko" and "Salt Peanuts", announced the new style.

Characteristics

Bebop combos were typically small — a rhythm section of piano, bass and drums plus one or two horns. Improvisers built solos on chord changes, often of popular songs, using extended and altered harmonies, chromatic passing notes and asymmetrical phrasing. Drummers shifted timekeeping to the ride cymbal and used the bass drum for accents ("dropping bombs").

Influence

Bebop transformed jazz from popular dance music into an art music listened to for its own sake, and laid the groundwork for cool jazz, hard bop and modal jazz.
