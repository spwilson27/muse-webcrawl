The Fairness Doctrine of the United States Federal Communications Commission (FCC), introduced in 1949, was a policy that required the holders of broadcast licences both to present controversial issues of public importance and to do so in a manner that fairly reflected differing viewpoints. In 1985, under FCC Chairman Mark S. Fowler, a communications policy lawyer who had worked on Ronald Reagan's presidential campaign, the FCC released a report stating that the doctrine hurt the public interest and violated free speech rights of broadcasters guaranteed by the First Amendment.

The FCC eliminated the policy in 1987 under Chairman Dennis R. Patrick and removed its language from the Federal Register in August 2011. The doctrine had two basic elements: it required broadcasters to devote some of their airtime to discussing controversial matters of public interest, and to air contrasting views regarding those matters. Stations were given wide latitude as to how to provide contrasting views, through news segments, public affairs shows, or editorials.

Origins

The doctrine grew out of the Radio Act of 1927 and the Communications Act of 1934, which licensed broadcasters to use scarce public airwaves in the "public interest, convenience, and necessity." Its immediate predecessor was the 1941 Mayflower Doctrine, which banned broadcast editorials outright. The FCC codified the Fairness Doctrine in 1949, permitting editorials but requiring balanced treatment of controversial issues. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld the doctrine in Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC (1969), reasoning that spectrum scarcity justified obligations not imposed on print media — a rationale the Court declined to extend to newspapers in Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo (1974).

Related rules

Two corollaries were the personal attack rule, requiring stations to notify and offer reply time to persons attacked during discussion of controversial issues, and the political editorial rule concerning endorsements of candidates. Both were repealed in 2000 after litigation. The separate equal-time rule (Section 315 of the Communications Act), governing legally qualified candidates, remains in force and is distinct from the Fairness Doctrine.

Repeal and aftermath

In Meredith Corp. v. FCC (1987), a federal appeals court held the doctrine was policy rather than statute, allowing the FCC to abolish it. Congress passed legislation to codify the doctrine, but President Reagan vetoed it, and a 1991 attempt under President George H. W. Bush also failed. The repeal is frequently credited (and blamed) for the subsequent rise of partisan talk radio in the United States, since stations no longer had to offer rebuttal time. Periodic proposals to revive the doctrine have been opposed by presidents and FCC chairs of both parties; the FCC's 2011 order formally striking the obsolete language from its rules closed the matter.
