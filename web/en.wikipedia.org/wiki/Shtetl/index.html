Shtetl
Yiddishשטעטל (shtetl, diminutive of shtot, "town")
RegionCentral and Eastern Europe, especially the Pale of Settlement
Period16th century – 1940s
PopulationAshkenazi Jewish communities, often a majority or large minority of the town

A shtetl (Yiddish: שטעטל, plural shtetlekh) was a small town with a large Ashkenazi Jewish population in Central and Eastern Europe, particularly within the Pale of Settlement in the Russian Empire and in Poland, Galicia and Romania. Shtetls existed from the early modern period until they were destroyed in the Holocaust. They developed a distinctive traditional way of life — Yiddish-speaking, organised around the synagogue, the market square, communal charity and religious learning — that became emblematic of pre-war East European Jewish culture.

Life in the shtetl

The shtetl typically centred on a market square where Jews, who were often the majority of the town's inhabitants and most of its traders and craftsmen, held market days serving the surrounding peasantry. Jewish occupations included shopkeeping, tailoring, shoemaking, carting, innkeeping and finance. Communal institutions — the kehilla (community council), synagogue, study house (beit midrash), ritual bath, cemetery and benevolent societies — structured a life closely bound by religious observance and mutual aid. Hasidism arose and spread through the shtetls of Ukraine and Poland in the 18th and 19th centuries, as did the opposing Mitnagdim tradition of rabbinic scholarship centred in Lithuania.

Decline and destruction

From the late 19th century, poverty, pogroms, and new opportunities drove mass emigration to the United States and other countries, while others left for the growing cities or for Zionist settlement in Palestine. The remaining shtetl world was annihilated during the Second World War, when German forces and their auxiliaries murdered the Jewish inhabitants of town after town in the Holocaust. Survivors' memoirs, Yizkor (memorial) books, and works of Yiddish literature — above all the stories of Sholem Aleichem, whose fictional Kasrilevke and whose Tevye stories (adapted as Fiddler on the Roof) evoke an idealised shtetl — preserved its memory.
