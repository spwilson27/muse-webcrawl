In mathematics, a fixed-point theorem is a result asserting that a function F will have at least one fixed point — a point x in the domain of F for which F(x) = x — under some stated conditions on F. Results of this kind are among the most powerful in mathematics, because they guarantee the existence of solutions to equations and equilibria without constructing them explicitly.

Major theorems

  • Brouwer fixed-point theorem (1910): every continuous function from a closed unit ball in Euclidean space to itself has a fixed point. Equivalently, there is no continuous retraction of the ball onto its boundary sphere.
  • Banach fixed-point theorem (1922), or contraction mapping theorem: every contraction on a non-empty complete metric space has a unique fixed point, obtainable by iterating the function — the basis of many existence proofs for differential equations (Picard–Lindelöf) and of fractal image compression.
  • Schauder fixed-point theorem: extends Brouwer's result to compact convex subsets of Banach spaces, applying to partial differential equations.
  • Kakutani fixed-point theorem (1941): generalises to set-valued maps; used by John Nash to prove the existence of Nash equilibria in game theory.
  • Knaster–Tarski theorem: every monotone map on a complete lattice has fixed points — central to order theory, denotational semantics of programming languages, and abstract interpretation.
  • Lefschetz fixed-point theorem: computes fixed points of maps on topological spaces via homology.

Applications

Fixed-point results underpin proofs of existence of solutions to differential and integral equations, the Perron–Frobenius theorem, equilibrium in economics (via Kakutani's theorem applied to excess demand correspondences), the hairy ball theorem (a corollary of Brouwer's theorem), fluid dynamics, and semantics of recursive programs. Familiar intuition: a map of a country laid on the ground contains a point exactly above the place it represents; and stirring a cup of coffee leaves at least one molecule unmoved — both consequences of Brouwer's theorem.
