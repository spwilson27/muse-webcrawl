Good Neighbor policy

Announced1933
PresidentFranklin D. Roosevelt
RegionLatin America
PrinciplesNon-intervention, non-interference, reciprocal trade

The Good Neighbor policy was the foreign policy of the administration of United States President Franklin D. Roosevelt toward Latin America, announced in his inaugural address of March 1933. It renounced armed intervention in the region, repudiating the interventionist legacy of the Roosevelt Corollary and "dollar diplomacy", and sought instead to build relations on non-intervention, non-interference in the internal affairs of other states, and mutually beneficial reciprocal trade.

Implementation

Under Secretary of State Cordell Hull, the United States accepted the principle of non-intervention at the Montevideo Convention of 1933 and the Buenos Aires conference of 1936. The Platt Amendment, which had authorised intervention in Cuba, was abrogated in 1934; American marines were withdrawn from Haiti and Nicaragua; and the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 reduced tariffs through bilateral treaties. The administration also declined to intervene during Mexico's expropriation of foreign oil companies in 1938, settling the dispute by negotiation. Cultural diplomacy, coordinated from 1940 by Nelson Rockefeller's Office of Inter-American Affairs, promoted exchanges, tourism and films such as Walt Disney's Saludos Amigos.

Legacy

The policy improved the standing of the United States in the hemisphere and secured Latin American cooperation during the Second World War, when most states in the region joined the Allied side. With the onset of the Cold War, Washington again intervened against governments it regarded as communist-leaning, beginning in Guatemala in 1954, and the Good Neighbor era is generally considered to have ended, though its principles remain a reference point in inter-American relations.
