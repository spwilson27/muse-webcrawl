Inductor

An inductor, also called a coil, choke, or reactor, is a passive two-terminal electrical component that stores energy in a magnetic field when electric current flows through it. An inductor typically consists of an insulated wire wound into a coil around a core of air or ferromagnetic material.

Inductance

The property that quantifies an inductor's opposition to changes in current is inductance, measured in henries (H) after Joseph Henry. The voltage across an inductor is proportional to the rate of change of current: V = L dI/dt. A changing current induces an electromotive force opposing the change, by Lenz's law.

Construction and types

Inductors range from tiny surface-mount beads to large power chokes. Core materials include air, iron powder, and ferrite; higher core permeability increases inductance for a given number of turns. Quality factor Q measures an inductor's efficiency relative to its losses.

Applications

Inductors are used in filters, tuned circuits, transformers, power supplies (buck and boost converters), motors, and as chokes that block high-frequency interference while passing low frequencies.
