Kimmeridge Clay

The Kimmeridge Clay Formation is a sedimentary deposit of fossiliferous marine clay which is of Upper Jurassic age and occurs widely in southern and eastern England and in the North Sea. It is named after the village of Kimmeridge on the coast of Dorset, England, where exposures of the formation are well developed in the cliffs.

Geology

The formation was deposited during the Kimmeridgian and Tithonian stages of the Late Jurassic, roughly 157 to 148 million years ago, in a deep marine environment. It consists largely of dark grey to black shales and mudstones with subordinate beds of limestone, including the distinctive cementstone bands known locally as Kimmeridge "coals". The formation reaches its greatest thickness of around 500 metres in the Wessex Basin.

Fossils

The Kimmeridge Clay is one of the most important sources of Jurassic marine fossils in Europe. It has yielded abundant ammonites, bivalves and other invertebrates, as well as the remains of large marine reptiles including ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and pliosaurs, giant fish such as Leedsichthys, and even occasional dinosaurs washed out to sea. In 2023 a remarkably complete pliosaur skull was recovered from the cliffs near Kimmeridge.

Economic significance

The organic-rich Kimmeridge Clay is the principal source rock for the oil fields of the North Sea, including the Brent and associated fields, and for the onshore oil field at Wytch Farm in Dorset. Historically, the oil shales of the formation were burnt locally as fuel and worked for alum, cement, and the production of oil.
