Rioni of Rome

The rioni of Rome (singular rione, from Latin regio) are the traditional administrative divisions of the historic centre of Rome, Italy. There are 22 rioni, each identified by a Roman numeral (R. I to R. XXII) and most bearing a coat of arms. They lie almost entirely within the Aurelian Walls and constitute the city's Municipio I.

History

The division descends from the 14 regions of Augustan Rome. In the Middle Ages the city re-organised into twelve regions, and the number and boundaries shifted over the centuries. The modern set of rioni took shape in the 18th and 19th centuries: in 1743–1744, under Pope Benedict XIV, the boundaries were fixed with marble plaques, many of which survive on street corners. Prati became the 22nd and last rione in 1921, the only one lying outside the Aurelian Walls, across the Tiber near the Vatican.

The 22 rioni

The rioni are: I Monti, the oldest, covering the Esquiline and Viminal hills; II Trevi, around the Trevi Fountain; III Colonna; IV Campo Marzio; V Ponte, facing Castel Sant'Angelo; VI Parione, around Piazza Navona; VII Regola; VIII Sant'Eustachio; IX Pigna, around the Pantheon; X Campitelli, including the Capitoline Hill and Roman Forum; XI Sant'Angelo, the old Jewish ghetto area; XII Ripa, along the Tiber with the Aventine; XIII Trastevere, across the river, famed for its medieval lanes; XIV Borgo, between the Vatican and Castel Sant'Angelo; XV Esquilino; XVI Ludovisi, around Via Veneto; XVII Sallustiano; XVIII Castro Pretorio, around Termini station; XIX Celio, around the Colosseum and Caelian Hill; XX Testaccio, the working-class quarter by the ancient river port; XXI San Saba; and XXII Prati.

Administration

Today the rioni are statistical and traditional units rather than autonomous governments; Rome is administered through larger municipi. Nevertheless the rioni retain strong local identities and are used in addresses, property descriptions and everyday speech to locate places in the historic centre.
