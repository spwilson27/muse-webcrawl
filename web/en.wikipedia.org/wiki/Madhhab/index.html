Madhhab

A madhhab (Arabic: مذهب, plural madhahib) is a school of thought within fiqh (Islamic jurisprudence). The schools interpret the sources of Islamic law — the Quran and the Sunnah — through established methodologies of legal reasoning, and they differ on points of ritual practice and law while generally recognising one another as legitimate.

Sunni schools

Four Sunni madhhabs have survived to the present day: the Hanafi school, founded on the teachings of Abu Hanifa and dominant in Turkey, Central and South Asia and Egypt; the Maliki school of Malik ibn Anas, predominant in North and West Africa; the Shafi'i school of al-Shafi'i, followed in East Africa, Yemen and Southeast Asia; and the Hanbali school of Ahmad ibn Hanbal, strongest in Saudi Arabia and Qatar.

Shia and other schools

The main Shia school is the Ja'fari (Twelver) madhhab, followed in Iran, Iraq, Lebanon and elsewhere; the Zaydi and Ismaili traditions maintain their own jurisprudence. The Ibadi school survives principally in Oman. Historically there were other schools, such as the Zahiri, which no longer have significant followings.

Role

For most lay Muslims, adherence to a madhhab means following its rulings on prayer, fasting, family law and other matters, often as codified in classical manuals and applied by local scholars. Reform movements have sometimes questioned strict taqlid (adherence to a school), but the madhhabs remain the framework within which most Islamic legal practice operates.
