Pierre Bézier, inventor of the curves that bear his name.

Pierre Étienne Bézier (1 September 1910 – 25 November 1999) was a French engineer and mathematician, best known as the inventor of Bézier curves and Bézier surfaces, which form the basis of most modern computer-aided design (CAD) and computer graphics vector systems.

Career

Bézier studied mechanical and electrical engineering at the École des Arts et Métiers and the École Supérieure d'Électricité, and later earned a doctorate in mathematics from the University of Paris. He joined the car manufacturer Renault in 1933 and remained there for 42 years, rising to director of mechanical development.

The UNISURF system

In the 1960s Bézier developed UNISURF, a system for designing car bodies directly in the computer using curves defined by control points. Because designers could shape a surface intuitively by moving a few poles, rather than by specifying thousands of points, the method revolutionised automotive styling and machining. Bézier publicised the mathematics freely, ensuring the curves bearing his name spread throughout industry.

Independently, Paul de Casteljau at Citroën had developed a similar method a few years earlier, the basis of the de Casteljau algorithm, but Citroën kept his work secret until the 1970s. The curves were named after Bézier because of his publications and the adoption of UNISURF.

Legacy

Bézier curves are embedded in PostScript, PDF, SVG, font formats such as TrueType and PostScript Type 1, and virtually every drawing and design program. The Solid Modeling Association awards the Pierre Bézier Award for contributions to geometric modelling.
