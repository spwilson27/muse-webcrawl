Guerrilla gardening

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Guerrilla gardening is the act of gardening — raising food plants or flowers — on land that the gardeners do not have the legal rights to cultivate, such as abandoned sites, areas that are not being looked after, or private property. It encompasses a diverse range of people and motivations, ranging from enthusiastic gardeners who spill over their legal boundaries to politically motivated protest gardening.

History

Precursors include the 17th-century Diggers of Gerrard Winstanley and, in the modern movement, the Green Guerrillas founded in New York City in 1973 by Liz Christy, who transformed a derelict lot on the Bowery into what became the first community garden in the city. In London in 2000, activists occupied Parliament Square in a May Day guerrilla gardening action, planting vegetables and a maypole.

Methods

Guerrilla gardeners often work at night to cultivate neglected verges, roundabouts, tree pits and railway embankments. A signature technique is the seed bomb — a ball of clay, compost and seeds that can be thrown into fenced-off land and left to germinate with rain. Some target food growing; others plant hardy flowers for pollinators or visibility.

Legal status and reception

Cultivating others' land without permission is technically trespass or criminal damage in many jurisdictions, but landowners and councils frequently tolerate or retrospectively endorse successful plots, and some have become officially recognised community gardens. Prominent advocates include London gardener Richard Reynolds, who documented the movement in his 2008 book On Guerrilla Gardening.
