Planck time

Symbol: tP
Value: ≈ 5.39 × 10−44 seconds
Definition: Time for light to travel one Planck length in vacuum

In particle physics and physical cosmology, the Planck time (tP) is a unit of time in the system of natural units known as Planck units. It is the time required for light to travel, in a vacuum, a distance of one Planck length, and is approximately 5.39 × 10−44 seconds. The unit is named after Max Planck, who proposed the system of natural units in 1899.

Definition

The Planck time is defined from three fundamental constants — the speed of light c, the gravitational constant G, and the reduced Planck constant ħ — as tP = √(ħG/c5). Because it is built only from these constants, it is independent of any human measurement convention.

Significance

The Planck time is widely regarded as the scale below which current theories of physics break down: at such intervals, quantum fluctuations of spacetime itself ("quantum foam") are expected to be so violent that the smooth notions of space and time used in general relativity and quantum mechanics cease to apply, and a theory of quantum gravity would be required. In cosmology, the first Planck time after the Big Bang — the Planck epoch, up to about 10−43 seconds — is the earliest period about which conventional physics can say anything at all. No experiment comes close to probing such intervals directly: the shortest times measured are some twenty orders of magnitude larger.
