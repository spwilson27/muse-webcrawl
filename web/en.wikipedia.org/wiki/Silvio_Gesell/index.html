Silvio Gesell

Silvio Gesell (17 March 1862 – 11 March 1930) was a German-Argentine merchant and theoretical economist, founder of the doctrine of Freiwirtschaft ("free economy"). His best-known proposal is Freigeld ("free money") — currency that loses value over time unless a stamp is affixed to it, a form of demurrage.

Life

Born in Sankt Vith (then Prussia, now Belgium), Gesell emigrated to Argentina in 1887 and prospered in business. Argentina's economic crises of the 1890s turned him to monetary theory: he argued that money, unlike goods, can be hoarded without spoiling, giving its holders an unjust advantage in the form of interest. His magnum opus, The Natural Economic Order (1916), combined Freigeld with Freiland, the socialisation of land rent. In 1919 he briefly served as finance minister of the short-lived Bavarian Soviet Republic, for which he was later tried and acquitted.

Ideas and influence

Gesell proposed that banknotes carry dated stamp scrip: holders must periodically buy small stamps to keep notes valid, discouraging hoarding and stimulating circulation. John Maynard Keynes praised Gesell in The General Theory (1936) as a "strange, unduly neglected prophet," and economist Irving Fisher championed stamp scrip in the United States during the Great Depression; the Austrian town of Wörgl ran a famous local experiment with demurrage currency in 1932–33. Gesell's ideas live on in local currency movements and periodic academic revivals of demurrage.
