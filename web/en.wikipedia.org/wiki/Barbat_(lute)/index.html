Barbat (lute)

The barbat (Persian: بربط) is a short-necked lute of Persian origin, characterized by a large pear-shaped body, a bent-back pegbox, and a short neck with tied frets. It is the ancestor, via the Arabic oud, of the European lute. The barbat flourished in pre-Islamic and early Islamic Persia and was carried westward along trade and conquest routes.

History

Images of short-necked lutes appear in Sasanian art, and the barbat was a central instrument of the Sasanian court; the legendary musician Barbad, after whom later tradition sometimes linked the instrument's name, was minstrel to Khosrow II. After the Arab conquest of Persia, the instrument was adopted and refined in the Islamic world as the oud ("wood"), probably named to distinguish its wooden soundboard from earlier skin-faced lutes. Through Muslim Spain the oud entered medieval Europe, where it evolved into the lute with gut frets and a vaulted back of ribs.

Construction and modern revival

The barbat differs from the modern oud principally in proportions and historical playing technique; it traditionally had four courses of strings (later five), a body of bent wooden staves, and was played with a quill plectrum (risha). In the twentieth century Iranian musicians such as Hossein Behroozinia revived the barbat as distinct from the Arab oud, and contemporary Iranian makers build it in both historical and concert forms.
