Conflict (process)

Conflict, as a process, is a state of disagreement, opposition, or incompatibility between two or more parties — individuals, groups, organizations, or states — arising from perceived differences in interests, goals, values, beliefs, or access to resources. Conflict processes are studied across psychology, sociology, organizational behaviour, political science, and peace and conflict studies.

The conflict process

A widely used model, developed by Louis Pondy, describes organizational conflict as unfolding in stages: latent conflict (underlying conditions such as scarce resources, divergent goals, or ambiguous roles), perceived conflict (the parties become aware of the incompatibility), felt conflict (the disagreement is personalized, producing stress, tension, or hostility), manifest conflict (overt behaviour such as argument, obstruction, competition, or aggression), and conflict aftermath (the outcome, which may resolve the issue or sow the seeds of future episodes). Later models emphasize escalation and de-escalation dynamics: conflicts tend to intensify through reciprocal negative behaviour, hardened positions, and the involvement of additional parties, and to subside through negotiation, fatigue, intervention, or structural change.

Types and levels

Conflicts are classified by their parties — intrapersonal (within a person), interpersonal, intragroup, intergroup, and international — and by their subject matter: conflicts over tasks and methods ("what" and "how") are often distinguished from relationship conflicts, which concern personal friction and identity. Research in management suggests moderate task conflict can improve group decision-making by surfacing alternatives, whereas relationship conflict is generally harmful to performance and cohesion.

Management and resolution

Approaches to handling conflict include avoidance, accommodation, competition, compromise, and collaboration (problem-solving), as systematized in the Thomas–Kilmann conflict mode instrument. Structured processes — negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and restorative practices — aim to transform manifest conflict into settlement, resolution, or constructive change. The modern view in organizational studies treats conflict not as inherently pathological but as an inevitable feature of collective life whose outcomes depend on how it is managed.
