Voodoo doll

A voodoo doll is a poppet or effigy used in various folk magic traditions, popularly associated in Western culture with sticking pins into a doll to harm or control the person it represents. Despite the name, such dolls have little to do with the actual religions of Haitian Vodou or Louisiana Voodoo; the practice descends mainly from European folk magic, in which image magic — acting on a likeness of a person to affect that person — has been widespread since antiquity.

History

Effigies used for cursing or healing appear in ancient Egyptian, Greek, and Roman practice, where lead figurines were bound, pierced, or inscribed with spells. In medieval and early modern Europe, poppets of clay, wax, or cloth were used in love magic, healing, and maleficium, and figured in witchcraft trials — the 1590–91 North Berwick trials involved claims of an image of King James VI being melted. The association with Vodou arose largely through 20th-century sensational journalism, travel writing, and Hollywood films, which merged African diasporic religions with European image magic into the "voodoo doll" stereotype.

In Vodou and Hoodoo

In Haitian Vodou, dolls may serve as devotional objects or vessels associated with the lwa (spirits), but ritual pin-sticking to inflict harm is not a characteristic Vodou practice. In African American Hoodoo and New Orleans folk practice, doll babies are worked for a range of purposes — love, protection, healing, or domination — often baptized in the target's name and containing personal concerns such as hair. Pins, where used, may fix an intention or command attention rather than cause pain.

Popular culture and psychology

The voodoo doll has become a stock symbol of sympathetic magic and a business and stress-relief novelty. Research and commentary on the psychology of ritual suggest that symbolic actions against an effigy can affect the practitioner's emotions and perceptions regardless of any supernatural mechanism.
