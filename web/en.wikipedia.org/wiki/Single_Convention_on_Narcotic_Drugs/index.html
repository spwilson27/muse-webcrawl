Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs

The Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs of 1961 is an international treaty that controls psychoactive substances and forms the foundation of the global drug control regime. It consolidated and replaced a series of earlier treaties dating back to the 1912 International Opium Convention, simplified the international control organs into the International Narcotics Control Board (INCB), and introduced controls on cannabis, coca and synthetic drugs. The Convention classifies controlled substances into four schedules according to their dependence liability and therapeutic value, and obliges signatory states to limit production, trade and possession of scheduled drugs to medical and scientific purposes.

History

Before 1961, international drug control rested on a patchwork of nine treaties concluded between 1912 and 1953. A United Nations conference in New York adopted the Single Convention on 30 March 1961 to unify this system; it entered into force on 13 December 1964. It was amended by a 1972 Protocol, which expanded treatment and rehabilitation provisions and strengthened the INCB. Alongside the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances and the 1988 Vienna Convention against illicit trafficking, it remains one of the three principal UN drug treaties.

Key provisions

  • Scheduling: Over 130 substances are listed in Schedules I–IV. Schedule I contains the standard controlled drugs (including cannabis, heroin, cocaine and morphine); Schedule IV lists drugs considered particularly dangerous with limited medical value, subject to special restrictions.
  • Estimates system: Parties must furnish the INCB with annual estimates of drug requirements and statistics on production, trade and stocks, creating a closed international accounting system for licit narcotics.
  • Penal provisions: Parties must make cultivation, production, trafficking and possession (for other than medical purposes) punishable offences under domestic law, though the precise obligations regarding personal possession remain a matter of longstanding legal debate.
  • Cultivation controls: The Convention regulates the opium poppy, coca bush and cannabis plant, requiring licensing and government agencies for licit cultivation.

Developments and debate

The Convention has near-universal membership, with 186 parties. Its prohibitionist framework has been criticised by reform advocates, and tensions have arisen as some states legalise cannabis for non-medical use. In December 2020, the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs voted to remove cannabis from Schedule IV — while keeping it in Schedule I — accepting a World Health Organization recommendation that recognised its medical utility. Bolivia temporarily withdrew and re-acceded with a reservation protecting traditional coca-leaf chewing, a mechanism for accommodating domestic practices.
