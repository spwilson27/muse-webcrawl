The Cumaean Sibyl was the priestess presiding over the Apollonian oracle at Cumae, a Greek colony near Naples and the oldest Greek settlement on the Italian mainland. The most famous of the sibyls of the ancient world, she was credited with selling the Sibylline Books to the last king of Rome and, in Virgil's Aeneid, with guiding the hero Aeneas through the underworld.

Legend

According to the story told by Ovid and others, the god Apollo offered the Sibyl anything she wished; she asked to live for as many years as the grains of sand in a handful she held — but forgot to ask for eternal youth. She therefore aged for a thousand years, shrinking until, in Petronius's Satyricon, she hangs in a jar and, when asked what she wants, replies "I want to die." Virgil names her Deiphobe, daughter of Glaucus.

The Sibylline Books

Legend held that the Sibyl offered nine books of prophecies to Tarquin, the last king of Rome. When he refused her price, she burned three, then three more, until he bought the remaining three at the original price. The books were kept in the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill and consulted by a special college of priests in times of crisis for centuries. The original collection was destroyed by fire in 83 BC; a replacement collection was later assembled. The Sibyl's cave at Cumae — a long trapezoidal gallery cut into the rock, identified in 1932 — remains a visitor site.

In literature and art

In Book 6 of the Aeneid the Sibyl guides Aeneas to the golden bough and leads him into Hades to meet his father's shade. Her Fourth Eclogue prophecy of a returning golden age was read by Christians as foretelling Christ, which earned her a place among the prophets of Renaissance art: Michelangelo depicted her as a powerful elderly woman on the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–1512), and she appears in the Dies Irae ("teste David cum Sibylla" — "as David and the Sibyl testify"). T. S. Eliot used the Petronius episode as the epigraph to The Waste Land (1922).
