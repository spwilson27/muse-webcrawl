Aeronomy is the scientific study of the upper atmosphere of the Earth and other planets — the regions in which dissociation and ionisation by solar radiation are important physical and chemical processes. It covers roughly the atmosphere above the stratopause: the mesosphere, thermosphere, ionosphere and exosphere. The word was proposed by the geophysicist Sydney Chapman in 1956 (from Greek aēr, air, and nomos, law).

Scope

Aeronomers study the composition, temperature, density and dynamics of the rarefied upper atmosphere; phenomena such as airglow, aurorae, noctilucent clouds and meteor ablation; ionospheric radio propagation; and the interaction of the upper atmosphere with the solar wind and magnetosphere above it and the weather systems below it. The discipline also extends to other planets: the ionospheres of Mars and Venus and the atmospheres of Titan and the giant planets are studied with the same toolkit.

Methods

Because the upper atmosphere is too high for balloons and too low for long-lived satellites, it was first explored by sounding rockets in the late 1940s. Modern aeronomy relies on satellite missions, ground-based incoherent-scatter radars, lidars, Fabry–Pérot interferometers measuring winds and temperatures from airglow, and global GPS measurements of ionospheric electron content.
