Clozapine

Clozapine is an atypical antipsychotic medication, the first of its class, used primarily to treat schizophrenia that has not responded to other antipsychotics, and to reduce the risk of suicide in schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder. Despite its unique effectiveness, its use is restricted by the risk of serious blood disorders.

History

Synthesised in 1956 and introduced in Europe in the 1970s, clozapine was withdrawn in many countries after cases of fatal agranulocytosis (a dangerous drop in white blood cells). Following the landmark 1988 Kane study demonstrating superiority in treatment-resistant schizophrenia, it was reintroduced (FDA approval 1989) under strict blood-monitoring requirements.

Medical uses

  • Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (after failure of at least two other antipsychotics)
  • Reduction of recurrent suicidal behaviour in schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder
  • Off-label: severe psychosis in Parkinson's disease at low doses

Adverse effects

Serious risks include agranulocytosis (about 1% of patients), seizures, myocarditis, orthostatic hypotension, and metabolic effects such as weight gain and diabetes. Regular absolute neutrophil count (ANC) monitoring is mandatory under programs such as the U.S. REMS registry. Common effects include sedation, dizziness, hypersalivation, and constipation.

Pharmacology

Clozapine acts on many receptors — dopamine D4 and D2, serotonin 5-HT2A, muscarinic, histamine H1, and adrenergic receptors — which likely underlies both its efficacy and its side-effect profile. It is metabolised mainly by CYP1A2, so smoking and interacting drugs alter levels.
