Lady Sings the Blues is a 1972 American biographical drama film produced by Motown Productions and released by Paramount Pictures, telling the story of jazz singer Billie Holiday. It stars Diana Ross in her film debut as Holiday, alongside Billy Dee Williams as her husband Louis McKay and Richard Pryor as the character "Piano Man." The screenplay, by Terence McCloy, Chris Clark, and Suzanne de Passe, was loosely based on Holiday's 1956 autobiography of the same name, co-written with William Dufty.

Plot

The film opens with Holiday's 1947 arrest on narcotics charges and proceeds in flashback through her difficult childhood in Baltimore, her work as a maid and prostitute, her discovery as a singer, touring with big bands in the Jim Crow South, her rise to fame, her addiction to heroin, and her relationships, ending with her comeback and death in 1959 at age 44. Though it takes substantial liberties with the facts of Holiday's life, the film was a commercial success and is credited with reviving interest in her music.

Reception and awards

Ross's performance was widely praised and earned her an Academy Award nomination for Best Actress and a Golden Globe Award for Most Promising Newcomer. The film received five Academy Award nominations in total, including for its screenplay, art direction, costume design, and Michel Legrand's score. Its soundtrack album, featuring Ross performing Holiday's songs, reached number one on the Billboard album chart.
