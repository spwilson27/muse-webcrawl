Menno van Coehoorn

Menno, Baron van Coehoorn
BornMarch 1641, Britsum, Friesland, Dutch Republic
Died17 March 1704, The Hague, Dutch Republic
OccupationSoldier, military engineer

Menno, Baron van Coehoorn (Dutch pronunciation: [ˈmɛnoː vɑŋ ˈkuɦoːr(ə)n]; March 1641 – 17 March 1704) was a Dutch soldier and military engineer of Swedish descent. He is regarded, together with his great French rival Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban, as one of the most capable siege engineers of the seventeenth century, and his name is attached to a system of fortification adapted to the low-lying terrain of the Netherlands.

Career

Coehoorn entered military service at sixteen and fought in the defence of Friesland during the Franco-Dutch War. He took part in the sieges of Maastricht, Grave, and other strongholds, and was present at the Battle of Seneffe in 1674. During the Nine Years' War he conducted the defence of Namur in 1692 against Vauban himself, and in 1695 helped William III retake the fortress. He directed sieges and built or rebuilt fortifications across the Dutch barrier, including Bergen op Zoom, which was considered a masterpiece of his art.

Fortification system

Coehoorn set out his ideas in Nieuwe vestingbouw op een natte of lage horisont ("New fortification building on a wet or low horizon", 1685). Suited to waterlogged Dutch ground, his system used broad, low earthen ramparts, multiple lines of defence, wide wet ditches, and numerous outworks, concentrating heavy mortar fire against besiegers. He also designed a small, portable bronze mortar — the coehorn — light enough to be carried by two men, which remained in use for two centuries and gave his name to English-language gunnery.

Death and legacy

Coehoorn died of a stroke at The Hague in 1704, while serving as master-general of the artillery and lieutenant-general of infantry in the War of the Spanish Succession. He is buried at Wijckel in Friesland, where a monument commemorates him.
