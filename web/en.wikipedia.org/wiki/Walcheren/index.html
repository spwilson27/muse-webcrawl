Walcheren (Dutch pronunciation: [ˈʋɑlxərə(n)]) is a region and former island in the Dutch province of Zeeland in the south-western Netherlands, at the mouth of the Scheldt estuary. It lies between the Oosterschelde in the north and the Westerschelde in the south and is roughly the shape of a rhombus. The two sides facing the North Sea consist of dunes and beach; the rest of its coastline is made up of dykes. Middelburg, the provincial capital, lies at Walcheren's centre; the city of Vlissingen (Flushing) is the region's main port city and lies on the south coast.

History

The Romans called the place Walacria. In the early Middle Ages Walcheren was a trading region contested by Franks and Vikings; Domburg was an important early-medieval trading settlement. The island's towns of Middelburg and Vlissingen grew wealthy through trade, and during the Dutch Golden Age Middelburg was one of the most important cities of the Dutch East India Company.

In 1809, during the Napoleonic Wars, Britain landed a large expeditionary force on Walcheren in an attempt to destroy the French fleet at Antwerp. The Walcheren Campaign ended in disaster: the advance stalled, and thousands of British soldiers died of "Walcheren fever", a combination of malaria and typhoid prevalent in the marshy island. The force withdrew by December, and the affair became a byword for military mismanagement.

Second World War

Walcheren was of immense strategic value in 1944: German guns on the island commanded the Scheldt estuary and hence access to the captured port of Antwerp, which the Allies urgently needed for supplies. In the Battle of the Scheldt, Allied bombers breached the island's sea dykes in October 1944, flooding most of Walcheren to force out the defenders. Operation Infatuate, an amphibious assault by British and Canadian forces in November 1944, finally cleared the island at heavy cost. The island's inundation was not fully repaired until 1946.

Geography and modern day

Walcheren ceased to be an island through land reclamation and causeway construction: the Sloedam connected it to Zuid-Beveland in 1871, and the Delta Works of the later twentieth century linked it further to the mainland via the Veerse Gatdam. Today the region is a popular holiday destination for its beaches and seaside towns such as Domburg, Zoutelande and Westkapelle.
