Pahlavi scripts

The Pahlavi scripts are the writing systems used to write Middle Iranian languages — principally Middle Persian — during the Sasanian period and for some centuries after the Muslim conquest of Persia, especially for Zoroastrian religious texts. Derived from the Imperial Aramaic alphabet, Pahlavi is written from right to left and is notorious for its extensive use of Aramaic heterograms (huzwāreš): words written as Aramaic but read aloud in Persian.

Varieties

Several forms are distinguished: Inscriptional Pahlavi, used in monumental inscriptions of the early Sasanian kings (3rd–4th centuries); Psalter Pahlavi, used for a Christian Psalter translation; and Book Pahlavi, the cursive book hand in which nearly all surviving Zoroastrian literature is written. Book Pahlavi reduced the alphabet to about fourteen letter shapes, many of them ambiguous, making the script notoriously difficult to read.

Heterograms

Because Middle Persian writing grew out of the Aramaic chancellery tradition, scribes wrote many common words — pronouns, prepositions and frequent nouns and verbs — with Aramaic spellings while pronouncing the Persian equivalent. For example, the written form MLKʾ (Aramaic malkā, "king") was read as šāh. These logograms are conventionally transcribed in capital letters.

Decline and legacy

After the Arab conquest, Persian gradually came to be written in the Arabic script (New Persian), but Zoroastrian priests continued copying sacred and learned works in Book Pahlavi for centuries. The Avestan alphabet was created in the Sasanian era partly in reaction to Pahlavi's ambiguities, to record the Avesta precisely. Pahlavi literature includes the Denkard, the Bundahishn and the romance Kār-Nāmag ī Ardašīr.
