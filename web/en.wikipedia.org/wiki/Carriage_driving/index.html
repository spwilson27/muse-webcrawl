Carriage driving

Carriage driving is a form of competitive horsemanship in which horses or ponies are hitched to a two- or four-wheeled carriage and driven in competition. It is one of the oldest equestrian disciplines and, in its combined driving form, is modelled on the three-day event of ridden eventing, with dressage, cones and a marathon cross-country phase.

Turnouts

A turnout consists of the horse or horses, the vehicle, harness and the driver, often assisted by a groom. Horses may be driven singly, in pairs (side by side), in tandem (one in front of the other), as a unicorn (a pair with a single leader) or as a team of four. Turnouts vary from light sporting carts to formal road coaches and state landaus used for ceremonial occasions.

Competition

Combined driving, governed internationally by the Fédération Équestre Internationale (FEI), comprises three phases held over three days: driven dressage, a marathon phase over a cross-country course with fixed obstacles, and a cones phase testing accuracy and speed through a course of narrowly spaced cones. World championships are held for singles, pairs and four-in-hand teams. Other forms include pleasure driving shows, where turnout elegance and manners are judged, and scurry driving, a fast arena competition over cones.

History

Driving was the principal mode of land transport for centuries. Mail coaches and stagecoaches spread road networks across Europe and North America, and four-in-hand driving became a fashionable sport of the wealthy in the 19th century. The revival of competitive driving in the 20th century owes much to Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, who helped frame the international rules and competed at world level into his eighties.
