Deinstitutionalization

Deinstitutionalization (or deinstitutionalisation) is the process of replacing long-stay psychiatric hospitals and other large residential institutions with less isolated community mental health services. The movement gained force in the mid-twentieth century in many Western countries and transformed the care of people with mental illness and developmental disabilities.

Causes

Several factors converged: exposés of appalling conditions in asylums; the arrival of antipsychotic medication such as chlorpromazine in the 1950s, which made management outside hospitals seem feasible; legal reforms strengthening patients' rights and restricting involuntary commitment; and the fiscal appeal of closing expensive institutions. In the United States, policy landmarks included the Community Mental Health Act of 1963, while census numbers in state mental hospitals fell from a peak of around 559,000 patients in 1955 to a small fraction of that by the 1990s.

Outcomes and criticism

Well-supported community care can offer people greater freedom and better quality of life. However, in many places community services were underfunded or never built at the promised scale, a failure associated with homelessness, incarceration, and the "revolving door" of repeated short admissions for some people with severe mental illness — a pattern sometimes called transinstitutionalization, as populations shifted from hospitals to prisons and nursing facilities. Similar deinstitutionalisation policies were applied to orphanages and institutions for people with intellectual disabilities, where the quality of replacement services likewise varies.
