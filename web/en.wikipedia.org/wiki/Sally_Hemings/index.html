Sally Hemings

Bornc. 1773, Virginia
Died1835, Charlottesville, Virginia
ChildrenSix, including Madison and Eston Hemings
ParentElizabeth Hemings; father believed to be John Wayles

Sarah "Sally" Hemings (c. 1773 – 1835) was an enslaved woman owned by Thomas Jefferson, the third president of the United States. She is believed to have borne six children by Jefferson, four of whom survived to adulthood. She was the daughter of Elizabeth (Betty) Hemings and, according to family tradition, of John Wayles, Jefferson's father-in-law — which would make her the half-sister of Jefferson's wife Martha Wayles Jefferson.

Life

Hemings came into Jefferson's ownership as an infant through his inheritance from the Wayles estate. In 1787, at about fourteen, she accompanied Jefferson's younger daughter Mary to Paris, where Jefferson was serving as American minister to France and where slavery was effectively unenforceable; there she served as a lady's maid and received training and wages. She returned with the Jefferson household to Virginia in 1789. According to her son Madison Hemings's 1873 memoir, she agreed to return after Jefferson promised that her children would be freed at age 21.

At Monticello she worked in the house, caring for Jefferson's daughters and grandchildren and doing sewing and other domestic work. Her four surviving children — Beverly, Harriet, Madison and Eston — were all freed: Beverly and Harriet were allowed to leave in 1822, and Madison and Eston were freed under Jefferson's will in 1826. Hemings herself was never formally freed; after Jefferson's death his daughter Martha Jefferson Randolph gave her informal freedom ("time"), and she lived her final years with her sons in Charlottesville.

Historical debate and evidence

Allegations of Jefferson's paternity appeared in print as early as 1802, in James Callender's newspaper attacks. For most of the 20th century mainstream historians rejected the relationship, but a 1998 DNA study of descendants of Eston Hemings, Jefferson's male-line descendants and the Carr family showed a match to the Jefferson male line and no match to the Carrs, who had been proposed as alternative fathers. Combined with documentary and oral-history evidence, the Thomas Jefferson Foundation and most historians now accept that Jefferson fathered Hemings's children. Monticello today interprets her life prominently, including a restored room identified as hers.
