Tracheotomy

Tracheotomy (tracheostomy is often used interchangeably) is a surgical procedure that consists of making an incision in the anterior aspect of the neck and opening a direct airway through an incision in the trachea (windpipe). The resulting stoma (hole) can serve independently as an airway or as a site for a tracheal tube to be inserted; this tube allows a person to breathe without the use of the nose or mouth. The opening may be temporary or permanent.

A tracheostomy tube inserted through the anterior neck into the trachea.

Indications

A tracheotomy may be performed in emergency situations, such as acute upper airway obstruction by a foreign body, facial trauma, or swelling, when endotracheal intubation is impossible; in such settings a related emergency procedure is a cricothyrotomy. Elective tracheotomy is commonly performed in intensive care for patients who require prolonged mechanical ventilation, because it facilitates suctioning of secretions, reduces dead space, may shorten ventilation time, and is generally more comfortable than a long-term oral endotracheal tube. It is also used for upper airway obstruction from tumours or stenosis, severe sleep apnoea in selected cases, and protection of the airway in patients unable to clear secretions.

Technique

In an open surgical tracheotomy, a horizontal incision is made in the neck, the strap muscles are separated, the thyroid isthmus is divided or retracted, and an opening is made in the second to fourth tracheal rings, into which a cuffed tube is inserted. A percutaneous dilatational tracheotomy, performed at the bedside in intensive care, uses the Seldinger technique with guidewire and dilators under bronchoscopic or ultrasound guidance. Comparative studies generally find similar outcomes, with percutaneous techniques being faster and cheaper in suitable patients.

Complications

Complications include bleeding, infection, accidental decannulation, tracheal stenosis, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, and the rare but catastrophic tracheo-innominate artery fistula. Long-term tubes may cause swallowing difficulties and interfere with speech, though speaking valves and fenestrated tubes can restore voice for many patients.

History

The procedure is ancient: tracheotomy-like operations are described in Egyptian texts and by Greek and Roman physicians, and it became established in modern practice in the 19th century, notably in the treatment of diphtheria. Chevalier Jackson standardised the modern surgical technique in the early 20th century, greatly reducing its mortality.

Terminology

Strictly, tracheotomy refers to the surgical incision into the trachea, while tracheostomy refers to the creation of a permanent or semi-permanent opening (stoma); in clinical practice the two terms are used interchangeably.
