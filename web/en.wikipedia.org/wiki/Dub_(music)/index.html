Dub (music)

The mixing console as an instrument: central to dub production

Dub is a musical style that originated in Jamaican sound system culture in the late 1960s and developed through the 1970s. Dub works are typically remixes of existing recordings — usually reggae tracks — in which the engineer strips the song down to its rhythm section (drums and bass, the "riddim"), drops the vocal in and out, and applies studio effects such as echo, reverb, and phasing as compositional elements in their own right.

Origins

Dub grew out of the practice of pressing instrumental "versions" of songs onto the B-sides of singles for sound system deejays to talk over. Engineers such as Ruddy Redwood and King Tubby (Osbourne Ruddock) began experimenting with the mixing desk itself, producing radical reconstructions of tracks. Tubby's work, and that of producers and engineers including Lee "Scratch" Perry, Errol Thompson and Scientist, defined the genre's classic era. The term "dub" derives from dubbing — copying and re-recording tape.

Characteristics

Signature techniques include dramatic muting of tracks, cavernous spring-reverb and tape-echo washes, emphasised basslines, fragmentary vocal and horn stabs, and sudden drops into silence or echo trails. Dub treated the recording studio as an instrument years before similar ideas became central to other genres.

Influence

Dub's influence is enormous: it is a direct ancestor of remix culture, hip hop production, jungle and drum and bass, dubstep, UK garage, trip hop and much electronic dance music. In Britain, dub converged with punk and post-punk (artists such as Adrian Sherwood and the On-U Sound label, Jah Wobble, and Dennis Bovell), and in the 1980s and 1990s a conscious "dub" revival and diaspora scene flourished in the UK and Europe, alongside the related genre of dub poetry.
