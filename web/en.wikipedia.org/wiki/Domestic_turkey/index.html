The domestic turkey (Meleagris gallopavo domesticus) is a large poultry bird descended from the wild turkey of North America, one of two domestications of the species (the other being the South Mexican wild turkey domesticated in Mesoamerica). Turkeys were brought to Europe in the early 16th century and are now raised worldwide for their meat, famously at Thanksgiving and Christmas dinners.

History

Indigenous peoples in what is now Mexico domesticated turkeys by around 800 BC. Spanish colonists took birds to Europe about 1519; from Europe the already-domesticated bird was later reintroduced to eastern North America by English settlers. The name "turkey" reflects early confusion with guineafowl imported via Turkey.

Breeding and production

Modern commercial production centres on the Broad Breasted White, selected for rapid growth, a high proportion of breast meat and white pin feathers. Such birds have grown so large that commercial strains reproduce by artificial insemination. Heritage breeds — Narragansett, Bourbon Red, Standard Bronze and others — retain natural mating and slower growth. The United States is the world's largest turkey producer.

Characteristics

Toms (males) display iridescent plumage, a fleshy snood over the beak and a beard of stiff feathers; both sexes have a wattle (dewlap) and caruncles on the head and neck. Domestic birds are generally too heavy for sustained flight, unlike their wild ancestors.
