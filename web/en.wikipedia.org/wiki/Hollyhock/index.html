Hollyhock

The common hollyhock (Alcea rosea) is an ornamental flowering plant in the mallow family, Malvaceae. Native to Asia and Europe and long cultivated in gardens, it is a classic cottage-garden flower, producing tall spikes of large, showy blooms through the summer.

Description

Hollyhocks are biennials or short-lived perennials, typically grown from seed sown in one year to flower the next. The stout, hairy stems can reach 2.5 metres. Large, rough, palmately lobed leaves form a basal rosette, and flowers — single or double, in colours from white and yellow through pink and red to near-black purple — open progressively up the spike. The fruit is a disc of joined carpels.

Cultivation

Hollyhocks prefer full sun and fertile, well-drained soil, and readily self-seed. They are susceptible to hollyhock rust (Puccinia malvacearum), a fungal disease producing orange-brown pustules on the leaves, which has limited their use in formal planting; resistant strains and fungicidal sprays are used to manage it.

History and uses

The plant was imported into Europe from China by the 15th–16th centuries and was valued in herbal medicine as an emollient. The name "hollyhock" combines "holy" (the plant was associated with crusader-era introductions from the Holy Land) with "hock", an old name for mallow. In Japan, the hollyhock (aoi) was the crest of the Tokugawa shogunate.
