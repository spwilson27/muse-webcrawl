The Taif Agreement (Arabic: اتفاق الطائف), also known as the National Reconciliation Accord (وثيقة الوفاق الوطني), is the agreement that provided the political foundations for ending the Lebanese Civil War and redefining Lebanon's political system. It was negotiated in Taif, Saudi Arabia, in September and October 1989 by the surviving members of Lebanon's 1972 parliament, and signed on 22 October 1989. Constitutional amendments implementing the accord were adopted in 1990. The last major fighting of the civil war ended in October 1990.

Background

By 1989 Lebanon had endured nearly fifteen years of civil war involving sectarian militias, the Palestine Liberation Organization, and Syrian and Israeli interventions. Lebanon's confessional political system — allocating the presidency to a Maronite Christian, the premiership to a Sunni Muslim, and the speakership to a Shia Muslim, with parliamentary seats divided six-to-five in favor of Christians — was widely blamed for entrenching the conflict. An Arab League committee of Saudi Arabia, Algeria, and Morocco sponsored talks among Lebanese deputies in Taif.

Provisions

The agreement rebalanced the confessional system rather than abolishing it. Its principal provisions included:

  • Equal representation: parliamentary seats divided equally between Christians and Muslims (replacing the 6:5 ratio), with proportional allocation among sects within each bloc
  • Shift of executive power: executive authority transferred from the (Maronite) president to the Council of Ministers collectively, making the president more of a head of state while strengthening the (Sunni) prime minister; the (Shia) speaker's term was extended to four years
  • Militia disarmament: all Lebanese and non-Lebanese militias to be disbanded and their weapons surrendered to the state within six months
  • Syrian role: Syria's military presence was legitimized temporarily, with redeployment to the Beqaa Valley within two years and subsequent withdrawal to be negotiated — a provision under which Syrian forces remained in Lebanon until 2005
  • Deconfessionalization as a goal: the agreement declared the abolition of political sectarianism a national objective, to be achieved in stages, but without a binding timetable
  • Affirmation of Lebanon's Arab identity and of "privileged relations" with Syria

Implementation and legacy

Implementation was uneven. Most militias disarmed, but Hezbollah retained its weapons, justified under the accord's framework as a "resistance" against Israeli occupation of the south. The equal-parity parliament and weakened presidency became permanent features of the postwar system, while the promised abolition of sectarianism was never carried out. The Taif system is credited with ending the war but is also criticized for institutionalizing sectarian power-sharing, contributing to the patronage networks and political paralysis that have characterized Lebanon since — debates that intensified after the 2019 financial collapse and protest movement.
