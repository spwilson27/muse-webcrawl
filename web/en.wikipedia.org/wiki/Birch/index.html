A birch is a thin-leaved deciduous hardwood tree of the genus Betula, in the family Betulaceae, which also includes alders, hazels, and hornbeams. Birches are distributed widely across the temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere and are pioneer species, among the first trees to colonize open or disturbed ground after glaciation or fire.

Description

Birches are generally small to medium-sized trees or shrubs. Their bark is distinctive: in species such as the silver birch (Betula pendula) and paper birch (B. papyrifera) it is smooth and white to silvery, peeling in horizontal strips and marked by dark lenticels. Leaves are alternate, doubly serrate, and triangular to oval. Birches are monoecious; male and female catkins appear on the same tree, and the tiny winged seeds are dispersed by wind.

Uses

Birch wood is fine-grained and pale, used for plywood, furniture, flooring, and pulpwood. The bark, being rich in oils and rot-resistant, has traditionally served for canoes, containers, roofing, and writing material (birch bark scrolls); it also yields tar and the sweetener xylitol comes from birch-derived xylan chemistry. Birch sap is drunk as a traditional spring beverage in northern Europe and Asia. Birch pollen is a major cause of seasonal allergic rhinitis.

Ecology

Birches host a wide range of fungi, insects, and birds; their light canopy allows a rich ground flora. They are short-lived compared with oaks but grow rapidly, stabilizing soils and shading out later-successional competitors in early forest succession.
