Birch bark scrolls and the birch tree

The smooth, separable bark of birch trees is the raw material of birch bark scrolls.

The inner bark of the birch tree is the material from which birch bark scrolls are made, and the properties of the tree explain why the scrolls exist at all. Birch bark separates naturally into thin, flexible, paper-like sheets, and its high content of betulin and other oils makes it remarkably resistant to water and decay — scrolls buried in soil for centuries have survived legible.

The material

Among the Ojibwe and other Algonquian peoples of the Great Lakes region, wiigwaas (birch bark) was harvested in spring, when it peels most easily, and could be written on by incising with a bone or metal point or by biting patterns (mazinibaganjigan). The Midewiwin society recorded songs, migration histories and ritual instructions on wiigwaasabakoon — sacred scrolls that could be laid end to end to form charts many feet long, the most famous being the Red Sky migration scroll. Because the scrolls were mnemonic devices for trained keepers, the birch's durability made them a medium of cultural transmission across generations.

Other birch-bark writing

Independent of the North American tradition, birch bark served as a writing material wherever birch grew: medieval Novgorod preserved more than a thousand everyday letters and documents scratched on bark; ancient Gandharan Buddhist texts were written on birch bark scrolls in Central Asia; and Kashmiri scribes used bhūrja bark for Sanskrit manuscripts. In each case the link to the tree is the same — a widely distributed northern species whose bark is nature's ready-made paper, needing no pulping or pressing.
