Birch sap and the birch tree

Sap is tapped from a living birch trunk in early spring.

Birch sap is the xylem sap drawn from a birch tree in early spring, before the leaves open. The physiology of the birch drives the practice: as soil temperatures rise, root pressure pushes stored sugars and minerals up the trunk to fuel bud break, and for a window of two to three weeks a healthy tree will yield several litres of sap per day through a small drilled hole without lasting harm, provided the hole is plugged afterwards.

Composition and uses

Fresh birch sap is a clear, slightly sweet liquid containing roughly 0.5–2 percent sugars (mainly glucose and fructose, unlike the sucrose-rich sap of maples), along with potassium, calcium, manganese and amino acids. It spoils within days unless refrigerated, so it is traditionally drunk fresh as a spring tonic in Russia, Ukraine, the Baltic states, Scandinavia and northern China. Fermented, it becomes birch wine or beer; boiled down, it yields a dark, tangy birch syrup, though around 100 litres of sap are needed per litre of syrup. Xylitol, the tooth-friendly sweetener, was first manufactured commercially from birch wood and bark.

The tree's perspective

Tapping is one of several long-standing human uses of birch that do not require felling the tree, alongside harvesting bark for scrolls and containers. Sustainable practice limits taps per tree and rotates tapping between seasons, since repeated heavy tapping can stress the tree and open wounds to fungal infection — a consideration that links sap production back to the health of birch stands in northern forests.
