Shadow banking in China

Shadow banking in China refers to financial intermediation conducted outside the formal, regulated banking system of the People's Republic of China. China's shadow banking sector grew rapidly after the 2008 financial crisis and the government's stimulus programme, becoming one of the largest in the world. It developed largely as a response to formal credit controls, interest-rate regulation and loan quotas that restricted bank lending to sectors such as real estate and local government financing vehicles.

Components

  • Wealth management products (WMPs) sold by banks off balance sheet, offering higher yields than deposit rates and investing in loans and bonds.
  • Trust companies and trust loans, a major channel of credit to property developers and local governments.
  • Entrusted loans between companies, arranged through banks.
  • Bankers' acceptances, microcredit companies, pawnshops, guarantee companies and informal private lending (for example in Wenzhou).
  • Later, peer-to-peer lending platforms, which grew rapidly before a regulatory crackdown and near-total shutdown around 2020.

Risks and regulation

Regulators and the IMF have highlighted risks from maturity mismatch, implicit guarantees on WMPs, interconnectedness with the formal banking system, and concentrated exposure to real estate. From 2017 the Chinese authorities pursued a deleveraging campaign: the China Banking and Insurance Regulatory Commission tightened rules, unified asset-management regulations in 2018 sought to break implicit guarantees and limit multi-layer channel business, and the shadow banking sector contracted as a share of GDP, even as concerns shifted toward property-sector and local-government debt.
