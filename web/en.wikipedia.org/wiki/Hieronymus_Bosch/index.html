Hieronymus Bosch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Garden of Earthly Delights (c. 1490–1510), centre panel: a fantastical landscape of nude figures, giant fruit and hybrid creatures around a blue fountain]

Jheronimus van Aken (c. 1450 – 9 August 1516), known as Hieronymus Bosch, was a Dutch/Netherlandish painter from 's-Hertogenbosch in the Duchy of Brabant. Famed for fantastic imagery, detailed landscapes and illustrations of religious concepts and narratives, he populated his panels with demons, hybrid creatures and surreal machines, and remains one of the most imitated and least fully understood artists of the Northern Renaissance.

His most famous work is the triptych The Garden of Earthly Delights (c. 1490–1510), now in the Prado, whose open wings show Eden, a teeming earthly garden of pleasures, and a musical Hell. Other major works include The Haywain, The Temptation of St Anthony in Lisbon, The Ship of Fools and The Last Judgment.

Life and milieu

Almost nothing certain is known of Bosch's life: he was born into a family of painters, joined the prestigious Brotherhood of Our Lady in 's-Hertogenbosch, married a wealthy wife, and was buried there in 1516 with a funeral mass sung by the Brotherhood. No writings or diaries survive, and few of the roughly 25 paintings now attributed to him are signed or firmly dated.

Interpretation and legacy

Bosch's imagery has been read as orthodox medieval moralising on sin and damnation, as heretical Adamite doctrine, and as alchemical or astrological allegory; modern scholarship mostly places him within the devout, bookish culture of the late-medieval Netherlands. Philip II of Spain collected his works avidly, which is why so many are in Madrid. In the twentieth century the Surrealists claimed him as a forerunner, and his crowded, dreamlike worlds have never left popular culture.
