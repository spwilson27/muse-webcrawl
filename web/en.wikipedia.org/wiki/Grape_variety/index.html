Grape variety

Thousands of grape varieties are cultivated for wine, table fruit and drying.

A grape variety is a cultivated type of grapevine, propagated vegetatively (usually by cuttings) so that all vines of a variety are genetically identical clones descending from a single original seedling. Most wine and table grapes belong to the species Vitis vinifera, native to the Mediterranean and Central Asia, though other species such as Vitis labrusca and hybrids are also grown.

Scale and identification

An estimated 5,000–10,000 varieties of V. vinifera exist, of which several hundred are commercially significant. The discipline of identifying and describing varieties is called ampelography, traditionally based on leaf and berry morphology; since the 1990s DNA profiling has revolutionised the field, revealing parentages (for example, that Cabernet Sauvignon is an offspring of Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc) and synonymies.

Major wine varieties

Widely planted "international" wine varieties include Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Chardonnay, Syrah (Shiraz), Pinot Noir, Sauvignon Blanc and Riesling. Many regions prize indigenous varieties such as Nebbiolo and Sangiovese in Italy, Tempranillo in Spain, and Malbec in Argentina. Table-grape breeding has produced popular seedless varieties like Thompson Seedless (Sultana) and Crimson Seedless, while Concord and Muscadine grapes dominate much North American juice and table production.

Clones and mutations

Within a variety, accumulated mutations give rise to recognised clones with subtly different traits, and berry-colour mutations can create distinct varieties — Pinot Noir, Pinot Gris and Pinot Blanc are colour mutations of one another.
