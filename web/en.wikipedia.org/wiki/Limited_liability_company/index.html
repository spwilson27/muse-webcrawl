Limited liability company

[Image: a small business owner unlocking the door of her shop in the morning]

A limited liability company (LLC) is a business structure, most developed in the United States, that combines the limited liability of a Corporation with the pass-through taxation and flexible management of a Partnership. Owners are called members; the company is a separate legal person, so members' personal assets are generally protected from business debts and lawsuits.

Features and spread

LLCs are formed by filing articles of organization with a state (Wyoming first, in 1977; the form spread nationwide by the 1990s) and governed by an operating agreement that can allocate profits and control flexibly, without corporate formalities such as boards and annual meetings. For US federal tax purposes an LLC may elect treatment as a partnership, sole proprietorship or corporation. The form became the most popular choice for new small businesses in the US and inspired analogues elsewhere (the UK limited liability partnership, the German UG), though rules differ: 'LLC' status is not automatically recognised abroad, and some industries (banks, insurers) are excluded in many states.
