The internal conflict in Peru was an armed conflict between the Government of Peru and insurgent groups, principally the Maoist Shining Path (Sendero Luminoso) and the Túpac Amaru Revolutionary Movement (MRTA), fought mainly between 1980 and 2000. Peru's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2003) estimated that nearly 69,280 people died or disappeared, attributing the largest share of deaths to the Shining Path, with state forces and other actors also responsible for serious abuses.

Background and course

The Shining Path, led by former philosophy professor Abimael Guzmán, launched its "people's war" in May 1980 in Ayacucho, attacking rural communities, infrastructure, and state officials. Violence escalated under the governments of Fernando Belaúnde and Alan García, amid counterinsurgency campaigns marked by massacres and disappearances. The MRTA began operations in 1984 and gained notoriety for the 1996–97 Japanese embassy hostage crisis in Lima.

Decline

The capture of Guzmán in September 1992 by police intelligence (GEIN) severely weakened the Shining Path. Under President Alberto Fujimori the state combined intelligence-led arrests, anti-terrorism legislation, and armed peasant patrols (rondas campesinas). By 2000 the insurgencies were largely defeated, though remnants of the Shining Path persist in the VRAEM coca-growing region, intertwined with drug trafficking.

Legacy

The conflict disproportionately affected poor, Quechua-speaking rural communities. Its legacy includes the Truth Commission's findings, trials of Fujimori and Guzmán, reparations programmes, and continuing debates over memory and human rights in Peru.
