Zellij (Arabic: زليج‎; also zellige, zelij or zillij) is a form of mosaic tilework made from individually hand-chiselled pieces of glazed terracotta set into plaster. It is a defining element of Moroccan and broader Maghrebi and Moorish architecture, covering fountains, walls, floors and tombs with intricate geometric patterns in brilliant colour.

Technique

Craftsmen (maâlems) first fire large glazed tiles in colours such as cobalt blue, turquoise, green, yellow, black and white. Each small piece is then cut by hand with a sharp hammer into precise shapes — stars, crosses, diamonds and polygons — following geometric designs worked out by master artisans. The pieces are laid face-down in a frame according to the pattern, and plaster or mortar is poured over the back; when set, the panel is turned over and installed. The slight irregularities of the hand-cut edges make the surface shimmer as light moves across it.

History

Zellij developed in the Maghreb and al-Andalus from earlier Byzantine and Islamic mosaic traditions, reaching maturity under the Marinid, Nasrid and Saadian dynasties between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries. Masterpieces survive in the Alhambra of Granada, the Bou Inania Madrasa and Attarine Madrasa in Fez, and the Saadian Tombs in Marrakesh. Fez remains the craft's capital, where workshops still produce tiles by traditional methods for mosques, palaces, hotels and private courtyards worldwide.

Design and symbolism

Patterns are generated from overlapping circles and radiating stars — eight-pointed stars (khatam), twelve- and sixteen-pointed stars, and complex strapwork — embodying the Islamic preference for geometric order and infinity in sacred decoration, avoiding figural imagery in religious settings. The mathematics underlying zellij, with its girih-like tilings, has attracted modern scholarly attention as an early application of sophisticated geometry in art.
