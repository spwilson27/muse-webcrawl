Edge-transitive graph

In the mathematical field of graph theory, an edge-transitive graph is a graph G such that, given any two edges e1 and e2 of G, there is an automorphism of G that maps e1 to e2. In other words, a graph is edge-transitive if its automorphism group acts transitively on its edges.

Properties

Every edge-transitive graph that is not vertex-transitive must be a bipartite graph: its vertices fall into two orbits under the automorphism group, and every edge joins a vertex from one orbit to a vertex from the other, so the graph is biregular — vertices in each part all have the same degree, though the two degrees may differ. A connected graph that is both vertex-transitive and edge-transitive but not symmetric is called half-transitive; the Holt graph is the smallest example.

Examples

  • Complete graphs Kn and complete bipartite graphs Km,n.
  • The Petersen graph, which is edge-transitive and vertex-transitive.
  • Stars K1,n, which are edge-transitive but not vertex-transitive.
  • The utility graph K3,3 and the edges of the Platonic solids' skeletons where applicable.
  • Every symmetric graph (arc-transitive graph) is edge-transitive.
