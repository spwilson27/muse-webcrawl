Archaeological survey

Archaeological survey or archaeological field survey is the method by which archaeologists search for archaeological sites and collect information about the location, distribution and organisation of past human cultures across a large area (for example, typically in excess of one hectare, and often in excess of many square kilometres). Survey is usually a preliminary, non-destructive stage of research, preceding excavation.

Methods

The classic method is fieldwalking: teams of surveyors walk systematically spaced transects across ploughed fields or terrain, recording the locations of artefacts visible on the surface, often with GPS. Finds densities are plotted to identify sites and activity areas. Other sources of evidence are integrated, including aerial photography, historic maps, place names, and local knowledge.

Remote sensing and geophysics

Modern surveys make extensive use of remote sensing: satellite imagery, lidar (which can reveal structures beneath forest canopy), and geophysical techniques such as magnetometry, ground-penetrating radar and resistivity, which detect buried features without digging. In coastal and underwater contexts, survey may employ sonar and diver reconnaissance.

Purposes

Surveys are undertaken for academic questions about settlement patterns and landscapes, and in cultural resource management, where developers and planners must identify and evaluate sites before construction. Because survey samples the surface, its results depend on visibility, ground cover and the preservation of material; surface scatters are interpreted with caution, as ploughing and erosion can move artefacts from their original contexts.
