The Ridda Wars (Arabic: حروب الردة, "Wars of Apostasy") were a series of military campaigns launched by the first caliph, Abu Bakr, in 632–633 CE against rebellious Arab tribes that rejected the authority of Medina following the death of the Prophet Muhammad. Their success reunified the Arabian Peninsula under the Rashidun Caliphate and made possible the subsequent Muslim conquests beyond Arabia.

Background

When Muhammad died in June 632, many tribes considered their allegiance — often sealed by personal agreements with him — to have lapsed. Some withheld the zakat tax; others rallied around rival prophetic claimants such as Musaylima in the Yamama, Tulayha ibn Khuwaylid, Sajah, and al-Aswad al-Ansi in Yemen. Abu Bakr, elected caliph at Medina, insisted that submission to Islam entailed continued payment of zakat and obedience to the caliphate, declaring he would fight those who separated prayer from almsgiving.

Campaigns

After defeating a probing attack on Medina itself, Abu Bakr dispatched eleven columns across Arabia. His ablest general, Khalid ibn al-Walid, defeated Tulayha at the Battle of Buzakha and the Banu Hanifa and Musaylima at the hard-fought Battle of Yamama (December 632), where many Qur'an reciters were killed — a loss that, tradition holds, prompted the first compilation of the Qur'an. Other armies subdued Bahrain, Oman, Yemen, and the Hadhramaut; the last major resistance, at Dibba and in the Mahra, was crushed by early 633.

Consequences

By 633 Arabia was unified under a single state for the first time. The wars seasoned the armies and commanders — above all Khalid — who immediately turned outward, beginning the conquests of Iraq and Syria that would transform the Near East.
