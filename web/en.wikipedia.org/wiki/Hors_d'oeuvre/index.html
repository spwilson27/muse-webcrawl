Hors d'oeuvre

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An hors d'oeuvre (French, literally "outside the work", i.e. outside the main course) is a small dish served before a meal in European cuisine. Some hors d'oeuvres are served cold, others hot. They may be served at the dinner table as part of the meal, or they may be served before seating, such as at a reception or cocktail party.

Terminology

Formerly, hors d'oeuvres were also known as appetisers or starters in British English usage, while in American English "appetizer" is the common term for a first course. Small one-bite items passed at receptions are often called canapés when served on a base of bread, toast, or pastry, and "finger food" more generally.

Types

Classic French hors d'oeuvres include crudités with dip, pâtés and terrines, oysters, smoked salmon, caviar, stuffed eggs, and vol-au-vents. Hors d'oeuvre assortments, served on a platter (hors d'oeuvres variés), were a standard opening course in formal French dining and were influenced by the Russian tradition of zakuski, which inspired Auguste Escoffier and other chefs of the 19th century.

Similar traditions

Many cuisines have an equivalent custom: Italian antipasti, Spanish tapas, Middle Eastern and Greek mezze, Chinese dim sum served before or as a meal, and the Scandinavian smörgåsbord all occupy a comparable place in the meal.
