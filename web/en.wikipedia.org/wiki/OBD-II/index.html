OBD-II

On-board diagnostics II (OBD-II) is a standardised vehicle self-diagnostic system that provides access to engine and emissions data through a universal 16-pin connector. Required on all cars and light trucks sold in the United States from the 1996 model year, and later adopted in similar forms in Europe (EOBD) and Japan (JOBD), OBD-II allows owners, mechanics, and emissions inspectors to read diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs) and monitor real-time sensor data.

Introduced1996 (United States)
Connector16-pin SAE J1962
ProtocolsCAN, ISO 9141, KWP2000, SAE J1850

History

The first generation of on-board diagnostics (OBD-I) was mandated in California in 1988 but used manufacturer-specific connectors and codes. The California Air Resources Board and the Society of Automotive Engineers developed OBD-II to standardise the connector, code definitions, and communication protocols, with emissions monitoring as the primary goal. The system continuously monitors components such as the catalytic converter, oxygen sensors, evaporative emissions system, and engine misfires, illuminating the "check engine" light when a fault is detected.

Usage

A scan tool plugged into the diagnostic port — typically located under the dashboard near the steering column — can retrieve stored and pending trouble codes, freeze-frame data, and live parameters such as engine speed, coolant temperature, and fuel trim. Consumer Bluetooth and Wi-Fi adapters have made OBD-II data available through smartphone apps, and insurers and fleet operators use the port for telematics and usage-based insurance.
