Cayley–Hamilton theorem

In linear algebra, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem (named after the mathematicians Arthur Cayley and William Rowan Hamilton) states that every square matrix over a commutative ring (such as the real or complex numbers) satisfies its own characteristic equation.

Statement

If A is a given n × n matrix and In is the n × n identity matrix, then the characteristic polynomial of A is defined as pA(λ) = det(λIn − A). The Cayley–Hamilton theorem states that substituting the matrix A for the variable λ in this polynomial gives the zero matrix:

pA(A) = 0.

Consequences

An important consequence is that the powers An, An+1, … can each be expressed as a linear combination of the lower powers I, A, …, An−1. This gives a practical method for computing high powers of a matrix and, when the constant term of the polynomial is nonzero, for expressing the inverse of A as a polynomial in A of degree at most n − 1. The theorem also implies that the minimal polynomial of a matrix divides its characteristic polynomial.

History and proof

Cayley stated the result in 1858 for 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 matrices, remarking that he had verified the general case without a complete proof; Hamilton had proved a related result for linear functions of quaternions in 1853. The first general proof was published by Frobenius in 1878. Modern proofs proceed by density of diagonalizable matrices over the complex numbers, or by direct algebraic arguments using the adjugate matrix — though the tempting "proof" that simply substitutes λ = A into the determinant is invalid.
