5-HT receptor

A serotonin (5-HT) receptor, a target of many psychiatric and migraine drugs.

5-HT receptors, or serotonin receptors, are a group of receptors for the neurotransmitter and hormone serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) found in the central and peripheral nervous systems and in many other tissues. They mediate both excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission and modulate the release of many other neurotransmitters, including glutamate, GABA, dopamine, and norepinephrine.

Classification

Serotonin receptors are divided into seven families, 5-HT1 through 5-HT7, comprising at least 14 distinct subtypes in humans. All but one are G protein-coupled receptors (metabotropic) with the classic seven-transmembrane structure; the exception, the 5-HT3 receptor, is a ligand-gated ion channel permeable to sodium, potassium, and calcium. The families signal through different pathways: 5-HT1 and 5-HT5 receptors generally inhibit adenylyl cyclase via Gi/o; 5-HT2 receptors act through Gq/11 and phospholipase C; 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 stimulate adenylyl cyclase via Gs.

Physiological roles and pharmacology

Through these receptors serotonin influences mood, anxiety, sleep, appetite, cognition, thermoregulation, gastrointestinal motility, platelet function, and vascular tone. They are therefore targets of many drug classes: triptans activate 5-HT1B/1D receptors in migraine treatment; SSRIs raise serotonin availability indirectly; atypical antipsychotics antagonize 5-HT2A receptors; 5-HT3 antagonists such as ondansetron are antiemetics; and 5-HT2B agonism is associated with the heart-valve damage that withdrew the appetite suppressant fenfluramine.
