Aether theories

Aether theories (also ether theories) in physics propose the existence of a medium, the aether, a space-filling substance thought necessary as a transmission medium for phenomena such as light and gravity. In 19th-century physics, the luminiferous aether was the postulated medium through which light waves propagated, much as sound waves travel through air.

Historical development

The idea descends from the classical element aether, the substance of the celestial spheres. Descartes, Huygens, and Newton all invoked forms of aether in their theories. In the 19th century, physicists including Fresnel, Kelvin, and Maxwell developed detailed mechanical models of the aether; Maxwell's electromagnetic theory was originally framed as describing stresses in such a medium.

Decline

The Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887 failed to detect any "aether wind" from Earth's motion through the medium. Attempts to save the theory, such as the FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction, were superseded by Einstein's special relativity (1905), which made the aether superfluous: electromagnetic waves require no material medium, and the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames. Modern physics uses the concept of fields in vacuum instead, though Einstein later remarked that general relativity's spacetime shares some properties with an aether without being a mechanical medium.
