Belt and Road Initiative

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Belt and Road Initiative
Announced: 2013, by Xi Jinping
Scope: Infrastructure and investment across Asia, Africa, Europe and beyond; 140+ participating countries

The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) is a global infrastructure and investment strategy adopted by the government of China in 2013, reviving the imagery of the ancient Silk Road: a "Silk Road Economic Belt" overland across Eurasia and a "Maritime Silk Road" through the Indian Ocean to Europe. It is the signature foreign-policy project of Xi Jinping and the largest infrastructure programme in history by ambition.

Scope and finance

More than 140 countries have signed cooperation documents. Chinese policy banks and state firms have financed and built ports (Gwadar in Pakistan, Piraeus in Greece, Hambantota in Sri Lanka), railways (China–Laos, Jakarta–Bandung high-speed), highways, pipelines, power plants and industrial parks across the developing world, with cumulative engagement estimated above a trillion dollars. It complements China's own build-out described under economy of China, and draws on the development model of Deng Xiaoping's opening projected outward.

Reception and criticism

Supporters credit the BRI with filling a vast infrastructure gap Western institutions neglected. Critics cite debt burdens (the "debt-trap diplomacy" charge — contested by researchers, who find hard bargaining rather than a single trap), opaque contracts, labour and environmental standards, and strategic dual-use of ports. Western responses include the G7's Partnership for Global Infrastructure. A leaner, greener "small and beautiful" BRI has been Beijing's watchword since the early 2020s.
