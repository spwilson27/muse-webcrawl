Penrose on a Penrose-tiled floor.

Sir Roger Penrose (born 8 August 1931) is a British mathematician, mathematical physicist and philosopher of science. He shared the 1988 Wolf Prize with Stephen Hawking for the Penrose–Hawking singularity theorems and received half of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics 'for the discovery that black hole formation is a robust prediction of the general theory of relativity'.

Penrose's 1965 singularity theorem — the first of its kind — proved that gravitational collapse to a singularity is inevitable once a trapped surface forms, using global topological methods he introduced to relativity. His other creations include Penrose diagrams, spin networks (a seed of Loop quantum gravity), twistor theory, the aperiodic Penrose tiling (with its quasicrystal echoes), and the Penrose triangle impossible object (also drawn by his father Lionel and by Escher). His books The Emperor's New Mind (1989) and The Road to Reality (2004) argue controversially that consciousness involves non-computable physics.

Work

Penrose's career spans Cambridge (PhD 1958), and posts at Birkbeck, Yeshiva and (from 1973) the Rouse Ball chair at Oxford. In cosmology he proposed the cosmic censorship hypothesis and, later, conformal cyclic cosmology — an aeon-spanning cyclic model. His 2020 Nobel recognised the theorem that made black holes a rigorous consequence of General relativity, not an artefact of symmetric idealisations.
