Radial engine

The radial engine is a reciprocating type internal combustion engine configuration in which the cylinders "radiate" outward from a central crankcase like the spokes of a wheel. The radial configuration was commonly used for aircraft engines before gas turbine engines became predominant.

Configuration: Cylinders arranged radially around a central crankshaft
Typical cylinder count: 3–9 per row (odd numbers)
Cooling: Usually air-cooled
Era of dominance: 1920s–1940s aircraft

Design

Because the cylinders are arranged in a circle, a radial engine has a short, stiff crankshaft with a single crankpin to which all pistons connect through a master-and-articulating-rod assembly. A single-row radial always has an odd number of cylinders so that a consistent every-other-cylinder firing order can be maintained. Large engines used multiple rows — the Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major had 28 cylinders in four rows of seven and produced around 3,500 hp, among the most powerful piston aircraft engines ever built.

Advantages and disadvantages

Radials are air-cooled, eliminating the weight and vulnerability of a liquid cooling system; their flat, circular shape suits air cooling, and battle damage to one cylinder need not disable the engine. Against this, the large frontal area creates drag, the bottom cylinders can collect oil when parked (risking hydraulic lock), and very large radials demand complex gearing and maintenance. Famous types include the Wright Cyclone, Pratt & Whitney Wasp series, and the Bristol Hercules sleeve-valve engine.

History

C. M. Manly built a five-cylinder water-cooled radial for Langley's Aerodrome in 1901, and the type flourished after the First World War in aircraft such as the DC-3, B-17 Flying Fortress and F4U Corsair. Gas turbines displaced radials in front-line service after the 1940s, but radials remain in use in agricultural aircraft, warbird restorations and vintage aviation.
