Old High German

The Hildebrandslied, written c. 830, is the oldest surviving German literary text.

Old High German (OHG; German: Althochdeutsch) is the earliest stage of the German language, conventionally covering the period from around 750 to 1050 AD. It followed the High German consonant shift, which distinguishes High German dialects from Low German and the other West Germanic languages. Old High German was not a single uniform language but a group of dialects — including Old Franconian, Alemannic, and Bavarian — with no standard written form.

Characteristics

The most significant feature of Old High German is the completion of the High German consonant shift (Second Consonant Shift), in which the voiceless stops /p t k/ became fricatives or affricates (e.g. English apple beside German Apfel; make beside machen). Old High German retained a rich inflectional system: nouns had four cases and three genders, and verbs distinguished strong and weak conjugations. The later period saw the beginnings of i-umlaut as a phonemic process.

Literature

Nearly all Old High German texts are religious in nature, produced in monasteries such as Fulda, St. Gall, and Reichenau. Notable works include the Hildebrandslied, a heroic lay and the only survivor of its kind; Otfrid of Weissenburg's Evangelienbuch, a rhymed Gospel harmony; the Abrogans, a Latin–Old High German glossary and the earliest German dictionary; and the works of Notker Labeo of St. Gall, who translated Latin classics. Secular survivals include the Merseburg Incantations, two pagan charms preserved in a theological manuscript.

Transition

Around 1050, the reduction of unstressed vowels to schwa marks the transition to Middle High German. Because manuscripts ceased to be produced in the same monastic traditions, the boundary is partly conventional.
