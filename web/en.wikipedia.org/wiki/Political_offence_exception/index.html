Political offence exception

[Image: Nineteenth-century cartoon of political exiles in London, whose presence prompted Britain's early extradition debates]

The political offence exception is a rule of extradition law under which a requested state may — or must — refuse to surrender a person for an offence that is of a political character. It is a near-universal feature of extradition treaties and statutes, reflecting the idea that states should not assist in the prosecution of political dissent and that political offenders deserve a measure of sympathy or at least neutrality.

Origins

The doctrine emerged in the nineteenth century, after the revolutionary upheavals in Europe: Belgium's Extradition Act of 1833 is commonly credited as the first statutory adoption, and the Franco-Belgian treaty of 1834 among the first treaty provisions. Liberal states such as Britain and France, which sheltered political exiles, insisted on the exception even while agreeing to extradite ordinary criminals. An early qualification, the attentat clause introduced by Belgium in 1856 after an attempt on Napoleon III, excluded attempts on the life of a head of state from protection.

Scope and tests

Legal systems distinguish "pure" political offences — treason, sedition, espionage, offences against the state with no ordinary-crime element — from "relative" political offences, ordinary crimes committed in connection with political activity. Anglophone courts developed an "incidence" test, asking whether the act was committed in the course of and incidental to a political uprising (the approach of the English case In re Castioni, 1891). Swiss law applies a predominance test weighing the political motive and character of the act against the common crime. The exception works alongside the requirement of double criminality and bars on extradition for military and capital offences in many treaties.

Narrowing in modern law

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century terrorism led states to carve offences out of the exception. The European Convention on the Suppression of Terrorism (1977) listed offences that contracting states agreed not to regard as political; subsequent anti-terrorism treaties, and the European Arrest Warrant system within the EU, have reduced the exception's practical scope among allied states. Courts and commentators continue to debate where protest, insurgency and political violence fall, and asylum law now performs part of the protective function the exception once carried alone.
