Aristocracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aristocracy is a form of government in which power is held by a small, privileged class — in the word's Greek origin (aristokratia), the rule of the best. In Aristotle's classification aristocracy is the virtuous form of rule by the few (its corruption being oligarchy, rule by the wealthy few in their own interest). Historically aristocracies have been hereditary nobilities defined by birth, land and legal privilege — the feudal nobility of Europe, the Venetian patriciate, Sparta's homoioi — ruling through councils, senates, courts and military command.

From the eighteenth century aristocratic government gave way across Europe to constitutional monarchy, democracy and meritocratic administration, though aristocrats retained political weight (Britain's House of Lords remained predominantly hereditary until the reforms of 1999 limited hereditary peers to 92) and aristocracy survived as a social class and a cultural idea. The term is also used loosely for any entrenched elite (“an aristocracy of wealth”, the “labour aristocracy” in Marxist debate), and Jefferson's distinction between a “natural aristocracy” of talent and virtue and an “artificial” one of birth framed the democratic answer to aristocratic theory.
