Social control

Social control is the process by which a society regulates the behavior of its members to maintain social order and conformity to norms. This regulation can be achieved through formal mechanisms such as laws and enforcement agencies, or through informal means such as socialization, public opinion, and community sanctions.

Types of social control

Formal social control

Formal social control is carried out by authorized agents such as police officers, employers, military officers, and school administrators. It includes laws, regulations, and institutional rules backed by coercive sanctions, from fines to imprisonment.

Informal social control

Informal social control is exercised by a society without explicitly stating or writing rules — through customs, mores, norms, facial expressions, body language, ridicule, sarcasm, criticism, and shunning. When internalized during socialization, values become part of an individual's personality, a process sociologists call internal social control.

History of the concept

The concept was formalized in early sociology; Edward A. Ross published Social Control: A Survey of the Foundations of Order in 1901. Later theorists, including Talcott Parsons and Travis Hirschi, developed the idea: Hirschi's social bond theory (1969) proposed that attachment, commitment, involvement, and belief restrain delinquency. Michel Foucault's work on discipline and surveillance shifted attention to diffuse, institutional forms of control.

Criticism

Critics argue that theories of social control can legitimate repression and pathologize nonconformity, and that the concept is often used selectively against marginalized groups.
