Conjecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A chalkboard covered with mathematical notation and a large question mark]

In mathematics, a conjecture is a statement believed to be true — usually supported by examples, patterns or partial results — but not yet proved. Conjectures organise research: proving one can settle a whole area, and refuting one by counterexample can be just as valuable.

Famous examples include the continuum hypothesis (later shown undecidable, so neither provable nor refutable), Goldbach's conjecture that every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes, and the Collatz conjecture, whose simple statement hides a proof that has resisted all attempts.

Fate of conjectures

Some conjectures stand for centuries: Fermat's Last Theorem was a marginal note of about 1637 that became a theorem only in 1995 with Andrew Wiles's proof. Others fall quickly to a single counterexample or a computer search.

The Millennium Prize Problems, announced by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000 with a million-dollar prize each, are mostly conjectures or conjecture-like questions; only the Poincaré conjecture has been solved so far.
