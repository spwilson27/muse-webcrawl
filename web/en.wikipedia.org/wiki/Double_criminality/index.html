Double criminality

[Image: An extradition hearing in a magistrates' court, where the double criminality of the alleged conduct is examined]

Double criminality (also dual criminality) is a principle of extradition and mutual legal assistance under which a person may be surrendered by one state to another only if the conduct alleged against them would constitute a criminal offence in both states. The requirement protects individuals against extradition for acts — such as political dissent, blasphemy or tax and currency offences of a kind unknown in the requested state — that the requested state does not regard as criminal.

Operation

Double criminality is a standard condition in bilateral extradition treaties, model treaties and national statutes such as the United Kingdom's Extradition Act 2003 and the US federal extradition statute. Courts generally apply a "conduct test": they examine the facts alleged, rather than the label or elements of the foreign offence, and ask whether equivalent conduct would be criminal under their own law, frequently with a minimum penalty threshold (commonly twelve months' imprisonment). Historically, some treaties instead listed specific extraditable offences, an approach largely superseded by the conduct-and-threshold method.

Rationale and limits

The principle expresses reciprocity and comity between states, and the liberal objection to punishing conduct that is lawful where it was done. It interacts with the political offence exception, the rule of speciality (the surrendered person may be tried only for the offences for which extradition was granted) and human-rights bars on surrender. Critics note that double criminality can shelter financial and cyber offenders where legal definitions diverge, and that informal transfer mechanisms — deportation and extraordinary rendition — have sometimes been used to circumvent it.

Abolition within the European Union

The European Arrest Warrant framework largely dispensed with double criminality among EU member states for a list of 32 categories of serious offence (including terrorism, human trafficking and murder), relying instead on mutual recognition of judicial decisions. The change was controversial, and the Court of Justice has qualified it where fundamental rights are engaged.
