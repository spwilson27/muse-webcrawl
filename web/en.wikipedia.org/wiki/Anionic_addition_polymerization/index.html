Anionic addition polymerization

Anionic addition polymerization is a form of chain-growth polymerization in which an anionic initiator transfers an electron (or a negative charge) to a monomer, which then becomes reactive and grows by successively adding more monomers. The active centre at the growing chain end is a carbanion. The technique is most famous as the classic example of a living polymerization.

History

Anionic polymerization was explored as early as 1910 with the polymerization of dienes by alkali metals. In 1956, Michael Szwarc demonstrated the "living" character of the anionic polymerization of styrene initiated by sodium naphthalenide: in the absence of impurities, the chain ends remain active indefinitely, producing polymers of predictable molecular weight and very narrow molecular-weight distribution.

Mechanism

Initiation may occur by electron transfer from an aromatic radical anion (e.g. sodium naphthalenide) or by nucleophilic attack of a strong base such as butyllithium on the monomer. Propagation proceeds by addition of the carbanion to the double bond of further monomer units. Because there is no inherent termination step, chains stop growing only when the monomer is exhausted or when the reaction is deliberately quenched, for example with water or carbon dioxide — the latter route giving carboxyl-terminated polymers.

Applications

Living anionic polymerization is used industrially to make block copolymers such as styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS) thermoplastic elastomers, and to prepare model polymers with precisely controlled architectures for research.
