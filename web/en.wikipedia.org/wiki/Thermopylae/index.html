Thermopylae

Thermopylae

Greek: Θερμοπύλαι ("hot gates")

Location: Phthiotis, Central Greece, near Lamia

Named for: Its hot sulphur springs

Famous for: Battle of Thermopylae, 480 BC

Thermopylae (Ancient Greek: Θερμοπύλαι, "hot gates") is a narrow coastal pass in Phthiotis, Greece, between the mountains and the Malian Gulf, named for the hot sulphur springs found there, which in Greek mythology were associated with Heracles and with an entrance to Hades. Because it controlled the main route between Thessaly and southern Greece, the pass was the site of several battles in antiquity and later history.

The battle of 480 BC

Thermopylae is best known for the battle of August 480 BC during the second Persian invasion of Greece, when a Greek force led by King Leonidas I of Sparta — famously including his 300 Spartans, together with Thespians, Thebans and other allies — held the pass for three days against the vastly larger army of Xerxes I. Betrayed by Ephialtes, who showed the Persians a mountain path around the position, Leonidas dismissed most of the army and fought to the death with a rearguard. The epitaph by Simonides — "Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie" — made the stand a byword for heroic sacrifice.

Later battles and the modern pass

The pass was fought over again in 279 BC against the invading Gauls under Brennus, in 191 BC (when Rome defeated the Seleucid Antiochus III there), and in April 1941, when Commonwealth forces delayed the German advance during the Second World War. Sediment deposited by the Spercheios River has since pushed the shoreline several kilometres seaward, so the modern coastline no longer resembles the narrow defile of 480 BC. A monument to Leonidas, erected in 1955, and a newer monument to the 700 Thespians stand at the site, near the national road between Athens and Thessaloniki.
