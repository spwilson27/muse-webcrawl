Authority is the legitimate or socially approved power which one person or group possesses and exercises over another. It is a central concept in sociology, political science and philosophy. Although often paired with power, authority is distinguished from coercion: authority rests on the recognition by those subject to it that its exercise is rightful, whereas mere power may rest on force alone.

Weber's typology

Max Weber analysed authority (in his terminology, legitimate domination, Herrschaft) under three ideal types. Traditional authority derives from long-established customs and the sanctity of tradition, as in hereditary monarchy or patriarchy. Charismatic authority rests on devotion to the exceptional qualities of an individual leader. Rational-legal authority rests on belief in the legality of enacted rules and the right of officeholders to issue commands under them; it is the characteristic form of the modern bureaucratic state.

Authority in political philosophy

Philosophers debate whether and when political authority generates a genuine obligation to obey. Social contract theorists ground authority in consent; critics such as philosophical anarchists argue that no state possesses the authority it claims. In moral and epistemic contexts, "authority" can also denote a person or text regarded as a reliable source of knowledge — an expert — whose testimony gives others reason to believe.

Authority and institutions

Institutional authority is codified in constitutions, statutes and organisational rules, which define jurisdictions, procedures and limits. Authority may be delegated, shared (as in federal systems), or contested; its stability depends on continuing acceptance by the governed, and its abuse gives rise to concepts such as authoritarianism and the abuse of power.
