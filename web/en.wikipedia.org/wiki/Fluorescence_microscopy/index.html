Fluorescence microscopy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fluorescence microscopy is a form of optical microscopy that uses fluorescence instead of, or in addition to, scattering and reflection, to study the properties of organic or inorganic substances. A fluorescence microscope illuminates the specimen with light of a specific wavelength that excites fluorescent molecules (fluorophores); the emitted light, at a longer wavelength, is separated from the illumination using excitation and emission filters (or a dichroic beamsplitter in epifluorescence designs) and forms an image.

Techniques

Most modern instruments are epifluorescence microscopes, in which excitation light passes through the objective lens. Specialised variants include confocal laser scanning microscopy, which rejects out-of-focus light for optical sectioning; two-photon excitation microscopy for deep-tissue imaging; total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy for surface-selective imaging; and super-resolution methods such as STED, PALM and STORM, which surpass the diffraction limit.

Labelling

Specimens may fluoresce naturally (autofluorescence) or be labelled: with fluorescent dyes such as DAPI for DNA, with antibodies conjugated to fluorophores (immunofluorescence), or genetically with fluorescent proteins — the green fluorescent protein (GFP) and its many coloured derivatives, whose development was recognised by the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Fluorescence microscopy is fundamental in cell biology, microbiology, neuroscience and diagnostics.
