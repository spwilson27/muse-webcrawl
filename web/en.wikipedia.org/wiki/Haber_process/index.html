Haber process

Haber process
ReactionN2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3
Conditions~450 °C, 150–300 atm, iron catalyst
Developed byFritz Haber (1909); scaled by Carl Bosch at BASF (1913)
ProductAmmonia, mainly for fertiliser

The Haber process, also called the Haber–Bosch process, is the main industrial procedure for the production of ammonia. The German chemists Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch developed it in the first decade of the 20th century. The process converts atmospheric nitrogen (N2) to ammonia (NH3) by a reaction with hydrogen (H2) using an iron metal catalyst under high temperatures and pressures.

Chemistry

The reaction N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 is exothermic and reversible. Because lower temperatures favour ammonia but slow the reaction, industry compromises at about 400–500 °C and pressures of 150–300 atmospheres over a promoted iron catalyst, recycling unreacted gases; only about 15% converts per pass. Hydrogen is supplied chiefly by steam reforming of natural gas.

Significance

Ammonia from the process underpins synthetic nitrogen fertiliser, which supports the food supply of roughly half of humanity — Vaclav Smil famously called it the most important invention of the 20th century. Haber received the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (controversially, given his role in chemical warfare), and Bosch shared the 1931 prize for high-pressure industrial chemistry.

Environmental impact

The process consumes about 1–2% of world energy production and emits substantial CO2 from hydrogen production, while fertiliser runoff drives eutrophication. 'Green ammonia' using electrolytic hydrogen from renewable power is an active area of development.
