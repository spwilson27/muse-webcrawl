Freedom to roam

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The freedom to roam, or everyman's right, is the general public's right of access to certain public or privately owned land, lakes and rivers for recreation and exercise. The right is sometimes called the right of public access to the wilderness or the right of access. It is most developed in the Nordic countries — Finland's jokamiehenoikeus, Sweden's allemansrätten and Norway's allemannsretten — and also exists, in differing forms, in Scotland, Estonia, and parts of central Europe.

In the Nordic countries, walkers may cross uncultivated land and camp regardless of ownership.

The Nordic model

Under the Nordic tradition, anyone may walk, ski, cycle or camp temporarily on uncultivated land, pick wild mushrooms, berries and flowers, and swim or boat on lakes, without the landowner's permission. The right carries a corresponding duty: visitors must not disturb, damage crops or property, light fires carelessly, or intrude on the privacy of homes and gardens. In Sweden the principle allemansrätten is protected in the constitution, though not defined in a single statute.

Other countries

Scotland codified a comparable right in the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003, which gives statutory access to most land and inland water for recreation, subject to the Scottish Outdoor Access Code. In England and Wales, the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 created a more limited right to walk on mapped "access land" — mountain, moor, heath, down and registered common land. Austria, Switzerland and the Baltic states recognise various rights of access to forests and uncultivated land, while in much of the world, including most of the United States, land is closed to the public unless access is expressly allowed.

Rationale

Supporters argue the freedom to roam gives the whole population the health and recreational benefits of nature, reflects a historic commons tradition, and encourages stewardship. Critics, including some landowners, raise concerns about litter, disturbance to livestock and wildlife, and the rights of property holders. In practice the Nordic system is widely credited with high levels of outdoor participation and public support for conservation.
