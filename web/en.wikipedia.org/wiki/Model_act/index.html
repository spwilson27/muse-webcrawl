Model act

TypeLegislative template
PurposePromote uniform or harmonised legislation
PromulgatorsUniform Law Commission, advocacy groups, professional bodies, industry groups

A model act, also known as a model law or model legislation, is a draft statute written by an organisation external to a legislature — such as a law reform commission, professional association, advocacy network or industry group — and offered to lawmakers as a ready-made template for enactment. Model acts are used chiefly in federal systems, such as the United States, where lawmaking power is divided among many jurisdictions, as a means of promoting legal uniformity or of advancing a policy programme across multiple states at once.

Purpose and process

Drafting legislation is costly and time-consuming. Model acts reduce duplication by supplying pre-drafted text that individual legislators can introduce, usually with local modifications. Bodies such as the Uniform Law Commission (National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws) produce "uniform acts" intended for verbatim adoption; other organisations produce model acts as vehicles for a particular policy position, expecting legislatures to adapt them. A model act typically includes explanatory reports or commentary to guide adopting jurisdictions and courts in interpreting the text.

Notable examples

Well-known American model and uniform acts include the Uniform Commercial Code (drafted jointly by the Uniform Law Commission and the American Law Institute), the Model Penal Code (1962) of the American Law Institute, the Model Business Corporation Act of the American Bar Association, and the Model State Emergency Health Powers Act drafted after the 2001 anthrax attacks. Outside the United States, UNCITRAL produces model laws on subjects such as international commercial arbitration and electronic commerce, which countries adapt into national legislation.

Criticism

Model acts are praised for improving consistency and legal quality, especially for smaller legislatures with limited drafting resources. Critics argue that they can transfer lawmaking influence to unelected or private bodies: controversy has surrounded the American Legislative Exchange Council (ALEC), a network of legislators and private-sector members that drafts conservative model bills — including "stand your ground" and voter identification laws — which member legislators then introduce in their states. Opponents argue this amounts to corporate influence over public law; supporters respond that legislators remain free to accept, amend or reject the drafts.
