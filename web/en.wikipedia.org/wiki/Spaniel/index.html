A spaniel is a type of gun dog designed to flush game from dense cover — particularly woodcock, pheasant and other birds — and to retrieve shot game, including from water in the case of the water spaniels. Spaniels are medium-sized dogs with long, silky coats, long drooping ears, and a famously eager, friendly disposition. The name derives from Old French espaigneul, "Spanish (dog)", reflecting a traditional belief in Iberian origins, though the modern breeds were developed overwhelmingly in Britain and Ireland.

History

Spaniel-type dogs were described by Gaston Fébus in the 14th century and by Dr John Caius in De Canibus Britannicis (1570), which divided them into land and water spaniels. Land spaniels further split by size into larger "springing" spaniels — which sprang game for nets, falcons and later guns — and smaller "comforter" spaniels kept as lapdogs. In the 19th century the types were standardised into distinct breeds, a process that also produced setters and pointers from related stock.

Breeds

Recognised spaniel breeds include the English Springer Spaniel, Cocker Spaniel (English and American), Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, Welsh Springer Spaniel, Clumber Spaniel, Sussex Spaniel, Field Spaniel, Irish Water Spaniel, Boykin Spaniel, Brittany (once the Brittany Spaniel) and the Papillon (a toy spaniel type), among others. Field-bred and show-bred lines diverge notably in size, coat and drive in several breeds.

Temperament and uses

Spaniels are intelligent, trainable and people-oriented — traits that, with their acute noses, suit them to work as detection dogs for explosives, narcotics and conservation surveys, as well as assistance and therapy roles. Their popularity as family pets rests on an affectionate, merry temperament, though working lines require substantial exercise and mental stimulation.
