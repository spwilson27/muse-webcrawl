Aztec writing

[ Image placeholder: Page from an Aztec codex ]

The Aztec or Nahuatl writing system is a pre-Columbian system combining pictorial logograms with phonetic (rebus and syllabic) elements, used by the Nahua peoples of central Mexico to record history, tribute, calendars and ritual in screenfold books called codices. It developed from earlier Mesoamerican traditions shared with the Mixtec and other cultures.

Structure

Place names and personal names were commonly written with logographic glyphs supplemented by phonetic complements based on the rebus principle — for example, a picture of teeth (tlantli) could supply the syllable tlan ("place of abundance"). Numbers were vigesimal, shown with dots, bars, flags and other signs; the Aztec calendar recorded dates in the 260-day tonalpohualli and 52-year cycles. Specialist scribes (tlacuiloque) painted codices on amatl bark paper or deerskin.

Destruction and survival

Most pre-conquest codices were destroyed during and after the Spanish conquest, often on religious grounds; surviving pre-Hispanic-style manuscripts include the Codex Borgia group and tribute records such as the Codex Mendoza (painted after the conquest for Spanish officials, c. 1541). Colonial-era Nahua scribes adapted the system and the Latin alphabet side by side, and large corpora of alphabetic Nahuatl documents were produced.

Decipherment debates

Scholars long debated how phonetic Aztec writing was; work since the late 20th century (notably by Alfonso Lacadena and others) has demonstrated a systematic syllabic component, overturning the older view of the script as purely pictographic.
