Boarding school

Boarding school
DefinitionA school where pupils live on the premises during term time
Historic examplesEton College, Harrow School, Winchester College
Staff in residenceHousemasters/housemistresses, matrons, tutors

A boarding school is a school where pupils live within the premises while being given formal instruction. The word "boarding" denotes accommodation and meals ("room and board"). Boarding schools have existed for many centuries, and the model is found across the world, from British public schools to monastic and military schools.

History

Residential education dates to medieval monastic and cathedral schools. In Britain, institutions such as Winchester (1382), Eton (1440) and Harrow (1572) developed into the elite "public schools" that educated the governing classes of the British Empire. The British model was exported worldwide and imitated in the United States (e.g., Phillips Exeter, Phillips Academy), Switzerland, India, Australia and elsewhere.

Daily life

Boarders typically live in houses supervised by housemasters, follow structured days of lessons, sport and supervised study ("prep"), and return home for holidays and some weekends. Schools emphasise pastoral care through house systems, tutors and matrons, and many offer weekly or flexible boarding alongside full boarding.

Debate

Supporters cite independence, community, facilities and pastoral structure. Critics raise the emotional costs of early separation from family — popularised by the concept of "boarding school syndrome" — and, in the colonial context, the use of residential schools to assimilate Indigenous children in Canada, Australia and the United States, which caused lasting harm now widely documented and apologised for. Numbers of boarders have declined in Britain over recent decades even as demand grows in parts of Asia.
