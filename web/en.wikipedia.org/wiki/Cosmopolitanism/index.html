Cosmopolitanism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cosmopolitanism is the idea that all human beings are, or should be treated as, members of a single community — citizens of the world (kosmopolitēs, the Cynic Diogenes' answer when asked his city). Its moral form holds that obligations of justice and concern do not stop at borders; its political form imagines institutions beyond the nation-state; its cultural form delights in plural belonging. Stoic universalism, Kant's Perpetual Peace (1795) with its cosmopolitan right of hospitality, and the post-1945 human-rights order are its waystations.

Modern debates

Contemporary cosmopolitans (Appiah's "rooted cosmopolitanism", Held's cosmopolitan democracy, Pogge and Singer on global poverty) press claims from aid duties to global institutional reform; critics from communitarian, nationalist and realist positions answer that special obligations to compatriots are morally basic or practically necessary, that world government would be a tyranny risk without exit, and that "citizen of the world" rhetoric (Theresa May's 2016 "citizen of nowhere" retort) floats free of any demos. The quarrel intensified with globalisation's backlash, yet the practical agenda — climate, pandemics, migration, tax arbitrage — keeps manufacturing cosmopolitan problems for nationalist politics.
