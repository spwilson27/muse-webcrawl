Qualcomm Incorporated is an American multinational corporation headquartered in San Diego, California, and incorporated in Delaware. It creates semiconductors, software, and services related to wireless technology. Qualcomm's name stands for "Quality Communications". The company owns patents critical to the 5G, 4G, CDMA2000, TD-SCDMA, and WCDMA mobile communications standards, and its Snapdragon line of system-on-chip processors powers a large share of the world's Android smartphones.

History

Qualcomm was founded in 1985 by Irwin Jacobs, Franklin Antonio, Andrew Viterbi, and four other partners. The company pioneered the commercialisation of CDMA (code-division multiple access) digital cellular technology, which became one of the two dominant 2G/3G standards and the foundation of all 3G networks. Its OmniTRACS satellite tracking system for trucking was an early success.

In 1999 Qualcomm sold its base station business to Ericsson and its handset business to Kyocera, refocusing on chipsets and licensing. Its patent-licensing model — charging royalties on nearly every smartphone sold — made the company highly profitable but drew antitrust scrutiny and litigation worldwide, including a major legal battle with Apple (settled in 2019) and regulatory fines in China, South Korea, and the European Union. Qualcomm has diversified into automotive chips, laptops (Snapdragon X), and Internet-of-things devices, and in 2021 Cristiano Amon became CEO.
