Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists

Abbreviation: OUN

Founded: 1929, Vienna

Ideology: Ukrainian nationalism

Leaders: Yevhen Konovalets; Stepan Bandera; Andriy Melnyk

The Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists (OUN; Ukrainian: Організація українських націоналістів) was a Ukrainian nationalist political organisation founded in 1929 in Vienna, uniting the Ukrainian Military Organization and radical nationalist groups. Its goal was the establishment of an independent, ethnically Ukrainian state on territory then divided between the Soviet Union and Poland, and it pursued that goal through political organisation, propaganda and armed struggle.

Interwar period

Under its first leader Yevhen Konovalets, the OUN operated underground in Polish-ruled Galicia, carrying out sabotage and assassinations — most notably of Polish interior minister Bronisław Pieracki in 1934 — against Polish pacification policies, while also combating Soviet agents; Konovalets himself was assassinated by an NKVD agent in Rotterdam in 1938. His succession split the movement in 1940 into the OUN-B of Stepan Bandera and the more conservative OUN-M of Andriy Melnyk.

Second World War

Both factions initially sought German support against the USSR, but the relationship was fraught: after German forces invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, the OUN-B proclaimed Ukrainian statehood in Lviv, which the Nazis suppressed, imprisoning Bandera in Sachsenhausen. In 1942 the OUN-B formed the Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA), which fought both Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, and which carried out the ethnic cleansing of Poles in Volhynia and Eastern Galicia in 1943–44, massacring tens of thousands of Polish civilians — events that remain at the centre of Polish–Ukrainian historical disputes. Elements of both factions at times collaborated with German intelligence and police formations, a record that, together with the OUN's integral nationalism, makes its legacy fiercely contested.

Postwar legacy

The UPA insurgency against Soviet rule continued into the early 1950s. In exile, Bandera was assassinated by a KGB agent in Munich in 1959. Soviet and later Russian propaganda demonised the OUN as fascist collaborators, while in independent Ukraine after 1991 the OUN and UPA were progressively commemorated as fighters for independence, including official recognition of UPA veterans — a commemoration criticised by Poland, Israel and Jewish organisations because of the Volhynia massacres and the Holocaust-era pogroms. The OUN's history remains one of the most debated topics in Eastern European memory politics.
