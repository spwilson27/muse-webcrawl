Underwater acoustics

Underwater acoustics (also known as hydroacoustics) is the study of the propagation of sound in water and the interaction of the mechanical waves that constitute sound with the water and its boundaries. The water may be in the ocean, a sea, lake, river, or tank. Typical frequencies associated with underwater acoustics range from 10 Hz to 1 MHz.

Propagation

Sound travels in seawater at roughly 1,500 metres per second, about 4.4 times faster than in air, but its speed varies with temperature, salinity, and pressure, producing refraction that bends sound paths. Because of these gradients, sound can be trapped in channels such as the deep sound (SOFAR) channel, where it propagates over thousands of kilometres with low loss. Absorption, spreading, reflection from the surface and seabed, and scattering by bubbles and marine life all affect transmission.

Applications

Underwater acoustics underpins sonar for submarine detection and navigation, echo sounders and multibeam bathymetry for charting the seafloor, seismic surveys for oil and gas exploration, fisheries research, underwater communication, and studies of marine mammal behaviour. Ambient noise from shipping, weather, and biological sources is an increasingly studied environmental concern.
