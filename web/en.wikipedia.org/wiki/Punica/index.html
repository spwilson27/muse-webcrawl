Punica

Punica is a small genus of fruit-bearing deciduous shrubs or small trees in the family Lythraceae, subfamily Punicoideae. Its best-known member is the pomegranate (Punica granatum). The only other accepted species is the Socotra pomegranate (Punica protopunica), endemic to the island of Socotra in Yemen.

Description

Species of Punica are shrubs or small trees reaching 5 to 10 metres in height, with glossy lance-shaped leaves, spiny branches, and showy trumpet-shaped flowers with crumpled petals, usually scarlet to orange. The fruit is a berry with a tough leathery rind enclosing hundreds of seeds, each surrounded by a juicy red aril.

Species

  • Punica granatum — the pomegranate, native from Iran to northern India, cultivated since antiquity throughout the Mediterranean, Asia, and the Americas for its fruit, juice, and ornamental flowers. Its name derives from the Latin pomum granatum, "seeded apple."
  • Punica protopunica — the Socotra pomegranate, considered a living relic of the lineage leading to the cultivated pomegranate, with pink (never red) flowers and smaller, less sweet fruit.

Taxonomy

The genus was established by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. It was long placed in its own family, Punicaceae, but genetic studies under the APG classification system place it within the loosestrife family, Lythraceae, as the subfamily Punicoideae. The name Punica comes from the Latin word for Phoenician or Carthaginian, reflecting the Romans' belief that the best pomegranates came from Carthage — malum punicum, the "Punic apple."
