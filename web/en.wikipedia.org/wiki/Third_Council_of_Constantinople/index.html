Third Council of Constantinople

Date: 680–681 AD
Location: Constantinople (modern Istanbul)
Convoked by: Emperor Constantine IV
Accepted by: Roman Catholic Church, Eastern Orthodox Church
Topic: Monothelitism

The Sixth Ecumenical Council

The Third Council of Constantinople, also known as the Sixth Ecumenical Council, was held in Constantinople from 7 November 680 to 16 September 681 under the Emperor Constantine IV. It addressed the heresy of Monothelitism, which taught that Christ had only one (divine) will.

Background

Monothelitism had been promoted by the Emperor Heraclius and Patriarch Sergius I as a compromise formula intended to reconcile the Chalcedonian churches with the Miaphysite Christians of Egypt and Syria. It was resisted in the West and defended by Maximus the Confessor and Pope Martin I, both of whom suffered exile and death for their opposition.

Proceedings and decrees

Between 100 and 289 bishops participated across eighteen sessions, held in the domed hall (the Trullus) of the imperial palace. With the agreement of the legates of Pope Agatho, the council condemned Monothelitism and defined that Christ has two natural wills and two natural operations — divine and human — in accordance with the teaching of the Council of Chalcedon.

The council anathematized several leading Monothelites, including Patriarch Sergius I and, controversially, Pope Honorius I, who was posthumously condemned for having failed to oppose the heresy.

Legacy

The council is counted as the sixth of the ecumenical councils by both the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches. Its decrees were followed a decade later by the Quinisext Council (691–692), which supplied the disciplinary canons the council itself had not issued.
