Radon transform
The Radon transform maps a function to its integrals over lines
Named afterJohann Radon
Introduced1917
Main applicationComputed tomography (CT) image reconstruction

In mathematics, the Radon transform is the integral transform which takes a function f defined on the plane to a function Rf defined on the (two-dimensional) space of lines in the plane, whose value at a particular line is equal to the line integral of the function over that line. The transform was introduced in 1917 by Johann Radon, who also provided a formula for the inverse transform.

The Radon transform is widely applicable to tomography, the creation of an image from projection data associated with cross-sectional scans of an object. If a function f represents an unknown density, then the Radon transform represents the scan data obtained as the output of a tomographic scan. Hence the inverse Radon transform can be used to reconstruct the original density from the scan data, forming the mathematical foundation of computed tomography.

Definition

For a line specified by its perpendicular distance s from the origin and its angle θ, the Radon transform integrates f along that line. The resulting two-dimensional function of (θ, s) is often displayed as a sinogram, because a point in the object traces out a sinusoid in the transform domain.

Inversion and reconstruction

Radon's inversion formula recovers f from its projections. In practice, CT scanners use the filtered back-projection algorithm: each projection is filtered with a ramp filter and then smeared back across the image plane, summing contributions from all angles. Iterative reconstruction methods have become common as computing power has increased.

Applications

Beyond X-ray CT, Radon-like transforms underpin positron emission tomography, single-photon emission computed tomography, seismic imaging, and the detection of straight lines in images via the related Hough transform.
