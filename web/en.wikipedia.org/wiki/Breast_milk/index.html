Breast milk (or breastmilk) is milk produced by the mammary glands in the breast of a human female. It is the primary source of nutrition for newborn infants, comprising fats, proteins, carbohydrates, and a varying composition of vitamins and minerals, as well as antibodies and other bioactive components that support the infant's developing immune system. The World Health Organization recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life.

Production

Under the influence of the hormones prolactin and oxytocin, the mammary glands produce milk following childbirth. The initial secretion, colostrum, is a thick, yellowish fluid rich in antibodies and protein, produced in small volumes during the first days. Transitional milk follows, and by about two weeks postpartum the supply becomes mature milk, produced in response to infant demand through a supply-and-demand feedback loop.

Composition

Mature human milk contains roughly 3–5% fat, 0.8–1% protein, 6.9–7.2% carbohydrate (primarily lactose), and 0.2% minerals, though composition varies within a feed, across the day, and over the course of lactation. Milk fat provides about half the energy content and includes long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids such as DHA. Human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs), the third most abundant solid component, are largely indigestible by the infant but serve as prebiotics for gut bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and as decoy receptors for pathogens.

Breast milk also contains living cells, including stem cells and leukocytes, enzymes such as lipase, growth factors, and secretory immunoglobulin A (IgA), which coats the infant's gut and provides passive immunity.

Benefits

For the infant

Breastfed infants have lower rates of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections, otitis media, and sudden infant death syndrome, and breastfeeding is associated with reduced risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes later in life.

For the mother

Breastfeeding is associated with reduced risks of breast and ovarian cancers, type 2 diabetes, and postpartum depression, and it accelerates postpartum recovery through oxytocin-mediated uterine contraction.

Storage and donation

Expressed milk can be stored at room temperature for several hours, refrigerated for several days, and frozen for months. Donor human milk banks screen donors, pool, pasteurize, and distribute milk, primarily to preterm and medically fragile infants whose mothers cannot provide sufficient milk.
