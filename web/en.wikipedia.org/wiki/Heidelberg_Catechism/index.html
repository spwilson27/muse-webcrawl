Heidelberg Catechism

The Heidelberg Catechism (1563), one of the Three Forms of Unity, is a Reformed catechism taking the form of a series of questions and answers intended as an instruction manual of Christian faith. Commissioned by Frederick III, Elector Palatine, it was prepared principally by Zacharias Ursinus (1534–1583), then teaching in Heidelberg, with the assistance of Caspar Olevianus (1536–1587), the court preacher. It was published in Heidelberg in 1563, originally in German, and soon translated into Latin, Dutch and many other languages.

Content

The catechism contains 129 questions and answers, divided into 52 sections or "Lord's Days" so that it can be preached through in the course of a year. Its celebrated opening question asks, "What is your only comfort in life and death?" and answers that the believer belongs, body and soul, to Jesus Christ. The body of the catechism follows the pattern of Romans: the misery of humanity under sin, deliverance through Christ, and gratitude expressed in a life of obedience—including expositions of the Apostles' Creed, the Ten Commandments, the Lord's Prayer and the sacraments.

Influence

Endorsed by the national Synod of Dort (1618–19) as one of the Three Forms of Unity (together with the Belgic Confession and the Canons of Dort), the catechism became the confessional standard of Dutch Reformed churches and was carried worldwide by Dutch, German, Hungarian, Swiss and later American Reformed denominations. Its warm, pastoral tone distinguishes it from many more scholastic confessions of the period.
