Goetia


A magic circle and triangle of the art, as prescribed in the Goetia.

Goetia (from the Greek goēteia, "sorcery" or "charm") denotes the practice of invoking or evoking demons and other spirits, in contrast to theurgy, the invocation of divine powers. In early modern usage the word acquired a strongly pejorative sense, referring to low, demonic magic as distinguished from natural or celestial magic.

The Ars Goetia

The term is best known from the Ars Goetia, the first section of the seventeenth-century grimoire known as The Lesser Key of Solomon. The text describes the seventy-two demons that King Solomon is said to have bound in a brass vessel, organised in a hierarchy of kings, dukes, princes, marquises, earls, knights and presidents, each with a rank, a seal (sigil), a legion of subordinate spirits, and powers ranging from teaching sciences and languages to revealing hidden treasure or the past and future. Famous entries include Bael, Paimon, Astaroth, Asmodeus, Belial and Bune. The magician is instructed to conjure the spirit into a triangle of art outside a protective magic circle, wearing or displaying the demon's seal. The catalogue draws in part on earlier demonologies, notably Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577), itself derived from Reginald Scot's Discoverie of Witchcraft tradition.

Later influence

The Ars Goetia was translated into English by Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers and published by Aleister Crowley in 1904 as The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King, becoming one of the most widely used grimoires in modern Western occultism. Its list of seventy-two demons has had an extensive afterlife in popular culture, supplying names and personalities for demons in novels, anime, role-playing games and, notably, the Ars Goetia of the game Genshin Impact and the demonic aristocracy of numerous fantasy settings.
