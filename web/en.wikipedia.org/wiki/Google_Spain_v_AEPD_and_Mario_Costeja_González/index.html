Google Spain v AEPD and Mario Costeja González

Google Spain SL, Google Inc. v Agencia Española de Protección de Datos, Mario Costeja González (C-131/12) is a decision of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) delivered on 13 May 2014. The Court held that an Internet search engine operator is responsible for the processing of personal data appearing in its results, and that under European data protection law individuals have the right, in certain circumstances, to obtain the removal of links to information about them that is "inadequate, irrelevant or no longer relevant, or excessive" — a right popularly known as the right to be forgotten.

Background

In 1998 the Spanish newspaper La Vanguardia published announcements of a forced sale of properties arising from social security debts owed by Mario Costeja González. By 2009, searches for his name in Google returned those announcements prominently, even though the matter had long been resolved. Costeja complained to Spain's data protection agency (AEPD), asking that the newspaper remove the information and that Google remove the links. The AEPD rejected the claim against the newspaper but upheld it against Google, which challenged the decision in the Spanish courts; the Audiencia Nacional referred questions to the CJEU.

Judgment

The Court ruled that a search engine's indexing activity constitutes "processing of personal data" and that the operator is a "controller" of that processing, even though the underlying publisher is separate. Where the data subject's rights under the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (Articles 7 and 8) override both the operator's economic interest and the general public's interest in finding the information, the operator must remove the links from results for searches on the person's name. The balance may shift where the individual plays a role in public life. Notably, the original publication remained lawful and untouched — the remedy is delisting, not erasure of the source.

Impact

The judgment was a landmark for privacy law. Google and other search engines set up web forms for delisting requests and received hundreds of thousands of applications within months; a large proportion were granted. The decision influenced the codification of a right to erasure in Article 17 of the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (2016). It also drew criticism from free-expression advocates, who warned of private censorship of lawful information, and sparked debate about whether delisting should apply globally — an issue the CJEU addressed in 2019, holding that EU law does not require worldwide delisting.
