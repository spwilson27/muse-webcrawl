Space exploration

Space exploration is the investigation of outer space by means of crewed and uncrewed spacecraft. Driven first by the Cold War rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union and later by international cooperation and commercial enterprise, it has produced satellites in Earth orbit, space stations, human landings on the Moon, robotic missions to every planet of the Solar System, and spacecraft now travelling in interstellar space.

Early history

The theoretical foundations were laid by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, Robert H. Goddard and Hermann Oberth, pioneers of rocketry in the early twentieth century. The German V-2 rocket of 1944 was the first human-made object to reach space. The launch of Sputnik 1 by the USSR on 4 October 1957 opened the Space Age and began the Space Race.

Milestones

Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961. The United States' Apollo programme landed the first humans on the Moon on 20 July 1969, with Apollo 11's Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin. Robotic probes followed: Mariner and Venera missions to the inner planets, the Viking landers on Mars (1976), the Voyager probes to the outer planets and beyond, and orbiters and rovers at Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and elsewhere. The Hubble Space Telescope (1990) and other observatories transformed astronomy. The International Space Station, assembled from 1998 in partnership by NASA, Roscosmos, ESA, JAXA and CSA, has been continuously occupied since November 2000.

Modern era

In the twenty-first century, China's space programme has grown rapidly — including crewed flights, lunar sample returns (Chang'e 5, 2020; Chang'e 6 far-side sample, 2024) and the Tiangong space station — while India's ISRO achieved lunar and Mars missions at low cost. Commercial companies, notably SpaceX with reusable Falcon rockets and crewed Dragon spacecraft, have sharply reduced launch costs. Current ambitions include the NASA-led Artemis programme to return humans to the Moon, continued robotic exploration of Mars, and long-term discussion of human missions to Mars.

Rationale and benefits

Motivations cited for space exploration include scientific discovery, Earth observation (weather, climate and communications), national prestige and security, and the long-term survival and expansion of humanity. Space technology has yielded wide practical benefits, from satellite navigation and telecommunications to materials and medical spin-offs, though the cost and human risk of exploration remain subjects of debate.
