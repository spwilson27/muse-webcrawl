Unam sanctam is a papal bull issued by Pope Boniface VIII on 18 November 1302. It is one of the most famous medieval statements of papal supremacy, declaring that there is one holy, catholic, and apostolic Church outside of which there is no salvation, and concluding with the solemn definition that "it is altogether necessary for salvation for every human creature to be subject to the Roman Pontiff".

Boniface VIII issued Unam sanctam at the height of his struggle with Philip IV of France.

Context and content

The bull arose from Boniface's escalating conflict with King Philip IV of France over the taxation of clergy and the trial of a French bishop. Drawing on the "two swords" doctrine, Boniface argued that both the spiritual and the temporal sword are in the Church's power: the temporal sword is wielded by kings for the Church, at the direction and sufferance of the priesthood, and the spiritual power may judge the earthly power if it errs—while the pope himself "is judged by no one".

Aftermath

Philip responded with propaganda and charges of heresy against Boniface; in September 1303 the king's agents seized the pope at Anagni in the "Outrage of Anagni", and Boniface died weeks later. The bull's claims were later moderated in practice and reinterpreted, but Unam sanctam remains a landmark text in the history of church–state relations and is frequently cited in discussions of papal authority and of the doctrine that there is no salvation outside the Church.
