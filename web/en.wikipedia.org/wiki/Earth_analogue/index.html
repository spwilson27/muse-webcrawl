An Earth analogue (also called an Earth analog, Earth twin or Earth-like planet) is an exoplanet or exomoon with environmental conditions similar to those found on Earth. The term usually implies a rocky (terrestrial) planet of comparable mass and radius, orbiting within its star's habitable zone where liquid water could exist on the surface, but usage varies; some authors require close similarity in atmosphere, temperature and stellar environment as well.

Criteria

Astronomers typically evaluate Earth-likeness using measurable parameters:

  • Size and mass — radius roughly 0.5–1.5 times Earth's and comparable mass, implying a rocky composition and sufficient gravity to retain an atmosphere.
  • Temperature — an equilibrium temperature near Earth's, taking account of likely greenhouse warming.
  • Orbit — location within the circumstellar habitable zone.
  • Host star — long-lived, stable stars (typically K and G dwarfs, or quiet M dwarfs) are favoured.
  • Composition and atmosphere — evidence for a secondary atmosphere and water; rarely yet measurable.

The Earth Similarity Index (ESI), proposed in 2011 by Abel Méndez and colleagues at the University of Puerto Rico at Arecibo, condenses radius, density, escape velocity and surface temperature into a value from 0 to 1, with Earth = 1. Mars scores 0.64 and only a handful of exoplanets exceed 0.8.

Notable candidates

  • Kepler-186f (2014) — first Earth-size planet confirmed in another star's habitable zone, about 580 light-years away.
  • Kepler-452b (2015) — an older, slightly larger cousin orbiting a Sun-like star, sometimes called "Earth 2.0" in press coverage.
  • Proxima Centauri b (2016) — a roughly Earth-mass planet in the habitable zone of the nearest star, 4.24 light-years away.
  • TRAPPIST-1 system (2017) — seven Earth-size planets, of which e, f and g lie in the habitable zone; TRAPPIST-1e is considered among the most Earth-like in density and temperature.
  • TOI 700 d and Kepler-1649c — further Earth-size habitable-zone worlds identified from TESS and Kepler data.

Frequency and detection

Estimates of the fraction of Sun-like stars hosting an Earth-size habitable-zone planet (ηEarth) range widely, from a few percent to over one in five, depending on definitions and extrapolation from Kepler statistics. Detection is biased toward larger planets and shorter orbits; true Earth analogues around Sun-like stars are at the limit of radial-velocity and transit surveys and are a prime target for future space telescopes designed for direct imaging and spectroscopy, such as the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory.

In science fiction

The search for a "second Earth" is a longstanding science-fiction theme, from early twentieth-century planetary romances to modern stories of interstellar colonisation, and has shaped public interest in exoplanet announcements.
