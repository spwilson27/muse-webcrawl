Energy in the United States

U.S. primary energy consumption by source

Energy in the United States describes energy production, consumption and policy in the United States, the world's second-largest energy consumer. Total primary energy consumption is on the order of 94 quadrillion British thermal units (quads) per year. The main sources are petroleum (about 36 percent), natural gas (about 33 percent), coal (about 10 percent), nuclear power (about 8 percent) and renewables (about 13 percent), though shares shift year to year.

Production

Since the shale revolution of the 2000s and 2010s — driven by hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling in formations such as the Permian Basin — the United States has become the world's largest producer of both petroleum and natural gas, and since 2019 a net exporter of total energy. The U.S. is also a major coal producer, though output has declined as gas and renewables displaced coal in electricity generation.

Electricity

U.S. electricity generation (about 4,200 TWh annually) comes mainly from natural gas (the largest source, over 40 percent), nuclear (around 18–19 percent, the world's largest nuclear fleet by output), coal (declining, around 16 percent), wind (around 10 percent), hydropower and a rapidly growing solar share. The grid is organised into three major interconnections — Eastern, Western and ERCOT (Texas).

Consumption and policy

Transportation consumes most petroleum; industry is the largest overall consuming sector. Federal energy policy spans the Department of Energy, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and the Environmental Protection Agency, and includes the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 directed hundreds of billions of dollars toward clean energy deployment. U.S. energy-related CO2 emissions peaked in the mid-2000s and have since declined moderately, driven by coal-to-gas switching, efficiency and renewables growth.
