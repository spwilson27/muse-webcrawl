Nahuas

Nahuas
Total population: approx. 2.5 million
Languages: Nahuatl, Spanish
Religions: Roman Catholicism, traditional beliefs

The Nahuas are a group of Indigenous peoples of Mexico and El Salvador who are historically known for speaking the Nahuatl language. They are the largest Indigenous ethnic group in Mexico, numbering roughly two and a half million people. The term "Nahua" derives from Nahuatl, the language of the Aztec (Mexica) Empire, and the Nahua peoples include the descendants of the Mexica, the Tlaxcaltecs, the Tepanecs, and other Uto-Aztecan-speaking groups of central Mexico.

History

Nahua peoples migrated into the Valley of Mexico from northern Mexico and the southwestern United States beginning around the 6th century CE. By the Postclassic period they dominated central Mexico, founding city-states such as Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan, and Tlaxcala. The Aztec Empire, ruled by the Mexica, was the most powerful Nahua polity at the time of the Spanish conquest in 1519–1521.

After the conquest, Nahuatl served for a time as a lingua franca of New Spain, and colonial authorities and missionaries produced extensive literature in the language. Over subsequent centuries Nahuatl lost ground to Spanish, though it remains one of Mexico's most widely spoken Indigenous languages.

Regions

Today Nahua communities are found mainly in the states of Puebla, Veracruz, Hidalgo, Guerrero, San Luis Potosí, Mexico, Tlaxcala, Morelos, Michoacán, Jalisco, and the Federal District. In El Salvador, the Pipil people are a Nahua group whose language, Nawat, is critically endangered.

Culture

Nahua culture retains elements of pre-Hispanic tradition, including the milpa system of agriculture (maize, beans, and squash), temazcal sweat baths, traditional medicine, and festivals that blend Catholic and Indigenous elements, such as Day of the Dead observances.
