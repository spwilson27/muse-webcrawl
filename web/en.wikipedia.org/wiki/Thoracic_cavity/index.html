Thoracic cavity

The thoracic cavity (or chest cavity) is the chamber of the body of vertebrates that is protected by the thoracic wall (the rib cage and associated skin, muscle, and fascia). It contains the heart and lungs, together with the thymus, trachea, oesophagus, and major blood vessels. The central compartment of the thoracic cavity is the mediastinum.

BoundariesThoracic wall, diaphragm (below), thoracic inlet (above)

Structure

The thoracic cavity is bounded above by the thoracic inlet (superior thoracic aperture), through which it communicates with the neck, and below by the diaphragm, which separates it from the abdominal cavity. Laterally and posteriorly it is enclosed by the twelve pairs of ribs, costal cartilages, sternum, and thoracic vertebrae.

Compartments

The cavity is divided into two pleural cavities, each containing a lung and lined by pleura, and the mediastinum between them, which contains the heart within the pericardium, the great vessels, the trachea and main bronchi, the oesophagus, the thymus, and lymph nodes.

Function

The thoracic cavity protects the organs of circulation and respiration and plays a central mechanical role in breathing: movements of the ribs and diaphragm change its volume, producing the pressure changes that draw air into and expel it from the lungs. Negative intrapleural pressure keeps the lungs expanded against their elastic recoil.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the cavity include pneumothorax (air in the pleural cavity), pleural effusion, haemothorax, and mediastinitis. In trauma, the cavity's large vascular structures make penetrating chest injuries potentially life-threatening.
