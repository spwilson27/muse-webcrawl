Typhoon Tip

Typhoon Tip
Formed: October 4, 1979
Dissipated: October 24, 1979
Highest winds: 305 km/h (190 mph)
Lowest pressure: 870 hPa (mbar) — world record
Diameter: ~2,220 km (1,380 mi) — largest on record
Fatalities: 99
Areas affected: Guam, Japan

Typhoon Tip, known in the Philippines as Typhoon Warling, was the largest and most intense tropical cyclone ever recorded. The nineteenth tropical storm and twelfth typhoon of the 1979 Pacific typhoon season, Tip developed in the Marshall Islands region in early October 1979, passed near Guam, and underwent explosive deepening over the Philippine Sea. On October 12, 1979, reconnaissance aircraft measured a central pressure of 870 hectopascals — the lowest sea-level pressure ever observed on Earth — with maximum sustained winds of about 305 km/h.

Meteorological history

Tip formed from a disturbance within a large monsoon trough on October 4, 1979. After a slow start, the storm intensified steadily as it tracked west-northwest. Its circulation grew to extraordinary size: at peak intensity, Tip's gale-force winds spanned a diameter of roughly 2,220 kilometres (1,380 miles), making it the largest tropical cyclone on record — its wind field covered an area comparable to half the contiguous United States.

After peaking, Tip gradually weakened as it recurved northeastward toward Japan, making landfall on Honshu on October 19 as a much weaker, though still damaging, typhoon. It became extratropical shortly afterwards and dissipated over the far eastern Soviet Union region.

Impact

In Japan, Tip caused widespread flooding and landslides; 42 people died in Japan, including deaths in a fire and flooding at a U.S. Marine Corps training camp (Camp Fuji) that came to be associated with the storm. In total the typhoon was responsible for 99 deaths. Despite its record intensity, most of Tip's life was spent over open ocean, and its landfall occurred well after its peak, limiting casualties.

Records

Typhoon Tip holds two enduring world records: the lowest central pressure (870 hPa) and the largest diameter of any tropical cyclone. No storm since has surpassed either mark, and Tip remains the standard against which extreme tropical cyclones are measured.
