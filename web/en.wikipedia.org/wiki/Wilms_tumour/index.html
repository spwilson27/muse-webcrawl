Wilms' tumour, also known as nephroblastoma, is a cancer of the kidneys that typically occurs in children, rarely in adults. It is named after Max Wilms (1867–1918), the German surgeon who first described it. It is the most common type of kidney cancer in children and the fourth most common type of childhood cancer overall, accounting for roughly 5% of paediatric cancers. Approximately 650 cases are diagnosed in the United States each year; most occur in children under five years of age, with a peak incidence around ages three to four.

Signs and symptoms

The classic presentation is a painless, palpable abdominal mass discovered by a parent or on routine examination. Other signs may include abdominal pain, blood in the urine (haematuria), fever, and high blood pressure. Most tumours involve only one kidney, though about 5% of cases are bilateral.

Causes and genetics

Wilms' tumour arises from embryonic kidney precursor cells that fail to develop normally. Mutations in the WT1 tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 11p13 are implicated in a subset of cases. The tumour is associated with several congenital syndromes, including WAGR syndrome (Wilms' tumour, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, and intellectual disability), Denys–Drash syndrome, and Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome. Under the microscope the tumour characteristically shows a "triphasic" pattern of blastemal, stromal, and epithelial cells, recapitulating stages of normal kidney development.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis typically begins with abdominal ultrasound, followed by CT or MRI imaging to define the extent of disease and check for spread to the lungs, the most common site of metastasis. Staging ranges from stage I (tumour confined to the kidney and completely removed) to stage V (bilateral involvement).

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment combines surgery (nephrectomy), chemotherapy, and, for higher-stage disease, radiotherapy. Two major cooperative groups guide treatment philosophy: the Children's Oncology Group in North America (historically NWTS), favouring upfront surgery, and the SIOP group in Europe, favouring preoperative chemotherapy. Outcomes have improved dramatically; overall five-year survival now exceeds 90% for localized disease, though prognosis is worse for anaplastic histology and recurrent disease.
