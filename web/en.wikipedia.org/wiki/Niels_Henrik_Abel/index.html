Niels Henrik Abel (5 August 1802 – 6 April 1829) was a Norwegian mathematician who made pioneering contributions in a variety of fields. His most famous result is the first complete proof demonstrating the impossibility of solving the general quintic equation in radicals — the Abel–Ruffini theorem. He also, independently of Carl Gustav Jacobi, developed the theory of elliptic functions, and his name is attached to Abelian groups, Abelian integrals, Abel's theorem, and many other concepts.

Life

Abel was born in Nedstrand, the son of a pastor, and grew up in poverty after Norway's economic decline during the Napoleonic Wars. At school in Christiania (Oslo), the teacher Bernt Michael Holmboe recognised his talent and mentored him. A government stipend allowed Abel to travel in 1825–27 to Berlin, where he met August Leopold Crelle (whose new journal published much of Abel's work), and to Paris, where his great memoir on elliptic transcendents was presented to the Academy but mislaid — it was published only in 1841.

Returning to Norway without a position, Abel tutored and worked intensely while his health failed from tuberculosis. He died in April 1829, aged 26 — two days before a letter arrived offering him a professorship in Berlin.

Legacy

Abel's reputation grew rapidly after his death; Hermite remarked that Abel left mathematicians enough to keep them busy for five hundred years. Norway commemorates him extensively, and in 2001 the Norwegian government established the Abel Prize, awarded annually since 2003 as a mathematics counterpart to the Nobel Prizes.
