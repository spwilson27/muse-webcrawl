Structured finance is a sector of finance, specifically financial law, that creates complex financial instruments and transactions, generally with legal and corporate entities and vehicles, to transfer and transform risk. It emerged in the late 1970s and 1980s with the first mortgage securitisations, and it typically involves pooling cash-generating assets and issuing claims against them in layers of seniority called tranches.

Securitisation

In a securitisation, an originator transfers a pool of assets—mortgages, auto loans, credit-card receivables, student loans—to a bankruptcy-remote special-purpose entity, which issues securities backed by the pool's cash flows. Payments are distributed according to a contractual "waterfall": senior tranches are paid first and carry the lowest risk and return, while junior ("equity") tranches absorb losses first. Credit ratings, overcollateralisation, reserve accounts and guarantees ("credit enhancement") shape the safety of each tranche.

Products

  • Asset-backed securities (ABS) — backed by consumer or commercial loans and receivables.
  • Mortgage-backed securities (MBS) — residential (RMBS) and commercial (CMBS) variants, including agency pass-throughs.
  • Collateralised debt obligations (CDOs) — securities backed by portfolios of debt, including synthetic CDOs referencing credit default swaps.
  • Collateralised loan obligations (CLOs) — backed by leveraged corporate loans.

Role and criticism

Structured finance diversifies funding, matches investor risk appetites, and can lower borrowers' costs. It is also blamed for opacity and misaligned incentives in the run-up to the 2007–2008 financial crisis: complex CDOs built from subprime mortgages received high ratings, losses were hard to locate, and "originate-to-distribute" lending weakened underwriting standards. Post-crisis reforms imposed risk-retention rules, disclosure requirements and tighter rating-agency oversight.
