Charon is the innermost and largest of the five known natural satellites of the dwarf planet Pluto. With a radius of 606 kilometres (377 mi) — about half that of Pluto — it is the largest known moon relative to its parent body in the Solar System, and the Pluto–Charon pair is often described as a double dwarf planet.

Discovery

Charon was discovered on 22 June 1978 by astronomer James W. Christy at the United States Naval Observatory, who noticed a small bulge in images of Pluto taken with the observatory's 1.55-metre reflector. Its orbit was resolved through a series of mutual eclipses between 1985 and 1990, and its mass was measured in 1992. The discoverer named it for the mythological ferryman Charon, with a personal nod to his wife Charlene ("Shar-on"); the IAU's formal pronunciation honours the classical root ("Kair-on").

Physical characteristics

Charon is slightly less dense than Pluto (about 1.7 g/cm3), consistent with a body that is mostly water ice with little rock, whereas Pluto is denser. Its surface is less varied than Pluto's, dominated by water ice and a striking reddish north polar region informally called Mordor Macula, thought to be deposited tholins from atmospheric escape from Pluto. In 2015 the New Horizons spacecraft flew through the system, imaging Charon in detail and revealing a vast canyon system (Serenity Chasma) and cryovolcanic features such as Kubrick Mons.

Orbit

Charon orbits Pluto every 6.387 days at a distance of about 19,600 km. The two bodies are tidally locked: Charon always presents the same face to Pluto, and Pluto always presents the same face to Charon — a synchronous rotation unique among major planet–moon pairs. The system's barycentre lies outside Pluto's surface, qualifying it as a binary dwarf planet by some definitions.
