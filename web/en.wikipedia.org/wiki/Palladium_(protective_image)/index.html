Palladium (protective image)

A palladium (plural palladia) is an image or object of great antiquity on which the safety of a city was believed to depend. The word derives from the Palladium of Troy, a wooden image of Pallas Athena said to have fallen from heaven; Troy could not be taken so long as it remained within the walls, and in Greek legend Odysseus and Diomedes stole it during the Trojan War.

The Palladium of Troy

The image was said to have been made by Athena herself in memory of her companion Pallas. In various accounts it was carried by Aeneas to Italy and preserved at Rome in the Temple of Vesta, where an object identified as the Palladium was among the sacred pledges (pignora imperii) guaranteeing Roman power. The Roman Palladium was rarely seen and was famously rescued from the burning temple by the pontifex Metellus in 241 BC.

Later usage

By extension, "palladium" came to denote any sacred object or institution regarded as a safeguard of a people's liberties — William Blackstone and later writers called trial by jury the "palladium of English liberty" — and in modern journalistic usage it is used figuratively for anything that protects or guarantees safety.

Other palladia

Cities of the ancient world claimed their own protective images and talismans, including statues of Athena at Athens and Argos, and in later centuries certain icons of the Virgin — such as the icon of the Theotokos of Vladimir — were treated as palladia of their cities and states.
