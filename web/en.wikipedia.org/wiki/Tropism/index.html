Tropism

A tropism (from the Greek tropos, "a turning") is a biological phenomenon, usually of a plant, indicating growth or turning movement of an organism in response to an environmental stimulus. In tropisms, the response depends on the direction of the stimulus, distinguishing them from nastic movements, which are non-directional. If the organism grows toward the stimulus it is a positive tropism; growth away from the stimulus is a negative tropism.

Types

  • Phototropism — response to light; shoots are positively phototropic, bending toward light.
  • Gravitropism (geotropism) — response to gravity; roots grow downward (positive) and shoots upward (negative).
  • Thigmotropism — response to touch; tendrils of climbing plants coil around supports.
  • Chemotropism — response to chemicals, as when a pollen tube grows toward an ovule.
  • Hydrotropism — response to water, shown by roots growing toward moisture.
  • Thermotropism and heliotropism — responses to temperature and to the sun's movement.

Mechanism

In plants, many tropisms are mediated by the hormone auxin. In the classic Cholodny–Went model, light or gravity causes auxin to accumulate on the shaded or lower side of a stem or root; because auxin promotes elongation in shoots but inhibits it in roots, the resulting asymmetric growth bends the organ. Statoliths — dense starch grains that settle within specialized cells — are believed to be the gravity sensors in gravitropism. Charles and Francis Darwin's experiments on phototropism in grass coleoptiles in the 1880s laid the foundations for the discovery of plant hormones.

Other uses

In microbiology and medicine, "tropism" also denotes the specificity of a virus or pathogen for particular host cells or tissues (tissue tropism), determined by the receptors the pathogen uses for entry.
