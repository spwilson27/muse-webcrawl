Louisiana Creole (Kréyòl La Lwizyàn) is a French-based creole language spoken by some members of the Louisiana Creole people, especially in south Louisiana around New Orleans, the River Parishes, and the Cane River area of Natchitoches Parish. It developed during the French and Spanish colonial periods from contact between French settlers, enslaved Africans and Native Americans.

The term "Louisiana Creole" also refers to the people and culture themselves — descendants of the colonial population of French Louisiana, of mixed European, African and Native ancestry — whose contributions include Creole cuisine, music and architecture. This page concerns the language; see Louisiana Creole people for the ethnic group.

History

The language crystallised on colonial plantations and in New Orleans during the 18th century. After the Louisiana Purchase (1803), Anglo-American influence and later compulsory English schooling drove a long decline. Louisiana French and Creole speaking was stigmatised in schools for much of the 20th century, and speaker numbers fell sharply; today the language is considered endangered, though revitalisation efforts and organisations such as CODOFIL have promoted French-language revival.

Characteristics

Louisiana Creole shares much vocabulary with Louisiana French but differs in grammar, using preverbal markers for tense and aspect (for example té for past, apé/ap for progressive, va/a for future). It is related to but distinct from Haitian Creole and the French creoles of the Caribbean.

Culture

Creole culture is closely associated with gumbo, jambalaya, zydeco music, the French Quarter of New Orleans, and the Creole townhouses and cottages built in characteristic colonial styles.
