The music of Cuba, including its instruments, performance, and dance, comprises a large set of unique traditions influenced mostly by West African and European (especially Spanish) music. Due to the syncretic nature of most of its genres, Cuban music is often considered one of the richest and most influential regional musics of the world. Genres such as son cubano, danzón, mambo, cha-cha-cha, bolero, and rumba originated on the island and went on to shape popular music across Latin America and the United States.

Roots

Cuban music fuses Spanish song forms, guitar traditions, and European classical harmony with African rhythms and percussion preserved by enslaved Africans and their descendants, including Santería (Regla de Ocha) religious drumming. The contradanza, arriving in the late 18th century, became the root of many later Cuban dance genres. The rhythmic cell known as clave — the five-stroke pattern played on claves — underlies most Afro-Cuban popular music.

Son cubano

Son cubano emerged in eastern Cuba (Oriente) in the late 19th century, combining Spanish décima singing and the tres guitar with African call-and-response and percussion. Brought to Havana in the 1920s, it was popularised by groups such as the Sexteto Habanero and the Trío Matamoros, and later expanded into the conjunto format by Arsenio Rodríguez, who added conga drum, piano, and trumpets — innovations that became foundational to modern salsa.

Danzón, mambo and cha-cha-cha

Danzón, Cuba's national dance, developed from the contradanza and was formalised by Miguel Faílde in 1879 with "Las alturas de Simpson". In the 1930s–40s, Orestes and Israel "Cachao" López introduced the nuevo ritmo into danzón, giving rise to mambo, popularised internationally by Dámaso Pérez Prado. In the early 1950s Enrique Jorrín created the cha-cha-cha from danzón, and it became a worldwide ballroom craze.

Rumba and Afro-Cuban traditions

Rumba is a secular Afro-Cuban genre of song, percussion, and dance, encompassing yambú, guaguancó, and columbia. Conga drums, cajón, and catá form its rhythmic core. Afro-Cuban jazz emerged in the 1940s through collaborations such as Machito and His Afro-Cubans and Dizzy Gillespie's work with Chano Pozo ("Manteca", 1947).

Classical and contemporary music

Cuba has a notable classical tradition, with composers such as Ernesto Lecuona, Amadeo Roldán, and Leo Brouwer. The Nueva Trova movement of the 1960s–70s, led by Silvio Rodríguez and Pablo Milanés, blended folk, poetry, and politics. In the 1990s the Buena Vista Social Club recordings revived international interest in traditional son, while timba — a high-energy fusion pioneered by NG La Banda and Los Van Van — dominated the island's dance music, and reguetón and urban genres gained popularity in the 2000s and 2010s.
