Spinodal

The spinodal (or spinode) is the boundary in a phase diagram that separates the region in which a mixture is absolutely unstable against small fluctuations in composition from the region in which it is merely metastable. It is defined mathematically as the locus of points at which the second derivative of the free energy with respect to composition (or density) is zero: ∂²G/∂x² = 0.

Binodal and spinodal

The binodal (coexistence curve) marks the compositions of the phases that coexist at equilibrium. Between the binodal and the spinodal, a homogeneous mixture is metastable: it can persist until a fluctuation large enough to nucleate a droplet of the new phase appears, so separation proceeds by nucleation and growth. Inside the spinodal, the mixture is unstable to fluctuations of any size; infinitesimal composition variations amplify spontaneously, so the system separates by spinodal decomposition, producing a characteristic interconnected, bicontinuous microstructure with a well-defined length scale. The same construction appears on pressure–volume isotherms of a van der Waals fluid, where the spinodal points bound the mechanically unstable region with (∂P/∂V)T > 0.

Significance

The spinodal and binodal meet at the critical point. Spinodal decomposition is exploited in materials science to produce finely dispersed alloys, glasses, and polymer blends, and the concept is central to the Cahn–Hilliard theory of phase separation.
