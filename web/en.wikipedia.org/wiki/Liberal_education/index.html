Liberal education

A liberal education is a system or course of education suitable for the cultivation of a free (Latin: liber) human being. It is based on the medieval concept of the liberal arts and the educational methods of the ancient Greeks. A liberal education aims to develop intellectual capacities broadly — critical thought, effective communication, and informed citizenship — rather than training for a specific occupation.

History

The idea descends from the classical conception of paideia and the Roman ideal of education for free citizens. Through the Middle Ages the seven liberal arts formed the basis of university study. In the nineteenth century, figures such as John Henry Newman, in The Idea of a University (1852), defended liberal knowledge pursued for its own sake as the proper end of a university. In the United States the model was embodied in the undergraduate liberal arts college and in general-education requirements adopted by many universities in the twentieth century.

Characteristics

A liberal education typically emphasises breadth across the humanities, social sciences, and natural sciences alongside depth in a chosen major; close reading and discussion of significant texts; writing and quantitative reasoning; and the cultivation of habits of independent inquiry. Proponents argue that such capacities remain valuable in a changing labour market, while critics contend that liberal programmes can be expensive and insufficiently connected to professional preparation.

Contemporary developments

In the twenty-first century, liberal education models have expanded internationally, with new liberal arts institutions founded in Europe and Asia, and organisations such as the Association of American Colleges and Universities promoting liberal learning goals under the heading of "liberal education and America's promise".
