Peak ground acceleration

Peak ground acceleration (PGA) is equal to the maximum ground acceleration that occurred during earthquake shaking at a location. PGA is equal to the amplitude of the largest absolute acceleration recorded on an accelerogram at a site during a particular earthquake. Earthquake shaking generally occurs in all three directions, so PGA is often split into horizontal and vertical components, with horizontal PGAs generally larger.

Measurement and use

PGA is measured in units of g (the acceleration due to gravity, approximately 9.81 m/s²) or in m/s². Unlike the Richter and moment magnitude scales, it is not a measure of the total energy of an earthquake, but rather of how hard the earth shakes at a given geographic point. In areas with little instrumentation, PGA can be estimated from the reported intensity of shaking, commonly using the Modified Mercalli intensity scale.

Engineering significance

PGA is a key parameter in seismic hazard assessments and in the earthquake engineering design of buildings, bridges, and other structures. Building codes in seismically active regions specify design ground motions in terms of PGA or spectral acceleration for the level of shaking expected with a given probability of exceedance over a structure's design life. Correlations between PGA and observed damage show that damage to ordinary buildings generally begins at PGAs around 0.1–0.2 g and becomes severe in vulnerable construction well before 1 g.

Recorded values

The largest PGA ever reliably recorded was approximately 4 g, measured during the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan. Large PGAs can also be produced by much smaller earthquakes close to the recording station.
