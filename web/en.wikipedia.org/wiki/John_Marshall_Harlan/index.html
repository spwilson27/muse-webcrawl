John Marshall Harlan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

John Marshall Harlan (1 June 1833 – 14 October 1911) was an American lawyer and politician who served as an associate justice of the US Supreme Court from 1877 until his death. A Kentuckian who had owned slaves and opposed emancipation before the Civil War, he became the Court's great dissenter of the Jim Crow era: the lone dissenter in the Civil Rights Cases (1883), which struck down the Civil Rights Act 1875, and in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), where his dissent declared that “our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens” — the sentence for which he is remembered.

Harlan's jurisprudence combined a broad reading of national power with an expansive view of the Reconstruction Amendments' protection of civil (not, in his view, “social”) equality, and his dissents (including in the Income Tax Cases and against incorporation restraint) were vindicated by the mid-twentieth-century Court often enough to earn him the retrospective title “the Great Dissenter”. His grandson, John Marshall Harlan II, also served on the Court (1955–71) — a judicial eminence of a very different, restraint-minded kind.
