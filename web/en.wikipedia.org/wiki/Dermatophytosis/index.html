Dermatophytosis

Dermatophytosis, also known as ringworm, is a fungal infection of the skin caused by dermatophytes — fungi in the genera Trichophyton, Microsporum, and Epidermophyton that feed on keratin. Despite the name, no worm is involved; the term comes from the characteristic ring-shaped, scaly, itchy rash.

Types

Infections are named by body site: tinea corporis (body), tinea capitis (scalp, common in children), tinea pedis (athlete's foot), tinea cruris (jock itch), tinea unguium (nails, a form of onychomycosis), and tinea barbae (beard). Some species are transmitted from animals such as cats and dogs (zoophilic), others from soil or from person to person.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical and can be confirmed by potassium hydroxide microscopy of skin scrapings, fungal culture, or Wood's lamp examination for some species. Most cases respond to topical antifungal creams such as terbinafine or azoles, while scalp and nail infections and extensive disease generally require oral antifungal therapy. Widespread misuse of topical steroid–antifungal combinations, particularly in India, has driven chronic, extensive infections and emerging drug resistance, notably in Trichophyton indotineae.
