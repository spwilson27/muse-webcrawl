Amarillo, Texas

Amarillo (Spanish for "yellow", pronounced [ˌæməˈrɪloʊ]) is a city in the U.S. state of Texas and the seat of Potter County. It is the 14th-most populous city in Texas and the most populous city in the Texas Panhandle. A portion of the city extends into Randall County. The population was 200,393 at the 2020 census, with a metropolitan population of about 308,000.

History

Amarillo was founded in 1887 as a railhead and cattle-shipping point on the Fort Worth and Denver City Railroad, in a section of the Llano Estacado near the seasonal Amarillo Lake and Amarillo Creek, named for the yellow wildflowers or yellow soil of the banks. It quickly became one of the world's busiest cattle-shipping centres. Natural gas was discovered nearby in 1918 and oil in 1921, bringing boom-era growth; the huge Panhandle–Hugoton gas field made Amarillo a centre of the helium industry, and the U.S. Bureau of Mines operated its helium plant here for decades — Amarillo long called itself the "Helium Capital of the World".

Economy and sites

The economy rests on cattle and meatpacking (one of the largest beef-processing complexes in the U.S. is nearby), oil and gas, agriculture, and the Pantex Plant, the United States' primary facility for assembling and disassembling nuclear weapons, located northeast of the city. Amarillo lies on historic U.S. Route 66; its Route 66 district and the Cadillac Ranch art installation (1974, ten Cadillacs buried nose-down in a field west of town) are famous landmarks. Palo Duro Canyon, the second-largest canyon in the United States, lies about 25 miles southeast. The American Quarter Horse Association is headquartered in Amarillo and maintains its Hall of Fame and Museum there.

Geography and climate

Amarillo sits at an elevation of about 3,600 feet (1,100 m) on the high plains, with a semi-arid climate, hot summers, and cold, windy winters with occasional snow. Rick Husband Amarillo International Airport serves the city, named for the NASA astronaut and Amarillo native who commanded the ill-fated shuttle Columbia (STS-107).
