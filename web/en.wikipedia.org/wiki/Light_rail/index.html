Light rail transit (LRT) is a form of passenger urban rail transit that uses rolling stock derived from tram technology while also having some features from heavy rapid transit. The term was coined in 1972 in the United States as an English equivalent for the German word Stadtbahn, meaning "city railway". Different definitions exist in different countries, but in the United States, light rail operates primarily along exclusive rights-of-way and uses either individual tramcars or multiple units coupled together, with a lower capacity and speed than a long heavy rail passenger train or rapid transit system.

Characteristics

Light rail systems generally occupy an intermediate position between street-running trams and grade-separated metros. They may run in reserved lanes, in tunnels, on elevated structures, or in street medians. Vehicles are typically articulated, increasingly with low floors for level boarding, and draw power from overhead catenary. Capacity typically ranges from about 2,000 to 20,000 passengers per hour per direction.

History

After the decline of streetcars in the mid-20th century, German cities upgraded tramways into Stadtbahn networks with tunnel sections. In North America, Edmonton opened the first purpose-built modern light rail line in 1978, followed by Calgary and San Diego in 1981. Since then, dozens of systems have opened in the United States, including in Los Angeles, Portland, Denver, Minneapolis and Seattle. Melbourne maintains the world's largest tram/light rail network, while Hong Kong's Light Rail serves the New Territories.

Variations

Related modes include light metros (such as Vancouver's SkyTrain and London's Docklands Light Railway), which use smaller automated trains on fully segregated tracks, and tram-trains, which run both on tramways and mainline railways, pioneered at Karlsruhe.
