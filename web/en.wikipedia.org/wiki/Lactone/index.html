Lactone

Lactones are cyclic carboxylic esters, containing a –C(=O)–O– group as part of a ring structure. They are formally derived from the corresponding hydroxycarboxylic acids by intramolecular esterification with loss of water. Lactones are widespread in nature and industry, appearing as flavour and fragrance compounds, antibiotics, and polymer precursors.

Nomenclature

Lactones are named for the parent acid with the suffix changed to -olide, together with a locant for the ring closure, or by Greek-letter prefixes indicating ring size. The Greek letter denotes the carbon bearing the ring oxygen relative to the carbonyl carbon:

NameRing sizeExample
α-lactone3-memberedα-propiolactone (unstable)
β-lactone4-memberedβ-propiolactone
γ-lactone5-memberedγ-butyrolactone
δ-lactone6-memberedδ-valerolactone
ε-lactone7-memberedε-caprolactone

Five- and six-membered (γ- and δ-) lactones form most readily because of favourable ring strain and entropy, and are the most common in nature.

Synthesis

Lactones are commonly prepared by intramolecular esterification of hydroxy acids, by Baeyer–Villiger oxidation of cyclic ketones, and by ring-closing reactions. Many lactones undergo ring-opening polymerisation: ε-caprolactone gives polycaprolactone, a biodegradable polyester.

Occurrence and uses

Glucono-δ-lactone is a food additive and acidulant; ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is an enediol lactone. Macrolide antibiotics such as erythromycin contain large lactone rings (macrolides). γ-butyrolactone (GBL) is an industrial solvent and chemical intermediate, while coumarin and many flavour compounds in fruit and wine are lactones. The β-lactone ring features in some drugs, and is distinct from the β-lactam ring of penicillin-type antibiotics.
