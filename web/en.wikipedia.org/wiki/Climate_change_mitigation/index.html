Climate change mitigation

Climate change mitigation consists of actions to limit global warming and its related effects, principally by reducing emissions of greenhouse gases from human activities and enhancing sinks that absorb them. It is one of the main responses to climate change, alongside adaptation. The Paris Agreement (2015) commits parties to holding warming well below 2 °C and pursuing efforts toward 1.5 °C; the IPCC finds that limiting warming to 1.5 °C requires global CO2 emissions to reach net zero around mid-century.

Renewable energy is central to mitigation pathways.

Measures

Major strategies include decarbonising electricity supply through wind, solar, hydro, geothermal, and nuclear power; electrifying transport, buildings, and industry; improving energy efficiency; halting deforestation and restoring ecosystems; reducing methane emissions from agriculture, waste, and fossil fuels; and changing diets and consumption. Carbon capture and storage and carbon dioxide removal (such as afforestation and direct air capture) feature in most deep-mitigation scenarios. Policy instruments include carbon taxes, emissions trading systems, regulations and standards, subsidies for clean technology, and public investment.

Economics and co-benefits

The IPCC concludes that mitigation costs are modest relative to the damages avoided, and that deep cuts are feasible with existing technologies in most sectors. Co-benefits include improved air quality and public health, energy security, and reduced exposure to fossil fuel price shocks, while trade-offs can involve land use, mineral demand, and distributional effects on workers and low-income households — concerns addressed in "just transition" frameworks.
