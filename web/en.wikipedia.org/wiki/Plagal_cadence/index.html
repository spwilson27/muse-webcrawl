Plagal cadence

Plagal cadence
ProgressionIV – I (subdominant to tonic)
Also known as"Amen cadence"
ContextWestern tonal harmony; church music

A plagal cadence is a chord progression from the subdominant chord (IV) to the tonic chord (I) at the end of a musical phrase. It is often called the "Amen cadence" because it is the standard harmonization of the word "Amen" at the conclusion of hymns in Western church music. In C major, a plagal cadence consists of an F major chord resolving to a C major chord.

Characteristics

Compared with the authentic cadence (V–I), the plagal cadence produces a softer, more settled sense of closure: because the subdominant is less dynamically driven than the dominant, the arrival on the tonic feels like a gentle settling rather than a decisive conclusion. For this reason plagal cadences frequently serve as a codetta after a stronger authentic cadence has already closed a piece, extending the ending with an atmosphere of benediction or repose.

Variants and usage

In minor keys the plagal cadence uses the minor subdominant (iv–i); a borrowed minor subdominant in a major key (iv–I) is a common expressive variant, familiar from film scores and popular ballads. The "plagal" terminology comes from the plagal modes of medieval church chant, whose final was approached from below. Many gospel, soul, and rock songs end with plagal motion, and the IV–I turnaround underlies the reflective conclusions of hymns by composers from Palestrina to modern worship music. Handel's Messiah concludes its "Amen" chorus in an extended plagal spirit, and countless hymnals end services with a two-chord "Amen" of this type.
