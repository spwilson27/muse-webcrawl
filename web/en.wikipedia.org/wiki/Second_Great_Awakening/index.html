Second Great Awakening

The Second Great Awakening was a Protestant religious revival movement during the late 18th century and first half of the 19th century in the United States. It spread religion through revivals and emotional preaching and sparked a number of reform movements. Revivals were a key part of the movement and attracted hundreds of converts to new Protestant denominations. The Methodist Church used circuit riders to reach people in frontier areas, and the movement contributed to the growth of Baptist, Methodist and other congregations.

History

The awakening began around 1790, gained momentum by 1800 and, after 1820, membership rose rapidly among Baptist and Methodist congregations, whose preachers led the movement. It was past its peak by the 1840s. On the southern frontier, the revival was marked by large camp meetings, most famously the Cane Ridge Revival in Kentucky in 1801, where thousands gathered for days of preaching.

In the Northeast, the renewed interest in religion inspired a wave of social activism, including abolitionism, temperance and women's rights. In the "Burned-over district" of western New York, the revival was so intense that the region became a centre for new religious movements, including Mormonism and the Millerite movement, and for reform crusades of all kinds.

Beliefs and methods

Unlike the Calvinist emphasis of the First Great Awakening, preachers of the Second Great Awakening stressed free will, the ability of sinners to choose conversion, and the possibility of Christian perfection. Charles Grandison Finney popularised "new measures" such as protracted meetings, the anxious bench, and praying for individuals by name. Revivalists also made extensive use of print, publishing hymns, tracts and sermons for mass distribution.

Legacy

The Second Great Awakening reshaped American religion, making evangelical Protestantism the dominant religious force in the country for much of the 19th century. It stimulated the founding of colleges, missionary societies and reform organisations, and its converts provided much of the energy behind the abolitionist and temperance movements.
