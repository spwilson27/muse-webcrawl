Image sensor format

In digital photography, the image sensor format is the size and shape of the digital image sensor. Sensor format determines the field of view obtainable with a given lens, and strongly influences image characteristics such as depth of field, noise, and low-light performance. Larger sensors generally gather more light per pixel, giving better image quality, at the cost of larger, more expensive cameras and lenses.

Relative sizes of common sensor formats, from medium format down to smartphone sensors.

Common formats

Full frame36 × 24 mm (same as 35 mm film)
APS-H~28.7 × 19 mm (crop ~1.3×)
APS-C~23.6 × 15.7 mm (crop ~1.5×; Canon ~1.6×)
Micro Four Thirds17.3 × 13 mm (crop 2×)
1-inch type13.2 × 8.8 mm (crop ~2.7×)
Smartphone sensorscommonly 1/1.3" to 1/2.55" designations

Full frame sensors match the 36 × 24 mm frame of 35 mm film and anchor the "crop factor" scale: a format's crop factor is the ratio of the full-frame diagonal to its own. Medium format digital sensors are larger still — for example 43.8 × 32.9 mm in some Fujifilm and Hasselblad bodies, and 53.4 × 40 mm in high-end Phase One backs.

The "type" system

Smaller sensors are often designated by a fractional-inch "type" (e.g. 1/2.3", 1/1.7"), a legacy of 1950s video camera tubes: the number refers to the diameter of the glass tube that once held the sensor, not to the sensor's actual diagonal, which is roughly two-thirds of the nominal figure. A "1-inch type" sensor, for instance, has a diagonal of about 16 mm rather than 25.4 mm.

Effects of format

Because smaller sensors use only the central portion of a lens's image circle, they produce a narrower field of view — equivalent to cropping — and give greater depth of field at the same aperture and framing. Sensor size also interacts with lens design: systems designed around a format, such as Micro Four Thirds, can use smaller, lighter lenses than full-frame systems of comparable capability.
