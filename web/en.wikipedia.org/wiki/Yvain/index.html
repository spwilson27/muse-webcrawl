Yvain, also spelled Ywein, Iwein, Uwain or Owain, is a knight of King Arthur's Round Table in Arthurian legend, in which he is often presented as the son of King Urien of Gore. He derives from the historical Welsh figure Owain mab Urien and is the hero of Chrétien de Troyes' romance Yvain, the Knight of the Lion (c. 1180), one of the best-known works of medieval literature.

In Chrétien's romance

In Chrétien's poem, Yvain sets out to avenge his cousin Calogrenant, who was defeated at a magical fountain in the forest of Brocéliande that produces a storm when water is poured on a stone. Yvain defeats and mortally wounds the fountain's guardian, Esclados, then falls in love with and marries the widow, Laudine, with the help of her maid Lunete. Persuaded by Gawain to return to tournamenting, Yvain promises to come back within a year but overstays; Laudine repudiates him, and he goes mad with grief. Cured with a magic ointment, he rescues a lion from a serpent, and the grateful beast becomes his companion — hence "the Knight of the Lion". Through a series of redemptive adventures defending the helpless, he eventually wins back Laudine's love.

Other traditions

The Welsh tale Owain, or the Lady of the Fountain, one of the Three Welsh Romances associated with the Mabinogion, tells a closely similar story. Hartmann von Aue adapted Chrétien's romance into Middle High German as Iwein (c. 1200). Yvain appears as a supporting knight in the Vulgate Cycle, the Prose Tristan and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, usually paired with Gawain as a model of chivalry.

Legacy

Yvain's story — a hero who must balance marriage and knightly adventure and is redeemed by humility and service — has been influential in studies of the medieval romance genre and is frequently retold in modern Arthurian adaptations.
