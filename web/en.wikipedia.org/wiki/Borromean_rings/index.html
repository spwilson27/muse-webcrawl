Borromean rings

The Borromean rings are three simple closed curves in three-dimensional space that are topologically linked and cannot be separated from each other, but that break apart into trivial unlinked circles if any one of the three is cut or removed. In other words, any two of the rings are unlinked when the third is ignored: the configuration is a Brunnian link with three components.

Geometrically, the rings are often depicted as three circles in mutually perpendicular planes, each passing through the centres of the other two. Although usually drawn as circles, a Borromean configuration cannot be realised with perfectly circular rings in Euclidean space; elliptical or otherwise deformed rings are required, because three congruent circles cannot be arranged so that each pair crosses in the required pattern.

History and symbolism

The rings take their name from the Italian House of Borromeo, whose coat of arms includes three interlaced rings arising from the unification of three noble families. The design appears much earlier, however: it is found in Norse picture stones depicting the valknut, in Buddhist art, and carved on the columns of the church of San Lorenzo in Florence. The Borromean rings have been used as a symbol of strength in unity, and a similar configuration appears in the logo of the International Mathematical Union.

Mathematical properties

In knot theory, the Borromean rings are a three-component link with linking number zero between each pair, yet with a nontrivial triple linking detected by the Milnor invariants. Removing any component leaves the other two completely unlinked, a property shared by all Brunnian links. The complement of the Borromean rings in the 3-sphere admits a complete hyperbolic structure of finite volume, decomposing into two ideal regular octahedra; the rings are thus a standard example in hyperbolic geometry.

Related topics include the Brunnian link, knot theory and the valknut.
