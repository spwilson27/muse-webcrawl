Five Civilized Tribes

The term "Five Civilized Tribes" was applied by European Americans in the colonial and early federal period of the United States to five Native American nations: the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek (Muscogee), and Seminole. They were called "civilized" because they had adopted many Anglo-American practices — written constitutions, centralised governments, Christianity, market agriculture, literacy, and in some cases plantation slavery — in an effort to coexist with expanding white settlement.

Background

The five nations were the dominant Indigenous peoples of the southeastern United States. During the late 18th and early 19th centuries they built schools, published newspapers (the Cherokee Phoenix, printed in both Cherokee and English using Sequoyah's syllabary), and codified laws. These adaptations did not protect them: the discovery of gold on Cherokee land in Georgia, and white demand for cotton land, intensified pressure for their removal.

Removal

Under the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the federal government forced the five nations to cede their southeastern homelands and move to Indian Territory in present-day Oklahoma. The Choctaw were removed first, in 1831; the Seminole resisted in the Second Seminole War; the Cherokee were driven west in 1838–39 on the journey remembered as the Trail of Tears, on which thousands died. Altogether some 60,000 people were displaced.

In Indian Territory

In Oklahoma the five nations re-established governments, schools and newspapers as the "Five Tribes." During the American Civil War they were divided, with factions supporting both the Union and the Confederacy. After the war the United States imposed new treaties, and in the 1890s the Dawes Act and the Curtis Act broke up their communal landholdings into individual allotments and dissolved their tribal governments in preparation for Oklahoma statehood in 1907. In the 20th and 21st centuries the five nations rebuilt their governments and today are among the largest federally recognised tribes in the United States.

The term today

Many Native people and historians regard "Five Civilized Tribes" as a dated and patronising label, since it implies that other Indigenous nations were "uncivilized" and measures worth by assimilation to settler norms. The term nonetheless persists in law, in the names of institutions such as the Inter-Tribal Council of the Five Civilized Tribes, and in the historical literature.
