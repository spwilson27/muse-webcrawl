Hollywood Ten

The Hollywood Ten were a group of ten film industry professionals who were cited for contempt of Congress in 1947 after refusing to answer questions before the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) about their alleged membership of the Communist Party USA. Their case became a defining episode of the postwar Red Scare and the Hollywood blacklist.

The hearings

In October 1947, HUAC investigated alleged communist infiltration of the motion picture industry. Nineteen "unfriendly witnesses" were called; ten appeared and refused to answer the committee's questions, challenging its authority on First Amendment grounds rather than invoking the Fifth Amendment. The ten were:

  • Alvah Bessie (screenwriter)
  • Herbert Biberman (director)
  • Lester Cole (screenwriter)
  • Edward Dmytryk (director)
  • Ring Lardner Jr. (screenwriter)
  • John Howard Lawson (screenwriter)
  • Albert Maltz (screenwriter)
  • Samuel Ornitz (screenwriter)
  • Adrian Scott (producer)
  • Dalton Trumbo (screenwriter)

Consequences

All ten were convicted of contempt of Congress and served prison sentences of six months to a year. In November 1947 the studio heads issued the Waldorf Statement, suspending the ten and instituting the blacklist, under which hundreds of artists were denied work for years. Edward Dmytryk later recanted and named names, resuming his career. The blacklist began to break in 1960 when Dalton Trumbo received open screen credit on Spartacus and Exodus.
