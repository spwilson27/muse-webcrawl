Navajo weaving

Navajo weaving refers to the blankets and rugs produced by Navajo (Diné) weavers, predominantly women, in the southwestern United States. Practised for at least three centuries, it is one of the most celebrated textile traditions of Native North America, known for bold geometric designs, fine tapestry weave and durable wool.

History

The Navajo learned weaving and acquired sheep from the Spanish and from Pueblo peoples in the 17th century; the churro sheep introduced by the Spanish supplied long-staple wool well suited to the upright loom. Early products were wearing blankets prized in trade throughout the Plains and Southwest. After the Navajo were confined at Bosque Redondo in the 1860s and returned to their homeland in 1868, traders encouraged production for eastern markets: aniline dyes and commercial Germantown yarns entered the craft, and the emphasis shifted from blankets to rugs for Anglo homes. Traders such as J. B. Moore at Crystal and Lorenzo Hubbell at Ganado influenced regional styles and motifs.

Technique and styles

Weaving is done on an upright loom with a continuous warp, producing a tapestry in which the weft completely covers the warp; traditional pieces have no fringe because the warp ends are woven back. Recognised regional styles include Two Grey Hills, Ganado red, Teec Nos Pos, Crystal, Wide Ruins (known for vegetal dyes and banded patterns) and "eye-dazzler" designs of the Germantown period. A famous motif is the yei figure drawn from sandpainting imagery. Fine contemporary weavings can take months to complete and command high prices; antique chief's blankets are among the most valuable Native American textiles.

Cultural significance

In Navajo tradition weaving was taught by Spider Woman, and the loom and its tools carry spiritual meaning. The craft remains an important source of income and cultural continuity, taught within families across the Navajo Nation.
