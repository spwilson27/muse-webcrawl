Banksia serrata

FamilyProteaceae
GenusBanksia
Common namesOld man banksia, saw banksia, saw-tooth banksia
RangeEastern Australia, from Queensland to Victoria, and Tasmania

Banksia serrata, commonly known as the old man banksia or saw banksia, is a species of woody shrub or tree in the genus Banksia in the family Proteaceae. Native to the east coast of Australia, it was among the first banksias collected by Joseph Banks at Botany Bay in 1770 and was formally described by Carl Linnaeus the Younger in 1782. It grows as a gnarled tree up to about 16 m tall, with serrated leaves and large cream flower spikes.

Description

Banksia serrata has a thick, rough, warty bark and dark green leaves, 8–24 cm long, with strongly toothed (serrate) margins. The cylindrical flower spikes, up to about 20 cm long, appear from summer to autumn and change from silvery-grey in bud to cream and gold as the individual flowers open. Old spikes ("cones") studded with persistent follicles are a familiar sight, and the plant regenerates after fire both from seed and from a lignotuber.

Ecology and cultivation

The species grows on sandy soils in coastal heath and dry sclerophyll forest from near Maryborough in Queensland south to Wilsons Promontory in Victoria, with a disjunct population in northern Tasmania. Its nectar-rich flowers attract honeyeaters, lorikeets, sugar gliders and insects. Widely grown in Australian gardens, it is valued as a hardy, bird-attracting tree; a dwarf form, "Pygmy Possum", is used as a groundcover. The gnarled old cones are thought to have inspired May Gibbs's "Banksia men" in the children's classic Snugglepot and Cuddlepie.
