Ablative case

In grammar, the ablative case ( Latin: casus ablativus, from ablatus, "carried away") is a grammatical case of nouns, pronouns and adjectives that expresses motion away from something, separation, source, or various related relations such as means, agent, cause and time. It is most famously a case of Latin, where it absorbed the functions of the Proto-Indo-European ablative, instrumental and locative cases.

In Latin

The Latin ablative has a wide range of uses, traditionally classified as the "true ablative" (separation: ab urbe, "from the city"), the instrumental ablative (means: gladio, "with a sword"; agent with a/ab in passives), and the locative ablative (place where and time when: nocte, "at night"). Other constructions include the ablative absolute — a noun and participle in the ablative forming a subordinate clause (urbe capta, "the city having been captured") — the ablative of comparison, of specification, of price and of quality.

In other languages

Sanskrit retains an ablative distinct from the instrumental and locative. Finnish and Hungarian use an ablative case (-lta/-ltä, -tól/-től) for motion from a surface or proximity. In English the term is occasionally applied to survivals in words such as "whence", and grammarians of languages like Quechua and Aymara use "ablative" for source markers. Modern descriptive linguistics often prefers finer-grained labels (elative, delative, egressive) for the meanings the classical ablative bundled together.
