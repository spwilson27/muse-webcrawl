Declarative memory

Declarative memory, also called explicit memory, is one of the two main divisions of long-term memory, the other being procedural (implicit) memory. Declarative memory is the conscious, intentional recollection of factual information, previous experiences, and concepts. It can be divided into two categories: episodic memory, which stores specific personal experiences, and semantic memory, which stores facts and general knowledge about the world.

History

The distinction between declarative and procedural memory emerged from studies of amnesic patients in the mid-20th century. The famous patient Henry Molaison (H.M.), who underwent bilateral medial temporal lobe resection in 1953, was unable to form new declarative memories yet could learn new motor skills, demonstrating that the two systems are dissociable. The terms "declarative" and "procedural" were popularized by cognitive psychologists including Larry Squire, who developed an influential taxonomy of memory systems.

Neural basis

Declarative memory depends critically on the medial temporal lobe system, including the hippocampus and surrounding entorhinal, perirhinal, and parahippocampal cortices, together with the diencephalon. Damage to these structures produces anterograde amnesia, an inability to form new declarative memories, while sparing skill learning. Over time, memories undergo systems consolidation, during which their storage becomes progressively more dependent on the neocortex.

Characteristics

Unlike procedural memory, declarative memories can be consciously recalled and "declared" verbally, are rapidly acquired (sometimes in a single trial), and are flexible — they can be applied in contexts different from the original learning situation. They are, however, vulnerable to forgetting and distortion, and their retrieval is influenced by encoding conditions, as captured by principles such as encoding specificity and levels of processing.
