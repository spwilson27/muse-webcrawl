Feasibility study

A feasibility study is an assessment of the practicality of a proposed plan, project, product, or system. It evaluates whether the proposal can be carried out successfully and whether it should be, taking into account technical, economic, legal, operational, and scheduling factors, and it usually concludes with a recommendation to proceed, modify, or abandon the proposal. Feasibility studies are standard practice before committing substantial resources to construction projects, new ventures, and major system developments.

Dimensions

A common framework, known by the acronym TELOS, examines five areas:

  • Technical feasibility — whether the required technology exists or can be developed, and whether the organisation has the expertise, equipment, and capacity to deliver it.
  • Economic feasibility — a cost–benefit analysis of development and operating costs against expected returns, including payback period and risk.
  • Legal feasibility — whether the proposal conflicts with laws, regulations, patents, zoning, or contractual obligations.
  • Operational feasibility — whether the solution fits the organisation's processes and culture and will actually be used by staff and customers.
  • Schedule feasibility — whether the project can be completed within the available time; a project that arrives too late may be worthless even if technically sound.

Studies in particular sectors add other dimensions: market feasibility for new products, resource feasibility for raw materials and staffing, and cultural or environmental feasibility for developments affecting communities.

Process and role

A study typically defines the proposal and its alternatives, gathers data on costs, markets, and constraints, analyses the alternatives against the feasibility criteria, and reports findings with a recommended course of action. In project management, the feasibility study belongs to the initiation phase, before detailed planning; a lighter preliminary version may be called a pre-feasibility study. Because studies are commissioned by proponents, their objectivity can be a concern — famously, feasibility studies for megaprojects have been criticised for systematic cost underestimation and benefit overstatement ("optimism bias"), leading some governments to require independent review and reference-class forecasting.
