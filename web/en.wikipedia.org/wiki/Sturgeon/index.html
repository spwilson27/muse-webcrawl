Sturgeon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sturgeon is the common name for the 28 species of fish belonging to the family Acipenseridae, among the oldest families of bony fish in existence, with fossils dating to the Late Cretaceous. Sturgeons are native to subtropical, temperate, and sub-Arctic rivers, lakes, and coastlines of Eurasia and North America. They are long-lived, late-maturing fishes with distinctive rows of bony plates (scutes) instead of scales, elongated bodies, and shark-like (heterocercal) tail fins.

Characteristics

Sturgeons are mostly bottom-feeders, using sensitive barbels in front of a protrusible, toothless mouth to detect molluscs, crustaceans, and small fish in murky water. Most species are anadromous, migrating from the sea into rivers to spawn, though some are entirely freshwater. The largest species, the beluga (Huso huso) of the Caspian and Black Sea basins, has been recorded at over 7 metres in length and more than 1,500 kilograms, making it one of the largest freshwater fishes in the world.

Caviar and conservation

Female sturgeons are the source of roe processed into caviar, with beluga caviar traditionally the most prized. Intense fishing pressure, poaching, damming of spawning rivers, and pollution caused catastrophic declines during the 20th century. Most sturgeon species are now classified as critically endangered, and international trade is regulated under CITES. Commercial demand is increasingly met by aquaculture, where sturgeon are farmed for both meat and caviar; farmed production has surpassed wild catch. Restoration programmes, including hatchery releases and fish-passage construction, are under way for species such as the Atlantic sturgeon and the lake sturgeon in North America and the European sturgeon in western Europe.
