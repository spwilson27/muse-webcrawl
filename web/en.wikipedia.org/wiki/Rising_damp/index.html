Rising damp

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Damage to interior plaster and paintwork caused by rising damp

Rising damp is a type of damp problem that affects the walls of buildings. It occurs when moisture from the ground rises up through the walls by capillary action — that is, through the tiny pores in masonry materials such as brick, stone and mortar. The moisture carries soluble salts from the ground and from the wall materials themselves, which are deposited as the water evaporates.

Identification

Rising damp is commonly identified by a "tide mark" on the lower part of internal walls, usually extending up to about one metre above floor level, accompanied by peeling paint and wallpaper, crumbling or "blown" plaster, and white salt deposits (efflorescence). Skirting boards and other timber in contact with the damp wall may show signs of rot.

Diagnosis can be confused with other forms of damp, such as condensation and penetrating damp, and specialists may use moisture meters and analysis of salts (particularly nitrates and chlorides, which indicate ground water) to confirm the source.

Causes

Most buildings are protected by a damp-proof course (DPC) — a barrier of impervious material built into the wall near ground level. Rising damp occurs where the DPC is absent (as in many older buildings), has failed or deteriorated, or has been "bridged" by raised ground levels, internal plaster, or render that allows moisture to bypass it.

Treatment

Treatment typically involves installing a new damp-proof course, most commonly by injecting a chemical damp-proofing cream or fluid into holes drilled along the mortar course, which lines the pores of the masonry and reduces capillary rise. Physical damp-proof courses may be cut into the wall in severe cases. Salt-contaminated plaster is usually removed and replaced with a salt-resistant render or plaster, since contaminated plaster continues to attract moisture from the air.

Debate

The extent and frequency of true rising damp has been debated among building professionals; some surveyors argue it is over-diagnosed and that many cases attributed to it are actually condensation or penetrating damp. Others maintain it is a genuine and common phenomenon in older buildings.
