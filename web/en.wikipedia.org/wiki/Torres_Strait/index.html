The Torres Strait is a strait between Australia and the Melanesian island of New Guinea, about 150 kilometres (93 miles) wide at its narrowest point. To the west lies the Arafura Sea and to the east the Coral Sea. The strait is dotted with the Torres Strait Islands, of which more than a dozen are inhabited by Torres Strait Islander peoples, whose distinct Melanesian culture differs from that of mainland Aboriginal Australians.

Geography

The strait is shallow and studded with reefs, shoals and islands, making navigation hazardous; a well-marked shipping channel, the Prince of Wales Channel, passes Thursday Island, the administrative centre of the region. The strait was formed when rising seas at the end of the last ice age flooded the land bridge (part of Sahul) between Australia and New Guinea roughly 8,000–10,000 years ago.

History

The strait is named after the Spanish navigator Luís Vaz de Torres, who in 1606 sailed through it while separating from the expedition of Pedro Fernandes de Queirós — the first recorded European navigation of the passage. In 1770 James Cook claimed the eastern coast of Australia for Britain after passing through the strait. Queensland annexed the islands progressively in the 1870s. A pearling industry flourished in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, drawing workers from Japan, the Pacific and Southeast Asia.

During the Second World War the strait was strategically important; Horn Island hosted an airbase and thousands of troops. In 1978 Australia and Papua New Guinea signed the Torres Strait Treaty, defining maritime boundaries and protecting the traditional way of life and free movement of the region's inhabitants.

People and culture

Torres Strait Islanders are recognised as a distinct Indigenous people of Australia, speaking languages including Meriam Mir and Kala Lagaw Ya, as well as Torres Strait Creole. Islander culture is known for its dance, headdresses (such as the feathered dhari), art and seafaring traditions. The 1992 Mabo decision of the High Court of Australia, originating with Murray Island (Mer) plaintiffs led by Eddie Koiki Mabo, overturned the doctrine of terra nullius and established native title in Australian law.
