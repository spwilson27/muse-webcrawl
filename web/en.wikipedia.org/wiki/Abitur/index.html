Abitur

The Abitur (from Latin abiturus, "one who is about to depart") is the German secondary-school leaving qualification, awarded after passing final examinations at a Gymnasium, usually after twelve or thirteen years of schooling. It confers the allgemeine Hochschulreife, the general higher-education entrance qualification, entitling the holder to study at any German university in any subject.

Examinations

In the final two years (the Qualifikationsphase), students take courses at basic and advanced levels; grades from these courses combine with results in final examinations — typically written papers in three subjects and an oral examination (or a presentation/"fifth component") in another — to produce the Abitur grade on a scale from 1.0 (best) to 4.0 (minimum pass), corresponding to a maximum of 900 or 840 points depending on the state. Education being a competence of the Länder, the structure and difficulty of the Abitur vary by state, a perennial controversy; several states have moved toward shared examination pools and more centralised exams.

History and equivalents

The Abitur was introduced in Prussia in 1788 and given its university-gatekeeping role in the 1830s under regulations associated with the Humboldtian reforms. Comparable qualifications include the Austrian and Swiss Matura, the French baccalauréat, and the Italian maturità; the Abitur is broadly comparable in level to British A-levels or Advanced Placement examinations, though it covers a wider subject range. A vocationally oriented variant, the fachgebundene Hochschulreife and Fachhochschulreife, grants more limited access.
