Fetch (geography)

The fetch, also called the fetch length, is the length of water over which a given wind has blown without obstruction. In oceanography and coastal geography, fetch is a primary determinant of the size of wind waves: the longer the fetch, the larger the waves the wind can generate, other factors being equal.

Role in wave formation

Wave height and period grow with wind speed, the duration for which the wind blows, and the fetch. A sea state is called "fetch-limited" when the distance available, rather than the duration of the storm, caps wave growth. Seas generated in small, enclosed bodies of water — lakes, bays, and narrow straits — remain small because the fetch is short, while oceanic storms blowing over thousands of kilometres can produce waves of ten metres or more. The concept underlies empirical forecasting methods such as the SMB (Sverdrup–Munk–Bretschneider) method used in coastal engineering.

Coastal effects

Fetch strongly influences coastal erosion and beach morphology. Coastlines exposed to long fetches — for example, shores facing the prevailing winds across an ocean — receive high-energy waves and tend to be erosional, while sheltered shores with short fetches accumulate sediment. On British coasts, for instance, south-western shores facing the Atlantic have fetches of thousands of kilometres, whereas parts of the English Channel coast have fetches of only a few kilometres to France. Fetch is therefore routinely measured in shoreline management studies, often as an average weighted over a range of wind directions.

Other uses

In lake limnology, fetch determines the depth of wind-driven mixing and the size of waves that can disturb shallow sediments. In meteorology the term is also used in snow science: the distance over which wind transports loose snow affects avalanche loading on lee slopes.
