Vehicle safety

Vehicle safety is the study and practice of design, construction, equipment and regulation to minimize the occurrence and consequences of traffic collisions involving motor vehicles. Road traffic safety more broadly includes roadway design.

Active and passive safety

Active safety refers to systems that help prevent a crash, including good visibility, braking and handling, anti-lock braking systems (ABS), electronic stability control, traction control, and advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS) such as automatic emergency braking, lane-keeping assistance, and blind-spot monitoring.

Passive safety refers to features that protect occupants during a crash: crumple zones that absorb energy, a rigid passenger safety cell, seat belts (including pretensioners and load limiters), airbags, head restraints, and child safety seats.

History

Early automobiles offered almost no occupant protection. Milestones include the introduction of laminated safety glass, padded dashboards, and the three-point seat belt, patented by Volvo engineer Nils Bohlin in 1959 and shared freely with other manufacturers. Consumer crash-test programmes — the U.S. New Car Assessment Program (NCAP) from 1979 and Euro NCAP from 1997 — used star ratings to pressure manufacturers to exceed minimum legal standards.

Regulation and testing

Vehicle safety is regulated by bodies such as the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) and through United Nations WP.29 regulations in much of the rest of the world. Compliance is assessed with instrumented crash tests using anthropomorphic dummies in frontal, side, offset, rollover, and pole impacts.

Statistics

Worldwide, road traffic injuries kill well over one million people per year, though fatality rates per distance travelled have fallen dramatically in high-income countries since the 1970s thanks to safer vehicles, seat-belt and drink-driving laws, and improved roads and trauma care.
