Cousin primes

In number theory, cousin primes are pairs of prime numbers that differ by four: that is, primes p and p + 4 are both prime. The name plays on the family terminology of prime gaps: twin primes differ by two, cousin primes by four, and "sexy primes" (from Latin sex, six) by six.

Examples

The first cousin prime pairs are:

(3, 7), (7, 11), (13, 17), (19, 23), (37, 41), (43, 47), (67, 71), (79, 83), (97, 101), (103, 107), (109, 113), (127, 131), (163, 167), (193, 197), (223, 227), (229, 233), (277, 281), and many more.

Note that 7 is a member of two cousin pairs, (3, 7) and (7, 11); apart from (3, 7), one member of each cousin pair is congruent to 1 mod 6 in a pattern analogous to that for twin primes: of any three numbers p, p + 2, p + 4 considerations aside, cousin pairs (other than the first) consist of primes of forms 6n + 1 and 6n + 5 arranged around multiples of six.

Properties and conjectures

It is conjectured, but not proved, that there are infinitely many cousin prime pairs — a special case of Polignac's conjecture and of the Hardy–Littlewood prime k-tuple conjecture, which predicts an asymptotic count closely parallel to that for twin primes, with the same Hardy–Littlewood constant. Brun's theorem implies that the sum of the reciprocals of cousin primes converges. As with twin primes, large cousin pairs have been found computationally with hundreds of thousands of digits.
