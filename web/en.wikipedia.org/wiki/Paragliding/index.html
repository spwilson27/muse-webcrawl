Paragliding

Paragliding is the recreational and competitive adventure sport of flying paragliders: lightweight, free-flying, foot-launched glider aircraft with no rigid primary structure. The pilot sits in a harness beneath a fabric wing — a ram-air aerofoil inflated by air entering vents at the leading edge — and controls the craft with brake lines, weight shift and a speed bar. Flights are launched from hills or by towing, and can last for hours, climbing in thermals and travelling cross-country for hundreds of kilometres.

History

The sport grew out of parachuting and hang gliding. Slope-soaring with modified parachutes began in the late 1970s, notably at Mieussy in France, and through the 1980s purpose-built wings, pioneered in France and Switzerland, rapidly improved glide performance. The first Paragliding World Championships were held in 1989. Modern wings reach glide ratios around 10:1, and distance records exceed 600 kilometres.

Equipment and safety

Equipment consists of the wing, harness (usually with a reserve parachute and back protection), helmet, and instruments such as a variometer and GPS. Wings are certified by performance and safety classes (EN/LTF ratings). Pilots train at schools, learning launch and landing techniques, active flying to prevent collapses, and emergency manoeuvres; weather assessment is a core skill, as paragliders are slow and vulnerable to strong winds and turbulence.
