CAR T cell

A chimeric antigen receptor T cell (also known as a CAR T cell) is a T cell that has been genetically engineered to produce an artificial T-cell receptor for use in immunotherapy. Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) are receptor proteins engineered to give T cells the ability to target a specific antigen; the receptor combines an extracellular antigen-binding domain (usually derived from an antibody) with intracellular T-cell signalling domains.

Treatment process

T cells are collected from the patient's blood by leukapheresis, genetically modified — typically with a viral vector — to express the CAR, expanded in the laboratory, and infused back into the patient after lymphodepleting chemotherapy. The engineered cells then seek out and kill cells bearing the target antigen.

Clinical use

The first CAR T-cell therapies were approved by the US FDA in 2017 for relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, targeting CD19. Later products target BCMA in multiple myeloma. Response rates in otherwise incurable cancers have been striking, though many patients relapse, sometimes through loss of the target antigen.

Safety

Serious adverse effects include cytokine release syndrome, a potentially fatal systemic inflammatory response, and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), managed with tocilizumab and corticosteroids.
