Monarchy of the North

The Monarchy of the North (Portuguese: Monarquia do Norte) was a short-lived counter-revolutionary restoration of the Portuguese monarchy proclaimed in Porto on 19 January 1919, during the First Portuguese Republic. Royalist forces under Henrique Mitchell de Paiva Couceiro seized control of much of northern Portugal and proclaimed the restoration of King Manuel II, but the movement collapsed within a month, by 13 February 1919.

Background

Portugal had become a republic after the revolution of 5 October 1910, which deposed Manuel II. Royalists never accepted the republic and mounted several incursions from Spain. The assassination of President Sidónio Pais in December 1918 left the republic in chaos, and monarchists judged the moment ripe for a rising.

The rising

On 19 January 1919, Paiva Couceiro's forces occupied Porto and raised the blue-and-white flag of the monarchy. A Governing Junta of the Kingdom was formed under Paiva Couceiro, claiming to govern in the name of the exiled king. The movement controlled much of the north, including Viana do Castelo, Braga and Vila Real, but a simultaneous royalist proclamation in Lisbon failed, and republican forces retained the capital and its arsenal.

Collapse

The royalists suffered from poor coordination, internal divisions between supporters of Manuel II and Miguelist legitimists, and the king's own refusal to sanction the adventure from exile. Republican troops counter-attacked, and on the night of 12–13 February 1919 royalist resistance in Porto collapsed after fighting on the Monte Pedral. Paiva Couceiro fled into exile in Spain.

Aftermath

The episode was the last serious armed attempt to restore the Portuguese monarchy. Participants faced trials and imprisonment, though many were later amnestied. Manuel II remained in exile in England until his death in 1932.
