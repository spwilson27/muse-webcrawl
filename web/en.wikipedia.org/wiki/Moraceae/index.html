The Moraceae — often called the mulberry family or fig family — are a family of flowering plants comprising about 40 genera and over 1,100 species, broadly distributed in tropical and subtropical regions, with a few in temperate climates. Most are trees and shrubs, though some are herbaceous. Members typically contain a milky latex and bear unusual fruit structures, including multiple fruits and syconia.

Characteristics

Plants in the family usually have alternate, simple leaves with stipules, small unisexual flowers borne in dense inflorescences (heads, spikes, or enclosed in a hollow receptacle, as in figs), and a milky sap. Fruits range from achenes and drupes to the multiple fruit of mulberry and jackfruit and the syconium of Ficus. Figs have a famous obligate mutualism with fig wasps, which pollinate them.

Important genera and uses

The largest genus is Ficus (about 850 species: figs, banyans, and the common fig), followed by Dorstenia. Economically important members include mulberry (Morus), whose leaves feed silkworms and whose fruit is eaten; breadfruit and jackfruit (Artocarpus); the paper mulberry (Broussonetia), used for barkcloth and paper; hemp relative Cannabis is not in this family (it is Cannabaceae) though historically the families were grouped together. Several Moraceae are important timber trees, ornamentals (rubber plant, Ficus elastica), and the source of natural rubber substitutes and dyes.
