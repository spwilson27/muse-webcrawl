Central bank

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A central bank is a public institution that manages the currency and monetary policy of a country or monetary union, and oversees its commercial banking system. Central banks typically hold a monopoly on issuing banknotes, act as banker to the government and to commercial banks, serve as lender of last resort in crises, and pursue price stability and, in some mandates, full employment or financial stability.

History

The Sveriges Riksbank (1668) and the Bank of England (1694) are the oldest central banks; the US Federal Reserve was created in 1913 after recurrent banking panics; the European Central Bank (1998) conducts monetary policy for the euro area. The twentieth century moved many central banks from treasury departments to operational independence — a reform wave of the 1990s (New Zealand 1989, the Bank of England 1997, the Bank of Japan 1998) based on evidence that independent banks with inflation targets delivered lower inflation without worse growth.

Functions and debates

Instruments include policy interest rates, open-market operations, reserve requirements and, since 2008, quantitative easing and macroprudential tools. Debates concern the democratic accountability of unelected technocrats, the limits of monetary policy at low interest rates, central-bank responses to climate and inequality, and digital currencies (CBDCs) that many banks are researching or piloting. In international politics, central banks also cooperate through the Bank for International Settlements and swap lines during crises.
