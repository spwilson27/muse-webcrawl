Intergroup relations

Intergroup relations examines how groups perceive and act toward one another.

Intergroup relations refers to interactions between individuals in different social groups, and to the interactions taking place between the groups themselves. A field of social psychology, it encompasses the study of prejudice, stereotyping, discrimination, intergroup conflict, and the conditions under which intergroup harmony can be achieved.

Major theories

Realistic conflict theory, developed from Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment, proposes that conflict between groups arises when they compete for scarce resources, and can be reduced by superordinate goals that require cooperation. Social identity theory, developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner, holds that people derive part of their self-concept from group memberships, and that even arbitrary "minimal groups" produce in-group favouritism as people seek a positive social identity. Integrated threat theory distinguishes realistic threats, symbolic threats, intergroup anxiety, and negative stereotypes as sources of prejudice.

Intergroup bias

Intergroup bias refers to the systematic tendency to evaluate one's own group and its members more favourably than an outgroup. It can manifest as in-group favouritism — preferential treatment of in-group members — without necessarily involving hostility toward outgroups, although the two often co-occur.

Reducing intergroup conflict

Approaches to improving intergroup relations include decategorization (emphasizing individuals), recategorization into a common in-group identity, crossed categorization, and structured intergroup contact. The intergroup contact theory of Gordon Allport holds that contact reduces prejudice when groups meet under conditions of equal status, cooperation, common goals, and institutional support.
