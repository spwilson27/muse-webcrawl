Phlegraean Fields

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Phlegraean Fields (Italian: Campi Flegrei, "burning fields") is a large volcanic caldera system west of Naples, Italy. It is a nested caldera about 13 km across, formed by enormous eruptions roughly 39,000 years ago (the Campanian Ignimbrite eruption, one of the largest known in Europe) and about 15,000 years ago (the Neapolitan Yellow Tuff). Much of the caldera lies under the Gulf of Pozzuoli, and its landward part includes the towns of Pozzuoli, Bacoli and Monte di Procida, home to several hundred thousand people.

Volcanism

The area contains two dozen craters and volcanic edifices. The Solfatara crater at Pozzuoli emits jets of steam and sulfurous fumaroles; Lake Avernus occupies a crater that Romans regarded as an entrance to the underworld. The most recent eruption, in 1538, built the cone of Monte Nuovo in about a week and destroyed the village of Tripergole.

Bradyseism

The caldera floor undergoes slow vertical movement — bradyseism — recorded since antiquity in the bored columns of the Roman market (macellum) at Pozzuoli, which show marine mollusc borings above sea level. In 1982–84 the ground at Pozzuoli rose by about 1.8 metres and tens of thousands of small earthquakes forced the evacuation of some 40,000 residents from the old town. Renewed uplift since the mid-2000s, with swarms of earthquakes, has kept the alert level under close scientific scrutiny.

History

The area was settled by Greeks at Cumae, their first colony on the Italian mainland, and under Rome the coast became a fashionable resort (Baiae) with ports at Misenum, base of the imperial fleet, and Puteoli (Pozzuoli).
