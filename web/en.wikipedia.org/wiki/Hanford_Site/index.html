Hanford Site

The Hanford Site is a decommissioned nuclear production complex occupying 586 square miles (about 1,500 km²) in Benton County, Washington, along the Columbia River. Established in 1943 as part of the Manhattan Project, it was the site of the B Reactor, the first full-scale plutonium production reactor in the world.

History

The site was chosen for its isolation, abundant hydroelectric power and cold river water for cooling. Residents of Hanford, White Bluffs and Richland were displaced as some 51,000 workers built the complex. Hanford produced the plutonium used in the Trinity test and in the "Fat Man" bomb detonated over Nagasaki. Production expanded during the Cold War to nine reactors and five plutonium-processing plants, making Hanford the source of most of the plutonium for the US nuclear arsenal; the last reactor, N Reactor, shut down in 1987.

Cleanup

Decades of production left enormous quantities of radioactive and chemical waste, including 177 underground storage tanks holding roughly 56 million gallons of high-level waste, contaminated groundwater and burial grounds. Hanford is now the focus of one of the largest and most expensive environmental cleanup projects in the world, conducted by the US Department of Energy under a 1989 Tri-Party Agreement with the EPA and Washington State. Portions of the site, including the B Reactor, form part of the Manhattan Project National Historical Park.
