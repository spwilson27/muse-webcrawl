Angular diameter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The angular diameter, also called the angular size or apparent size, is an angular measurement describing how large a sphere or circle appears from a given point of view. It is the angle subtended by the object at the observer's position, and it depends both on the object's true physical diameter and on its distance from the observer.

QuantityAngle

Formula

For an object of diameter d at distance D from the observer, the angular diameter is δ = 2 arctan(d / 2D). When the object is much farther away than it is large, the small-angle approximation gives δ ≈ d / D in radians. In practice, angular diameters in astronomy are often measured in arcseconds or milliarcseconds.

In astronomy

Angular diameter is a fundamental observable in astronomy because most celestial objects are too distant for their true sizes to be measured directly. The Sun and the Moon both have angular diameters of about half a degree as seen from Earth — a coincidence that makes total solar eclipses possible. The planets range from a few arcseconds (distant Neptune) to about an arcminute for Venus at its closest approach. Stars are so remote that their angular diameters are typically measured in milliarcseconds, requiring interferometry; among the largest in apparent size are Betelgeuse and Antares, at roughly 40–50 milliarcseconds.

Combining a measured angular diameter with a known distance yields an object's physical size. Conversely, for objects of known size — the basis of the angular diameter distance method — the angular size gives an estimate of distance, a technique used in cosmology. At very large distances, relativistic expansion makes angular diameter distance behave non-intuitively: beyond a certain redshift, objects of fixed size appear larger again as distance increases.

Human vision

The angular diameter of an object is closely related to the size of the image it projects on the retina. The Moon subtends roughly the same angle as a coin held at arm's length, and the apparent enlargement of the Moon near the horizon — the Moon illusion — occurs even though its angular diameter is essentially unchanged.
