Bronze medal

A bronze medal in sports and other similar areas involving competition is a medal made of bronze awarded to the third-place finisher of contests or competitions such as the Olympic Games, Commonwealth Games, etc. The outright winner receives a gold medal and the second place a silver medal.

A bronze medal denotes third place.

History

The convention of gold, silver and bronze descends from the ages of man in Greek mythology and was loosely mirrored in military awards. At the first modern Olympic Games in 1896, winners received silver and runners-up bronze; gold was introduced in 1904, when the now-standard three-medal system became established. The medals themselves are rarely solid bronze; Olympic bronze medals are typically an alloy of copper and zinc (red brass), and modern "gold" medals are mostly silver with gold plating.

Bronze-medal matches

In knockout tournaments and combat sports, third place may be decided by a bronze-medal match or play-off between the losing semi-finalists — as in the FIFA World Cup third-place play-off. In Olympic judo, boxing and taekwondo, two bronze medals are awarded per event, one to each of two repechage or semi-final losers. In some sports, such as Olympic boxing, both losing semi-finalists receive bronze without a play-off.

Psychology

A well-known 1995 study by Medvec, Madey and Gilovich found that bronze medallists often appear happier than silver medallists, because silver medallists compare themselves upward to the gold they narrowly missed, while bronze medallists compare downward to finishing with no medal at all.
