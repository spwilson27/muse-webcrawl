DVD+RW is a rewritable optical disc format based on CD-RW technology, created by the DVD+RW Alliance, a consortium led by Philips, Sony and Hewlett-Packard. A standard single-layer DVD+RW disc holds 4.7 GB of data and can be rewritten roughly 1,000 times. The format was introduced in 2001 as a rival to the DVD Forum's DVD-RW and to DVD-RAM, in the so-called "format war" among rewritable DVD standards.

Technical features

DVD+RW uses phase-change recording and supports "lossless linking", which allows editing and replacement of data in 32 KB blocks without writing guard zones, making random write access more efficient than on DVD-RW. Background formatting lets a partially formatted disc be used quickly while formatting completes in the background. The companion write-once format is DVD+R. Because the +RW format was not sanctioned by the DVD Forum, drives and discs originally could not use the official DVD logo, though in practice most players read the discs, especially when the recorder sets the "book type" field to identify the disc as DVD-ROM.

DVD+RW DL and legacy

A dual-layer variant, DVD+RW DL, with 8.5 GB capacity, was demonstrated but saw minimal commercial release. As flash memory, hard drives and network storage displaced rewritable optical media during the 2000s and 2010s, DVD+RW production wound down, though the discs remain in use for archival and legacy recording tasks.
