Non-lethal weapon


Labelled placeholder: less-lethal weapon use

Non-lethal weapons, also called nonlethal weapons, less-lethal weapons, less-than-lethal weapons, non-deadly weapons, compliance weapons, or pain-inducing weapons are weapons intended to be less likely to kill a living target than conventional weapons such as knives and firearms with live ammunition. It is often understood that unintended or incidental casualties are risked wherever force is applied; however, non-lethal weapons attempt to minimise the risk of casualties (e.g. serious/permanent injuries or deaths) as compared with lethal weapons.

Non-lethal weapons are used in policing and combat situations to limit the escalation of conflict where employment of lethal force is prohibited or undesirable, in riot control, where there are innocent bystanders present, or in situations where the rules of engagement require minimum force.

History

Prior to the development of modern non-lethal weapons, police officers used flexible billy clubs or sap gloves, and riot-control relied on fire hoses and police dogs. Research into non-lethal alternatives expanded in the late 20th century; the United States established a Joint Non-Lethal Weapons Program in the 1990s.

Types

  • Chemical agents: tear gas (CS gas), pepper spray (OC), malodorants.
  • Conducted energy weapons: electroshock devices such as Tasers.
  • Kinetic impact projectiles: rubber bullets, bean bag rounds, sponge grenades.
  • Directed energy: the Active Denial System (millimetre-wave), dazzling lasers.
  • Acoustic devices: long-range acoustic devices (LRAD) and acoustic hailing devices.
  • Other: water cannon, stun grenades, nets, and entangling agents.

Effectiveness and risks

While formally "non-lethal," such weapons can still cause serious injury or death — for example when kinetic projectiles strike the head or chest, or when chemical agents are used in enclosed spaces or on vulnerable people. The term "less-lethal" is thus often preferred by manufacturers and policy documents.
