Country music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A fiddle, banjo and guitar on the stage of the Grand Ole Opry in Nashville, in a still of country's institutional home]

Country music is a genre of American popular music originating in the rural Southern United States in the 1920s, from the folk ballads, fiddle tunes and hymns of Appalachia mixed with blues and western swing. The 1927 Bristol sessions — the 'big bang of country music' — launched its first stars, the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers, and Nashville's Grand Ole Opry its permanent capital.

Through honky-tonk (Hank Williams), the Nashville sound (Patsy Cline), outlaw country (Willie Nelson, Waylon Jennings), country rock and the stadium era of Garth Brooks, Shania Twain and Taylor Swift, country has remained American music's storyteller — and, in recurring arguments from Ray Charles's country albums to Beyoncé's Cowboy Carter (2024), its contested borderland.

History in outline

Radio barn dances and race records built the industry in the twenties; the Depression made the singing cowboy and the Carter Family's 'Wildwood Flower' national property. Postwar honky-tonk electrified the music; Chet Atkins's Nashville sound sweetened it for pop; the outlaws of the seventies roughened it again. Every cycle since — neo-traditionalism, the 1990s boom, bro-country, Americana — has replayed the genre's founding argument between roots and reach.

Institutions

The Country Music Association (founded 1958), the CMA Awards, the Country Music Hall of Fame and the Opry (the longest-running radio broadcast in US history) make Nashville an unusually institutionalised genre capital; its songwriting economy — Music Row's publishing houses — remains the industry's model of craft professionalism.
