Steeplechase (horse racing)

A steeplechase is a distance horse race in which competitors are required to jump over diverse fence and ditch obstacles. Originating in Ireland in the 18th century, the sport takes its name from early races in which riders raced cross-country toward a church steeple, jumping fences and negotiating whatever obstacles the landscape presented. Steeplechasing is most prominent in Britain, Ireland, France, and the United States, and is known in Britain and Ireland as National Hunt racing.

History

The first recorded steeplechase is traditionally dated to 1752, when Cornelius O'Callaghan and Edmund Blake wagered on a race of about 4.5 miles cross-country in County Cork, Ireland, from the church steeple of Buttevant to that of Doneraile, negotiating stone walls, ditches, and streams along the way. Cross-country match races spread through Ireland and Britain, and the sport became organized in the 19th century under the National Hunt Committee. The Grand National, first run at Aintree in 1839, became the sport's most famous race.

Types of races

In modern National Hunt racing, horses jump one of two kinds of obstacle. Hurdles are smaller, flexible barriers at least 3 feet 6 inches (1.07 m) high, while steeplechase fences are larger, rigid obstacles at least 4 feet 6 inches (1.37 m) high, often incorporating ditches (open ditches) or water jumps. Chases are generally longer than hurdle races and test stamina as well as jumping. Novice hurdlers frequently progress to chasing later in their careers. Related formats include cross-country chases run over varied natural terrain, and in France cross-country races at courses such as Pau.

Major races

The Grand National at Aintree, run over 4 miles 2½ furlongs with 30 fences, is the most valuable jump race in Europe and is watched worldwide. The Cheltenham Festival hosts the Gold Cup, Champion Hurdle, and Queen Mother Champion Chase. Ireland's premier events include the Irish Grand National at Fairyhouse and the Punchestown Festival, while the Velká pardubická in the Czech Republic is continental Europe's best-known steeplechase. In the United States, the richest steeplechase is the Grand National Hurdle Stakes at Far Hills, New Jersey.

Horses and safety

Steeplechasers are usually mature horses, often geldings, bred or selected for stamina and jumping ability; because the horses jump at racing speed over substantial fences, the sport carries elevated risk of injury to both horses and riders. Animal-welfare criticism has prompted reforms including modified fence construction, safer brush hurdles, reduced field sizes, and stricter veterinary checks. Champion jump jockeys such as A. P. McCoy, Ruby Walsh, and Richard Dunwoody became among the best-known sports figures in Britain and Ireland.
