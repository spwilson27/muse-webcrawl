Twelve Apostles

In Christian theology and ecclesiology, the apostles, particularly the Twelve Apostles (also called the Twelve Disciples or simply the Apostles), were the primary disciples of Jesus according to the New Testament. Jesus chose twelve men during his ministry to be his closest followers, witnesses to his resurrection, and leaders of the early Church.

The Twelve

The canonical lists (Matthew 10, Mark 3, Luke 6, Acts 1) name: Simon Peter, Andrew, James son of Zebedee, John, Philip, Bartholomew, Thomas, Matthew the tax collector, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus (Jude), Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot. After Judas's betrayal and death, Matthias was chosen by lot to replace him (Acts 1:26).

Mission

According to the Gospels, Jesus sent the Twelve out to preach, heal the sick, and cast out demons. After the Resurrection, the Great Commission charged them with making disciples of all nations. Tradition holds that most were martyred for their faith, spreading Christianity across the Roman world and beyond — Peter and Paul in Rome, Thomas, by tradition, in India, James in Jerusalem.

Theology

The number twelve evokes the twelve tribes of Israel, symbolising the renewal of God's people. In Revelation, the foundations of the New Jerusalem bear the names of the twelve apostles. Churches teaching apostolic succession trace their bishops' authority to ordination by the apostles.
