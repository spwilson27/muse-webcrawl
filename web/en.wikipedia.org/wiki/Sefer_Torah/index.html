Sefer Torah

A Sefer Torah (Hebrew: סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה, "Book of the Torah"; plural Sifrei Torah) is a handwritten copy of the Torah, the five books of Moses, produced by a religious scribe (sofer) on parchment (klaf) using a quill and special ink, following strict rules governing letter forms, spacing, and materials. It is the most sacred object in Jewish religious life and is used for public Torah reading in synagogue services.

Production

A Sefer Torah contains 304,805 letters and typically takes a trained scribe between one and two years to complete. The parchment is made from the skin of a kosher animal, and the sheets are sewn together with sinew thread and mounted on wooden rollers (atzei chaim, "trees of life"). A single error in a letter can render the scroll pasul (unfit) until corrected. Ashkenazi scrolls are written in the Ashkenazi script, while Sephardi and Yemenite communities maintain their own scribal traditions.

Use and adornment

The scroll is stored in the Torah ark (aron hakodesh) of the synagogue and is dressed with a cloth mantle, a breastplate, a crown or finials (rimmonim), and read with the aid of a pointer (yad). Sephardi scrolls are often housed in a rigid decorative case (tik). During services the scroll is carried in procession and read on a regular cycle, completing the Torah annually (or over three years in some communities) culminating in the festival of Simchat Torah.

Completion and dedication

The commandment to write a Sefer Torah is counted as the last of the 613 commandments. Completing a new scroll is celebrated with a siyum ceremony in which community members are honoured with writing the final letters, followed by processions with music and dancing (hachnasat sefer Torah). If a scroll is damaged beyond repair, it is buried in a Jewish cemetery.
