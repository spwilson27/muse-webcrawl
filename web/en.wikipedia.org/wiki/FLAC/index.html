FLAC (the Free Lossless Audio Codec) is an audio coding format for lossless compression of digital audio. Developed by Josh Coalson and released in 2001, later maintained under the Xiph.Org Foundation, FLAC compresses audio to roughly half its original size with no loss of information: decoding reproduces the original PCM data bit-for-bit. It is royalty-free and open, and has become the de facto standard for lossless music distribution and archiving.

Technical features

FLAC works by interchannel decorrelation (for stereo) followed by linear predictive coding of each channel and Rice coding of the residual signal. It supports sample depths of 4–32 bits, sample rates up to 655.35 kHz, and up to 8 channels. The format is designed for fast decoding and streaming: frames are independently decodable, seekable, and each frame carries a CRC check, while the full stream embeds an MD5 checksum of the uncompressed audio for verification. Native FLAC files use the Vorbis comment metadata system; FLAC may also be carried in Ogg or Matroska containers.

Comparison and adoption

Compared with lossy formats (MP3, AAC, Opus), FLAC files are much larger but preserve full quality; compared with uncompressed WAV or AIFF they save substantial space; its main rivals are ALAC (Apple Lossless), WavPack, Monkey's Audio, and TTA. FLAC is supported by most media players, many streaming and download stores (as the standard lossless offering), car and home audio systems, and most modern web browsers and Android devices. In 2017 FLAC decoding was incorporated into HTML5-era browser stacks, and the format was later adopted as an IETF standards-track specification (RFC 9639).
