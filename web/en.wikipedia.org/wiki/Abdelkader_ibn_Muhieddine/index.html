Abdelkader ibn Muhieddine (Arabic: عبد القادر ابن محي الدين; September 6, 1808 – May 26, 1883), usually known as Emir Abdelkader or Abdelkader El Hassani El Djazairi, was an Algerian religious and military leader who led a long resistance (1832–1847) against the French conquest of Algeria. In exile in Damascus he became internationally famous in 1860 for rescuing thousands of Christians during sectarian violence.

Resistance to France

A scholar of the Qadiriyya Sufi order whose father led a zawiya near Mascara, Abdelkader was proclaimed emir (commander of the faithful) by western Algerian tribes in 1832, two years after the French landing at Algiers. He built a proto-state with a capital, a regular army, taxation, diplomacy and mints, alternating warfare with treaties — the Desmichels Treaty (1834) and the Treaty of Tafna (1837), which recognised his rule over much of the interior. War resumed in 1839; under Governor-General Bugeaud the French applied scorched-earth tactics (razzia), destroyed his mobile capital (the smala, captured in 1843), and drove him into Morocco, whose army France defeated at Isly in 1844. He surrendered in December 1847.

Exile

Despite promises of passage to the Middle East, Abdelkader was imprisoned in France (at Pau and Amboise) until Louis-Napoleon released him in 1852. He settled in Damascus in 1855, devoting himself to scholarship and Sufi teaching.

Damascus, 1860

During the Druze–Maronite conflict that spread to Damascus in July 1860, mobs attacked the Christian quarter. Abdelkader and his Algerian followers sheltered thousands of Christians in his residence and the citadel, at personal risk, saving among others foreign consuls and the future notables of the city. He was honoured worldwide — awarded the Grand Cross of the Légion d'honneur by France, decorated by other powers, and praised by Abraham Lincoln, who sent him a pair of engraved pistols.

Legacy

Abdelkader died in Damascus in 1883; his remains were repatriated to Algiers in 1966 and reburied in El Alia Cemetery. He is honoured in Algeria as a national hero and founder-figure of the Algerian state; the town of El Kader, Iowa, is named after him.
