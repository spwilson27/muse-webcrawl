Civil service examination

Civil service examinations are examinations implemented in various countries for recruitment and admission to the civil service. They are intended to ensure merit-based selection of government officials, replacing appointment by patronage or inheritance.

History

The earliest and most influential system was the imperial examination of China, developed from the Han and Sui dynasties and fully institutionalised under the Song dynasty. For more than a thousand years it selected scholar-officials through competitive written tests on the Confucian classics, and it strongly influenced examination systems elsewhere in East Asia, including Korea, Vietnam and Japan.

In the 19th century, European and American reformers adopted competitive examinations on similar principles: the Northcote–Trevelyan Report (1854) in Britain and the Pendleton Act (1883) in the United States established modern merit systems, and British reforms were themselves partly inspired by the Chinese model and by the Indian Civil Service examinations.

Modern systems

Today civil service examinations are used in many countries. India's Civil Services Examination, conducted by the UPSC, fills posts in the IAS, IPS and other services. China's National Civil Service Examination attracts millions of applicants annually. Japan, South Korea, France (through its grandes écoles and concours) and the European Union (EPSO competitions) operate comparable systems. Such examinations are praised for fairness and openness but also criticised for encouraging rote preparation and high-pressure cram industries.
