Siege of Ladysmith

The Siege of Ladysmith was a protracted engagement in the Second Boer War, fought in the town of Ladysmith, Natal, in modern-day South Africa. British forces under Lieutenant General Sir George White were besieged by Boer commandos of the South African Republic and the Orange Free State from 2 November 1899 until the town was relieved on 28 February 1900, after 118 days.

Background

After initial British defeats in northern Natal in October 1899, including the disaster at Nicholson's Nek, White's field force fell back into Ladysmith, a rail junction town, and was invested by Boer forces under Commandant-General Piet Joubert. Rather than storming the town, the Boers settled into a blockade, shelling it intermittently with heavy artillery including the famous "Long Tom" guns, while the bulk of their forces moved south to block relief at the Tugela River.

The siege

Inside Ladysmith, food ran short, rations were cut repeatedly, and horseflesh ("chevril" soup was made from boiled horse) supplemented dwindling supplies. Disease, especially enteric fever and dysentery, killed more men than Boer fire; some 800 of the garrison and townspeople died, the great majority from disease. The Boers made one major assault, on 6 January 1900, attacking the southern defences at Wagon Hill and Caesar's Camp; after a day of close fighting the British held their positions, at considerable cost.

Relief

General Sir Redvers Buller's relief force fought a costly series of battles on the Tugela — Colenso, Spion Kop, and Vaal Krantz — before finally breaking through at the Battle of the Tugela Heights in late February 1900. The Boers lifted the siege, and Buller's cavalry entered Ladysmith on 28 February, the same day news arrived of the surrender of Piet Cronjé at Paardeberg. The relief, together with those of Kimberley and Mafeking, became a celebrated episode of the war in Britain.
