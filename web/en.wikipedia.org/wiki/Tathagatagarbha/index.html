Tathāgatagarbha

[Image: The tathāgatagarbha is likened to a treasure hidden within each being]

Tathāgatagarbha (Sanskrit: तथागतगर्भ, "embryo/womb/essence of the Tathāgata") is a central doctrine of Mahāyāna Buddhism teaching that all sentient beings possess within them the inherent potential for Buddhahood, commonly translated as Buddha-nature. The doctrine is expounded in a group of texts known as the tathāgatagarbha sūtras, composed in India from roughly the 3rd century CE.

Origins and texts

The earliest text of the tradition is the Tathāgatagarbha Sūtra, which illustrates the doctrine with nine similes: a Buddha image inside a withered lotus, honey surrounded by bees, a kernel inside its husk, gold fallen into filth, a treasure buried under a poor man's house, a sprout inside a seed, a golden statue wrapped in rags, a universal monarch in a destitute woman's womb, and a golden image inside a clay mould. Later influential works include the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra, the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, and the treatise Ratnagotravibhāga (Uttaratantra).

Interpretations

Interpretations vary widely. In some readings the garbha is an unconditioned, luminous reality veiled by adventitious defilements; in others it is identified chiefly with emptiness or with the mind's potential to awaken. In East Asian Buddhism the doctrine deeply influenced the Tiantai, Huayan, Chan and Pure Land schools, where it underwrote the idea that even the deluded layperson already participates in awakening. In Tibet, debates between rangtong ("empty of self") and shentong ("empty of other") understandings of Buddha-nature became a major axis of philosophical dispute.

Criticism

Some modern scholars, particularly in the Japanese "Critical Buddhism" movement, have argued that tathāgatagarbha thought resembles a substantialist ātman doctrine incompatible with early Buddhist teachings of non-self; others defend it as a skilful means (upāya) expressing the universality of liberation.
