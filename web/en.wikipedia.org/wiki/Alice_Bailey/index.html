Alice Ann Bailey (16 June 1880 – 15 December 1949) was a writer of more than twenty-four books on theosophical subjects, one of the first writers to use the term New Age. Born in Manchester, England, she moved to the United States in 1907, where she became a prominent — and controversial — figure in the Theosophical movement.

Early life

Born into a wealthy family, Bailey was orphaned young and raised in a strict Anglican environment. She worked as an evangelist and missionary in India before an unhappy first marriage took her to California, where she joined the Theosophical Society in 1915. Influenced by the works of Helena Blavatsky, she rose quickly within the society but clashed with its leadership under Annie Besant.

Writings

In 1919 Bailey claimed to begin telepathic communication with a "Master of the Wisdom" she called the Tibetan, identified as Djwal Khul. Over thirty years she produced a large body of work she attributed to this dictation, including A Treatise on Cosmic Fire (1925), A Treatise on White Magic (1934) and the five-volume A Treatise on the Seven Rays. Her system blended Theosophy with astrology, healing, psychology and a doctrine of spiritual hierarchy and world service.

Organisations

Expelled from the Theosophical Society in the early 1920s, Bailey and her husband Foster Bailey founded the Lucis Trust (1922) and the Arcane School (1923) in New York to disseminate the teachings. She also promoted the "Great Invocation", a prayer she released in 1945, and supported the founding of the United Nations, which her followers regard as significant in the externalisation of the spiritual hierarchy.

Reception

Bailey's books remain in print and influential within esoteric and New Age circles. Critics have noted antisemitic and racial passages in some works, which her organisations contextualise as products of their era; the writings continue to attract both devoted students and scholarly scrutiny of early twentieth-century occultism.
