Masahiko Kimura (木村 政彦, September 10, 1917 – April 18, 1993) was a Japanese judoka, widely considered one of the greatest judoka of all time. He won the All-Japan Judo Championships three consecutive times (1937–1939) and is said to have lost only four times in his entire career. His victory over Brazilian jiu-jitsu founder Hélio Gracie in 1951 made the reverse ude-garami armlock he used famous worldwide under the name "Kimura."

Judo career

Born in Kumamoto, Kimura took up judo at age nine and was promoted to yondan (4th dan) at eighteen. In 1937, at age twenty, he won the All-Japan Championships — then an open competition against the country's best — and repeated the feat in 1938 and 1939, an unprecedented three straight titles; he won the championship again in 1949. At the 1940 Ten-Ran Shiai tournament held before the Emperor, he won with an ippon in the final. His training regimen was legendary: reportedly up to a thousand push-ups and many hours of daily practice, including training against trees.

In 1950 Kimura became a professional wrestler and toured internationally. His professional career caused friction with the Kodokan authorities, who declined to promote him beyond 7th dan.

Bout with Hélio Gracie

In 1951, on a judo tour of Brazil, Kimura faced Hélio Gracie, co-founder of Brazilian jiu-jitsu, at the Maracanã stadium in Rio de Janeiro before a huge crowd. Kimura threw Gracie repeatedly and controlled him on the ground; in the second round he applied the double wristlock (reverse ude-garami), but Gracie refused to submit even as his arm was broken, until his corner threw in the towel after about thirteen minutes. In Brazilian jiu-jitsu the hold has ever since been called the "Kimura," and Kimura is revered in Brazil as well as Japan.

Later life

Kimura later taught judo at Takushoku University, coaching future Olympic and world champions, and wrote an autobiography, My Judo. He died of lung cancer in 1993, aged 75.
