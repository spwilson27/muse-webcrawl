Iconoclasm

Statues defaced during the iconoclastic fury of 1566 in the Low Countries.

Iconoclasm is the social belief in the importance of the destruction of icons and other images or monuments, most frequently for religious or political reasons. People who engage in or support iconoclasm are called iconoclasts, a term that has come to be figuratively applied to any individuals who challenge "cherished beliefs or venerated institutions on the grounds that they are erroneous, pernicious, or superstitious".

Byzantine iconoclasm

The most famous episode began in the Byzantine Empire in 726/730 when Emperor Leo III removed the icon of Christ from the Chalke gate and banned religious images, blaming military defeats and a volcanic eruption on divine anger at idolatry. A first period of iconoclasm lasted until 787, when the Second Council of Nicaea restored icon veneration, distinguishing worship due to God alone from honour paid to images. A second period ran from 814 until 842/843, ending with the "Triumph of Orthodoxy" under Empress Theodora. The controversy devastated early Byzantine art, although icons survived in regions beyond imperial control, notably at Saint Catherine's Monastery in Sinai.

Reformation iconoclasm

During the Protestant Reformation, reformers such as Zwingli and Calvin condemned images in churches, and waves of destruction — most violently the Beeldenstorm of 1566 in the Low Countries — stripped altars, smashed stained glass and whitewashed frescoes across parts of northern Europe. England saw official iconoclasm under Edward VI and again during the Civil War.

Other instances and modern usage

Islamic aniconism has periodically produced the destruction of figural art, from early mosque decoration disputes to the Taliban's demolition of the Buddhas of Bamiyan in 2001. Political iconoclasm — toppling statues of rulers, from Pharaoh-era damnatio memoriae to fallen dictators' monuments — continues the pattern. In everyday language, an iconoclast is now simply someone who attacks settled orthodoxies.
