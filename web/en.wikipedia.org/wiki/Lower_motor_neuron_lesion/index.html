Lower motor neuron lesion

A lower motor neuron lesion is damage to the lower motor neurons — the motor neurons whose cell bodies lie in the anterior (ventral) horn of the spinal cord or in the motor nuclei of the cranial nerves, and whose axons run in the peripheral nerves to innervate skeletal muscle. Because these neurons are the "final common pathway" to muscle, their lesions produce a characteristic pattern of weakness quite different from that of upper motor neuron lesions.

Signs and symptoms

The classic features of a lower motor neuron lesion are:

  • Flaccid paralysis — weakness with reduced muscle tone (flaccidity);
  • Marked muscle atrophy (wasting) of the affected muscles;
  • Fasciculations — spontaneous twitching of groups of muscle fibres;
  • Absent or reduced reflexes (hyporeflexia or areflexia) in the affected segments;
  • Absent Babinski sign — the plantar response is flexor or absent, never extensor.

Weakness follows the distribution of the damaged nerve roots or peripheral nerves, rather than the whole-limb patterns seen with upper motor neuron lesions.

Causes

Common causes include poliomyelitis, which selectively attacks anterior horn cells; Bell's palsy (lesion of the facial nerve); peripheral nerve injuries and entrapment neuropathies; radiculopathy from a prolapsed intervertebral disc; Guillain–Barré syndrome; and motor neuron disease (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis), in which lower and upper motor neuron signs characteristically coexist. Bulbar palsy is a lower motor neuron lesion of cranial nerves IX–XII, distinguished from pseudobulbar palsy, its upper motor neuron counterpart.

Comparison with upper motor neuron lesions

Where the lower motor neuron lesion gives flaccid weakness, wasting, fasciculations and lost reflexes, an upper motor neuron lesion gives spasticity, weakness without marked wasting, no fasciculations, brisk reflexes and an extensor plantar response. Distinguishing the two patterns is a cornerstone of clinical neurological localization.
