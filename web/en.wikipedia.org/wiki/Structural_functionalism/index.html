Structural functionalism

Structural functionalism, or simply functionalism, is "a framework for building theory that sees society as a complex system whose parts work together to promote solidarity and stability". This approach looks at society through a macro-level orientation, which is a broad focus on the social structures that shape society as a whole, and believes that society has evolved like organisms.

The approach gained prominence through the work of Émile Durkheim in France, functionalist anthropologists Bronisław Malinowski and A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, and mid-20th-century sociologists Talcott Parsons and Robert K. Merton in the United States.

Key concepts

Functionalists analyse institutions — such as the family, education, religion, and the economy — in terms of the functions they perform for the maintenance of the social system. Parsons developed the AGIL schema of functional imperatives (adaptation, goal attainment, integration, latency). Merton distinguished manifest functions (intended and recognised) from latent functions (unintended and unrecognised), and introduced the concept of dysfunctions, noting that institutions can have negative consequences for some groups.

Criticism

Structural functionalism was the dominant theoretical paradigm in American sociology after World War II but declined from the 1960s. Critics charged that it was static and conservative, emphasising order and consensus while neglecting conflict, inequality, and social change — themes taken up by conflict theory, symbolic interactionism, and feminist sociology.
