Demon core

A re-creation of the 1946 experiment: the plutonium core within beryllium reflector shells

The demon core was a spherical 6.2-kilogram (14 lb) subcritical mass of plutonium manufactured during World War II as the fissile core of an early atomic bomb. It gained its name after causing two fatal criticality accidents at Los Alamos in 1945 and 1946.

The accidents

On August 21, 1945, physicist Harry Daghlian accidentally dropped a tungsten carbide brick onto the core while performing neutron-reflection experiments; the added reflection made the core go critical, giving Daghlian a fatal dose of radiation — he died 25 days later. On May 21, 1946, physicist Louis Slotin was demonstrating a criticality experiment in which two beryllium hemispheres were lowered around the core separated by a screwdriver; the screwdriver slipped, the shells closed, and the core went critical. Slotin died of acute radiation syndrome nine days later.

Aftermath

The accidents ended hands-on criticality experiments at Los Alamos, which were thereafter performed by remote control. The core itself, originally intended for a third wartime bomb that was never used after Japan's surrender, was eventually melted down and its material recycled into other cores.
