Body camera

A body camera, or body-worn camera, is a wearable audio, video, or photographic recording system. Body cameras have been adopted most prominently by law enforcement as body-worn video (BWV), with related devices also used in healthcare, journalism, military, and commercial settings. Police body cameras are typically small, wide-angle cameras clipped to an officer's chest or glasses, recording interactions with the public; the footage is uploaded to secure storage at the end of a shift and may be used as evidence, for training, or released to the public.

History

Body-worn recording has roots in helmet-mounted and sunglasses cameras used by soldiers and cyclists. The first modern police trials of body cameras were held in the United Kingdom in the mid-2000s. In the United States, interest accelerated after 2014, when high-profile police shootings and a study from Rialto, California — which reported large reductions in complaints and use of force after cameras were introduced — prompted many departments to adopt the technology. Major manufacturers include Axon (originally TASER International), Motorola Solutions, and Visual Labs.

Research findings

Research on body cameras' effects is mixed. Early studies suggested reductions in citizen complaints and use of force, attributed to a "civilizing effect" on both officers and citizens. However, larger randomized trials, notably a 2017–2019 study of more than 2,000 officers in Washington, D.C., found no statistically significant effects on use of force, complaints, arrests, or other measured outcomes. Systematic reviews conclude that effects on force are inconsistent, though reductions in complaints are more commonly observed; outcomes may depend heavily on activation policies and whether officers have discretion over recording.

Policy and privacy issues

Body camera programs raise questions about when recording must begin, retention periods, public access under freedom-of-information laws, redaction of bystanders and victims, and the privacy of people filmed inside homes or hospitals. Civil-liberties advocates support cameras as accountability tools but warn against surveillance uses such as facial recognition on footage. Policies vary widely between jurisdictions, and footage release decisions — especially after critical incidents — remain politically contentious.

Other uses

Outside policing, body cameras are used by paramedics and hospital staff (to deter aggression), by journalists in conflict zones, by referees and athletes in sports, and by private citizens as dashcam-like personal recorders. Related wearable cameras include consumer "lifelogging" devices such as Narrative Clip and action cameras worn on the body.
