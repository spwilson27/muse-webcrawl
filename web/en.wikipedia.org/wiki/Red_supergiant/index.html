Red supergiants (RSGs) are supergiant stars of spectral type K or M — among the largest stars in the universe by volume, and among the coolest and most luminous. They are evolved massive stars that have exhausted the hydrogen in their cores and left the main sequence, burning helium (and later heavier elements) in a core surrounded by hydrogen-burning shells, while their outer envelopes expand and cool to red temperatures of about 3,400–4,100 K. Well-known examples include Betelgeuse in Orion and Antares in Scorpius, both visible to the naked eye with a distinct orange-red colour.

Properties and evolution

Stars with initial masses of roughly 8 to 40 solar masses become red supergiants. Their radii range from a few hundred to over 1,500 times the Sun's: if Betelgeuse replaced the Sun, its surface would engulf the orbit of Mars and possibly Jupiter. Despite their enormous size they are tenuous — average densities far below that of air at the visible surface — and they lose mass copiously through slow, dusty winds, enriching the interstellar medium. Many are semiregular variables, pulsating in brightness; Betelgeuse's unexpected "Great Dimming" of 2019–2020, caused by a mass ejection and dust formation, attracted worldwide attention.

End of life

Red supergiants are the immediate progenitors of Type II supernovae: after fusing successively heavier elements up to iron, the core collapses, producing a neutron star (or, at the highest masses, a black hole) and ejecting the envelope. Observations show that the most massive red supergiants (above ~17–20 M☉) seem under-represented among Type II progenitors — the "red supergiant problem" — suggesting some collapse directly to black holes or evolve back to hotter temperatures before exploding. The largest known stars by radius, such as UY Scuti and Stephenson 2-18, are red supergiants or hypergiants with radii estimated near or above 1,500–2,000 R☉, though such figures carry large uncertainties.
