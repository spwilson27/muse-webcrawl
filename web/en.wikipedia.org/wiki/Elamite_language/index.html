Elamite language

RegionElam (southwestern Iran)
Erac. 2800 BCE – c. 300 BCE (later survival possible)
FamilyLanguage isolate
ScriptsProto-Elamite, Linear Elamite, Elamite cuneiform

Elamite is an extinct language spoken by the ancient Elamites in what is now southwestern Iran, roughly corresponding to the modern provinces of Khuzestan and Fars. It is generally regarded as a language isolate, unrelated to its Semitic, Sumerian, or Indo-European neighbours, though a proposed Elamo-Dravidian connection remains unproven.

History and scripts

Elamite is attested for over two thousand years. The earliest writing in the region, Proto-Elamite (c. 3200–2700 BCE), remains undeciphered, as does the later Linear Elamite script. From about 2200 BCE Elamite was written in a cuneiform script adapted from Akkadian. Under the Achaemenid Empire, Elamite became one of the official administrative languages of Persia: thousands of clay tablets from Persepolis are written in it, and it appears in royal trilingual inscriptions alongside Old Persian and Babylonian.

Grammar

Elamite is an agglutinative language with a system of noun classes distinguishing animate and inanimate referents, suffixing morphology, and subject–object–verb word order. Verbs inflect with suffixes that agree with the subject, and nouns take case-like suffixes and possessive markers.

Decline

Written Elamite fades from the record after the fall of the Achaemenid Empire in the late 4th century BCE, though the spoken language may have survived in the region into the early medieval period; some scholars connect it with the later Khuz language mentioned by Arab geographers.
