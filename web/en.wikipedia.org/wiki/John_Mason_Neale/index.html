John Mason Neale (24 January 1818 – 6 August 1866) was an English Anglican priest, scholar and hymnwriter. He translated a large number of Latin and Greek hymns into English, many of which remain in regular use, and was a leading figure in the Anglo-Catholic Oxford Movement. His best-known works include the carol "Good King Wenceslas" and translations such as "O come, O come, Emmanuel", "All glory, laud and honour" and "Jerusalem the golden".

Life

Neale was born in London and educated at Sherborne School and Trinity College, Cambridge. At Cambridge he was a founder of the Cambridge Camden Society (later the Ecclesiological Society), which promoted the study of Gothic church architecture and more elaborate ritual in worship — interests that shaped his whole career and made him controversial within the Church of England.

Ordained in 1842, ill health forced Neale to spend time in Madeira, where he researched medieval hymnology. In 1846 he became warden of Sackville College, an almshouse at East Grinstead in Sussex, a modest post he held for the remaining twenty years of his life. There he founded the Society of Saint Margaret, a religious order of Anglican nuns devoted to nursing and teaching.

Hymns and translations

Neale's Hymns of the Eastern Church (1862) introduced English congregations to Greek liturgical poetry, while his earlier collaborations with Thomas Helmore on The Hymnal Noted adapted medieval Latin plainsong hymns for parish use. Among the many translations that became standard are "Come, ye thankful people, come" is not his — his undoubted classics include "O come, O come, Emmanuel" (from the Latin Veni, veni, Emmanuel), "To thee before the close of day", "Art thou weary, art thou languid" and "Jerusalem the golden" (from Bernard of Cluny). His original carol "Good King Wenceslas", published in 1853, set a medieval Bohemian legend to a spring carol tune.

Legacy

Neale died at East Grinstead in 1866, worn out by overwork and chronic ill health. He is commemorated in the calendars of several Anglican churches on 7 August. Scholars credit him more than any other Victorian with restoring ancient and medieval hymnody to English-language worship.
