Partial least squares regression

Partial least squares (PLS) regression is a statistical method that bears some relation to principal components regression and multiple linear regression. Instead of finding components of maximum variance in the predictors alone, PLS finds a linear regression model by projecting both the predicted variables and the observable variables onto new latent spaces chosen so that the covariance between them is maximised. The method is especially useful when the predictors are numerous, highly collinear, or more numerous than the observations.

History

PLS grew out of the work of the Swedish statistician Herman O. A. Wold, who developed iterative least-squares procedures for latent-variable models in the 1960s and 1970s. His son Svante Wold and co-workers adapted and popularised PLS regression in chemistry during the 1980s, where it became a standard tool of chemometrics for analysing spectral data. The family also includes PLS path modelling used in the social sciences.

Given a matrix X of predictors and a matrix Y of responses, PLS extracts a sequence of latent components (score vectors) that are linear combinations of the original variables. Unlike principal component regression, the extraction is supervised: directions in X are favoured when they also predict Y. After each component is extracted, both matrices are deflated and the process repeats. The classic computational scheme is Wold's NIPALS algorithm (nonlinear iterative partial least squares); the SIMPLS algorithm of de Jong computes the components directly without deflating X. The number of components is typically chosen by cross-validation to balance fit against overfitting.

Applications

PLS regression is a benchmark technique in chemometrics, where it relates near-infrared or other spectra to composition. It is also widely used in bioinformatics, econometrics, social-science path modelling, and brain imaging, where voxel data are high-dimensional and strongly correlated.
