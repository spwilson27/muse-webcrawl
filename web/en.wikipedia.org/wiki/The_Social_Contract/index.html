The Social Contract

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Social Contract, originally published as Du contrat social; ou, Principes du droit politique (Of the Social Contract; or, Principles of Political Right), is a 1762 treatise by Jean-Jacques Rousseau. It opens with the celebrated line: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." The book argues that legitimate political authority rests on a social contract in which each person, giving themselves to all, gives themselves to no one — creating a moral and collective body whose laws express the general will.

Doctrines

Key doctrines include: sovereignty as inalienable and indivisible, residing in the people as law-makers (government is a mere commission, revocable); the general will as the standard of law, distinct from the aggregate of private wills; the Legislator, an extraordinary founder who frames a people's institutions; civil religion as social glue; and a preference for small, virtuous republics where citizens can assemble. Banned and burned in Paris and Geneva on publication, the book became the French Revolution's most cited — and most blamed — inspiration, and its tensions (freedom through obedience to self-given law; being "forced to be free") still organise the interpretation literature between democratic and totalitarian readings.
