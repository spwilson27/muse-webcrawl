Theresienstadt Ghetto

Theresienstadt was a hybrid concentration camp and ghetto established by the SS during World War II in the fortress town of Terezín, in the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia (now the Czech Republic). Operating from November 1941 to May 1945, it served principally as a transit camp for Czech, German, Austrian, Dutch and Danish Jews on their way to extermination camps in the occupied east, and as a propaganda tool: Nazi authorities presented it as a benign "retirement settlement" for elderly and prominent Jews, a deception epitomised by the staged Red Cross visit of June 1944 and the propaganda film made there shortly afterwards.

History

Terezín was built in the 1780s as a Habsburg fortress town. In 1941 the SS converted the garrison town into a ghetto under a Jewish self-administration (the Council of Elders, headed successively by Jakob Edelstein, Paul Eppstein and Benjamin Murmelstein) supervised by an SS commandant's office; the nearby Small Fortress served as a Gestapo prison. The ghetto's population peaked in September 1942 at nearly 58,000, in a town designed for about 7,000. Overcrowding, starvation rations and disease caused roughly 33,000 deaths in the ghetto itself.

From January 1942 transports left Theresienstadt for ghettos and extermination camps in the east — Riga, Izbica, Treblinka and above all Auschwitz-Birkenau, where a special "Theresienstadt family camp" was maintained for propaganda purposes before its inmates were gassed in 1944. Of some 88,000 people deported from Theresienstadt eastward, only about 4,000 survived. Despite the conditions, prisoners sustained an extraordinary cultural life of lectures, concerts, cabaret and clandestine education for children — including the children's opera Brundibár and the art and poems of children preserved after the war.

The "model ghetto" deception

In June 1944, after months of "beautification" — shops, a café, a bank issuing ghetto currency, gardens and fake storefronts — a delegation of the International Committee of the Red Cross and Danish officials was shown a scripted façade and reported favourably. Weeks later the SS had the prisoner Kurt Gerron direct a propaganda film (commonly called The Führer Gives a City to the Jews), after which most of the cast and crew, Gerron included, were deported to Auschwitz and murdered. In April 1945 some 1,200 prisoners were released to Switzerland and several hundred Danes were evacuated by the Swedish White Buses. The ghetto was handed to the Red Cross on 3 May 1945 and liberated by the Red Army on 8 May; of the approximately 144,000 Jews sent to Theresienstadt, about 17,000 were alive there at liberation.

Legacy

The Terezín Memorial, established in 1947, preserves the ghetto and Small Fortress as a museum and place of remembrance, including the Ghetto Museum opened in 1991 and the drawings of the ghetto's child prisoners, of whom some 15,000 passed through Theresienstadt and only a few hundred survived.
