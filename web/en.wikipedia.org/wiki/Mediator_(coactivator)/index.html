Mediator (coactivator)

[Image: The Mediator complex bridging an activator and RNA polymerase II]
Mediator transmits regulatory signals to the transcription machinery

Mediator is a multiprotein complex that functions as a transcriptional coactivator in all eukaryotes. It was discovered in the early 1990s in the laboratory of Roger Kornberg, work that contributed to his 2006 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for studies of the molecular basis of eukaryotic transcription.

Mediator interacts with transcription factors and RNA polymerase II. Its main function is to transmit signals from the transcription factors bound at enhancer regions to the polymerase, thereby controlling whether and how strongly a gene is transcribed. The complex, of roughly 25–30 subunits in mammals (about 1.2 MDa), is organised into head, middle, tail and a detachable kinase module (CDK8 module). The tail binds gene-specific activators, the head and middle engage polymerase II and the general transcription factors, and the kinase module can repress or fine-tune activity.

Function and significance

Mediator is required for the regulated expression of nearly all protein-coding genes, helps assemble and stabilise the pre-initiation complex, stimulates phosphorylation of the polymerase C-terminal domain, and participates in chromatin looping between enhancers and promoters. Mutations in subunits such as MED12, MED13L, MED17 and MED25 cause human developmental and intellectual-disability syndromes, and dysregulation of Mediator components is implicated in several cancers, making it a subject of drug-discovery interest.
