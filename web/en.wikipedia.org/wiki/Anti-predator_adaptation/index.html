Anti-predator adaptation

Anti-predator adaptation
FieldEvolutionary biology, behavioural ecology
MechanismsAvoiding detection; preventing attack; resisting capture; escape
ExamplesCamouflage, mimicry, aposematism, thanatosis, mobbing
Counter-adaptationsPredator sensory and hunting coevolution (arms race)

Anti-predator adaptations are mechanisms developed through evolution that assist prey organisms in their constant struggle against predators. Adaptations occur at every stage of a predation sequence: prey may avoid detection, prevent or deter an attack, resist capture, or escape after being caught. Because predators evolve counter-adaptations in response, prey and predator are locked in an evolutionary arms race — an idea formalised in the life–dinner principle, which notes that selection on prey is stronger because the prey runs for its life while the predator runs only for its dinner.

Avoiding detection

The first line of defence is not to be noticed. Many animals use camouflage — cryptic colouration, countershading and disruptive patterns — to blend into their background, while nocturnality and burrowing reduce exposure. Some species freeze when threatened; others, like certain moths, mask their acoustic signature against echolocating bats.

Preventing attack

Once detected, prey may advertise unprofitability. Aposematism — warning colouration — signals chemical defences, as in poison dart frogs and monarch butterflies, and is exploited by harmless Batesian mimics that copy the warning without paying the cost of toxicity. Deimatic (startle) displays, such as the eyespots of moths and the frill of the frill-necked lizard, may cause a predator to hesitate. Group living provides vigilance and dilution benefits, and many prey give alarm calls — though these may also serve to signal to the predator that it has been seen.

Resisting and escaping

Mechanical defences include shells, spines, armour and autotomy — the shedding of a tail or limb that continues to twitch while the prey escapes, as in many lizards. Chemical defences range from skunk spray and bombardier beetle discharges to sequestered plant toxins. Behavioural last resorts include thanatosis (playing dead), mobbing of predators by birds, protean erratic escape movements, and deflection of attacks toward expendable body parts, as in the false heads of hairstreak butterflies.

Evolution

Anti-predator traits illustrate key evolutionary principles: frequency-dependent selection maintains mimicry systems; signal honesty is stabilised by the cost of defences; and geographic mosaics of predator presence produce local variation in prey defence. Plants, though outside the animal focus of most surveys, possess parallel defences — thorns, toxins and indirect recruitment of predators of herbivores.
