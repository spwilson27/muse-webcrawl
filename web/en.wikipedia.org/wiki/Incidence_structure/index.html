Incidence structure

In mathematics, an incidence structure is an abstract system consisting of two types of objects — usually called points and blocks (or lines) — together with a single relationship between them, called incidence. If a point lies on a line, the two are said to be incident. Incidence structures generalise geometric configurations such as finite geometries, as well as combinatorial designs.

Definition

Formally, an incidence structure is a triple (P, B, I) where P is a set of points, B is a disjoint set of blocks, and I ⊆ P × B is the incidence relation. A flag is a pair of mutually incident point and block. Structures are often represented by an incidence matrix or by a bipartite Levi graph.

Examples

Familiar examples include finite projective planes such as the Fano plane, affine planes, block designs used in the design of experiments, and the point-line structure of ordinary Euclidean geometry. Graphs can also be viewed as incidence structures, with vertices as points and edges incident with their endpoints.

Duality

Every incidence structure has a dual obtained by interchanging the roles of points and blocks. Many theorems of projective geometry come in dual pairs, and self-dual structures, where the structure is isomorphic to its dual, are of particular interest. The concept of incidence abstracts away notions of distance and measurement, retaining only the combinatorics of "which points lie on which lines".
