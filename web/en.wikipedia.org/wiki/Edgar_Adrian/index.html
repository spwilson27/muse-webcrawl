Edgar Douglas Adrian, 1st Baron Adrian (30 November 1889 – 4 August 1977) was an English electrophysiologist and a founder of modern neuroscience. He shared the 1932 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Sir Charles Sherrington "for their discoveries regarding the functions of neurons".

Work

Working at Cambridge, Adrian used capillary electrometer and valve-amplifier techniques to amplify the minute electrical signals of single nerve fibres — an improvement in sensitivity of several orders of magnitude. In the 1920s he showed that a nerve fibre responds to a stimulus above threshold with impulses of constant size (the "all-or-none" principle) and that the intensity of a sensation is encoded by the frequency of impulses, which adapt over time. He went on to record the electrical activity of the brain, supporting Hans Berger's work on the electroencephalogram and studying the rhythm named after him.

Later life

Adrian was professor of physiology at Cambridge from 1937 to 1951, president of the Royal Society (1950–1955), Master of Trinity College (1951–1965) and Chancellor of the University of Cambridge (1968–1975). He was created Baron Adrian in 1955. His books include The Basis of Sensation (1928) and The Physical Background of Perception (1947).
