German Reformation

Date1517–1555 (to the Peace of Augsburg)
LocationElectorate of Saxony and the Holy Roman Empire
Key figureMartin Luther
ResultDivision of Western Christianity; Lutheran territorial churches

The German Reformation was the initial phase of the Protestant Reformation in the Holy Roman Empire, begun in 1517 when the Augustinian friar and theology professor Martin Luther published his Ninety-five Theses challenging the sale of indulgences. The movement spread rapidly through the German lands, aided by printing, and led to a permanent division of Western Christianity.

Origins

Luther's theses, posted at Wittenberg on 31 October 1517, attacked the theology of indulgences and questioned papal authority. Summoned to account at the Diet of Augsburg (1518) and the Diet of Worms (1521), Luther refused to recant and was placed under the imperial ban by the Edict of Worms. Protected by Frederick the Wise, Elector of Saxony, he translated the New Testament into German while in hiding at the Wartburg.

Spread and conflict

Reforming ideas spread through sermons, pamphlets and the new printing presses; many imperial cities and princes adopted them. The movement also inspired more radical currents and coincided with the German Peasants' War of 1524–1525, which Luther condemned. At the Diet of Speyer in 1529, evangelical princes issued the Protestation that gave "Protestants" their name, and in 1530 the Augsburg Confession set out Lutheran doctrine. The princes formed the Schmalkaldic League for mutual defence, and after the Schmalkaldic War the Peace of Augsburg (1555) recognised Lutheranism, allowing each ruler to determine the religion of his territory (cuius regio, eius religio).

Legacy

The German Reformation reshaped the religious, political and cultural map of Europe, promoted vernacular Bible translation and literacy, and served as the starting point for reformations elsewhere in Europe.
