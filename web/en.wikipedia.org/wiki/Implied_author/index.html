Implied author
Coined byWayne C. Booth, The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961)
FieldNarratology, literary theory
Related conceptsImplied reader, narrator, unreliable narrator

The implied author is a concept in literary theory and narratology introduced by Wayne C. Booth in The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961). It denotes the image of the author that a reader constructs from a text: the guiding sensibility, values and norms embodied in the work as a whole, as distinct from both the real, flesh-and-blood author and the narrator who speaks within the text.

The concept

Booth argued that in writing, an author creates "a second self" — an implied author — who may differ markedly from the real person: a writer may be careless in life yet meticulous in the implied personality of the work, or personally virtuous while producing a book whose implied author is cynical. The implied author is inferred by the reader from choices of style, structure, tone and what the narrative rewards or condemns.

Debate

Later theorists refined and contested the idea. Seymour Chatman incorporated the implied author into his communication diagram of narrative; Gérard Genette questioned its necessity, preferring to attribute textual norms directly to the real author; and some critics regard the concept as an unnecessary or incoherent intermediary. It remains a standard tool in discussions of irony and unreliable narration, where the gap between narrator and implied author signals how the reader is meant to judge the narration.
