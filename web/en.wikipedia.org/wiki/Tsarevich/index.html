Tsarevich

Tsarevich
Russian:царевич
Meaning:"Son of the tsar"
Female forms:Tsarevna (daughter), Tsesarevna (wife of heir)

Tsarevich (Russian: царевич) is a Slavic title given to the sons of a tsar. The female equivalent for a tsar's daughter is tsarevna. Under the house laws of the Russian Empire formalised by Paul I, the title of the heir apparent was Tsesarevich (цесаревич), while his wife was Tsesarevna; younger sons of the emperor were tsareviches in the generic sense, though from 1885 they more precisely bore the title Grand Duke.

History

The title came into use in Russia in the 16th and 17th centuries for the sons of the ruling tsar. The best-known early bearer was Tsarevich Ivan Ivanovich, elder son of Ivan the Terrible, whose death in 1581 — traditionally said to have followed a blow from his father's staff — extinguished the direct Rurikid line and led toward the Time of Troubles. Another famous figure was Tsarevich Alexei Petrovich, son of Peter the Great, who was condemned for treason and died in prison in 1718.

Last tsesarevich

The last heir to hold the title was Alexei Nikolaevich (1904–1918), the haemophiliac youngest child and only son of Nicholas II. His illness was the channel through which Grigori Rasputin gained influence at court. Alexei was murdered with his family at Yekaterinburg in July 1918. Because of the pretenders who later claimed to be him, and the long delay in finding his remains (identified by DNA testing only in 2007–2009), the young tsarevich became an enduring figure of popular fascination.
