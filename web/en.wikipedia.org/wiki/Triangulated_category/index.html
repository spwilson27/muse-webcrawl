Triangulated category

In mathematics, a triangulated category is an additive category equipped with an automorphism (the translation or suspension functor) and a distinguished class of triples of morphisms called distinguished triangles, satisfying axioms modelled on the behaviour of exact sequences and cofibre sequences. Triangulated categories, introduced by Verdier in his 1963 thesis work building on ideas of Grothendieck and Puppe, are the natural home of derived categories.

Definition

A triangulated category consists of a category T, an autoequivalence [1] called translation, and a collection of sequences of the form X → Y → Z → X[1] designated as distinguished triangles. The axioms require that the class of triangles is closed under isomorphism and rotation, that every morphism extends to a triangle, and that the octahedral axiom holds — a compatibility condition relating triangles built from composites of morphisms, analogous to the third isomorphism theorem.

Examples

The prototypical example is the derived category of an abelian category, where distinguished triangles arise from short exact sequences of complexes. The stable homotopy category of spectra is another fundamental example, with triangles coming from cofibre sequences. Homotopy categories of chain complexes and stable module categories also carry triangulated structures.

Properties and limitations

Cohomological functors out of a triangulated category send distinguished triangles to long exact sequences, which makes the formalism powerful for homological algebra. However, triangulated categories lose some information: mapping cones are not functorial, and certain constructions are awkward, which has motivated enhancements such as dg-categories, A-infinity categories, and stable infinity-categories.
