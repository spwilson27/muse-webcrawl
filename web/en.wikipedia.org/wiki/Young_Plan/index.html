Young Plan

The Young Plan was a programme for settling Germany's World War I reparations, drawn up in 1929 and adopted in 1930 as a replacement for the Dawes Plan. Named for the American banker Owen D. Young, who chaired the committee that produced it, the plan reduced the total amount Germany owed, stretched payments over 59 years, and created the Bank for International Settlements to administer transfers. It was swept away by the Great Depression within two years.

Terms

The plan set Germany's final bill at 112 billion gold marks (about US$26.3 billion), payable in annual instalments rising from about 1.7 billion to 2.4 billion marks until 1988. Part of each annuity was unconditional, while the remainder could be postponed in times of hardship. The plan ended foreign controls over the German economy, provided for the evacuation of the occupied Rhineland by 1930—five years ahead of the Versailles schedule—and established the Bank for International Settlements in Basel as trustee for the payments.

Politics in Germany

German nationalists, including Alfred Hugenberg's DNVP and Adolf Hitler's Nazi Party, campaigned against the plan in a 1929 referendum on the so-called "Liberty Law", which would have repudiated reparations entirely. The referendum failed badly, winning only about 14 per cent of the vote, but the campaign gave Hitler national exposure and a platform alongside mainstream conservatives.

Collapse

The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 and the ensuing depression destroyed the plan's assumptions. As German banks failed and unemployment soared, US President Herbert Hoover proclaimed a one-year moratorium on intergovernmental debts in June 1931. The Lausanne Conference of 1932 effectively ended reparations, reducing the remaining obligation to a token sum that was never paid.
