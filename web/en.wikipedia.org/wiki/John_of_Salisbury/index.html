John of Salisbury (c. 1115/1120 – 25 October 1180) was an English author, philosopher, diplomat and bishop of Chartres. One of the most learned men of the twelfth-century renaissance, he is known for the Policraticus and the Metalogicon, and for his association with Thomas Becket.

Education and career

John studied in Paris and Chartres under many of the leading masters of the age, including Peter Abelard, William of Conches and Thierry of Chartres. He later recalled his twelve years of study in the Metalogicon. Returning to England, he served as secretary first to Archbishop Theobald of Canterbury and then to Theobald's successor, Thomas Becket, undertaking frequent missions to the papal court.

John supported Becket in his quarrel with Henry II and shared his exile in France; he was present in Canterbury in December 1170 shortly before Becket's murder, and wrote an early life of the martyr. In 1176 he was elected bishop of Chartres, where he died in 1180.

Works

The Policraticus (1159), subtitled "Of the Frivolities of Courtiers and the Footprints of Philosophers", is a wide-ranging work of political and moral philosophy famous for its organic image of the commonwealth as a body and for its qualified discussion of tyrannicide. The Metalogicon defends the study of logic and the trivium against critics of education. His Historia Pontificalis and his extensive letters are major sources for the politics and intellectual life of his century. John is also the reported source of Bernard of Chartres's image of moderns as "dwarfs standing on the shoulders of giants".
