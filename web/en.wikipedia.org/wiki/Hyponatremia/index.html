Hyponatremia

Hyponatremia (also spelled hyponatraemia) is a low concentration of sodium in the blood, generally defined as a serum sodium below 135 mmol/L. It is the most common electrolyte disorder encountered in hospital practice. Causes are numerous and include the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), heart failure, cirrhosis, kidney failure, certain medications such as thiazide diuretics, and excessive water intake. Severe or rapidly developing hyponatremia can cause cerebral oedema, seizures, and death.

Signs and symptoms

Mild chronic hyponatremia may be asymptomatic or cause subtle gait instability, falls, and impaired attention. As sodium falls or drops rapidly, symptoms progress from nausea, malaise, and headache to confusion, vomiting, seizures, coma, and respiratory arrest due to cerebral oedema and brain herniation. Symptoms generally reflect the rate of change as much as the absolute level.

Causes

Hyponatremia almost always reflects an excess of water relative to sodium, usually due to impaired water excretion driven by antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin). Causes are commonly grouped by volume status:

  • Hypovolemic: gastrointestinal losses, burns, third-space losses, and diuretic use, with sodium and water both lost but sodium predominating.
  • Euvolemic: SIADH (from malignancy, lung disease, central nervous system disorders, or drugs), hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and primary polydipsia.
  • Hypervolemic: heart failure, cirrhosis with portal hypertension, nephrotic syndrome, and advanced kidney failure, in which total body sodium and water both increase but water more so.

Pseudohyponatremia is a laboratory artefact seen with severe hyperlipidaemia or paraproteinaemia when sodium is measured with certain methods, and hyperglycaemia lowers measured sodium by drawing water into the blood (translocational hyponatremia).

Diagnosis

Evaluation includes confirming a low serum osmolality, measuring urine osmolality and urine sodium, assessing volume status, and checking thyroid and adrenal function and glucose and lipid levels. A urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg indicates ADH action; a low urine sodium suggests extrarenal losses or low effective arterial blood volume.

Treatment

Management depends on severity, duration, and cause. Acutely symptomatic patients receive hypertonic (3%) saline in controlled boluses to raise sodium by a few mmol/L and relieve cerebral oedema. Chronic hyponatremia is corrected slowly — generally no more than about 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours — because overly rapid correction can cause osmotic demyelination syndrome, a devastating injury to the pons and other brain regions. Other measures include fluid restriction, stopping offending drugs, treating the underlying disease, and in selected cases vasopressin receptor antagonists ("vaptans") or oral urea.

Prognosis

Hyponatremia is associated with increased mortality in many conditions, partly as a marker of disease severity. Even mild chronic hyponatremia is linked with falls, fractures, and cognitive impairment in older adults.
