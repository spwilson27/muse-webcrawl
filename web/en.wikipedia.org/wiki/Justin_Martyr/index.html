Justin Martyr

Bornc. 100 AD, Flavia Neapolis, Syria Palaestina
Diedc. 165 AD, Rome
Feast1 June (Roman Catholic); 14 April (Eastern Orthodox)
Known forChristian apologetics; the First Apology; Dialogue with Trypho

Justin (c. 100 – c. 165 AD), known posthumously as Justin Martyr and also as Justin the Philosopher, was an early Christian apologist and philosopher who converted to Christianity and defended the faith in writings addressed to the Roman emperor and Senate. He is regarded as a saint by the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Lutheran and Anglican churches.

Life

Justin was born in Flavia Neapolis (modern Nablus), in Samaria, to a Greek-speaking pagan family. In the opening of his Dialogue with Trypho he recounts his philosophical pilgrimage through Stoic, Peripatetic, Pythagorean and Platonist schools before an encounter with an old man by the sea led him to the Hebrew prophets and Christianity. He continued to wear the philosopher's cloak (pallium) and taught in Ephesus and Rome, where he debated the Cynic Crescens.

Around 165, during the prefecture of Junius Rusticus, Justin and several companions were tried and beheaded for refusing to sacrifice to the gods; the surviving Acts of Justin purport to record the trial.

Works

His principal surviving writings are the First Apology (c. 155–157), addressed to Antoninus Pius, arguing that Christians are unjustly punished for the name alone; the Second Apology; and the Dialogue with Trypho, the earliest surviving Christian work devoted to debate with Judaism. Justin's theology of the Logos — the divine Word — allowed him to claim that truths found among Greek philosophers belonged to Christians, since pagans had possessed "seeds of the Logos" (logos spermatikos). His First Apology also contains one of the earliest detailed descriptions of Christian baptism and Eucharist, and of Sunday worship.

Legacy

Justin is considered the foremost of the second-century apologists and a bridge between Greek philosophy and Christian theology. Pope Leo XIII instituted his feast in the Roman calendar; he is the patron saint of philosophers.
