Whisky

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A glass of whisky with a barrel and grain]

Whisky (or whiskey) is a distilled alcoholic beverage made from fermented grain mash — barley, corn, rye or wheat — aged in wooden casks, generally oak. Major traditions include Scotch whisky, Irish whiskey, American bourbon and rye, Canadian whisky and Japanese whisky, each protected by law and shaped by climate and custom.

The name derives from Gaelic uisce beatha, "water of life". Distillation reached Scotland and Ireland by the late Middle Ages; the oldest licensed distillery claim belongs to Bushmills (1608). Scotch must be aged at least three years in oak in Scotland; single malts from a single distillery command prestige, while blends dominate volume.

Contents
1. Production

Production

Malted or unmalted grains are mashed, fermented into a beer-like wash, distilled (pot stills for malt Scotch and Irish pot still whiskey; column stills for grain and bourbon), and matured in casks, often ex-bourbon or sherry oak. The "angels' share" evaporates during ageing; water source, peat smoke and wood define regional character.
