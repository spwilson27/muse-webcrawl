Progress

Progress is movement toward a goal or to a further stage of development. This article is about the idea of progress in history and philosophy.

Progress is the idea that human societies advance — in science, technology, economic development, and social organization — toward an improved, better state. The idea of progress holds that advances accumulate over time and that the human condition has improved, and will continue to improve, through applied knowledge and social reform.

History of the idea

Although ancient thinkers such as Xenophanes and some Roman writers noted cumulative advances in knowledge, the idea of progress as a general law of history emerged in early modern Europe, in works by Francis Bacon and René Descartes, and flourished during the Enlightenment. Thinkers such as Turgot, Condorcet (whose Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind, 1795, divided history into ten ascending epochs), and later Auguste Comte and Herbert Spencer made progress central to their philosophies of history.

Measures and debates

Modern discussions of progress often appeal to measurable indicators: life expectancy, literacy, child mortality, income, and indices such as the Human Development Index. Writers such as Steven Pinker have argued on such evidence that violence has declined and well-being increased over long periods. Critics counter that progress in one dimension may accompany regress in others (such as environmental degradation), that the idea of overall progress is culturally relative, or that it serves as an ideology justifying colonialism and unchecked industrialization. Earlier skeptics included Jean-Jacques Rousseau, and twentieth-century thinkers disillusioned by world war and totalitarianism.

Related concepts

Progress is closely connected with modernization theory, the Whig interpretation of history, and notions of social evolution. Its political legacy includes progressive movements advocating reform through government action, education, and science.
