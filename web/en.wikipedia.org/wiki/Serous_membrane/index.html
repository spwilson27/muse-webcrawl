A serous membrane consists of a parietal layer lining the cavity wall and a visceral layer covering the organs.

The serous membrane (or serosa) is a smooth tissue membrane of mesothelium lining the contents and inner walls of body cavities, which secrete a lubricating serous fluid. Serous membranes line and enclose several body cavities, known as serous cavities, where they secrete fluid that reduces friction from muscle movement and allows organs to glide against one another and the cavity wall.

Structure

Each serous membrane consists of a layer of simple squamous epithelium called the mesothelium, resting on a thin layer of connective tissue. The membrane is arranged as a continuous sac with two layers: the parietal layer, which lines the wall of the cavity, and the visceral layer, which is reflected over and covers the organs within the cavity. Between the two layers lies a potential space containing a small amount of serous fluid.

Principal serous membranes

  • Peritoneum — lines the abdominal cavity and covers the abdominal organs.
  • Pleura — surrounds each lung and lines the thoracic cavity.
  • Pericardium — the serous pericardium encloses the heart within the pericardial cavity.
  • Tunica vaginalis — a serous sac around the testis.

Development and clinical significance

Serous membranes develop from the mesoderm of the embryo, from the lining of the primitive body cavity (coelom). Inflammation of a serous membrane is called serositis; specific conditions include peritonitis, pleuritis (pleurisy), and pericarditis. Excess fluid accumulation in a serous cavity is termed a serous effusion—for example, pleural effusion or ascites in the peritoneal cavity.
