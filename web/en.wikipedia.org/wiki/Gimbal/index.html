Gimbal

A gimbal is a pivoted support that permits rotation of an object about an axis. A set of three gimbals, one mounted on the other with orthogonal pivot axes, may be used to allow an object mounted on the innermost gimbal to remain independent of the rotation of its support — for example, to keep a ship's compass or chronometer level despite the vessel's pitching and rolling.

History

The gimbal was first described by the Greek engineer Philo of Byzantium (3rd century BC) in the context of an ink pot that could be turned any way without spilling. In China, gimbals were used in incense burners from the Han dynasty. In early modern Europe, gimbal suspensions became standard for marine compasses, chronometers, and later for gyroscopes in navigation.

Applications

Gimbals are used in inertial navigation systems, where a gyroscope is mounted in a gimbal set to isolate it from the vehicle's motion; in rocket engines, where the thrust chamber is gimballed to steer the vehicle; and in modern camera stabilizers and drone mounts, where motorized three-axis gimbals counteract the operator's movements in real time.

Gimbal lock

A well-known limitation of a three-gimbal system is gimbal lock: when two of the pivot axes align, the mechanism loses one degree of freedom of rotation. This problem famously threatened the Apollo 8 mission's navigation and is avoided in modern systems by using quaternions in software or adding a fourth gimbal.
