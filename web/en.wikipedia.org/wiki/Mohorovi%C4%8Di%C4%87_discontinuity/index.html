The Mohorovičić discontinuity, usually called the Moho, is the boundary between the Earth's crust and the mantle. It is defined by the distinct change in velocity of seismic waves as they pass through changing densities of rock. It was first identified in 1909 by Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić, who noticed that seismograms from shallow earthquakes showed two sets of P-waves and S-waves, implying a sharp compositional change at depth.

Characteristics

The Moho lies at a depth of about 5 to 10 kilometres beneath the ocean floor and typically 20 to 90 kilometres beneath continents, averaging about 35 kilometres. Across the boundary, seismic wave velocities increase abruptly as rocks change from the feldspar-rich rocks of the crust to the denser olivine- and pyroxene-rich peridotites of the upper mantle.

The boundary is not everywhere a simple compositional change; in places it may reflect a phase transition or partial melting, and beneath some oceanic regions it is marked by layers of gabbro above mantle peridotite.

Exploration

Attempts to drill to the Moho began with Project Mohole in the 1960s, which was abandoned before reaching the boundary. Ocean drilling vessels such as JOIDES Resolution and the Japanese ship Chikyū continue the goal of penetrating the oceanic crust to sample the Moho directly.

Seismic reflection and refraction surveys remain the principal tools for mapping Moho depth, which varies with crustal thickness and is used to study mountain roots, rift zones, and plate boundaries.
