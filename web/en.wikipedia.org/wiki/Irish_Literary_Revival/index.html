Irish Literary Revival

Periodc. 1880s–1920s
Key figuresW. B. Yeats, Lady Gregory, J. M. Synge, Douglas Hyde
InstitutionsAbbey Theatre, Gaelic League

The Irish Literary Revival, also called the Irish Literary Renaissance or Celtic Revival, was a flowering of Irish literary talent in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, closely linked to cultural nationalism and the growth of interest in Gaelic heritage.

Origins

Scholars and writers drew on Irish mythology, folklore and history — the tales of Cú Chulainn and the Fianna — to create a distinctive national literature in English (and, via Douglas Hyde's Gaelic League, founded 1893, in Irish). Lady Gregory collected folklore in the west of Ireland and wrote in the Kiltartan dialect.

Yeats and the Abbey

W. B. Yeats was the movement's central figure, co-founding the Irish Literary Theatre and, in 1904, the Abbey Theatre in Dublin with Lady Gregory and Edward Martyn. J. M. Synge's plays, notably The Playboy of the Western World (1907), provoked riots for their unsentimental view of peasant life. Other figures include George Russell (Æ), Padraic Colum, James Stephens and Seán O'Casey, whose Dublin plays carried the revival into the 1920s.

Legacy

The revival helped shape the identity of independent Ireland and earned Yeats the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923. Later writers such as James Joyce reacted both against and within its legacy. See also the broader Home Rule era.
