Right to roam

The right to roam, also known as freedom to roam or everyman's right, is the general public's right to access certain public or privately owned land, lakes and rivers for recreation and exercise. The right is sometimes called the right of public access to the wilderness.

In Scotland, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Austria, the Czech Republic and Switzerland, the freedom to roam takes the form of general public rights which are sometimes codified in law. The access is ancient in parts of northern Europe and has been regarded as sufficiently fundamental that it was not formalised in law until modern times. However, the right usually does not include any substantial economic exploitation, such as fishing or hunting, nor does it include unauthorised use of motor vehicles.

In England and Wales, following a long-running campaign by the Ramblers and others, the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 (CRoW Act) gave a limited statutory right of access to mapped areas of mountain, moor, heath, down and registered common land, amounting to about 8% of the land area. The Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 went further, formalising a universal right of responsible access to most land and inland water in Scotland, building on the older Scottish tradition of a presumed right of access.

Nordic countries

The customary right is known as allemannsretten in Norway, allemansrätten in Sweden and jokamiehenoikeus (now jokaisenoikeus) in Finland. In broad terms it permits walking, skiing, cycling and short-term camping on uncultivated land, swimming and boating on lakes and rivers, and picking wild berries, mushrooms and flowers, provided no harm or disturbance is caused. The right carries a corresponding duty of care, often summarised in Norway's formulation "leave no trace". Commercial harvesting and access to gardens, farmyards and cultivated fields are generally excluded.

History

In medieval Europe, common land provided grazing, fuel and foraging to local people, and many early legal systems treated uncultivated wilderness (the Norse almenningr) as open to all. Enclosure and the growth of exclusive private ownership in the early modern period curtailed these customs in much of Europe, but they survived most strongly in Scandinavia, Scotland and Alpine regions. In England, grievances over access helped found the Ramblers' movement; the mass trespass of Kinder Scout in 1932 became a symbolic episode in the campaign that eventually produced the national parks legislation of 1949 and the CRoW Act half a century later.

Elsewhere

Estonia allows access to uncultivated private land on foot during daylight unless fenced or signposted. In the Czech Republic, the concept of veřejná přístupnost krajiny (public accessibility of the landscape) is written into nature protection law. Several Austrian states permit recreational access to forests under the 1975 Forestry Act. In the United States, by contrast, there is no general right to roam over private land, though public lands administered by federal agencies are broadly open, and a few states preserve rights to uncultivated land for hunting and fishing.
