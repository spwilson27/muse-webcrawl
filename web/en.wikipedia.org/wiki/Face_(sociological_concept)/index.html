Face (sociological concept)

In sociology, face refers to the positive social value a person effectively claims for themselves in interaction — their public self-image, reputation, dignity, and prestige. The concept entered English-language social science through studies of Chinese culture, where notions of miànzi (面子) and liǎn (臉) denote social standing and moral reputation.

Goffman

Erving Goffman developed the concept in his essay "On Face-work" (1955, reprinted in Interaction Ritual, 1967), defining face as the positive social value a person claims through the line others assume they have taken during a contact. People engage in "facework" — defensive and protective practices — to maintain their own face and that of others; embarrassment and "losing face" occur when claimed face is discredited.

Politeness theory

Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson built on Goffman in Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage (1987), distinguishing positive face (the desire to be approved of and appreciated) from negative face (the desire for autonomy and freedom from imposition). Speech acts that threaten these wants are "face-threatening acts", mitigated by politeness strategies.

Cross-cultural use

"Saving face" and "losing face" are widely used in diplomacy, business, and everyday life, and researchers have examined how face concerns vary between individualist and collectivist cultures.
