Rutebeuf (fl. 1245–1285) was a French trouvère, one of the most distinctive poetic voices of thirteenth-century Paris. His name is often explained as a pun on "rude bœuf" ("rude ox") — he himself wrote "Rutebeuf, qui est dit rude et bœuf" ("Rutebeuf, which means rude and ox").

Life

Little is known of Rutebeuf's life beyond what can be gleaned from his own complaints of poverty, gambling losses, and the ingratitude of patrons. Probably born in Champagne, he lived in Paris and wrote for noble and clerical patrons. He married and, by his own rueful account, fell into destitution; poems such as the Complainte Rutebeuf and La Pauvreté Rutebeuf ("The Poverty of Rutebeuf") turned his misfortunes into literature. He died around 1285.

Works

Some fifty-six works are attributed to him across an unusually wide range of genres: satires attacking the mendicant friars (in support of the University of Paris in its quarrel with the Franciscans and Dominicans), crusade poems urging aid to the Holy Land, fabliaux, saints' lives, personal lyric complaints, and the miracle play Le Miracle de Théophile — the earliest surviving French miracle play — in which the clerk Théophile sells his soul to the Devil and is rescued by the Virgin Mary. His poem Le Dit de l'Herberie is a celebrated comic monologue of a quack herbalist.

Rutebeuf's mixture of invective, self-irony, and wordplay marks him as a forerunner of François Villon, whose own persona of the impoverished poet owes much to him. In the 1950s the chansonnier Léo Ferré set Rutebeuf's complaint ("Que sont mes amis devenus…", in an adaptation often attributed to the poem Les Pauvres de Paris tradition) to music as "Pauvre Rutebeuf", reviving his fame in modern France.
