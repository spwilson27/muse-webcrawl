The Celtic nations or Celtic countries are a cultural area and federative concept referring to the territories in northwestern Europe where Celtic languages or cultural traits have survived into the modern period. Six territories are conventionally recognised: Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany (in France), the Isle of Man, and Cornwall (in England).

History of the concept

The idea of a shared "Celtic" identity among these peoples was developed by antiquarians and linguists from the 17th century onward — the Welsh scholar Edward Lhuyd's comparative work on the languages was foundational — and became a political and cultural movement in the 19th and 20th centuries through the Celtic Revival, Pan-Celtic congresses, and organisations such as the Celtic League (founded 1961). Regions such as Galicia and Asturias in Spain, which have Celtic heritage but no surviving Celtic language, are sometimes associated with the wider Celtic world but are not counted among the six nations.

Culture and co-operation

Shared institutions include inter-Celtic festivals such as the Festival Interceltique de Lorient in Brittany, language-revitalisation movements, and sporting events. The Celtic nations cooperate politically in various forums, and several — Ireland as a sovereign state, Scotland and Wales as devolved nations within the United Kingdom, the Isle of Man as a Crown Dependency, and Brittany and Cornwall as regions — pursue distinct constitutional paths.
