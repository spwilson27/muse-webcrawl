Woe from Wit

Woe from Wit (Russian: «Горе от ума», Gore ot uma) is a verse comedy in four acts by Alexander Griboyedov, written in 1823–24 and first published in full in 1862. It is considered one of the masterpieces of Russian literature and a cornerstone of the Russian theatre, whose lines have passed into the language as proverbs.

Plot

Alexander Chatsky, a young nobleman, returns to Moscow after three years abroad and visits the house of the official Famusov, hoping to renew his love for Famusov's daughter Sophia. He finds that Sophia has transferred her affections to Molchalin, her father's obsequious secretary, and that Moscow society has no patience for Chatsky's wit, idealism, and contempt for servility, corruption, and Francophilia. After a disastrous ball, rumours — started by Sophia herself — spread that Chatsky is mad, and he leaves Moscow in despair.

Characters

  • Alexander Andreyevich Chatsky — the idealistic, satirical hero.
  • Pavel Afanasyevich Famusov — a senior official who embodies traditional Moscow society.
  • Sophia Famusova — his daughter.
  • Alexey Stepanovich Molchalin — Famusov's secretary, Sophia's secret lover.
  • Colonel Skalozub — the dim-witted officer Famusov hopes Sophia will marry.
  • Liza — the maid, whose common sense punctures the pretensions of her employers.

History and influence

The play circulated widely in manuscript copies because censorship delayed its full staging; an expurgated version appeared in 1831, two years after Griboyedov's death in Tehran. Its aphorisms — such as "Happy people don't watch the clock" — are still quoted daily in Russian. The play's contrast between the lone intelligent man and a conformist society has made it a touchstone of Russian culture ever since.
