Donegal tweed is a woven woollen fabric made in County Donegal, Ireland, for centuries. It is characterised by a plain-weave ground flecked with brightly coloured slubs — specks of contrasting yarn in the warp and weft that evoke the colours of the Donegal landscape — and by patterns such as herringbone and checks. It is used for jackets, caps, suits and furnishings and is world-renowned alongside Scotland's Harris tweed.

History

Hand-weaving in Donegal grew from a cottage industry promoted in the late 19th century by figures such as Alice Rowland Hart, who marketed the cloth in London. Mills at Ardara, Kilcar and Donegal Town — home to Magee of Donegal (founded 1866) — mechanised production while hand-weaving survived in the county's west. A fifth-generation handweaver, the McNutt and Studio Donegal workshops, and others continue the craft.

Characteristics

Traditional Donegal tweed is a plain weave with flecks of colour sprinkled through a muted base; modern versions add bolder colours and finer counts for fashion houses. Unlike Harris tweed, "Donegal" is not protected by a certification mark, so the name is used loosely worldwide — genuine cloth is woven in the county itself.
