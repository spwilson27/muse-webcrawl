Endocrine system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The endocrine system is the collection of glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream, coordinating growth, metabolism, reproduction, mood and homeostasis across the body. Its major glands include the pituitary (the "master gland", itself directed by the hypothalamus), thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.

Hormones are chemical messengers: insulin from the pancreas lowers blood glucose; thyroxine from the thyroid sets metabolic rate; adrenaline from the adrenal medulla prepares the body for "fight or flight"; cortisol, sex steroids, growth hormone and many others tune systems over timescales from seconds to decades. Feedback loops — typically negative — keep hormone levels in range, and the endocrine and nervous systems interlock so closely that the field of neuroendocrinology bridges them.

Endocrine disorders include diabetes (the most common), thyroid over- and under-activity, Addison's disease and Cushing's syndrome. The science of endocrinology began in the early twentieth century — the word "hormone" was coined in 1905 for secretin — and hormone therapies, from insulin to contraceptive steroids, are among medicine's most consequential products.
