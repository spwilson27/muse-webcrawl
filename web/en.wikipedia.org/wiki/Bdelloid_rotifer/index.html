Bdelloid rotifer

Phylum: Rotifera
Class: Bdelloidea
Species: More than 450 described

Bdelloid rotifers are a class (Bdelloidea) of microscopic freshwater invertebrates in the phylum Rotifera. They are famous in evolutionary biology as an ancient "asexual scandal": the entire class has reproduced without males, sex, or meiosis for tens of millions of years, yet has diversified into hundreds of species.

Biology

Bdelloids range from about 150 to 700 micrometres in length. Like other rotifers they possess a ciliated crown (corona) used for feeding and swimming, a muscular pharynx (mastax) with hard jaws (trophi), and in bdelloids a characteristic creeping, leech-like manner of movement (their name derives from the Greek bdella, "leech"). Females produce eggs that develop parthenogenetically into genetically identical daughters. No male bdelloid has ever been observed.

Asexuality and survival

The persistence of bdelloids contradicts the expectation that asexual lineages accumulate harmful mutations and go extinct quickly. Proposed explanations include efficient DNA repair, gene conversion, and extensive horizontal gene transfer: roughly 8–10% of a bdelloid's genes appear to be of non-animal origin, acquired from bacteria, fungi, and plants. Bdelloids are also extraordinarily resistant to desiccation and ionising radiation; in 2021 a bdelloid revived after some 24,000 years frozen in Siberian permafrost.

Ecology

Bdelloids live in freshwater habitats worldwide — in rivers, ponds, mosses, lichens, and soil water films — feeding on bacteria, algae, and organic detritus. They tolerate drying by entering a dormant state (anhydrobiosis) and can be dispersed by wind as dry "tuns".
