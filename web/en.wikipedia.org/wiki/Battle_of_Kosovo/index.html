The Battle of Kosovo took place on 15 June 1389 between an army led by Prince Lazar Hrebeljanović of Serbia and an invading army of the Ottoman Empire under Sultan Murad I. Fought on Kosovo Polje ("Field of Blackbirds") near modern Pristina, it is one of the most famous battles of medieval Europe: both commanders were killed, and although the immediate outcome was inconclusive, the losses accelerated Serbia's fall under Ottoman domination. In Serbian tradition it became the centrepiece of the Kosovo myth, commemorated on Vidovdan (St Vitus Day).

Background

Since their victory at the Maritsa in 1371 the Ottomans had steadily expanded into the Balkans. Prince Lazar, ruler of Moravian Serbia, assembled a coalition including troops of his son-in-law Vuk Branković and a Bosnian contingent under Vlatko Vuković, joined by Knights Hospitaller and Albanian and other allied forces. Sultan Murad marched to meet them with his sons Bayezid and Yakub, Anatolian and Rumelian troops, and Serbian vassal contingents of his own.

The battle

The Serbian heavy cavalry, led by Lazar in the centre, broke into the Ottoman left wing, while the Bosnian wing under Vuković defeated the Ottoman right. The battle turned when Bayezid's reserve counter-attacked and overwhelmed the Serbs; Lazar was captured and executed. Murad himself was killed — in the best-known account, assassinated in his tent by the Serbian knight Miloš Obilić, who had feigned desertion; other accounts have him killed on the field or by a wounded soldier when touring the battlefield. Command passed to Bayezid, who, according to tradition, had his brother Yakub strangled to secure the succession — the first royal fratricide of Ottoman history.

Aftermath and legacy

Both armies withdrew in ruins; the Ottomans, with far greater reserves, recovered faster. Lazar's widow Milica accepted Ottoman vassalage, and Serbia was fully annexed in 1459. Epic poetry transformed the defeat into a sacred covenant: Lazar, offered a choice between an earthly and a heavenly kingdom, chose the heavenly one. The 600th anniversary commemoration in 1989, including Slobodan Milošević's Gazimestan speech, gave the myth renewed and fateful political weight.
