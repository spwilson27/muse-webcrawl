English-language spelling reform

English-language spelling reform refers to the many proposals, made over several centuries, to change the spelling of the English language to make it more regular, more phonetic, or easier to learn. English orthography is famously irregular: the same sound may be written in many ways (as in to, too, two), and the same letter combination may represent many sounds, a fact celebrated in the constructed word ghoti, jokingly said to be pronounceable as "fish". Reformers have argued that simplifying spelling would reduce the time children spend learning to read and write, cut printing costs, and make English more accessible to learners worldwide.

Early proposals

Suggestions for reforming English spelling date back at least to the sixteenth century, when scholars such as John Hart and William Bullokar devised phonetic alphabets. In the eighteenth century, Benjamin Franklin proposed a new alphabet for English that dropped several letters and added new characters for sounds such as "sh" and "ng". None of these early schemes gained official adoption.

Noah Webster and American spelling

The most successful spelling reformer was the American lexicographer Noah Webster, whose dictionaries in the early nineteenth century standardised many distinctively American spellings: color for colour, center for centre, traveled for travelled, and defense for defence. Webster believed a distinct American spelling would help define the young nation's cultural independence.

The Simplified Spelling Board

In 1906 the industrialist Andrew Carnegie funded the Simplified Spelling Board in the United States, which published lists of recommended simplified forms such as thru, catalog and program. President Theodore Roosevelt ordered the Government Printing Office to adopt about 300 of the board's spellings, but Congress pushed back and the order was largely reversed. A few of the forms, notably catalog and program, nevertheless became standard in American usage.

Twentieth-century systems

Later reformers produced complete alternative alphabets and systems, including the Shavian alphabet (funded by a bequest from George Bernard Shaw and designed by Kingsley Read), Sir James Pitman's Initial Teaching Alphabet used experimentally in British schools in the 1960s, and the long-running work of the Simplified Spelling Society (now the English Spelling Society), founded in 1908, whose journal and cut-spelling proposals kept the cause alive. Harry Lindgren's SR1 system in Australia proposed changing only the spelling of the short "e" sound as a first step.

Arguments for and against

Supporters argue that English children take roughly two years longer to learn to read than children in countries with transparent orthographies, such as Finland or Italy. Opponents counter that English spelling preserves etymology and word relationships (sign/signature), that a single phonetic system cannot serve all accents, and that reform would make the existing body of literature hard to read. In practice, change has come gradually and piecemeal, through dictionaries, publishers' style guides and the informal spellings of texting and advertising.
