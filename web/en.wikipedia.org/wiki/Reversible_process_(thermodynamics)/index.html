Reversible process (thermodynamics)

In thermodynamics, a reversible process is a process, involving a system and its surroundings, whose direction can be reversed by infinitesimal changes in some properties of the surroundings, such as pressure or temperature. Such a process proceeds through a continuous sequence of equilibrium states, and is called quasi-static. Throughout an entire reversible process, the system is in thermodynamic equilibrium with its surroundings.

Entropy

Having been reversed, a reversible process leaves no net change in either the system or the surroundings, and the change in total entropy is zero. Reversible processes produce the maximum amount of useful work from an engine, and require the minimum work input for a refrigerator or heat pump; the Carnot cycle is the classic example of a reversible cycle and sets the upper efficiency limit for any heat engine operating between two temperatures.

Irreversibility

All real processes are irreversible to some degree, because of friction, unrestrained expansion, finite temperature differences during heat transfer, and other dissipative effects, which generate entropy. A process that is not reversible is termed irreversible. Reversible processes are nevertheless useful idealizations: they define limits of performance and appear in the definitions of quantities such as entropy, which is computed as the heat transferred reversibly divided by temperature.
