Polarisation density

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In classical electromagnetism, polarisation density (or electric polarisation, or simply polarisation) is the vector field that expresses the density of permanent or induced electric dipole moments in a dielectric material. When a dielectric is placed in an external electric field, its molecules gain an electric dipole moment and the dielectric becomes polarised. The electric dipole moment induced per unit volume of the dielectric material is called the electric polarisation of the dielectric.

Polarisation density also describes how a material responds to an applied electric field as well as the way the material changes the electric field, and can be used to calculate the forces that result from those interactions. It can be compared to magnetisation, which is the measure of the corresponding response of a material to a magnetic field in magnetism.

Definition

The polarisation density P is defined as the average electric dipole moment per unit volume:

P = (1/V) Σ pi

Its SI unit is the coulomb per square metre (C/m²). In a linear, homogeneous, isotropic dielectric, polarisation is proportional to the electric field through the electric susceptibility χe:

P = ε0χeE

where ε0 is the electric constant. The electric displacement field D is then given by D = ε0E + P = εE for a linear material with permittivity ε.

Bound charges

Polarisation gives rise to bound charges: a volume bound-charge density ρb = −∇·P and a surface bound-charge density σb = P·n̂. These bound charges, unlike free charges, cannot move freely through the material. The relation between P, free charge and the displacement field underlies the formulation of Maxwell's equations in matter.

Nonlinear and anisotropic media

In anisotropic crystals P is related to E by a tensor susceptibility. In ferroelectric materials a spontaneous polarisation persists even without an applied field and exhibits hysteresis. At high field strengths, nonlinear polarisation terms (proportional to E², E³, …) give rise to nonlinear optical effects such as frequency doubling and the Kerr effect.
