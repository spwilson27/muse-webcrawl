Software quality

In software engineering, software quality refers to the degree to which a software product meets its specified requirements and the needs and expectations of its users. Quality is commonly analysed along two broad dimensions: functional quality — how well the software does what it is supposed to do — and structural quality — how well the code and architecture are built.

Quality models

Several standard models decompose quality into characteristics. The ISO/IEC 25010 standard defines eight product quality characteristics: functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security, maintainability, and portability. Earlier models include McCall's model (1977), Boehm's model, and the FURPS framework (Functionality, Usability, Reliability, Performance, Supportability).

Measurement

Software quality is measured through metrics such as defect density, test coverage, cyclomatic complexity, mean time between failures, and customer-reported problems. Static code analysis tools can detect structural weaknesses — sometimes aggregated as "technical debt" — while dynamic testing measures behaviour in execution.

Quality assurance

Software quality assurance (SQA) encompasses the processes used to ensure quality throughout the development lifecycle: requirements review, coding standards, peer review, continuous integration, automated testing, and process frameworks such as ISO 9001 and the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI). A distinction is drawn between quality assurance (process-oriented, preventive) and quality control (product-oriented, detective, e.g. testing).

Economics

Defects are generally far cheaper to fix when found early — a principle summarised by Barry Boehm's finding that the cost of fixing a defect can rise by orders of magnitude between the requirements phase and production use. Poor software quality has been estimated to cost the US economy trillions of dollars annually in operational failures and technical debt.
