The Allies of World War I, formally the Allied and Associated Powers, were the coalition of countries that opposed the Central Powers during the First World War (1914–1918). The coalition grew out of the Triple Entente of France, Russia and the United Kingdom, formed before the war through the Franco-Russian Alliance, the Entente Cordiale and the Anglo-Russian Convention.

Membership

Serbia and Belgium were at war from 1914, joined by Japan (1914), Italy — which switched from the Triple Alliance under the secret Treaty of London (1915) — Portugal and Romania (1916), and the United States and Greece (1917), among many others; China declared war on Germany in 1917 and contributed labour corps. The Russian Empire left the coalition after the October Revolution, signing the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with the Central Powers in March 1918, which freed German divisions for the Western Front but also brought American troops arriving in large numbers.

Coordination

The Allies coordinated imperfectly for most of the war: each power pursued its own strategy, linked by conferences and, from late 1917, the Supreme War Council. In spring 1918, under the pressure of the German spring offensive, Ferdinand Foch was appointed generalissimo of the Allied armies in France. Allied advantages included naval blockade, greater combined population and industrial capacity, and access to global resources and American credit.

Outcome

The Hundred Days Offensive of August–November 1918 forced Germany to seek an armistice, signed on 11 November 1918. The principal Allies then dominated the Paris Peace Conference of 1919, imposing the Treaty of Versailles and its companion settlements on the defeated powers.
