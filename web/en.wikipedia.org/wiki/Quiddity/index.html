Quiddity

Quiddity
From Latin: quidditas, "whatness"
Field: Metaphysics, scholastic philosophy
Contrast term: Haecceity ("thisness")

In scholastic philosophy, quiddity (Latin quidditas) is another term for the essence of an object — literally, its "whatness": the properties a thing must have for it to be that kind of thing, which it shares with all other members of its kind. The word derives from the Latin interrogative quid, "what", answering the question "What is it?" (quid est?).

History

The term renders into Latin the Greek to ti ēn einai ("the what-it-was-to-be") of Aristotle, transmitted through the Arabic philosophy of Avicenna, who distinguished a thing's quiddity (māhiyya) from its existence. Medieval thinkers debated the relation between essence and existence extensively: for Thomas Aquinas, in creatures the two are really distinct, while in God they are identical.

Quiddity and haecceity

Duns Scotus contrasted quiddity — what a thing is, in common terms — with haecceity (Latin haecceitas, "thisness"), the positive feature that makes an individual this particular individual and no other. Together the pair frames a central medieval question: if essence is shared, what accounts for individuation?

Later usage

In modern non-technical usage, "quiddity" can also mean a quibble or subtle academic distinction, and in literary contexts the irreducible essence or distinctive character of a person or thing. Hamlet's "quiddities" and "quillets" in Shakespeare reflect the word's association with legalistic hair-splitting.
