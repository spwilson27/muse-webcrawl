Multics (Multiplexed Information and Computing Service) was an influential early time-sharing operating system, begun in 1964 as a joint project of MIT, Bell Labs, and General Electric. It pioneered many concepts now standard in operating systems.

History

Multics grew out of MIT's Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS). It was designed as a "computing utility" providing continuous service like electricity. First running on the GE-645 mainframe in 1969, it was later commercialised by Honeywell. Bell Labs withdrew from the project in 1969; two Bell researchers who had worked on it, Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie, went on to create Unix, whose name is a pun on Multics. The last Multics installation, at the Canadian Department of National Defence in Halifax, was shut down on 30 October 2000. Honeywell released the source code in 2007, and it is now preserved as open historical software.

Innovations

Multics introduced, among other things: a hierarchical file system with access control lists; a single-level store unifying files and memory segments; dynamic linking; ring-based security (protection rings); hot-swappable hardware reconfiguration while running; and implementation largely in a high-level language (PL/I). Its security architecture influenced later protection models, and its design goals shaped Unix, which deliberately simplified Multics' ambitions.
