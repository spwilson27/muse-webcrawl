Anodizing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anodizing is an electrolytic passivation process used to increase the thickness of the natural oxide layer on the surface of metal parts. The process is called anodizing because the part to be treated forms the anode electrode of an electrolytic cell. It is used most extensively on aluminium alloys, and also on titanium, magnesium, zinc and other non-ferrous metals.

Process

The workpiece is immersed in an acid electrolyte bath — commonly sulfuric acid for aluminium — and an electric current is passed through it. Oxygen released at the anode surface reacts with the metal to build a controlled, porous aluminium oxide layer far thicker than the natural film. The porous layer can absorb organic or inorganic dyes, giving durable coloured finishes, and is then "sealed" by boiling in water or steam to close the pores.

Applications

Anodizing increases corrosion resistance and wear resistance, provides better adhesion for paint and glues than bare metal, and produces decorative finishes. Hard anodizing (Type III) yields especially thick, hard coatings used on cookware, hydraulic components and architectural fittings. Coloured anodized aluminium is familiar in consumer electronics housings, bicycle parts and flashlights.

History

Anodizing was first used industrially in 1923 to protect seaplane parts from corrosion, using a chromic-acid process developed at the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough; sulfuric-acid anodizing followed in 1927.
