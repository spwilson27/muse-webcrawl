The AD–AS model (aggregate demand–aggregate supply model) is a standard macroeconomic model used to explain the price level and output of an economy through the interaction of aggregate demand and aggregate supply. It is plotted with the overall price level on the vertical axis and real GDP on the horizontal axis.

Aggregate demand

The aggregate demand (AD) curve shows the total quantity of goods and services demanded at each price level. It slopes downward for three main reasons: the wealth effect (higher prices reduce the real value of money holdings), the interest-rate effect (higher prices raise interest rates and depress investment), and the exchange-rate effect (higher domestic prices reduce net exports).

Aggregate supply

The short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve slopes upward, reflecting sticky wages and prices: at higher price levels, firms expand output because some input costs are fixed. In the long run, supply is represented by a vertical long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve at potential output, since the price level does not affect the economy's productive capacity once all prices and wages adjust.

Equilibrium and shocks

Short-run equilibrium occurs where AD intersects SRAS; long-run equilibrium adds LRAS. Demand shocks — changes in consumption, investment, government spending, net exports or the money supply — shift AD and move output around its potential, while supply shocks (such as oil price spikes) shift SRAS, producing stagflation when adverse. The model underlies textbook accounts of business cycles and of the differing effects of monetary and fiscal policy in the short and long run.
