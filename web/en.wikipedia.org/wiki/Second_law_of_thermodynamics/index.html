Second law of thermodynamics

The second law of thermodynamics is a physical law based on universal empirical observation concerning heat and energy interconversions. A simple statement of the law is that heat always moves from hotter objects to colder objects (or "downhill"), unless energy in some form is supplied to reverse the direction of heat flow. Another definition is: "Not all heat energy can be converted into work in a cyclic process." More fundamentally, the law states that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease over time, and is constant if and only if all processes are reversible.

Second law of thermodynamics
StatementThe entropy of an isolated system never decreases
Key formulatorsSadi Carnot, Rudolf Clausius, Lord Kelvin, Max Planck
Related conceptEntropy; heat death of the universe

Formulations

The law has been expressed in many equivalent ways. The Clausius statement holds that no process is possible whose sole result is the transfer of heat from a cooler to a hotter body. The Kelvin–Planck statement holds that no cyclic process is possible whose sole result is the absorption of heat from a reservoir and its complete conversion into work. In statistical mechanics, Ludwig Boltzmann related entropy to the number of microscopic configurations, S = kB log W.

History

The theory grew out of Sadi Carnot's 1824 analysis of steam engines, which established that the efficiency of a heat engine depends only on the temperatures between which it operates. Rudolf Clausius formulated the law in the 1850s and introduced the concept of entropy in 1865, memorably summarising the laws: "The energy of the universe is constant; the entropy of the universe tends to a maximum."

Consequences

The second law underlies the impossibility of perpetual motion machines of the second kind, sets the maximum (Carnot) efficiency of heat engines and refrigerators, and supplies the arrow of time in thermodynamics. Applied cosmologically, it suggests the universe will tend toward a state of maximum entropy, the so-called heat death.
