The Commissar Order (German: Kommissarbefehl) was an order issued by the German High Command (Oberkommando der Wehrmacht) on 6 June 1941, shortly before Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of the Soviet Union. It directed Wehrmacht troops to summarily execute any Soviet political commissars captured — Red Army political officers — along with officials and Communist functionaries, rather than treat them as prisoners of war. Together with the Barbarossa decree suspending military jurisdiction over crimes against Soviet civilians, it formed part of the criminal orders that shaped the war of annihilation (Vernichtungskrieg) in the East.

Content and implementation

The order declared that political commissars were not to be recognised as soldiers; they were to be "finished off at once" when captured in battle or in rear areas, and civilian officials suspected of resistance were to be shot. Guidelines minimised record-keeping so the executions would leave little trace. Although a number of senior officers, including some army commanders, disliked or quietly limited the order, it was widely implemented in the invasion's first months; historians estimate that several thousand commissars were murdered under it. The order was effectively suspended in mid-1942, partly because executing commissars stiffened Soviet resistance.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials and the subsequent High Command trial, the Commissar Order was treated as a paradigmatic criminal order. Defendants argued duress and superior orders; several commanders were convicted for transmitting or implementing it. The order remains a central historical document in debates about the Wehrmacht's complicity in Nazi crimes, countering the post-war "clean Wehrmacht" myth.
