Long-term care

Long-term care (LTC) is a variety of services which help meet both the medical and non-medical needs of people with a chronic illness or disability who cannot care for themselves for long periods. Long-term care is focused on individualized and coordinated services that promote independence, maximise patients' quality of life, and meet patients' needs over a period of time.

It is common for long-term care to provide custodial and non-skilled care, such as assisting with normal daily tasks like dressing, feeding, using the bathroom, meal preparation, home cleaning, and monitoring of safety or medication administration. Long-term care is provided in home-based settings, community settings (such as adult day care), and residential facilities including nursing homes and assisted living.

Who needs long-term care

Need rises steeply with age, and most recipients are older adults, but younger people with disabilities, serious mental illness, or conditions such as dementia, Parkinson's disease or the effects of stroke also rely on it. Worldwide, women make up about two-thirds of those who need long-term care.

Provision and funding

Systems vary greatly between countries. Some nations operate public social-insurance schemes dedicated to long-term care (such as Germany's Pflegeversicherung and Japan's long-term care insurance), others fund it through general health or social care budgets, and in the United States much nursing-home care is paid by Medicaid after personal resources are exhausted, while Medicare covers only limited skilled care. In all countries, informal care by family members provides the largest share of support, with major economic and health consequences for caregivers.

Workforce

Formal long-term care is delivered by nurses, nursing aides, home health aides, personal care assistants, therapists and social workers. The sector is characterised by staff shortages and high turnover in many countries, a challenge intensified by population ageing.
