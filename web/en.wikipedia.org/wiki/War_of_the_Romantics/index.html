War of the Romantics

The War of the Romantics was an aesthetic and polemical schism in nineteenth-century European classical music, in which the conservative circle around Johannes Brahms and the critic Eduard Hanslick opposed the progressive "New German School" of Franz Liszt, Richard Wagner and their followers.

The two camps

The progressive camp championed programmatic music, the symphonic poem, expanded harmony and Wagner's ideal of the "artwork of the future", drawing on the legacy of Hector Berlioz. The conservative camp defended absolute music and the classical forms of sonata, symphony and chamber music inherited from Beethoven, Mendelssohn and Schumann, arguing that musical meaning resided in form itself.

History

The conflict sharpened around 1860, when a prematurely leaked manifesto signed by the young Brahms and Joseph Joachim attacked the Liszt circle. Hanslick's treatise On the Musically Beautiful (1854) gave the conservative position its theoretical statement, while journalists aligned with Liszt and Wagner promoted the opposing cause in the press. Personal and critical exchanges were often bitter, though modern scholars view the divide as more fluid than the rhetoric suggested; Brahms admired much of Wagner's music, and Wagner respected Brahms.

The "war" gradually faded as both camps' music entered the canon, and it is now studied chiefly as a landmark episode in the history of music criticism and aesthetics.
