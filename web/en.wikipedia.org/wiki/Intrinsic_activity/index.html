Intrinsic activity


Field: Pharmacology
Related concept: Efficacy

Intrinsic activity (IA) or efficacy refers to the relative ability of a drug–receptor complex to produce a maximum functional response. It must be distinguished from affinity, which is a measure of the ability of the drug to bind to its molecular target, and from potency, which depends on both. The term was proposed by R. P. Stephenson in 1956 to describe the way drugs of different efficacy can produce responses of varying intensity even when they occupy the same proportion of receptors.

Values

Intrinsic activity is expressed as a proportion of the maximum response of a reference full agonist, conventionally between 0 and 1. A full agonist has IA = 1, producing the system's maximum response; a partial agonist has IA between 0 and 1, producing a submaximal response even at full receptor occupancy; and a silent antagonist has IA = 0, binding without activating the receptor. Inverse agonists reduce constitutive receptor activity below baseline and can be described as having negative efficacy.

Clinical significance

Partial agonists such as varenicline at nicotinic receptors or aripiprazole at dopamine D2 receptors exploit intrinsic activity to stabilise a system, activating it when endogenous tone is low and blocking it when tone is high.
