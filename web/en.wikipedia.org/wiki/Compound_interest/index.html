Compound interest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Compound interest is interest accumulated from a principal sum and previously accumulated interest. It is the result of reinvesting or retaining interest that would otherwise be paid out, so that interest in the next period is earned on the principal sum plus previously accumulated interest. In contrast, simple interest is interest calculated only on the principal amount.

Compound interest is standard in finance and economics. It is the usual method by which savings grow in bank accounts and by which debt grows on loans and credit cards when balances are not paid down.

Compounding frequency

The compounding frequency is the number of times per year accumulated interest is capitalised (credited to the account). Interest may compound annually, half-yearly, quarterly, monthly, weekly, daily, or continuously. The more frequently interest compounds, the greater the amount of compound interest.

Formula

The total compounded amount after t years, with principal P, nominal annual interest rate r, and n compounding periods per year, is:

A = P (1 + r/n)nt

The compound interest earned is A − P. For a single year with annual compounding this reduces to A = P(1 + r).

If interest is compounded continuously, the amount is A = Pert, where e is Euler's number, approximately 2.71828. The effective annual rate accounts for compounding within the year: EAR = (1 + r/n)n − 1.

Example

Suppose $1,000 is deposited at a nominal annual rate of 10%, compounded monthly. After one year the balance is 1000 × (1 + 0.10/12)12 ≈ $1,104.71, an effective annual yield of about 10.47%. Over long horizons compounding produces exponential growth: the same deposit left for 30 years at 10% compounded annually grows to about $17,449.

History

Compound interest was known in ancient Mesopotamia, where clay tablets record problems calculating it. Medieval Italian merchants used it widely, and the Florentine mathematician Fibonacci discussed it in his Liber Abaci (1202). Richard Witt's Arithmeticall Questions (1613) was an early text devoted substantially to the subject. Jacob Bernoulli's study of continuous compounding led to his discovery of the constant e.

Rule of 72

A common approximation, the rule of 72, estimates the time needed for a sum to double: divide 72 by the annual percentage rate. At 8% per year, money doubles in about 72/8 = 9 years.
