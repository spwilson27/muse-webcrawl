French Renaissance

The French Renaissance was the cultural and artistic movement in France between the 15th and early 17th centuries, conventionally dated from the French invasion of Italy in 1494 during the reign of Charles VIII until the death of Henry IV in 1610. It saw the importation of Italian Renaissance art, architecture and humanist learning, adapted to French traditions.

Background

French campaigns in Italy exposed the nobility to the art of Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael and to the palaces of the Italian courts. Francis I (reigned 1515–47), the movement's great royal patron, invited Leonardo da Vinci to spend his last years at Clos Lucé near Amboise, and employed Italian artists including Rosso Fiorentino and Francesco Primaticcio, whose work at Fontainebleau formed the first School of Fontainebleau.

Architecture

The châteaux of the Loire Valley — Chambord, Blois, Chenonceau, Azay-le-Rideau — embody the French Renaissance style: medieval silhouettes of turrets and steep roofs married to classical orders, symmetry and Italianate ornament. In Paris, Pierre Lescot's work on the Louvre set the pattern for French classicism.

Literature and thought

François Rabelais's Gargantua and Pantagruel celebrated humanist learning and appetite; the poets of the Pléiade, led by Pierre de Ronsard and Joachim du Bellay, set out to make French a literary language equal to Latin. Michel de Montaigne essentially invented the essay in his Essais (1580). Humanists such as Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples and Guillaume Budé advanced Greek studies and biblical scholarship.

Decline

The Wars of Religion (1562–98) devastated patronage and the country alike. The movement shaded into Mannerism and, under Henry IV and his successors, into the Baroque and the classicism of the Grand Siècle.
