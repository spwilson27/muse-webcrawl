The Paimio Sanatorium (Finnish: Paimion parantola) is a former tuberculosis sanatorium in Paimio, southwest Finland, designed by the Finnish architect Alvar Aalto and completed in 1933. It is one of the most internationally recognised works of modernist architecture, and a landmark of functionalist design conceived around the needs of the patient.

Design

Aalto won the architectural competition for the sanatorium in 1929. The building consists of a tall, slim A-wing containing the patients' rooms, oriented to the south to maximise sunlight, connected to a lower B-wing of communal spaces — dining hall, library, workrooms and lecture hall — with a rooftop sun terrace running the length of the patient wing. Aalto designed the building and its interiors as a total work of art: colour schemes were chosen for their calming effect, washbasins were shaped to run silently, and lighting was designed not to glare into the eyes of bedridden patients.

For the sanatorium Aalto and his wife Aino also designed furniture, including the famous Paimio Chair (1931–32), a bent birchwood chair whose angle was calculated to help tuberculosis patients breathe more easily. The furniture was produced by the company Artek, which Aalto co-founded.

Later use

As tuberculosis treatment changed with antibiotics, the sanatorium was converted to a general hospital in the 1960s. It later served as a rehabilitation centre and has been proposed for inclusion on UNESCO's World Heritage List. The building is now managed by the Paimio Sanatorium Foundation, which preserves it and presents exhibitions on Aalto's work.
