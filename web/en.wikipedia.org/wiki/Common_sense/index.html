Common sense

Common sense is sound, practical judgement concerning everyday matters, or a basic ability to perceive, understand, and judge in a manner that is shared by ("common to") nearly all people. It is distinguished from specialised knowledge and from abstract reasoning: common sense concerns the beliefs and propositions that most people hold to be prudent and of sound judgement, without dependence upon study or research.

History of the concept

The term derives from Aristotle's notion of a "common sense" (koine aisthesis), an inner faculty that integrates the deliverances of the five external senses into unified perceptions. In Roman usage, sensus communis came also to denote shared social sensibility. During the Enlightenment the term acquired its modern meaning of ordinary, untutored judgement about practical matters.

The Scottish philosopher Thomas Reid founded the Scottish school of common sense, arguing against the scepticism of David Hume that certain first principles — the existence of an external world, of other minds, of reliable memory — are self-evident to common sense and cannot coherently be doubted. Thomas Paine's 1776 pamphlet Common Sense appealed to the same ideal in political argument.

Philosophical discussion

Philosophers have debated whether common sense is a reliable guide to truth. G. E. Moore defended common-sense beliefs against sceptical arguments, holding that we are more certain that we have hands than of any premise used to doubt it. Critics note that widely shared beliefs have sometimes proven false — for example, the everyday impression that the sun revolves around the Earth — and that what counts as "common sense" varies across cultures and eras. Antonio Gramsci distinguished common sense as the uncritically absorbed folklore of a society from the reflective "good sense" that can be developed from it.

In artificial intelligence

In artificial intelligence research, common-sense knowledge — the vast body of tacit facts about how the world works that people take for granted — has proven remarkably difficult to encode. Projects such as Cyc have attempted to build large knowledge bases of common-sense assertions, and "commonsense reasoning" remains an active benchmark area for machine learning systems.
