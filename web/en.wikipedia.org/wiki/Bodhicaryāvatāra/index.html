Bodhicaryāvatāra

The Bodhicaryāvatāra or Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra (Sanskrit: बोधिसत्त्वचर्यावतार; "A Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life") is a Mahāyāna Buddhist text written c. 700 AD in Sanskrit verse by the Indian monk Shantideva (Śāntideva), a scholar of the Nālandā monastic university. It is one of the most influential texts in Mahāyāna Buddhism, and is perhaps the single most widely read and practiced text in Tibetan Buddhism, where it is studied as a core guide to cultivating bodhicitta and the perfections (pāramitās) of the bodhisattva path.

Contents

The text has ten chapters, each devoted to the development of a particular perfection or theme. The first three chapters concern the benefits of bodhicitta, confession of faults, and taking hold of the awakening mind. Chapter four treats conscientiousness, chapter five mindfulness and introspection, chapter six patience (kṣānti), chapter seven heroic perseverance (vīrya), chapter eight meditative concentration (dhyāna), chapter nine wisdom (prajñā), and chapter ten the dedication of merit.

Famous passages include the meditation on the equality and exchange of self and others in chapter eight:

"All those who suffer in the world do so because of their desire for their own happiness. All those happy in the world are so because of their desire for the happiness of others."

Chapter nine and Madhyamaka philosophy

The ninth chapter, on wisdom, is a celebrated exposition of the Madhyamaka philosophy of emptiness (śūnyatā) associated with Nāgārjuna. It presents arguments against realist opponents — including Hindu schools and the Buddhist Yogācāra school — concerning the two truths, the emptiness of inherent existence, and the refutation of a substantially existent self or mind. In Tibet, the chapter became a focal point for centuries of commentary and debate.

Influence

The Bodhicaryāvatāra was translated into Tibetan in the eighth and ninth centuries and later revised; it survives in Sanskrit manuscripts from Nepal and in numerous commentaries, of which more than a hundred were composed in India and Tibet. The 14th Dalai Lama has said that "if I have any understanding of compassion and the bodhisattva path, it all comes from studying this text." It remains a foundational text for the study and practice of the bodhisattva path across Mahāyāna traditions.

English translations

Notable English translations include those by Stephen Batchelor (1979), the Padmakara Translation Group (The Way of the Bodhisattva, 1997), and Kate Crosby and Andrew Skilton (The Bodhicaryāvatāra, Oxford World's Classics, 1995). A translation by Wallace and Wallace appeared in 1997 under the title A Guide to the Bodhisattva Way of Life.
