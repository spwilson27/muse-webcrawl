Leoš Janáček (3 July 1854 – 12 August 1928) was a Czech composer, music theorist, folklorist, and teacher. Rooted in the Moravian folk music of his homeland, he developed one of the most original voices in early twentieth-century music, building his melodies from the rhythms and inflections of spoken Czech — his theory of "speech melodies" — and achieving international fame only in the last decade of his life.

Early life and career

Janáček was born in Hukvaldy, Moravia, the son of a schoolmaster. He sang as a chorister at the Augustinian monastery in Brno, studied in Prague, Leipzig, and Vienna, and in 1881 founded the Brno Organ School, which he directed for decades. With František Bartoš he collected Moravian folk songs, publishing influential editions that fed directly into his mature style. His early works drew on Dvořák and Czech nationalism; the breakthrough toward his personal idiom came with the opera Jenůfa (1904), composed over nine years shadowed by the deaths of both his children. Jenůfa's triumph in Prague in 1916, when the composer was 62, finally brought national recognition.

Mature works

The last decade of Janáček's life produced a remarkable sequence of masterpieces, fuelled in part by his intense, unconsummated love for Kamila Stösslová, a much younger married woman who inspired many of them. The operas Káťa Kabanová (1921), The Cunning Little Vixen (1924), The Makropulos Affair (1926), and the posthumously premiered From the House of the Dead (1930) are now cornerstones of the operatic repertoire, distinguished by terse, speech-driven vocal lines and luminous orchestral writing. His orchestral Taras Bulba (1918) and Sinfonietta (1926), the Glagolitic Mass (1926), two string quartets ("Kreutzer Sonata", 1923, and "Intimate Letters", 1928), and the piano cycle On an Overgrown Path complete a legacy that places him beside Dvořák and Smetana among Czech composers — and ahead of them in influence on modern opera.

Style

Janáček built themes from short, repetitive cells derived from speech rhythms and folk song; harmony moves by sudden juxtaposition rather than smooth progression, and the orchestration is spare, pungent, and unmistakable. His theoretical writings on speech melody, harmony, and rhythm were published in his lifetime and remain central to understanding his art.
