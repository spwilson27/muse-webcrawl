Solar deity

A solar deity is a god or goddess who represents the Sun, or an aspect of it, usually in terms of its power and strength. Solar deities and sun worship occur throughout most of recorded history in religions and mythologies worldwide. The Sun is variously imagined as a traveler crossing the sky by boat or chariot, as an eye of a greater god, as a dying-and-rising figure mirroring the daily and yearly cycle, or as the supreme deity itself.

Examples by culture

  • Ancient Egypt — Ra, who sails the heavens in a solar barque and battles the serpent Apophis nightly; also Amun-Ra, Aten (the sun disk, focus of Akhenaten's monotheistic experiment), and Horus.
  • Mesopotamia — Utu (Sumerian) / Shamash (Akkadian), god of the sun and justice.
  • Greece and Rome — Helios, who drives a sun chariot, later merged functionally with Apollo; the Roman Sol, elevated by Aurelian as Sol Invictus, whose festival on December 25 influenced later tradition.
  • Norse — Sól, a goddess pursued across the sky by the wolf Sköll, to be devoured at Ragnarök.
  • Hinduism — Surya, one of the principal deities, addressed in the Gayatri mantra.
  • Japan — Amaterasu, the sun goddess and mythical ancestress of the imperial house, central to Shinto.
  • Aztec and Inca — Huitzilopochtli and Tonatiuh among the Aztecs; Inti, the Inca sun god, ancestor of the Sapa Inca.
  • China — the myth of the ten suns and the archer Yi, and the sun goddess Xihe.

Solar vehicles and symbolism

Many solar deities travel in a vehicle: the Egyptian solar barque, Helios's chariot, Surya's seven-horsed chariot, and the Bronze Age Trundholm sun chariot from Denmark, in which a horse pulls a gilded disk. Common solar symbols include the sun cross, the swastika in its ancient usage, rayed crowns, and the solar disk. Solar myths frequently pair the sun with the moon and with cycles of death and renewal marked by solstices and equinoxes.

Interpretation

Nineteenth-century comparative mythologists such as Max Müller sometimes over-attributed solar meanings to myths, leading to the satirical backlash of "solar mythology." Modern scholarship treats sun worship more cautiously, while noting the genuinely central place of solar cults in states such as pharaonic Egypt and the Inca Empire, where the ruler claimed descent from the sun.
