Coevolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coevolution is the process by which two or more species reciprocally affect each other's evolution through natural selection. When one species evolves a new trait in response to another, and that change in turn alters the selection pressures on the second species, the pair are said to coevolve. The term was popularised by Paul Ehrlich and Peter Raven in their 1964 study of butterflies and their host plants.

Forms of coevolution

Coevolution may be mutualistic, as between flowering plants and their pollinators: flowers evolve shapes, colours and nectar rewards matched to the bodies and behaviour of insects or birds, which in turn evolve mouthparts and foraging patterns suited to the flowers. The extremely long nectar spur of the Madagascar orchid Angraecum sesquipedale, matched by the proboscis of a hawkmoth, was predicted by Charles Darwin before the moth was discovered.

Antagonistic coevolution occurs between predators and prey, hosts and parasites, and competitors. This is often described as an evolutionary arms race: prey evolve defences, predators evolve countermeasures, and the cycle repeats. Leigh Van Valen's Red Queen hypothesis proposes that organisms must evolve continuously merely to maintain their fitness relative to the organisms coevolving with them, so that extinction risk stays roughly constant over time.

Examples

Well-studied cases include rough-skinned newts and the garter snakes that evolve resistance to their tetrodotoxin; fig trees and fig wasps, in which each fig species is pollinated by a specific wasp; cuckoos and their hosts, whose eggs and recognition abilities evolve in response to one another; and the gene-for-gene interactions between flax plants and flax rust fungus that underlie much of plant disease resistance. Coevolution also operates at the community level ("diffuse coevolution"), where a trait evolves in response to a suite of interacting species rather than a single partner.

Significance

Coevolution is a major engine of biodiversity: reciprocal adaptation can drive speciation, maintain genetic variation, and structure ecosystems. It is also central to applied problems such as pesticide and antibiotic resistance, the emergence of infectious diseases, and biological control.
