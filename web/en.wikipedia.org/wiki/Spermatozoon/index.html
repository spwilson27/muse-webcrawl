Sperm

Sperm (from the Greek sperma, "seed") is the male reproductive cell, or gamete, in anisogamous forms of sexual reproduction. Animals produce motile sperm with a tail known as a flagellum, which are known as spermatozoa, while some red algae and fungi produce non-motile sperm cells, known as spermatia. In humans, a spermatozoon is about 50–60 micrometres long and carries a haploid set of 23 chromosomes.

Structure

A human spermatozoon consists of a head, containing the highly condensed nucleus and capped by the acrosome, a vesicle of enzymes used to penetrate the egg; a midpiece packed with mitochondria that generate energy; and a long tail (flagellum) whose undulations propel the cell. The acrosome reaction, triggered near the egg, releases enzymes that help the sperm cross the zona pellucida.

Production

Sperm are produced in the seminiferous tubules of the testes through spermatogenesis, a process taking about 74 days, after which they mature and are stored in the epididymis. A healthy ejaculate typically contains tens to hundreds of millions of sperm, though only a small fraction reach the site of fertilisation.

Fertilisation

After ejaculation, sperm undergo capacitation in the female reproductive tract, becoming capable of fertilising the oocyte. One sperm penetrates the egg, their nuclei fuse, and a diploid zygote is formed; the sperm also determines the chromosomal sex of the offspring by contributing either an X or a Y chromosome. Sperm cells were first described in 1677 by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek and his student Johan Ham, who observed them through a microscope.
