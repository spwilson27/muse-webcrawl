Sir Austin Bradford Hill CBE FRS (8 July 1897 – 18 April 1991) was an English epidemiologist and statistician who pioneered the modern randomised controlled trial and, together with Richard Doll, demonstrated the connection between cigarette smoking and lung cancer. He is known for proposing the Bradford Hill criteria for inferring causality from statistical associations.

Life and career

The son of the physiologist Leonard Erskine Hill, he served as a pilot in the Royal Naval Air Service during the First World War before tuberculosis ended his flying career. While convalescing he took a correspondence degree in economics and turned to statistics, working under Major Greenwood. In 1933 he succeeded Greenwood at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, becoming professor of medical statistics and later dean of the school. His textbook Principles of Medical Statistics (1937) became the standard introduction to the subject for generations of physicians.

Randomised controlled trial

Working with the Medical Research Council, Hill designed the 1948 trial of streptomycin for pulmonary tuberculosis, generally regarded as the first properly randomised clinical trial, in which patients were allocated to treatment or bed rest by random numbers with sealed envelopes. The trial demonstrated both the drug's effectiveness and the template for modern trial methodology.

Smoking and lung cancer

With Richard Doll, Hill conducted the case-control study published in 1950 linking smoking to carcinoma of the lung, and then the prospective British Doctors Study (begun 1951), which followed more than 34,000 doctors and provided powerful evidence of the dose-dependent increase in mortality from lung cancer and other diseases among smokers.

Bradford Hill criteria

In his 1965 President's Address to the Royal Society of Medicine, "The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?", Hill set out nine viewpoints for considering whether an observed association is causal: strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, and analogy. Only temporality—that the cause precedes the effect—is strictly necessary. The criteria remain widely used, and frequently debated, in epidemiology and public health.
