1556 Shaanxi earthquake

Date23 January 1556 (Ming dynasty)
EpicentreHua County, Shaanxi, China
MagnitudeEstimated ~8.0
Deaths~830,000 (deadliest earthquake recorded)
Affected areaShaanxi, Shanxi, Henan, Gansu and neighbouring provinces

The 1556 Shaanxi earthquake, known in China as the Jiajing earthquake (嘉靖大地震), occurred on 23 January 1556 in Huaxian, Shaanxi, during the reign of the Jiajing Emperor of the Ming dynasty. With an estimated death toll of around 830,000 people, it is the deadliest earthquake in recorded history and one of the deadliest natural disasters of all time. Modern estimates place its magnitude at about 8.0, based on geological data and contemporary descriptions of damage.

Earthquake

The quake's epicentre was in the Wei River valley near Hua County and Weinan in Shaanxi. It occurred in the early morning hours, along faults bounding the Ordos Plateau. Damage was reported over an area some 840 kilometres (520 miles) wide: in some counties an average of 60 percent of the population died. Many towns had their walls, temples, government offices and houses destroyed outright, and deep ground fissures opened, in places reported to be 20 metres deep. Landslides and soil liquefaction compounded the destruction, and aftershocks continued for months.

Yaodong cave dwellings

A major factor in the catastrophe was the region's housing. Millions of people in Shaanxi and Shanxi lived in yaodong, dwellings carved into loess cliffs — the soft, wind-deposited silt of the Loess Plateau. Loess is highly vulnerable to seismic shaking: the cliff faces collapsed wholesale, burying entire households, and the artificially carved caves offered little resistance to landslides. Most victims died inside these collapsing cave homes rather than in masonry buildings.

Aftermath and record

Imperial records describe devastation across Shaanxi, Shanxi, Henan, Gansu and other provinces, and the scholar Qin Keda, who survived the quake, recorded lessons urging people to move out of caves rather than rebuild in them and to construct more resilient houses. The death toll of approximately 830,000 — about 60 percent of the population in the worst-hit region — is drawn from imperial annals and local gazetteers. The disaster also contributed to famine and population decline in the region in subsequent years, as survivors faced destroyed homes, lost harvests and secondary flooding where landslides dammed rivers.
