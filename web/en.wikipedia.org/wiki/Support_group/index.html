Support group

Support groups bring together people facing a common problem or life circumstance.

In a support group, members provide each other with various types of help, usually non-professional and non-medical, for a shared, often burdensome characteristic or situation. The help may take the form of relating personal experiences, listening with empathy, offering practical advice and information, and simply providing a setting in which members feel understood by people with the same problem. Support groups may be led by peers, by professional facilitators, or by a combination, and they meet in person, by telephone, or online.

History and types

The best-known support group model is the twelve-step fellowship, beginning with Alcoholics Anonymous (AA), founded in 1935; its structure has been adapted for narcotics, overeating, gambling, and many other issues, and for family members through groups such as Al-Anon. Beyond addiction, support groups exist for bereavement, chronic and terminal illness, disability, caregiving, parenting, mental health conditions, survivors of abuse, and rare diseases for which members may never otherwise meet another person with the same diagnosis.

Benefits and research

Research on support groups finds benefits that include reduced isolation, improved coping, exchange of practical knowledge about treatments and services, and greater feelings of control. Studies of professionally led groups for cancer patients and other populations have reported improvements in quality of life and mood, though evidence for effects on medical outcomes such as survival is mixed. Online groups extend access to rural members, people with rare conditions, and those who prefer anonymity, though they can also spread misinformation and lack moderation.
