White pine blister rust

White pine blister rust is a fungal disease of five-needle pines caused by the rust fungus Cronartium ribicola. Accidentally introduced to North America from Europe in the early 20th century, it has become one of the most destructive forest diseases on the continent.

Life cycle

The fungus requires two unrelated hosts to complete its life cycle. On pines it produces spores that can only infect plants of the genus Ribes — currants and gooseberries — and spores produced on Ribes leaves in autumn are the ones that infect pines. Infection enters through the needles and grows into the bark, forming perennial cankers. In spring the cankers blister and release bright orange spores; over years the canker girdles and kills the branch or trunk above it.

Impact and control

The disease devastated eastern white pine forests and has severely damaged western white pine, sugar pine, limber pine and the high-elevation whitebark pine, which is now listed as threatened largely because of the rust. Control efforts historically included massive (and largely unsuccessful) campaigns to eradicate Ribes bushes near pine stands. Modern management focuses on breeding and planting rust-resistant pine stock, pruning infected branches, and protecting high-value trees.
