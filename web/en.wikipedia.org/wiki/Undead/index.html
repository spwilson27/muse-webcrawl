Undead

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The undead — beings neither truly alive nor wholly dead — recur in folklore worldwide.

Undead is a collective name for fictional, mythical or legendary beings that are deceased yet behave as if alive. Undead may be incorporeal, like ghosts, or corporeal, like vampires and zombies. The term is used most prominently in modern fantasy, horror fiction and role-playing games, but the underlying beliefs are ancient and widespread in the folklore of many cultures.

History of the concept

Belief in revenants — corpses returning from the grave — was common in medieval Europe, where chronicles record bodies being exhumed, staked or burned to stop them troubling the living. In Haitian folklore, the zombie is a dead person revived by a sorcerer. The word "undead" itself was used by Bram Stoker as the original subtitle of Dracula (1897), Dracula, or The Un-Dead, which fixed the term in popular culture.

Common types

  • Vampires — blood-drinking revenants found in Slavic and wider European folklore.
  • Zombies — from Haitian tradition; in modern fiction, often reanimated by infection or radiation.
  • Ghosts — the disembodied spirits of the dead.
  • Mummies — preserved corpses reanimated, popularised by Egyptian-themed horror.
  • Skeletons, liches and wights — staples of fantasy literature and games.

In popular culture

The undead are central antagonists in horror cinema from Nosferatu (1922) and George A. Romero's Night of the Living Dead (1968) onward, and form a standard class of monsters in tabletop and video games such as Dungeons & Dragons, where "undead" is a formal creature type. Philosophers and cultural critics read undead figures as metaphors for plague, otherness, consumerism and the fear of death itself.
