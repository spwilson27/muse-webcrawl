Mammography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mammography (also called mastography) is the process of using low-energy X-rays (usually around 30 kVp) to examine the human breast for diagnosis and screening. The goal of mammography is the early detection of breast cancer, typically through detection of characteristic masses or microcalcifications.

Procedure

The breast is compressed between two plates to spread the tissue and reduce the X-ray dose needed; standard views are the craniocaudal and mediolateral oblique projections. Digital mammography has largely replaced film, and digital breast tomosynthesis ("3D mammography") obtains multiple low-dose images from different angles.

Screening

Many countries run population screening programmes, though recommendations on age ranges and intervals differ: the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends biennial screening for women aged 40 to 74. Screening mammography is credited with reducing breast cancer mortality, but it also produces false positives, overdiagnosis of indolent cancers, and cumulative radiation exposure, and the balance of benefits and harms remains debated.

Diagnostic use

Diagnostic mammography is used to evaluate specific symptoms such as a lump, pain, nipple discharge, or an abnormality found on a screening study, and may be supplemented by ultrasound, MRI, or biopsy.
