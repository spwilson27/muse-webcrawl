Seed bomb

A seed bomb (or seed ball) is a small ball of seeds mixed with clay and compost or soil, dried, and then scattered onto land where plants are wanted. The clay casing protects the seeds from birds, insects, and drying out until rain breaks the ball down and conditions suit germination. Seed bombs are strongly associated with guerrilla gardening and community greening of vacant or derelict land.

History

Making seed balls was practised in ancient Egypt along the Nile floodplain, and was revived in Japan by the farmer-philosopher Masanobu Fukuoka, who called them tsuchi dango ("earth dumplings") in his natural farming method, using them to sow rice, barley, and green manure crops without tillage. In 1970s New York, artist Liz Christy and the Green Guerrillas adapted the technique — packed with wildflower seed — to green vacant lots, tossing "seed green-aids" over fences; the practice spread with the guerrilla gardening movement worldwide.

Use and considerations

Typical recipes mix roughly five parts dry clay (powdered red or local clay) with one part compost and one part seed, moistened and rolled into marble-sized balls. Advocates use them for revegetating hard-to-reach ground, pollinator strips, and erosion-prone banks. Ecologists caution that seed bombs should contain locally native species: scattering non-native or invasive seed can damage the very ecosystems the technique aims to help, and introducing plants to land without permission may be unlawful.
