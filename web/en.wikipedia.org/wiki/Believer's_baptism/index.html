Believer's baptism

Believer's baptism, also called credobaptism, is the Christian practice of baptising only those who make a personal profession of faith in Jesus Christ, as distinct from infant baptism (paedobaptism). It is most commonly administered by full immersion, though some traditions pour water. Adherents argue baptism in the New Testament always followed belief and repentance.

History

Anabaptists of the Radical Reformation, beginning with the Swiss Brethren baptisms at Zurich in 1525 under Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz, revived the practice and were persecuted by both Catholic and magisterial Protestant authorities. Baptists in 17th-century England — from John Smyth's congregation in Amsterdam (1609) and the Particular Baptists of the 1630s–40s — made it a defining doctrine, as did later Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites, Pentecostals and most modern evangelical and non-denominational churches. The 1689 London Baptist Confession codified it for many Baptists.

Theology

Credobaptists cite the order of the Great Commission (make disciples, then baptise), household baptisms interpreted as involving believers, and baptism as a public symbol/ordinance of conversion, burial and resurrection with Christ (Romans 6). Paedobaptist critics argue for covenant continuity and cite household baptisms and the absence of an explicit age requirement. Because many credobaptist churches do not recognise infant baptism as valid, converts from paedobaptist traditions are typically baptised again — historically the origin of the name 'Anabaptist' ('rebaptiser').
