Omertà

OriginSouthern Italy (Sicily, Campania, Calabria)
TypeCode of silence / code of honour
Associated withMafia organisations

Omertà (Italian pronunciation: [omerˈta]) is a code of silence and code of honour that places importance on silence in the face of questioning by authorities or outsiders; non-cooperation with authorities, the government, or outsiders; and willfully ignoring and generally avoiding interference with the illegal activities of others. It originated and remains common in Southern Italy, where the Sicilian Mafia, the Camorra and the 'Ndrangheta hold strong influence.

The code

The basic principle of omertà is that it is not "manly" to seek the aid of legally constituted authorities to settle personal grievances. A person who breaks the code by informing — an infame or, in Mafia terminology, a pentito when he turns state's evidence — risks violent retribution. The code extends beyond criminals: witnesses to crimes traditionally refused to cooperate with police, a practice that long hampered prosecutions in Sicily.

The word is thought to derive from the Sicilian omu ("man") or from the Italian umiltà ("humility"). From the late twentieth century, high-profile turncoats such as Tommaso Buscetta, whose testimony enabled the Maxi Trial of 1986–87, began to break the code, and anti-Mafia movements in Sicily have campaigned to replace the culture of silence with civic cooperation.
