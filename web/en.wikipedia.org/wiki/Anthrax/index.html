Anthrax

Anthrax is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Bacillus anthracis. It occurs naturally in soil worldwide and commonly affects domestic and wild animals; humans become infected through contact with infected animals or animal products, or through inhalation of bacterial spores. The name derives from the Greek anthrax ("coal"), after the black skin lesions characteristic of the cutaneous form.

Cause: Bacillus anthracis
Types: Cutaneous, inhalational, gastrointestinal, injection
Prevention: Vaccination, antibiotics
Treatment: Antibiotics, antitoxin

Forms

Cutaneous anthrax, the most common form, begins as a small painless sore that develops into an ulcer with a black center (eschar). Inhalational anthrax, historically called woolsorter's disease, begins with flu-like symptoms and can progress rapidly to severe respiratory distress and shock; untreated, it is usually fatal. Gastrointestinal anthrax follows consumption of contaminated meat.

History

Anthrax was one of the first diseases shown to be caused by a microorganism: Robert Koch demonstrated B. anthracis as the causative agent in 1876, and Louis Pasteur developed an early livestock vaccine in 1881. The bacterium's durable spores made it a focus of biological weapons programs; in 2001, letters containing anthrax spores mailed in the United States killed five people and infected 17 others.
