Strategic bombing is a military strategy used in a total war with the goal of defeating the enemy by destroying its morale or its economic ability to produce and transport materiel to the theatre of military operations, or both. It involves a systematic, organised attack from the air on targets viewed as vital to the enemy's war-making capacity.

Origins

The concept emerged in the First World War with German airship and Gotha bomber raids on London, and was theorised in the 1920s by Italian general Giulio Douhet, who argued that command of the air would decide future wars. During the Second World War it reached its fullest expression in the Anglo-American Combined Bomber Offensive against Germany and the USAAF's campaign against Japan, culminating in the firebombing of Tokyo and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Effectiveness debate

Historians dispute the payoff: post-war surveys concluded that area bombing had limited effect on German industrial output until the oil and transport campaigns of 1944, and that it stiffened rather than broke civilian morale. Precision-guided munitions have since allowed bombing to target military infrastructure more discriminately, though legal and ethical debates continue under international humanitarian law.

Later examples

Strategic bombing was employed in the Korean War, the Vietnam War (notably Operation Rolling Thunder and Linebacker II), the Gulf War, and the 2015–2019 campaign against the Islamic State.
