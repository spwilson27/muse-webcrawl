Zoomorphic architecture is the practice of designing buildings whose form represents or is inspired by animals. The term covers both literal constructions shaped like animals — a tradition within novelty or mimetic architecture — and subtler works that abstract animal anatomy, movement, or behaviour into built form.

History and examples

Animal imagery has adorned buildings since antiquity, from Egyptian sphinxes to Gothic gargoyles, but buildings wholly shaped as animals became prominent in twentieth-century roadside architecture in the United States, where structures such as Lucy the Elephant (1881, Margate City, New Jersey) and the Big Duck (1931, Long Island) advertised businesses by their silhouette. In India, the Matsya Kanya statue and various temple forms draw on zoomorphic symbolism, while Santiago Calatrava's Milwaukee Art Museum (2001) evokes a bird in flight with its movable brise-soleil "wings".

Zoomorphism and biomimicry

Contemporary zoomorphic design overlaps with biomimetic architecture, which borrows functional principles from nature — termite-mound ventilation, bone-like structural lattices — rather than surface appearance. Critics distinguish symbolic zoomorphism from genuinely functional biomimicry, though many buildings combine both.
