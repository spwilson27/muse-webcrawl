Ehlers–Danlos syndromes

SpecialtyMedical genetics, rheumatology
SymptomsJoint hypermobility, stretchy and fragile skin, abnormal scarring, easy bruising
CauseGenetic defects affecting collagen and connective tissue
Classification13 subtypes (2017 international classification)

The Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (EDS) are a group of inherited connective tissue disorders caused by defects in the structure, production, or processing of collagen, or of proteins that interact with it. They are named after the dermatologists Edvard Ehlers of Denmark and Henri-Alexandre Danlos of France.

Signs and symptoms

Common features include overly flexible joints that can dislocate easily, skin that is stretchy, soft, and fragile, slow or abnormal wound healing with atrophic scars, and easy bruising. Severity ranges widely: the vascular type can cause rupture of blood vessels and internal organs and is life-threatening, while the hypermobile type is associated with chronic pain and joint instability.

Classification

The 2017 international classification recognises 13 subtypes, including classical, classical-like, cardiac-valvular, vascular, hypermobile, arthrochalasia, dermatosparaxis, kyphoscoliotic, and others. Most types have an identified causative gene; hypermobile EDS (hEDS), the most common type, is diagnosed clinically and its genetic basis remains under investigation.

Management

There is no cure. Management focuses on physiotherapy to stabilise joints, pain management, cardiovascular monitoring for at-risk types, and preventive care to protect fragile skin and vessels.
