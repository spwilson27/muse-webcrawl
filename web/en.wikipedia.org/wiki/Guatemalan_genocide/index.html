Guatemalan genocide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Guatemalan genocide, also known as the Maya genocide, refers to the mass killing of Maya civilians during the Guatemalan Civil War (1960–1996), particularly the scorched-earth campaigns of 1981–1983 under the governments of Romeo Lucas García and Efraín Ríos Montt. The UN-sponsored Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH) concluded in 1999 that state forces had committed acts of genocide against Maya peoples in several regions.

Background

The civil war began after the 1954 CIA-backed coup against President Jacobo Árbenz, whose land reforms had threatened the United Fruit Company. Successive military governments fought leftist guerrilla movements; the army, treating indigenous highland communities as the guerrillas' support base, adopted a doctrine of draining the "sea" in which the "fish" swam. The internal conflict killed an estimated 200,000 people, the great majority Maya civilians, and displaced over a million.

The massacres

The CEH documented 626 massacres by state forces. Under Ríos Montt's "Victory 82" plan and the strategy summarised as frijoles y fusiles ("beans and bullets"), the army destroyed hundreds of villages: at Dos Erres in December 1982, Kaibil special forces murdered more than 200 villagers; in the Ixil Triangle, whole communities were annihilated. The CEH attributed 93% of human rights violations to state forces and found that between 70% and 90% of some Maya-Ixil communities were eliminated. Victims included children and the elderly; sexual violence and forced civil patrols were systematic.

Accountability

The 1996 peace accords ended the war. In 2013 a Guatemalan court convicted Ríos Montt of genocide and crimes against humanity — the first time a former head of state was convicted of genocide in a national court — but the constitutional court annulled the verdict on procedural grounds days later; his retrial was still unresolved when he died in 2018. Other proceedings, including the Dos Erres cases, produced convictions, and exhumations by forensic anthropologists continue.
