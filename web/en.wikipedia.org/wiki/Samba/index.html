Samba

Samba is a broad term for many related rhythms and dances of Afro-Brazilian origin, and one of Brazil's most recognisable national symbols. Emerging in early 20th-century Rio de Janeiro from the Afro-Brazilian communities descended from enslaved people, samba music is characterised by a 2/4 time signature, syncopated rhythms, call-and-response singing and a percussion ensemble (bateria) built around the surdo bass drum, tamborim, agogô and cuíca. Samba is the music of Rio Carnival, where samba schools parade in the Sambadrome.

History

The word "samba" probably derives from the Angolan semba. Samba crystallised in the homes of Afro-Brazilian matriarchs such as Tia Ciata in Rio's Praça Onze district. "Pelo Telefone", recorded in 1916 and released for the 1917 carnival, is usually called the first samba recording. In 1928 the group Deixa Falar pioneered the modern percussion format and the concept of the "samba school". Under Getúlio Vargas in the 1930s samba was promoted as national music, and radio spread singers such as Carmen Miranda and composers like Ary Barroso ("Aquarela do Brasil"). Later styles include the intimate samba-canção, the bossa nova fusion of the late 1950s, and the pagode revival of the 1980s.

Samba schools and carnival

Samba schools are large neighbourhood associations that spend the year preparing for the carnival parade: composing a theme song (samba-enredo), building floats and costumes, and rehearsing thousands of performers judged on percussion, harmony, choreography and theme. The top schools compete in the Sambadrome Marquês de Sapucaí, designed by Oscar Niemeyer and opened in 1984.

Dance

Samba dance includes the solo carnival samba no pé, the partner dance samba de gafieira, and the international ballroom samba, which differs substantially from Brazilian forms. The rolling hip action and rapid footwork are defining features.
