Red deer

Red deer
Scientific name: Cervus elaphus
Family: Cervidae
Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)
Distribution: Europe, North Africa, western and central Asia

The red deer (Cervus elaphus) is one of the largest deer species. It inhabits most of Europe, the Caucasus, Asia Minor, Iran, and parts of western and central Asia, with a small population in the Atlas Mountains of North Africa. It has been widely introduced elsewhere, including New Zealand, Argentina, and Australia.

Description

The red deer is a large ungulate: stags stand about 1.2–1.4 m at the shoulder and can weigh 100–240 kg. The coat is reddish-brown in summer and grey-brown in winter, with a pale rump patch. Only the males carry antlers, which grow each spring, are shed in late winter, and develop increasing numbers of points (tines) with age; a fine head may bear twelve or more points — a "royal". Females (hinds) are smaller and antlerless.

Behaviour and ecology

Red deer are ruminant grazers and browsers living in woodland and open moorland. The sexes live apart for most of the year; during the autumn rut, stags roar to advertise themselves, gather harems of hinds, and fight rivals with their antlers. Calves are born in late spring, spotted and hidden in vegetation for their first weeks. In Scotland's deer forests the red deer is the iconic quarry of stalking, while its browsing pressure — with natural predators such as wolves and lynx long extinct in Britain — shapes debates over woodland regeneration.

Taxonomy

The North American elk or wapiti was long treated as a subspecies (C. elaphus canadensis) but is now usually classified as the separate species Cervus canadensis on genetic evidence; central Asian forms are similarly debated.
