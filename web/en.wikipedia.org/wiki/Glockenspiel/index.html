Glockenspiel

The glockenspiel (German for "play of bells"; in French: carillon, also called orchestral bells) is a percussion instrument composed of a set of tuned metallic bars arranged in a keyboard layout and played with mallets. Its bright, bell-like tone typically sounds two octaves above the written pitch. The glockenspiel is a member of the metallophone family and is a standard part of the modern orchestra and concert band.

Description

The bars are made of steel (historically brass or aluminium) and are graduated in size, mounted in a case in two rows corresponding to the white and black keys of a piano. The standard orchestral range is roughly 2½ to 3 octaves, from G5 to C8 sounding pitch. Players strike the bars with hard mallets — plastic, rubber, wood or brass — choosing the mallet to control brightness and attack.

History and repertoire

The glockenspiel derives from sets of tuned bells used in the Low Countries; a keyboard version (distinct from the tower carillon) appeared in the 18th century. Handel used a related "carillon" in Saul (1739), and Mozart wrote for the glockenspiel in The Magic Flute (1791), where it represents Papageno's magic bells. It became a colour instrument in 19th- and 20th-century orchestral music — notably in Tchaikovsky's ballets, Wagner's operas, Saint-Saëns' Danse macabre, Mahler's symphonies and Holst's The Planets — and is ubiquitous in marching bands in a portable lyre-shaped form (the bell lyra). A keyboard-operated variant, the celesta, was patented in 1886.
