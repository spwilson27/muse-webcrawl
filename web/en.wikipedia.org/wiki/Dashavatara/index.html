The Dashavatara (Sanskrit: "ten avatars") are the ten principal avatars of the Hindu god Vishnu, the preserver within the Trimurti. In Vaishnava theology Vishnu descends to earth in these forms whenever dharma declines and evil threatens the cosmic order. The list is given, with minor variations, in texts such as the Mahabharata, the Garuda Purana and the Bhagavata Purana.

The ten avatars

The most widely accepted list is: Matsya, the fish, who saved the sage Manu from a great flood; Kurma, the tortoise, who supported Mount Mandara during the churning of the ocean of milk; Varaha, the boar, who rescued the earth goddess from the demon Hiranyaksha; Narasimha, the man-lion, who slew the demon king Hiranyakashipu; Vamana, the dwarf, who humbled King Bali in three strides; Parashurama, the axe-wielding Brahmin warrior; Rama, the hero of the Ramayana; Krishna, the central figure of the Mahabharata and the Bhagavad Gita (in some lists replaced by his brother Balarama); Buddha, included in many later lists; and Kalki, the prophesied future avatar who will end the present age, the Kali Yuga.

Interpretation

Some commentators have drawn parallels between the sequence — aquatic life, amphibian, land animal, half-human, human forms — and the theory of evolution, an analogy popularised in the nineteenth century but regarded by scholars as coincidental. The Dashavatara are a favourite subject of temple sculpture and painting, and Jayadeva's twelfth-century Gita Govinda opens with a celebrated hymn enumerating them.
