Differentiation (economics)

FieldEconomics, marketing

Differentiation in economics and marketing is the process of distinguishing a product or service from others to make it more attractive to a particular target market. This involves differentiating it from competitors' products as well as from a firm's own product range. The concept was proposed by Edward Chamberlin in his 1933 Theory of Monopolistic Competition and is a cornerstone of the theory of imperfect competition.

Types

Economists distinguish vertical differentiation, in which products differ in quality and consumers agree on the ranking (at equal prices everyone prefers the higher-quality product), from horizontal differentiation, in which products differ in characteristics — style, location, flavour — and consumers disagree about which is best. Harold Hotelling's linear-city model (1929) is the classic analysis of horizontal differentiation through location. Real markets usually involve both dimensions.

Effects

Successful differentiation softens price competition: because products are imperfect substitutes, each firm gains some market power and can charge a markup. It can raise consumer welfare by better matching products to tastes, but it can also impose costs — excessive variety, persuasive advertising expenditure, and higher prices. In Michael Porter's generic strategies framework, differentiation (achieving a product perceived as unique industry-wide) is one of the principal routes to competitive advantage, alongside cost leadership and focus.
