Autochrome Lumière

MakerLumière brothers
Introduced1907
Discontinued1931 (plates); 1938 (film)
TypeColour photographic plate/film

The Autochrome Lumière was an early colour photography process patented in 1903 by the Lumière brothers in France and first marketed in 1907. It was the principal colour photography process in use before the advent of subtractive colour film in the mid-1930s.

Structure and process

An Autochrome plate is a glass plate coated with a mosaic of microscopic grains of potato starch dyed orange-red, green and blue-violet — about four million grains per square inch — acting as colour filters. Lampblack fills the spaces between grains, and the whole is pressed flat and coated with a panchromatic black-and-white emulsion. The plate is exposed through the glass, so light passes through the starch filters before striking the emulsion. Reversal processing yields a positive transparency in which the grains reconstruct the colours of the scene.

Autochromes have a soft, pointillist, impressionistic look and rich colour. Their drawbacks were long exposure times (many times those of monochrome plates), the need for bright light, and dimness when projected, because the filters absorb most of the light.

Use and legacy

Marketed worldwide, Autochromes were used by professionals and wealthy amateurs; Albert Kahn's Archives of the Planet project sent photographers around the world from 1909 to 1931 to make Autochrome records of human cultures. Plate production in Lyon ended in 1931; sheet-film (Filmcolor) versions survived until 1938, displaced by Kodachrome and Agfacolor.
