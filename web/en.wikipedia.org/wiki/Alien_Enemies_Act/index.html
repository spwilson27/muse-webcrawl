Alien Enemies Act

The Alien Enemies Act (formally the Alien Enemy Act of 1798) is a United States federal law enacted in 1798 as part of the Alien and Sedition Acts. It authorises the President, whenever there is a declared war or an invasion or predatory incursion is perpetrated or threatened, to apprehend, restrain, secure and remove citizens or subjects of the hostile nation or government who are within the United States. Codified at 50 U.S.C. §§ 21–24, it is the only one of the 1798 acts that remains in force.

Enactment

Passed by the Federalist-controlled Congress during the Quasi-War with France and signed by President John Adams on 6 July 1798, the Act was aimed at enemy aliens in wartime. Unlike the companion Alien Friends Act, which expired in 1800, the Alien Enemies Act had no sunset clause and was re-enacted in amended form in 1918.

Invocations

The Act was invoked during the War of 1812 against British subjects, and extensively in both world wars. Presidential proclamations in 1917 and 1941 subjected German, Italian and Japanese nationals to registration, restrictions and, in some cases, internment; the related — but legally distinct — mass removal of Japanese Americans in 1942 was carried out under Executive Order 9066. In Ludecke v. Watkins (1948), the Supreme Court upheld deportation under the Act with minimal judicial review. In March 2025 the Act was invoked in peacetime against alleged members of the Venezuelan gang Tren de Aragua, prompting extensive litigation over whether an "invasion" within the statute had occurred.

Provisions

Upon declaration of war or proclamation of invasion or predatory incursion, male and female natives, citizens, denizens or subjects of the hostile nation aged fourteen and upward who are not naturalised may be "apprehended, restrained, secured, and removed as alien enemies". The Act grants broad presidential discretion and has historically been construed to limit judicial interference.

Criticism

Civil-liberties groups have long criticised the Act as an archaic wartime statute permitting detention and removal without individualised findings of disloyalty, and bills to repeal it have been introduced in Congress repeatedly, though none has passed.
