Canadian art

Canadian art refers to the visual arts (including painting, photography, and printmaking) originating from the geographical area of contemporary Canada. Art in Canada is marked by thousands of years of Indigenous artwork, followed by the colonial period's European-influenced art and, in the 20th century, the development of distinct modern and contemporary movements concerned with the Canadian landscape and national identity.

Indigenous art

Indigenous peoples have produced art in what is now Canada for millennia, including Inuit carving in stone, bone, and ivory; Northwest Coast formline art and totem poles; Woodlands painting; beadwork, quillwork, and birchbark art. In the 20th century, artists such as Kenojuak Ashevak in Inuit printmaking and Norval Morrisseau, founder of the Woodland School, brought Indigenous art to national prominence; contemporary Indigenous artists remain central to Canadian art.

Colonial period and 19th century

Under French and British rule, painting served religious, topographical, and portrait purposes. Paul Kane and Cornelius Krieghoff documented Indigenous life and habitant scenes in the mid-19th century. Landscape painting developed with artists such as Homer Watson, while the Royal Canadian Academy of Arts was founded in 1880.

Group of Seven and modernism

The Group of Seven (1920–1933) — Franklin Carmichael, Lawren Harris, A. Y. Jackson, Frank Johnston, Arthur Lismer, J. E. H. MacDonald, and Frederick Varley, together with associates such as Tom Thomson and Emily Carr — defined a national style of bold landscape painting of the Canadian Shield and wilderness. Later modernists included the Montreal automatistes led by Paul-Émile Borduas, whose 1948 manifesto Refus global challenged Quebec's conservative establishment, and the Toronto abstract group Painters Eleven.

Contemporary art

Postwar and contemporary Canadian art spans conceptual art (the N.E. Thing Co., General Idea, Jeff Wall's Vancouver School photography), pop art, and installation. Major institutions include the National Gallery of Canada in Ottawa, the Art Gallery of Ontario, and the Musée d'art contemporain de Montréal. Canada presents a national pavilion at the Venice Biennale.
