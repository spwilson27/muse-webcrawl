Passamaquoddy

Passamaquoddy (Peskotomuhkati)
Regions: Maine (USA), New Brunswick (Canada)
Language: Maliseet-Passamaquoddy (Eastern Algonquian)
Status: Federally recognized tribe in the United States

The Passamaquoddy (Passamaquoddy: Peskotomuhkati, "those who pursue the pollock") are an Indigenous people of North America whose traditional homeland lies around Passamaquoddy Bay, along what is now the border between Maine in the United States and New Brunswick in Canada. Together with the Penobscot, Maliseet, Mi'kmaq, and Abenaki peoples, they are part of the Wabanaki Confederacy.

History

At the time of European contact, the Passamaquoddy were a hunting, fishing, and gathering people whose economy centered on the rich marine resources of the bay — including the pollock from which their name derives — as well as moose, and seasonal camps along rivers and the coast. They were involved in the fur trade with French and later English colonists, and their history was shaped by conflicts including King William's War and the American Revolutionary War, in which many sided with the Americans. The 1794 Jay Treaty recognized their right of free movement across the international border.

Modern era

In 1980, the Passamaquoddy were parties to the Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act, which resolved their land claims and provided funds for land purchases and economic development. There are two federally recognized Passamaquoddy communities in Maine: the Passamaquoddy Tribe at Pleasant Point (Sipayik) and the Passamaquoddy Tribe at Indian Township (Motahkomikuk). A third community at St. Andrews, New Brunswick, is not recognized by Canada. The Maliseet-Passamaquoddy language, shared with the Maliseet, is endangered but the subject of revitalization efforts.
