Art of ancient Iran

The art of ancient Iran (Persia) encompasses the artistic production of the Iranian plateau from prehistory to the Islamic conquest of the 7th century AD. Successive civilisations — Elamite, Median, Achaemenid, Parthian and Sasanian — produced distinctive architecture, relief sculpture, metalwork and luxury arts that influenced the wider ancient world.


Apadana reliefs at Persepolis

Elam and the early plateau

Elam, in the southwest, produced sophisticated bronze and glazed-brick work; the ziggurat of Chogha Zanbil (c. 1250 BC) remains one of the best-preserved outside Mesopotamia. Later, the so-called Luristan bronzes (c. 1000–650 BC) — cast standards, pins and horse gear with animal motifs — became famous in Western collections.

Achaemenid art (550–330 BC)

The Achaemenid Empire fused motifs from across its vast territories into a court art of great refinement: the palaces of Persepolis with their processional reliefs of tribute bearers, glazed-brick friezes at Susa, fine metal vessels (rhyta) and the Oxus Treasure's goldwork. Its columned halls and royal iconography projected universal kingship.

Parthian and Sasanian art

Parthian art (247 BC – AD 224) blended Hellenistic and Iranian traditions, developing the iwan and frontal portraiture. Sasanian art (AD 224–651) is famed for rock reliefs celebrating royal victories, stucco decoration, silk textiles and above all silver-gilt vessels showing royal hunts, which were traded as far as China and influenced Byzantine and early Islamic art.
