Formalism (art)

Formalism in art is the study and evaluation of works of art by attention to their form — the elements of composition such as line, colour, shape, texture, and spatial organisation — rather than their subject matter, narrative content, historical context, or the artist's intentions. In formalist criticism, what a picture depicts matters less than how its visual elements are arranged; the meaning and value of the work are taken to reside in that arrangement itself.

Development

Roots of formalism lie in the aesthetics of Immanuel Kant, who emphasised disinterested pleasure in form. In the late 19th century, theorists such as Konrad Fiedler argued for the autonomy of visual art. The idea was developed by the art historian Heinrich Wölfflin, whose Principles of Art History (1915) analysed style through paired formal categories (linear/painterly, plane/recession, closed/open form), and by Alois Riegl's concept of Kunstwollen.

Bell and Fry

The British critics Clive Bell and Roger Fry brought formalism to prominence in the English-speaking world. In Art (1914) Bell proposed that all works of visual art share one quality, "significant form" — combinations of lines and colours that provoke a distinct aesthetic emotion — and that representational content is aesthetically irrelevant. Fry championed Post-Impressionism in Britain and similarly defended art's formal order against mere illustration.

Greenberg and modernism

The American critic Clement Greenberg made formalism the dominant framework for mid-20th-century modernism. In essays such as "Modernist Painting" (1960) he argued that each art should purify itself of effects borrowed from other arts, so painting should embrace its essential condition — flatness — an account that supported abstract expressionism and later colour field painting and minimalism. Greenberg's formalism was enormously influential in art schools, museums, and the market.

Criticism

Formalist criticism has been attacked for ignoring historical, social, and political meaning; Marxist and feminist art historians, and later postmodern critics, argued that form itself is ideologically loaded. Greenberg's account was challenged by Michael Fried's critics, by conceptual art's demotion of the visual object, and by the "social history of art" associated with T. J. Clark. Nevertheless, close formal analysis remains a basic tool of art criticism and teaching.
