Aciclovir (also spelled acyclovir) is an antiviral medication used primarily for the treatment of infections caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella zoster virus (VZV), including genital herpes, cold sores, shingles, and chickenpox. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Aciclovir decreases the severity and duration of herpes outbreaks and is used both to treat acute episodes and, in suppressive regimens, to reduce recurrences. Intravenous aciclovir is the treatment of choice for herpes simplex encephalitis and for serious infections in newborns and immunocompromised patients. It does not eradicate latent virus from nerve ganglia and therefore does not cure herpes infections.

Mechanism of action

Aciclovir is a nucleoside analogue of guanosine. It is selectively activated in infected cells: viral thymidine kinase phosphorylates it to aciclovir monophosphate, after which cellular enzymes produce the triphosphate. Aciclovir triphosphate competitively inhibits the viral DNA polymerase and, once incorporated into the growing viral DNA chain, terminates elongation because it lacks a 3′ hydroxyl group. Because activation depends on a viral enzyme, the drug is far more potent in infected than in uninfected cells, accounting for its favourable safety profile.

History

Aciclovir was discovered in the mid-1970s by researchers at Burroughs Wellcome, including Howard Schaeffer and Gertrude Elion, and was introduced clinically in the early 1980s. Its development is regarded as a landmark in rational drug design; Elion shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in part for work that included this drug. The prodrug valaciclovir, which has much better oral bioavailability, was developed later.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, and headache. With intravenous use and inadequate hydration, aciclovir can crystallise in the kidneys and cause reversible kidney injury; high doses may also cause neurotoxicity. Resistance, usually through mutations in viral thymidine kinase, is uncommon in immunocompetent patients but more frequent in the immunocompromised.
