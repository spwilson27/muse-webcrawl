A cultured pearl is a pearl created under controlled conditions by human intervention in a pearl farm. A technician implants a small nucleus — typically a spherical bead of freshwater mussel shell, together with a piece of mantle tissue — into a living oyster or mussel. The mollusc coats the irritant with layers of nacre, the same process that produces a natural pearl, and after months or years the finished pearl is harvested. Today virtually all pearls sold in jewellery are cultured.

History

Chinese growers attached small objects to freshwater mussels to produce blister pearls as early as the 13th century, but the modern industry began in Japan. In 1893 Kokichi Mikimoto produced his first semispherical cultured pearls on the island now called Mikimoto Pearl Island, and in the early 1900s Tokichi Nishikawa and Tatsuhei Mise independently developed the method for perfectly round pearls; the key patent was granted in 1916. Japanese Akoya pearls dominated the world market for much of the 20th century, transforming pearls from rare luxuries into widely available gems.

Types

  • Akoya — from the saltwater oyster Pinctada fucata, mainly in Japan and China; typically 6–8 mm, white or cream with high lustre.
  • South Sea — from the large Pinctada maxima oyster in Australia, Indonesia and the Philippines; 10–15 mm, white, silver or golden.
  • Tahitian — from the black-lip oyster Pinctada margaritifera in French Polynesia; naturally dark grey, green and "peacock" colours.
  • Freshwater — grown in mussels in lakes and rivers, overwhelmingly in China; usually tissue-nucleated, produced in large quantities and many shapes and colours.

Cultivation process

Oysters are raised for two to three years before nucleation. Skilled technicians make a small incision and insert the bead and mantle graft; the animals are then returned to the water in nets or lines, cleaned regularly to remove fouling organisms, and protected from storms, red tides and predators. Saltwater pearls grow for roughly 6 months to 2 years; South Sea pearls may take up to 4 years. At harvest the pearls are cleaned, sorted and graded by size, shape, lustre, surface quality and colour.

Value and trade

Because cultured pearls are far more abundant than natural pearls, they are graded rather than unique, and prices range from a few dollars for freshwater strands to very large sums for matched South Sea necklaces. Trade rules in many countries require cultured pearls to be sold explicitly as "cultured"; X-ray examination can distinguish bead-nucleated pearls from natural ones.
