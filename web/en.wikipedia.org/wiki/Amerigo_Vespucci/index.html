Amerigo Vespucci

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Waldseemüller map of 1507, the first to name America]

Amerigo Vespucci (9 March 1454 – 22 February 1512) was an Italian explorer, financier and navigator from Florence, in whose honour the Americas are named. Sailing in Spanish (1499–1500) and Portuguese (1501–1502) service along the coast of South America, Vespucci recognised — and publicised in letters printed across Europe — that these lands were not Asia but a 'New World' (Mundus Novus), a fourth continent unknown to Ptolemy and the ancients. The German cartographer Martin Waldseemüller, reading the letters, inscribed 'America' (the feminine Latin form of Amerigo) on his 1507 world map for the new continent's southern part; the name stuck and spread to the whole hemisphere, though Columbus had reached the mainland first and died insisting it was Asia.

Voyages and letters

The documentary record is genuinely tangled: four voyages are attributed (1497–98, 1499–1500, 1501–02, 1503–04), of which two are solidly documented (the Spanish voyage with Alonso de Ojeda, reaching the Amazon mouth and Venezuela — 'little Venice' — and the Portuguese expedition with Gonçalo Coelho that charted Brazil's coast to Patagonia). His two famous letters — Mundus Novus (1503) and the letter to Piero Soderini (1504/05, of debated authenticity in its printed form) — were publishing sensations, running through dozens of editions while Columbus' own reports languished, and they carried his name and his claim into every European print-shop.

The naming

Waldseemüller and the Saint-Dié circle, in their Cosmographiae Introductio (1507), proposed naming the fourth part of the world 'America, after its discoverer Americus, a man of penetrating intellect' — later editions quietly dropped the suggestion, but the great map's thousand copies had done their work. Vespucci himself, appointed Spain's first piloto mayor (chief pilot) in 1508, spent his last years at Seville examining returning pilots and standardising charts — the working navigator behind the legend. Whether he sought the honour or merely benefited from others' promotion remains debated; the injustice to Columbus was already protested by contemporaries (Las Casas) and has never quite faded.
