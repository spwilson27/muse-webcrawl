Liberal conservatism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liberal conservatism is a political ideology combining conservative policies with liberal stances, especially on economic issues and social matters. Liberal conservatives favour private property, free markets and limited intervention, together with the Rule of law, gradual change and representative institutions; on social questions they range from moderately traditionalist to permissive.

The tendency is strongest in Europe, where many centre-right parties — Christian democratic and conservative parties in Germany, Scandinavia and the Low Countries among them — are liberal conservative in character: economically liberal, supportive of the welfare state in moderated form, and committed to European integration. The tradition descends in part from the nineteenth-century liberal-conservative governing parties that displaced both absolutism and radicalism, and from figures such as Alexis de Tocqueville and Edmund Burke read together.

Contrast with neighbours

Liberal conservatism differs from national conservatism in its openness to trade, migration and supranational cooperation; from classical liberalism in its greater respect for tradition, institutions and gradualism; and from social liberalism in preferring lower taxes, lighter regulation and a smaller state. In Britain the strand runs through parts of the Conservative Party; in the United States the nearest equivalents are the moderate or “country-club” wings of the Republican Party.
