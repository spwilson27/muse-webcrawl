Government shutdowns in the United States

In the United States, a government shutdown occurs when funding legislation required to finance the federal government is not enacted before the start of the fiscal year or the expiration of a continuing resolution. During a shutdown, affected agencies furlough non-essential employees and curtail activities, while essential services such as national security, public safety, and benefit payments continue.

Legal basis

Shutdowns arise from the Antideficiency Act, which prohibits federal agencies from spending or obligating funds in advance of appropriations, as interpreted by opinions issued in 1980-1981 by Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti. Before those opinions, funding gaps did not generally halt government operations.

Notable shutdowns

  • 1995-1996: Two shutdowns totalling 26 days during the Clinton administration, centred on a budget dispute with Congress.
  • 2013: A 16-day shutdown over the Affordable Care Act and the federal budget.
  • 2018-2019: A 35-day partial shutdown — the longest in U.S. history — over funding for a border wall, affecting about 800,000 federal employees.

Effects

Shutdowns close national parks and museums, delay regulatory and permitting work, and leave hundreds of thousands of federal workers without pay until funding resumes (back pay is guaranteed by law since 2019). The Congressional Budget Office estimated the 2018-2019 shutdown cost the economy about $11 billion, including $3 billion in permanently lost output.
