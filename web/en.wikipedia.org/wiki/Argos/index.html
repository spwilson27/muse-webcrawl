Argos (Greek: Ἄργος) is a city in Argolis, Peloponnese, Greece, and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, with a history reaching back to the Neolithic period. It lies on the fertile Argolic plain, beneath the hill of Larissa, which carries the remains of its ancient acropolis and a medieval castle.

History

In Greek mythology, Argos was founded by Argus, son of Zeus and Niobe, and was a centre of the cult of Hera; the Argive Heraion was one of the most important sanctuaries of the goddess. In the Iliad, Argos is associated with Diomedes and the wider Mycenaean world of Agamemnon. During the Archaic period, Argos was a leading power in the Peloponnese, defeating Sparta at the Battle of Hysiae in 669 BC; it long remained Sparta's chief rival and remained neutral or pro-Persian during the Persian Wars. The city was famous for its bronze sculptors, including Polykleitos, and for its Argive shield and phalanx tactics.

In the Hellenistic era, Argos joined the Achaean League. Under Roman rule it prospered as a provincial town; it declined in the Byzantine and Ottoman periods but revived after Greek independence.

Mythology

Argos is central to the myth of Io, priestess of Hera, who was transformed into a cow and guarded by the many-eyed giant Argus Panoptes, whose eyes, after his slaying by Hermes, were set in the peacock's tail. The mythical kings of Argos include Inachus, Danaus (whose fifty daughters were the Danaids), Perseus, and the house of Atreus.
