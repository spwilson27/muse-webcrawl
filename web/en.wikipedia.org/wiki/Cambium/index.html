Cambium

The vascular cambium lies between the xylem (wood) and the phloem.

A cambium (plural cambia or cambiums), in plants, is a tissue layer that provides partially undifferentiated cells for plant growth. Cambia are lateral meristems: their activity increases the girth of stems and roots, a process called secondary growth. Two types are especially important: the vascular cambium and the cork cambium (phellogen).

Vascular cambium

The vascular cambium is a cylinder of meristematic tissue between the primary xylem and primary phloem. Its cells divide to produce secondary xylem (wood) toward the inside and secondary phloem toward the outside. Fusiform initials give rise to the axial system of tracheary elements and fibres, while ray initials produce the radial parenchyma rays that transport materials across the stem. Seasonal variation in this activity produces annual growth rings in temperate trees.

Cork cambium

The cork cambium arises further out in the stem or root and produces cork cells (phellem) outward and phelloderm inward; together these form the periderm, which replaces the epidermis as the protective outer tissue of woody plants — in other words, bark.

Other cambia

Some monocots and certain dicots with anomalous secondary growth possess additional or unusual cambia. In grafting, successful union depends on aligning the cambium layers of stock and scion. The word derives from medieval Latin cambium, "exchange".
