Monoamine hypothesis

The monoamine hypothesis is a biological theory about the cause of mood disorders, most notably depression, which proposes that a deficiency of certain neurotransmitters — the monoamines serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine — in the central nervous system underlies depressive illness. It emerged in the 1960s from clinical observations about how drugs affected mood and formed the basis for the development of most modern antidepressant medications.

History

In the 1950s, the antihypertensive drug reserpine, which depletes monoamine stores, was observed to induce depression-like symptoms in some patients, while the tuberculosis drug iproniazid, a monoamine oxidase inhibitor, elevated mood. Joseph Schildkraut's 1965 catecholamine hypothesis of affective disorders and Arvid Carlsson's related work formalised the idea that depression reflected deficient monoaminergic transmission, and that antidepressants worked by restoring it — tricyclics and MAO inhibitors first, then selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine.

Evidence and criticism

Evidence cited in favour includes the mood-lowering effects of acute tryptophan depletion in some recovered patients and genetic findings concerning the serotonin transporter. However, the hypothesis has been widely criticised as an oversimplification: antidepressants raise synaptic monoamine levels within hours yet take weeks to work; some effective antidepressants do not act primarily on monoamines; and direct measures of serotonin in depression have produced inconsistent results. A 2022 umbrella review concluded there was no consistent evidence of an association between serotonin concentration or activity and depression, prompting debate about how the hypothesis had been communicated to the public.

Modern views

Contemporary research treats monoamine systems as one component of broader models involving neuroplasticity, stress hormones, inflammation, and neural circuitry. Newer treatments such as esketamine act on glutamate systems, reflecting a shift beyond the original hypothesis while monoamine-based drugs remain widely used.
