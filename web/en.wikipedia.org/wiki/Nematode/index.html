Nematode

Nematodes

Phylum: Nematoda

Described species: ~28,000; estimated total far higher

Habitat: Virtually all environments — soil, marine, freshwater, hosts

Model organism: Caenorhabditis elegans

The nematodes (phylum Nematoda), or roundworms, are a diverse animal phylum inhabiting a broad range of environments. They are among the most abundant animals on Earth — famously, Nathan Cobb wrote that removing all matter except nematodes would leave a ghostly outline of the world recognizable by its worms.

Biology

Nematodes are bilaterally symmetric, unsegmented worms with a tough cuticle molted as they grow, a complete digestive tract, and no circulatory or respiratory system. Most are microscopic; the largest, Placentonema gigantissima in sperm-whale placentas, reaches over 8 metres. They feed on bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals, or live as parasites.

Importance

Free-living soil nematodes drive nutrient cycling. Parasitic species cause major crop losses (root-knot and cyst nematodes) and human diseases including ascariasis, hookworm infection, filariasis, and trichinellosis. Caenorhabditis elegans was the first multicellular organism to have its genome fully sequenced (1998) and remains a central model in developmental biology and neuroscience.
