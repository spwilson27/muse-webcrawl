Homi Jehangir Bhabha (30 October 1909 – 24 January 1966) was an Indian nuclear physicist who is widely credited as the "father of the Indian nuclear programme". He was the founding director and professor of physics at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR), and the founding director of the Atomic Energy Establishment at Trombay (later renamed Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, BARC, in his honour).

Early life and education

Bhabha was born into a wealthy Parsi family in Bombay. He studied at the Royal Institute of Science in Bombay before going to Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, initially to study mechanical engineering to join the family business interests of the Tata group. Under the influence of Paul Dirac and others, he turned to theoretical physics, earning his PhD in 1934. He worked with Niels Bohr in Copenhagen and with Enrico Fermi, and published important work on cosmic rays and electron–positron scattering — the process now known as Bhabha scattering. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1941.

Building Indian science

Returning to India, Bhabha became a reader at the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore under C. V. Raman. In 1945, with support from J. R. D. Tata, he founded the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research. After Indian independence, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru made Bhabha the central figure of India's atomic energy programme: he became secretary of the Department of Atomic Energy (founded 1954) and first chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission. He promoted India's three-stage nuclear power programme, designed to exploit the country's large thorium reserves.

Bhabha also played a key international role: he was president of the first United Nations Conference on the Peaceful Uses of Atomic Energy in Geneva in 1955 and advocated of nuclear disarmament through control of fissile material.

Death and legacy

Bhabha died on 24 January 1966 when Air India Flight 101 crashed near Mont Blanc in the French Alps. His death, weeks before the programme's first major decisions on weapons capability, has been the subject of speculation, though the crash was officially attributed to a misunderstanding between the pilot and air traffic control. The Atomic Energy Establishment, Trombay was renamed the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre shortly after his death.
