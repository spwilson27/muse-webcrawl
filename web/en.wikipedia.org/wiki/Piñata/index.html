Piñata

A piñata is a container, often made of papier-mâché, pottery, or cloth, decorated with colourful paper and filled with candy, toys or fruit. It is suspended from a height at parties and celebrations, and blindfolded participants take turns striking it with a stick until it breaks, releasing the treats inside. Piñatas are most strongly associated with Mexico, where they are a fixture of birthdays and of the Christmas posadas.

A traditional star-shaped piñata with seven points.

History

The tradition may have roots in China, where seed-filled figures were broken at New Year; Marco Polo is said to have brought the custom to Europe, where it became associated with Lent. Spanish missionaries brought it to Mexico in the sixteenth century, possibly adapting an existing Aztec practice of breaking clay pots filled with offerings. The traditional Mexican piñata is made over a clay pot, shaped as a star with seven cones said to represent the seven deadly sins, with the treats inside symbolising the rewards of faith.

Modern use

Today piñatas are made in countless shapes — animals (the donkey is a classic), cartoon characters, stars and numbers — and are common at children's parties in Mexico, the United States and many other countries. Pull-string piñatas, which open without being struck, are often used for younger children.
