Electronic literature


A hypertext work displayed on screen

Electronic literature, or e-lit, is a genre of literature encompassing works created exclusively on and for digital devices, such as computers, tablets, and mobile phones. Such writing often combines text with images, sound, animation, and interactivity, and exploits the capabilities of the digital environment in ways that print cannot.

The Electronic Literature Organization (ELO), founded in 1999, defines electronic literature as work with "an important literary aspect that takes advantage of the capabilities and contexts provided by the stand-alone or networked computer." Scholars commonly distinguish e-lit from digitized print literature: an e-book of a novel is not usually considered electronic literature, while a hypertext fiction, a generative poem, or a story told through chat messages is.

History

Early experiments included Christopher Strachey's love-letter generator (1952) and the text adventure games of the 1970s. The genre expanded in the late 1980s with hypertext authoring systems such as Storyspace, used for Michael Joyce's afternoon, a story (1987), often cited as the first hypertext novel. The World Wide Web brought new forms in the 1990s and 2000s: web-based hypertext, Flash poetry, interactive fiction, and later works built for social media and mobile platforms.

Forms

Recognized forms include hypertext fiction, interactive fiction, digital poetry, generative literature, bots that post algorithmically composed texts, network fiction, locative narrative, and codeworks that foreground the code itself.

Preservation

Because e-lit depends on specific hardware and software, preservation is a central problem; the ELO's Electronic Literature Collections and archives such as the Electronic Literature Directory document works whose original platforms may no longer run.
