Breathalyzer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A handheld fuel-cell breathalyzer used in roadside screening (image placeholder)

Breathalyzer is a trademarked name — and, in North America, a generic term — for instruments that estimate blood alcohol content (BAC) from a breath sample. A person blows into the device, which measures the concentration of ethanol in deep-lung (alveolar) air and converts it to an estimated BAC using a partition ratio of about 2100:1.

History

The Breathalyzer was developed by Robert F. Borkenstein of the Indiana State Police, patented in 1958. It used a chemical oxidation reaction: alcohol in the breath reduced potassium dichromate in the presence of sulphuric acid and a silver nitrate catalyst, and the colour change was measured photometrically. Borkenstein's device displaced earlier instruments such as the Drunkometer and became the standard police tool for decades.

Technology

Modern evidentiary instruments use infrared spectroscopy or electrochemical fuel cells, which oxidise ethanol and measure the resulting current. Semiconductor sensors are used in cheap personal devices. Accuracy depends on calibration, an adequate observation period (to avoid residual mouth alcohol), and correct sampling of end-expiratory air — issues frequently litigated in drink-driving cases.

Legal use

Roadside screening devices provide preliminary readings; evidential tests at a police station, or blood/urine samples, are generally required for prosecution. Legal limits vary by jurisdiction — commonly 0.08 g/dL in the United States and England and Wales, 0.05 in Scotland and most of Europe, and 0.02 or zero in some countries.
