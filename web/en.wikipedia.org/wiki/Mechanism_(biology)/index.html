Mechanism (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the science of biology, a mechanism is a system of causally interacting parts and processes that produce one or more effects. Scientists explain phenomena by describing and identifying mechanisms — organized structures and processes whose components, activities, and organizational relations generate the phenomenon to be explained.

Characterizations

Philosophers of biology have offered several influential characterizations. Machamer, Darden, and Craver (2000) defined mechanisms as "entities and activities organized such that they are productive of regular changes." Peter Bechtel emphasized decomposing a system into parts and localizing functions within it, then recomposing to show how the whole works. In molecular and cell biology, "mechanism" typically refers to the detailed causal chain — for example, the mechanism of protein synthesis or of enzyme catalysis — by which an input is transformed into a biological output.

Analysis

Mechanistic explanation follows a strategy of decomposition and localization: a system is broken into component parts and operations, each assigned a function, and the organized interaction of the parts is shown to produce the phenomenon. This approach contrasts with purely phenomenological models that describe regularities without identifying the underlying causal structure. The discovery of the mechanism of heredity — DNA replication, transcription, and translation — is a canonical example.

Mechanism and teleology

Because biological mechanisms are described functionally, mechanistic explanation must accommodate the appearance of purpose in organisms without invoking vitalism. Contemporary accounts treat functions as contributions of a part to the organized activity of a mechanism within a containing system.
