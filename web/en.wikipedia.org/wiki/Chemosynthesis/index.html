In biochemistry, chemosynthesis is the biological conversion of one or more carbon-containing molecules (usually carbon dioxide or methane) and nutrients into organic matter using the oxidation of inorganic compounds (e.g., hydrogen gas, hydrogen sulfide) or ferrous ions as a source of energy, rather than sunlight, as in photosynthesis. Chemoautotrophs, organisms that obtain carbon this way, are mostly bacteria and archaea living in inhospitable environments, and are the primary producers in most energy chains in such settings.

History

The Russian microbiologist Sergei Winogradsky first described chemosynthesis in the 1880s–1890s from his studies of sulfur and nitrifying bacteria. The concept gained dramatic confirmation in 1977, when the submersible Alvin discovered lush communities of giant tube worms, clams and shrimp around hydrothermal vents on the Galápagos Rift, ecosystems entirely independent of sunlight and powered by bacterial oxidation of hydrogen sulfide.

Processes

Different organisms exploit different chemical energy sources. Sulfur-oxidising bacteria oxidise H₂S to elemental sulfur or sulfate; nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonia to nitrite and then nitrate, a key step of the nitrogen cycle in soils; hydrogen bacteria oxidise H₂; iron bacteria oxidise Fe²⁺; and methanotrophs consume methane. The energy released drives ATP synthesis and the reduction of CO₂ into sugars, commonly via the Calvin cycle or the reverse Krebs cycle.

Ecological significance

Chemosynthetic communities support oases of life in the deep ocean around hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, in dark caves such as Movile Cave in Romania, and within rocks and aquifers. Because chemosynthesis requires only chemical energy, it is a model for possible life in subsurface oceans on icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus.
