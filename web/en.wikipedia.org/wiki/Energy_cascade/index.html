Energy cascade

Energy transfer from large to small scales in turbulence

An energy cascade is the transfer of energy from large scales to smaller scales (or, in some systems, the reverse) in a dynamical system. The concept is most famous in fluid turbulence, where kinetic energy injected at large scales is transferred through an inertial range of eddy sizes to the smallest scales, where it is dissipated as heat by viscosity.

In turbulence

The picture of the turbulent energy cascade was articulated qualitatively by Lewis Fry Richardson in the 1920s — "big whirls have little whirls that feed on their velocity; and little whirls have lesser whirls and so on to viscosity" — and given quantitative form by Andrey Kolmogorov in his 1941 theory (K41). In Kolmogorov's theory, the energy spectrum in the inertial range follows the famous −5/3 power law, E(k) ∝ ε2/3 k−5/3, where ε is the mean rate of energy dissipation per unit mass and k is the wavenumber.

Other systems

Energy cascades appear in many other physical settings:

  • Plasma and magnetohydrodynamic turbulence, including the solar wind, where energy cascades to small scales and heats the plasma.
  • Wave turbulence in oceans, elastic plates and nonlinear optics.
  • Two-dimensional turbulence, where energy cascades inversely, from small to large scales (feeding large vortices such as Jupiter's Great Red Spot), while enstrophy cascades forward.
  • High-energy physics, where "cascade" describes the branching shower of secondary particles produced when a cosmic ray strikes the atmosphere (an air shower).

Energy cascading in energy systems

In energy engineering, "cascading" also denotes using energy successively at decreasing quality — for example using waste heat from electricity generation for district heating, then for greenhouses — in order to raise overall efficiency; this is closely related to cogeneration and exergy analysis.
