Origins: Gasprinsky

Jadidism was the modernist, reformist movement among Muslim intellectuals of the Russian Empire from the 1880s to the 1920s. Its founder, the Crimean Tatar educator and publisher Ismail Gasprinsky (1851–1914), opened the first "new method" (usul-i jadid) school at Bakhchisarai in 1884, teaching children to read by phonetics in weeks rather than years, alongside secular subjects. Through his newspaper Tercüman ("Interpreter," est. 1883) — long the only Turkic-language newspaper in Russia — he preached the unity "in language, thought, and work" of the empire's Turkic Muslims.

Spread and programme

New-method schools spread across the Volga-Ural region, the Caucasus, and Russian Turkestan, training a generation of reformers: Abdurauf Fitrat, Mahmud Khoja Behbudiy, Munawwar Qari, and Cholpon in Central Asia; Musa Bigiev and the Volga Tatars. Beyond pedagogy, the Jadids advocateded women's education, vernacular literature and theatre, religious reform, and national cultural autonomy, clashing bitterly with the conservative qadimist ("old method") ulama.

Revolution and suppression

After 1917 the Jadids briefly governed experiments such as the Kokand autonomy and staffed the cultural institutions of the new Soviet republics, hoping to build national cultures within a socialist frame. Stalin's terror destroyed them: virtually the entire Jadid intelligentsia was executed or imprisoned in the 1930s, and Jadidism was written out of official Soviet historiography as bourgeois nationalism.

Legacy

Since 1991, the Jadids have been rehabilitated across Central Asia as founding fathers of modern Uzbek, Tatar, and Azerbaijani national culture; Fitrat and Cholpon are now canonical authors in Uzbekistan, and the Jadid debate over Islam, modernity, and nationhood remains a live reference point in the region.
