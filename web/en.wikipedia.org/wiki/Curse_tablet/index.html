Curse tablet


A lead defixio from the ancient Greek world

A curse tablet (Latin: tabella defixionis, defixio; Greek: κατάδεσμος, katadesmos) is a small tablet with a curse written on it from the Greco-Roman world. The tablets were generally written on thin sheets of lead, scratched with a stylus, then rolled, folded, or pierced with nails and deposited in places considered close to the underworld gods — graves, wells, springs, and sanctuaries of chthonic deities such as Hecate, Persephone, and Hermes.

Content and purposes

The inscriptions "bind" (defixio, katadein) a named victim, invoking gods and spirits to restrain, harm, or deliver them to punishment. Surviving tablets curse thieves, legal opponents (especially before trials), athletic and theatrical rivals, and faithless lovers; erotic binding spells form a large category. Texts often include voces magicae, magical names and character-symbols, retrograde writing, and elaborate analogies ("just as this lead is cold and useless, so may X be cold and useless"). More than 1,600 Greek and Roman examples survive, from the fifth century BC in Sicily and Attica through to late antiquity.

Notable finds

Large deposits have been recovered from the sanctuary of Demeter at Cnidus, from wells in the Athenian Agora and Kerameikos cemetery, and from the sacred spring of Sulis Minerva at Bath, England, where over 100 tablets — many cursing the thieves of stolen clothing and goods — were thrown into the waters. The Bath tablets include some of the earliest written evidence of everyday Latin in Britain.
