Volcanology is the study of volcanoes, lava, magma and related geological, geophysical and geochemical phenomena. A volcanologist is a geologist who studies eruptive activity, the formation and structure of volcanoes, and the deposits they produce, with the practical goal of understanding eruptions well enough to forecast them and protect populations at risk.

What volcanologists do

Modern volcanology combines field mapping of past deposits with instrumental monitoring of active volcanoes. Warning signs of an impending eruption include swarms of small earthquakes as magma rises, swelling or deformation of the edifice measured by GPS and radar (InSAR), changes in gas emissions — especially sulfur dioxide and the ratio of gases — and changes in heat flow. Volcano observatories such as those of the US Geological Survey (including the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, founded 1912) maintain such networks and advise authorities on evacuations. Eruption size is classified on the logarithmic Volcanic Explosivity Index, from gentle Hawaiian flows (VEI 0–1) to supervolcanic eruptions (VEI 8).

Hazards

Volcanologists study each of the volcano's weapons: lava flows, ash fall and aviation-threatening ash clouds, pyroclastic flows, lahars (volcanic mudflows, which killed some 23,000 people at Armero, Colombia in 1985), volcanic gases and tsunami generated by flank collapse. The work is dangerous: volcanologists David Johnston (Mount St. Helens, 1980), Maurice and Katia Krafft and Harry Glicken (Unzen, 1991) were killed by the eruptions they were studying. The Kraffts' films and photographs of eruptions such as Pelée-type flows did much to educate the public and officials about volcanic danger.
