Green roof

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Green roof
Also calledLiving roof, eco-roof, vegetated roof
TypesExtensive, intensive, semi-intensive
Key benefitsStormwater retention, insulation, habitat

A green roof or living roof is a roof of a building that is partially or completely covered with vegetation and a growing medium, planted over a waterproofing membrane. It may also include additional layers such as a root barrier, drainage layer, and an irrigation system. Green roofs serve several purposes for a building, such as absorbing rainwater, providing insulation, creating a habitat for wildlife, increasing benevolence, and helping to lower urban air temperatures and mitigate the heat island effect.

Green roofs fall into two main categories: extensive green roofs, which are relatively thin (typically 6–20 cm of growing medium) and support low-growing, drought-tolerant plants such as sedums, and intensive green roofs, which have a deeper substrate (20 cm to over a metre) and can support shrubs, small trees, and even rooftop gardens and parks. Intensive roofs are heavier and require more maintenance and structural support.

History

Green roofs are not a new invention: the hanging gardens of Babylon are sometimes cited as an early example, and sod roofs have been used for centuries in Scandinavia and other cold climates, where a layer of turf over birch bark provided insulation. The modern green roof movement developed in Germany in the 1960s and 1970s, when lightweight extensive systems were commercialised. Germany remains a leader, with cities such as Stuttgart mandating green roofs on suitable new buildings. In the United States, notable examples include the green roof on Chicago City Hall, installed in 2001.

Environmental benefits

Green roofs retain rainwater in the substrate and vegetation, reducing and delaying stormwater runoff into drainage systems — extensive roofs can retain 50–90% of typical rainfall events. They insulate buildings, reducing heating and cooling energy demands, and they extend the life of the underlying waterproofing membrane by shielding it from ultraviolet radiation and temperature extremes. Vegetated roofs also provide habitat for insects and birds in urban areas, absorb carbon dioxide, filter pollutants from the air, and counteract the urban heat island effect by cooling through evapotranspiration.

Components

A typical green roof system consists, from the bottom up, of the roof structure and insulation, a waterproof membrane, a root barrier, a drainage and water-retention layer, a filter fabric, the growing medium (often a lightweight mineral-based substrate rather than ordinary soil), and the vegetation itself. Container gardens on roofs, where plants are maintained in pots, are not generally considered true green roofs, although they provide some similar benefits.

Costs and considerations

Green roofs cost more to install than conventional roofs, and intensive systems in particular require ongoing maintenance, irrigation, and structural reinforcement. However, studies suggest their longer membrane lifespan and energy savings can offset the initial expense over the life of the building. Many cities offer incentives such as tax abatements, floor-area bonuses, or stormwater fee reductions to encourage green roof construction.
