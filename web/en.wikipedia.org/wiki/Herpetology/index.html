Herpetology

Herpetology (from Greek ἑρπετόν herpetón, meaning "reptile" or "creeping animal") is the branch of zoology concerned with the study of amphibians (including frogs, toads, salamanders, newts and caecilians) and reptiles (including snakes, lizards, turtles, tortoises, crocodilians and tuataras). The animals studied are informally called "herps".

Scope

Herpetology covers the taxonomy, anatomy, physiology, ecology, distribution and conservation of amphibians and reptiles — two groups united historically rather than by close evolutionary kinship, since reptiles as traditionally defined exclude birds. Studies range from the roles of these animals in global ecosystems to the structure and medical potential of reptile venoms. Batrachology is the sub-discipline concerned with amphibians alone.

Importance

Amphibians are widely used as environmental indicators because of their permeable skin and biphasic life cycles; global amphibian declines, driven by habitat loss and chytrid fungi, are a major conservation concern. Reptile venoms have yielded important drugs, including the blood-pressure medicine captopril, modelled on a peptide from the venom of the Brazilian pit viper.

Practice

Herpetologists work in universities, museums, zoos, conservation organisations and environmental consultancies. Many people also keep reptiles and amphibians as pets and contribute observations through citizen-science projects. Professional bodies include the Society for the Study of Amphibians and Reptiles and the Herpetologists' League.
