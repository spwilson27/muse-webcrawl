Recurrent nova

Recurrent nova
Type: Cataclysmic variable star
Known systems: about 10 in the Milky Way
Recurrence timescale: roughly 10–100 years

A recurrent nova is a cataclysmic variable star system — a white dwarf accreting hydrogen-rich material from a companion star — that has been observed to erupt in a nova outburst more than once. Only about ten such systems are known in the Milky Way, though many classical novae are suspected to recur on timescales longer than the historical record.

Mechanism

As in a classical nova, gas transferred from the companion accumulates on the white dwarf's surface until pressure and temperature at the base of the layer trigger a thermonuclear runaway, ejecting the layer and brightening the system by many magnitudes. In recurrent novae the white dwarf is typically very massive — close to the Chandrasekhar limit — and the accretion rate is high, so a critical layer accumulates again within decades rather than the thousands of years typical of classical novae.

Known systems

  • T Pyxidis — eruptions in 1890, 1902, 1920, 1944, 1966, and 2011; the 2011 outburst came decades later than predicted.
  • RS Ophiuchi — eruptions recorded in 1898, 1933, 1958, 1967, 1985, 2006, and 2021; the companion is a red giant.
  • T Coronae Borealis — the "Blaze Star", erupted in 1866 and 1946.
  • U Scorpii — one of the fastest known novae, with eruptions roughly every decade.
  • V407 Cygni, V745 Scorpii, V3890 Sagittarii, V2487 Ophiuchi, IM Normae, and CI Aquilae complete the usual Galactic list.

Significance

Because recurrent-nova white dwarfs gain mass over time if they retain part of each accreted layer, they are candidate progenitors of Type Ia supernovae. Their short recurrence times also make them valuable laboratories for studying nova physics.
