Happiness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Happiness is a positive emotional state characterised by feelings of joy, contentment, satisfaction and well-being. It is studied across philosophy, psychology, economics and religion, and is variously understood as a fleeting emotion, a general mood, or an overall evaluation of one's life (life satisfaction). The term is also used in philosophical contexts to translate the Greek eudaimonia, meaning flourishing or living well.

Philosophy

Aristotle taught that eudaimonia, achieved through a life of virtue and reason, is the highest human good. Epicurus located happiness in modest pleasures and freedom from pain and fear, while the Stoics found it in virtue and acceptance of what cannot be changed. Utilitarian philosophers such as Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill made happiness the criterion of morality: actions are right in proportion as they increase the general happiness. Bhutan famously adopted "Gross National Happiness" as a guiding principle of government.

Psychology

Positive psychology, pioneered by Martin Seligman and others, studies what makes people flourish, including the role of positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning and accomplishment (the PERMA model). Research suggests happiness is influenced by genetics (a "set point"), life circumstances and intentional activities. People tend to adapt to both good and bad fortune—the hedonic treadmill—though major events such as unemployment can have lasting effects. The economics of happiness studies how income and policy relate to reported well-being.

Measurement

Happiness is typically measured by self-report surveys, such as the Cantril ladder used in the annual World Happiness Report, which ranks countries by life evaluations; Nordic countries such as Finland have repeatedly ranked highest. Critics note that happiness measures can be culturally biased and that average scores conceal distribution and meaning.
