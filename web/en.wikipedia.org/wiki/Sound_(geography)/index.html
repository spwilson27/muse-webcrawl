Sound (geography)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geography, a sound is a large arm of the sea or ocean, larger than a bay, deeper than a bight, and wider than a fjord; or a narrow sea or ocean channel between two bodies of land (see also strait or channel).

Terminology

The term sound derives from the Old English sund, meaning a strait or narrow sea, related to the verb "to sunder" or separate, and to the Germanic word for swimming — a sound was originally a channel narrow enough to be swum across. There is no consistent distinction between sounds and other coastal water bodies; usage varies by region and language, and many bodies called sounds would elsewhere be called bays, inlets, or straits.

Formation

Sounds are commonly formed when a river valley is flooded by the sea (a ria), or when glaciers carve a trough later inundated by rising sea levels. Along the east coast of the United States, the large estuarine sounds of North Carolina — Pamlico Sound and Albemarle Sound — lie behind barrier islands, while glacially formed examples include Puget Sound in Washington and Long Island Sound between Connecticut and Long Island.

Examples

Notable sounds include Puget Sound and Long Island Sound (United States), Pamlico Sound (the largest lagoon on the U.S. East Coast), Milford Sound in New Zealand (actually a fjord), Plymouth Sound and The Solent (England), Øresund between Denmark and Sweden, and numerous sounds in Scotland, Canada, Australia, and the Caribbean.
