Management by objectives

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Management by objectives (MBO), also known as management by planning (MBP), was first popularized by Peter Drucker in his 1954 book The Practice of Management. It is a process of defining specific objectives within an organization that management can convey to organisation members, then deciding how to achieve each objective in sequence.

Principles

In MBO, organisational goals are cascaded down: senior management sets overall objectives, which managers and employees translate into specific, measurable personal goals agreed with their supervisors. Performance is periodically reviewed against those objectives, and rewards are linked to achievement. Drucker argued that involving employees in goal-setting increased commitment and self-control.

Practice and criticism

MBO became widely used in corporations and public administration from the 1960s, and later frameworks such as SMART goals and Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) descend from it. Critics, notably W. Edwards Deming, argued that an excessive focus on numerical targets encourages short-termism and gaming, and that many outcomes are beyond an individual employee's control.
