Augsburg Confession

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Title page of the Augsburg Confession (1531 edition)

The Augsburg Confession, also known as the Augustan Confession or the Augustana from its Latin name, Confessio Augustana, is the primary confession of faith of the Lutheran Church and one of the most important documents of the Protestant Reformation. The Augsburg Confession was written in both German and Latin and was presented by a number of German rulers and free-cities at the Diet of Augsburg on 25 June 1530.

Background and composition

Emperor Charles V called the Diet of Augsburg in 1530 in the hope of resolving the religious division in the Holy Roman Empire and uniting it against the Ottoman threat. Elector John of Saxony commissioned Martin Luther, Philipp Melanchthon, and their colleagues to prepare a statement of evangelical teaching. Because Luther, an outlaw under the Edict of Worms, could not attend, Melanchthon took the leading role in drafting, drawing on the Schwabach Articles, the Marburg Articles, and the Torgau Articles. The resulting document was presented to the Emperor on 25 June 1530, read aloud in German before the Diet.

Contents

The Confession consists of 28 articles. The first 21 present the chief articles of faith, deliberately showing that the evangelical churches taught nothing contrary to Scripture or the catholic tradition—covering God, original sin, the Son of God, justification by faith (Article IV, regarded as the centre of the Confession), the ministry, the church, baptism, the Lord's Supper, confession, repentance, and good works. The remaining seven articles describe abuses that had been corrected, including communion under one kind, clerical marriage, the Mass, monastic vows, and the power of bishops.

Response and aftermath

Catholic theologians answered with the Confutatio Pontificia (Pontifical Confutation), to which Melanchthon replied with the Apology of the Augsburg Confession (1531), a substantial defence that itself became a Lutheran confessional document. Melanchthon later issued revised editions (the Variata of 1540), whose softer wording on the Lord's Supper caused lasting controversy. The Confession's legal standing was eventually recognised in the Peace of Augsburg (1555), and the unaltered Confession (Invariata) was included in the Book of Concord of 1580.

Significance

The Augsburg Confession remains the foundational doctrinal standard of Lutheran churches worldwide and a landmark of Reformation theology, notable for its irenic tone and its aim of demonstrating continuity with the historic church while asserting justification by grace through faith.
