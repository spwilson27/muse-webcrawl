Phillips curve

[Image: a chalkboard graph showing a downward-sloping curve of inflation against unemployment]

The Phillips curve is an economic model relating Inflation to Unemployment: A. W. Phillips (1958) showed that in Britain, 1861–1957, wage inflation was lower when unemployment was higher. Samuelson and Solow recast it as a price-inflation trade-off that seemed to offer policymakers a menu — accept more inflation for permanently lower unemployment — guiding 1960s Keynesian demand management.

Breakdown and expectations

The 1970s stagflation — high inflation and high unemployment — destroyed the simple trade-off, as Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps had predicted in 1967–68: once expectations adjust, unemployment returns to its 'natural rate', and the long-run Phillips curve is vertical. The expectations-augmented, short-run-only trade-off became orthodoxy and underlies modern models in which credible central banks anchor expectations. Debates persist: the curve appeared very flat in the low-inflation 2010s (the 'missing disinflation' of the Great Recession), then steepened in the 2021–23 inflation surge.
