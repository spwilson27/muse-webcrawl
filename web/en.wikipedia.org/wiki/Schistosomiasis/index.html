Schistosomiasis, also known as bilharzia or snail fever, is a disease caused by parasitic flatworms called schistosomes. The urinary tract or the intestines may be infected. It is one of the most important neglected tropical diseases, affecting well over 200 million people worldwide, mostly in Africa.

The schistosome life cycle involves freshwater snails and humans.

Life cycle and transmission

Eggs are shed in human urine or faeces into fresh water, where they hatch and infect freshwater snails. Inside the snail the parasite multiplies and is released as free-swimming larvae (cercariae), which penetrate the skin of people who wade, swim or wash in the water. Adult worms live in blood vessels around the bladder or intestines, sometimes for years.

Symptoms

Many infections are initially symptom-free apart from a rash or itchy skin ("swimmer's itch"). Weeks later, fever, chills, cough and muscle aches may occur (Katayama syndrome). Chronic infection is driven mainly by eggs trapped in tissues: intestinal disease can cause abdominal pain, diarrhoea and blood in the stool; urinary disease causes blood in the urine and, over time, bladder fibrosis and an increased risk of bladder cancer. Long-term infection in children can cause anaemia, stunted growth and impaired learning.

Treatment and prevention

The drug of choice is praziquantel, given in mass drug administration programmes in endemic areas. Prevention focuses on safe water supplies, sanitation, snail control and avoiding contact with infested water. The disease was first described by the German physician Theodor Bilharz in Egypt in 1851.
