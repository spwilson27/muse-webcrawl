Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy, in psychology, is the belief in one's own capacity to execute the behaviours necessary to produce specific performance attainments. The concept was introduced by Albert Bandura in a 1977 paper and developed in his social cognitive theory and his 1997 book Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control. Self-efficacy reflects confidence in the ability to exert control over one's own motivation, behaviour, and social environment, and is specific to tasks and domains rather than a general trait.

Sources

Bandura identified four sources of efficacy beliefs:

  • Mastery experiences — succeeding at a task is the most powerful source; failure undermines efficacy, especially early on.
  • Vicarious experience — seeing similar others succeed by sustained effort raises observers' beliefs in their own capabilities.
  • Verbal persuasion — encouragement from credible others can sustain effort, though its effects are weaker and easily undone by failure.
  • Physiological and affective states — stress, fatigue, and mood are read as information about one's competence; reducing anxiety raises efficacy judgements.

Effects and applications

People with high self-efficacy set more challenging goals, persist longer in the face of difficulty, attribute failure to effort rather than ability, and recover more quickly from setbacks; low self-efficacy leads to avoidance, anxiety, and early quitting. Self-efficacy predicts outcomes across education, health behaviour (smoking cessation, exercise, chronic-disease management), work performance, addiction recovery, and phobia treatment — Bandura's guided mastery therapy for phobias being an early demonstration. It differs from self-esteem, which concerns overall self-worth rather than perceived capability, and from locus of control, which concerns whether outcomes depend on one's actions at all. Collective efficacy extends the idea to groups' shared beliefs in their joint capabilities.
