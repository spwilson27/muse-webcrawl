Cognitive psychology

Cognitive psychology is the scientific study of mental processes such as attention, language use, memory, perception, problem solving, creativity and reasoning. It emerged as a distinct discipline in the 1950s and 1960s during the "cognitive revolution", a reaction against the behaviourist view that psychology should study only observable behaviour. Cognitive psychologists treat the mind as an information-processing system, drawing analogies with computation.

History

Although philosophers from Plato and Descartes onward debated the nature of thought, experimental study of cognition began with figures such as Hermann Ebbinghaus, who charted the forgetting curve of memory in the 1880s, and Frederic Bartlett, who showed that remembering is reconstructive. Behaviourism dominated American psychology in the first half of the 20th century, but several developments undermined it: Noam Chomsky's 1959 critique of Skinner's account of language, George Miller's work on the limits of short-term memory ("The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", 1956), and the rise of computer science and information theory. Ulric Neisser's 1967 textbook Cognitive Psychology gave the field its name and manifesto.

Main topics

Key areas include attention (how the mind selects information, studied with dichotic listening and visual search), memory (sensory, short-term/working memory and long-term memory systems), perception, language, decision-making (including the heuristics and biases documented by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky), and problem solving. Baddeley's model of working memory and the Atkinson–Shiffrin model are influential frameworks.

Methods and relations

Cognitive psychology uses laboratory experiments, reaction-time measures, eye tracking, computational modelling and neuroimaging. It overlaps with cognitive science, cognitive neuroscience, artificial intelligence and linguistics, and informs cognitive therapy in clinical psychology, a field associated with Aaron Beck. Critics note the limitations of laboratory tasks for capturing real-world ("ecological") cognition.
