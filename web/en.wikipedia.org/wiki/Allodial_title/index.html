Allodial title

[Image: A medieval land charter, representing allodial ownership]
A medieval charter granting land in allodium.

Allodial title constitutes ownership of real property (land, buildings, and fixtures) that is independent of any superior landlord. In common legal use, allodial title is real property that is owned "free and clear" of any superior landlord. In the feudal system, all land was held of a superior, and grants of land were made in return for services or payments. Land held allodially, by contrast, is held absolutely and is not subject to such obligations.

History

The concept derives from the allodium of early medieval Europe, particularly among the Germanic peoples, where land inherited from one's ancestors was held in full ownership, in contrast to land held as a benefice or fief from a lord. Under the feudal system that spread across Europe after the Carolingian period, allodial land was progressively converted into feudal tenure, since holders found it advantageous, or were compelled, to commend their land to a lord in exchange for protection.

Allodial holdings survived longest in parts of France, Germany, Italy, and Scandinavia. In France, the alleu persisted in some southern regions where Roman law influence was strong. The French Revolution abolished feudal dues and confirmed allodial ownership as the norm for property in land.

Modern usage

In modern legal systems, true allodial title is rare. In most common-law jurisdictions, including England and the United States, land is theoretically held of the Crown or the state, although the practical incidents of this tenure are minimal. In the United States, land is generally held in fee simple, which is the closest equivalent to allodial ownership, though it remains subject to governmental powers such as taxation, eminent domain, escheat, and police power regulation.

Some jurisdictions maintain special forms of allodial title. Land in the U.S. state of Nevada could, under a program established in 1997 and later suspended, be converted to allodial title upon payment of taxes in advance. Certain lands held by Native American tribes and under some state constitutions also possess allodial characteristics. In the British Isles, the island of Sark retained a feudal structure until reforms in 2008, while udal law in Orkney and Shetland preserves an allodial tradition derived from Norse law.
