Clathrate hydrate

Clathrate hydrates, or gas hydrates, are crystalline, ice-like solids in which water molecules form cages that trap small guest molecules such as methane, carbon dioxide or hydrogen sulfide. The guest is held by van der Waals forces rather than chemical bonds. Hydrates form at low temperatures and high pressures and are stable in deep ocean sediments and in permafrost.

Significance

Methane clathrate is by far the most important natural hydrate: the amount of carbon locked in oceanic and permafrost hydrates is estimated to exceed that of all other fossil fuels combined, making hydrates a potential future energy source — Japan and China have run test extractions — but also a climate concern, since destabilised hydrates could release large amounts of the greenhouse gas methane (the "clathrate gun" hypothesis).

In industry, hydrates were first noticed as a nuisance that plugged natural-gas pipelines, and engineers add inhibitors to prevent their formation; conversely, artificial hydrates are studied for gas storage, transport, carbon capture and desalination. On other worlds, clathrate ices are thought to be common in comets, icy moons and Mars.
