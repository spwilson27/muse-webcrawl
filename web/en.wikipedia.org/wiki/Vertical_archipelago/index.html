Vertical archipelago

The vertical archipelago is a model of Andean economic organisation proposed by anthropologist John Murra in 1972. In the steep Andes, sharply different ecological zones — coast, maize valleys, high potato fields and camelid pasture — are stacked within short horizontal distances. Murra argued that Andean ethnic groups secured access to the products of all these "floors" not by trade but by maintaining permanent colonies of their own people in each zone, like islands in an archipelago, linked by reciprocity and redistribution to a highland core.

Evidence

Murra developed the model from sixteenth-century Spanish administrative inspections (visitas), especially of the Lupaca kingdom on Lake Titicaca, which held colonies days away on the Pacific coast and in eastern valleys producing coca and maize. The Inca state later applied vertical control on an imperial scale, moving whole populations (mitma) between zones.

Legacy

The model reshaped Andean studies by showing that market exchange was not the only, or even the main, way pre-modern societies obtained diverse goods. Critics note that exchange and markets also existed in the Andes, and the model's applicability varies by period and region.
