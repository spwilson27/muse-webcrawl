Solar eclipse of August 18, 1868

A total solar eclipse occurred on August 18, 1868, sometimes known as the "King of Siam's eclipse". The path of totality crossed the Red Sea, Arabia, India, the Malay Peninsula and the Gulf of Siam, with a maximum duration of totality of 6 minutes 47 seconds — one of the longest of the 19th century. The eclipse is celebrated in the history of astronomy for the spectroscopic discoveries made during it, including the first detection of the element helium.

Observations

King Mongkut of Siam had calculated the eclipse's time and path more accurately than the French expedition's astronomers and observed it from a camp in Prachuap Khiri Khan province, where he contracted the malaria that killed him weeks later. Expeditions from Britain, France and Germany observed across India, at stations including Guntur and Masulipatam.

Discovery of helium

French astronomer Pierre Janssen, observing from Guntur with a spectroscope, recorded a bright yellow line in the spectrum of the solar prominences at wavelength 587.49 nm, close to but distinct from the sodium D lines. He also realised the prominences could be observed without an eclipse using a spectroscope. Norman Lockyer observed the same line from London that October; the unknown element was named helium, from the Greek helios (sun), and was only isolated on Earth in 1895. During the same eclipse, spectral observations advanced the study of the chromosphere and corona.

Significance

The 1868 eclipse is regarded as the birth of solar spectroscopy and astrophysics as an observational science: for the first time, the chemical composition of the Sun could be analysed in detail, and prominences were shown to be gaseous. Eclipse expeditions of this era also refined values related to the solar constant.
