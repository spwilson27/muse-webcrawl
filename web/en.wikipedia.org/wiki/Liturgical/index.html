Liturgy

Liturgy is the customary public ritual of worship performed by a religious community. As a religious phenomenon, liturgy represents a communal response to and participation in the sacred through activities reflecting praise, thanksgiving, remembrance, supplication, or repentance. It forms a basis for establishing a relationship with a divine agency, as well as with other participants in the liturgy.

Technically speaking, liturgy forms a subset of ritual and is always sacred. In general, the term liturgy is restricted to the public worship services of particular traditions, especially the Christian Eucharist and the Divine Office, but other religions — Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism, and others — have formal rites that may also be described as liturgical.

Celebration of the liturgy

Etymology

The word liturgy, derived from the technical term in ancient Greek (leitourgia), meaning "public service, public work," originally signified the often expensive offerings made in support of the gods and public institutions by wealthy Athenians. Through the leitourgia, the rich carried a financial burden and were correspondingly rewarded with honours and prestige. In the Septuagint and the New Testament the word was used for the public worship of the community.

Christianity

In Christian tradition, liturgy designates the participation of the people of God in the work of God, above all the celebration of the Eucharist and the sacraments. The major historic liturgical families include the Roman Rite of the Latin Church, the Byzantine Rite of Eastern Orthodoxy and Eastern Catholicism, the Alexandrine, Antiochene, Armenian, and East and West Syriac rites. The twentieth-century Liturgical Movement sought the fuller, conscious, and active participation of the faithful, leading to reforms such as those of the Second Vatican Council in the Roman Catholic Church.

Liturgical year

Most liturgical traditions order worship through an annual cycle of seasons and feasts — Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter, and Ordinary Time in the West; a corresponding cycle of feasts and fasts in the East — together with a lectionary of Scripture readings and a sanctoral cycle of saints' days.

Judaism

Jewish liturgy centres on the recitation of prayers and blessings at set times: the morning (Shacharit), afternoon (Mincha), and evening (Maariv) services, the Shema, the Amidah, readings from the Torah, and the elaborate liturgies of the Sabbath and festivals, preserved in the siddur and machzor.

Islam

Although Islam has no priestly sacramental liturgy in the Christian sense, the five daily prayers (salah), the Friday congregational prayer, and the recitation of the Qur'an follow fixed ritual forms that are often described as liturgical; the term liturgical is also applied to the calendar of Ramadan and the feasts of Eid.

Other traditions

Buddhist liturgy includes chanting, offerings, and devotional services before images of the Buddha, especially in Mahayana traditions. Hindu worship (puja) follows prescribed sequences of offerings and hymns. Scholars of religion use "liturgy" broadly for any fixed, communal, sacred ritual order, in contrast to private or spontaneous devotion.
