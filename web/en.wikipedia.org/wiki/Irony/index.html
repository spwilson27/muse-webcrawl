Irony

Irony
Socratic irony: professed ignorance as a method of questioning.
FieldRhetoric, literary device, philosophy
EtymologyGreek eirōneía, "dissembling, feigned ignorance"
TypesVerbal, dramatic, situational, Socratic, cosmic

Irony, in its broadest sense, is the expression of meaning through the use of language or events that normally signify the opposite, often for humorous or emphatic effect. It is one of the most widely discussed concepts in rhetoric and literary theory, with definitions varying from the narrowly verbal — saying the opposite of what is meant — to broad philosophical notions of the ironic structure of existence itself.

Types of irony

Verbal irony occurs when a speaker says one thing but means the opposite, typically with the expectation that the audience will recognise the discrepancy. Sarcasm is a harsh or bitter form of verbal irony.

Dramatic irony is a device of theatre and fiction in which the audience knows something the characters do not — as when Oedipus hunts for a murderer the audience already knows is himself. Tragic irony is its grimmest form.

Situational irony (or irony of fate) describes events that turn out contrary to what was expected or intended: a fire station burning down, or a traffic policeman receiving a parking ticket. Alanis Morissette's song "Ironic" is frequently cited as an example of the public confusion between genuine situational irony and mere unfortunate coincidence.

Socratic irony is the feigned ignorance by which Socrates, in Plato's dialogues, draws out his interlocutors' assumptions in order to expose their contradictions — the origin of the word itself, from Greek eirōneía.

Cosmic irony (or irony of fate) is the philosophical notion that an indifferent or hostile universe frustrates human purposes — a theme of Thomas Hardy's novels, whose subtitle "a novel of character and environment" hints at forces beyond the characters' control.

Romantic and philosophical irony

German Romantic theorists such as Friedrich Schlegel developed "Romantic irony" as an aesthetic stance in which the artist simultaneously creates and undermines an illusion, revealing the limits of art. Søren Kierkegaard treated irony as a stage of existential development in The Concept of Irony (1841), and later writers from Nietzsche to Richard Rorty have made irony a central category of modern thought — Rorty's "ironist" being one who doubts her own final vocabulary.
