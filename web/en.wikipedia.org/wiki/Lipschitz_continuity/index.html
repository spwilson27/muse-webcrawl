Lipschitz continuity

In mathematical analysis, Lipschitz continuity, named after the German mathematician Rudolf Lipschitz, is a strong form of uniform continuity for functions. Intuitively, a Lipschitz continuous function is limited in how fast it can change: there exists a real number, the Lipschitz constant, such that for every pair of points on the graph of the function, the absolute slope of the line connecting them is no greater than this constant.

Definition

A function f defined on a subset of the real numbers is called Lipschitz continuous if there exists a constant K ≥ 0 such that, for all x1 and x2 in the domain,

|f(x1) − f(x2)| ≤ K |x1 − x2|.

The smallest such K is called the Lipschitz constant of f. The definition extends directly to functions between arbitrary metric spaces.

Properties

Every Lipschitz continuous function is uniformly continuous, and hence continuous. A differentiable function with bounded derivative on an interval is Lipschitz there (by the mean value theorem). Conversely, by Rademacher's theorem, a Lipschitz function on an open subset of Rn is differentiable almost everywhere. Lipschitz continuity in the time and space variables is the standard hypothesis guaranteeing existence and uniqueness of solutions to ordinary differential equations (the Picard–Lindelöf theorem).

Examples

The function f(x) = √x on [0, ∞) is uniformly continuous but not Lipschitz, because its slope is unbounded near 0. The absolute value function is Lipschitz with constant 1. The function f(x) = x2 is Lipschitz on any bounded interval but not on the whole real line.
