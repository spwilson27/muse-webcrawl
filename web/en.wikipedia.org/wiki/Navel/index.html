Navel

The navel (clinically the umbilicus, colloquially the belly button or tummy button) is the scar left on the abdomen of placental mammals where the umbilical cord was attached before birth. In humans it is a prominent feature of the front of the abdomen, located roughly at the level of the third and fourth lumbar vertebrae.

Formation

During development the umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta, carrying oxygenated blood through the umbilical vein and returning deoxygenated blood through the umbilical arteries. After birth the cord is clamped and cut; the residual stump dries and falls away within one to two weeks, leaving the navel. Whether a navel is an "innie" or an "outie" depends on how the skin and scar tissue heal, not on how the cord was cut.

Clinical significance

The navel is a useful anatomical landmark and a common port of entry in laparoscopic surgery. Conditions involving it include umbilical hernias, in which abdominal contents protrude through the abdominal wall at the navel — common and usually harmless in infants — infections such as omphalitis in newborns, and discharge from remnants of embryonic structures. Navel lint, an accumulation of clothing fibres, collects readily in inward navels.

Culture

Attitudes toward displaying the navel have varied across eras and societies; it has featured in fashion debates, religious symbolism (as a centre of the world, the omphalos), and idioms such as "navel-gazing" for self-absorbed contemplation. The navel is also a site for body piercing.
