Icelandic sagas

The sagas of Icelanders (Icelandic: Íslendingasögur), also known as family sagas, are a body of prose narratives written in Old Norse, principally in Iceland in the 13th and 14th centuries, describing events that mostly took place in Iceland in the 9th, 10th and early 11th centuries, the so-called Saga Age. They form the best-known category of the wider saga literature, which also includes kings' sagas, legendary sagas, contemporary sagas, bishops' sagas and chivalric sagas.

Content and style

The family sagas recount the lives, feuds, travels and settlements of Icelandic farmers, chieftains and outlaws, with a characteristic laconic prose style, heavy use of dialogue, and an outwardly objective narrative voice. Famous examples include Njáls saga (Brennu-Njáls saga), the longest and generally considered the greatest; Egils saga, attributed to Snorri Sturluson; Laxdæla saga; Gísla saga; Grettis saga; and Eyrbyggja saga.

Historical value

The sagas were written two to three centuries after the events they describe, and scholars debate how much oral tradition, and how much literary invention, they contain. Whatever their factual accuracy, they are a central source for the history, law, religion and society of Viking Age and Commonwealth Iceland, and a major achievement of medieval European literature.

Manuscripts and legacy

The sagas survive in vellum manuscripts such as Möðruvallabók and in later paper copies made after many originals were lost; the return of the Árni Magnússon manuscript collection from Denmark to Iceland in the 20th century was a landmark of Icelandic cultural nationalism. The sagas continue to influence Icelandic culture and modern literature worldwide.
