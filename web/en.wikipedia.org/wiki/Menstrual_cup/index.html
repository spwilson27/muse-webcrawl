Menstrual cup

A menstrual cup is a menstrual hygiene device inserted into the vagina during menstruation. Its purpose is to collect menstrual fluid (blood from the uterine lining mixed with other fluids). Menstrual cups are usually made of flexible medical-grade silicone, latex, or a thermoplastic isomer, and are shaped like a bell with a stem or ring. The stem is used for insertion and removal, and the bell-shaped cup seals against the vaginal wall just below the cervix and collects menstrual fluid.

Use

Unlike tampons and pads, cups collect menstrual fluid rather than absorbing it. A cup can be worn for up to 12 hours depending on flow, after which it is removed, emptied, rinsed, and reinserted. Most cups are reusable for several years, making them a lower-waste, lower-cost option over time, though disposable single-use versions also exist.

History

Early versions of menstrual cups were patented in the 19th and early 20th centuries. American actress and inventor Leona Chalmers patented a rubber menstrual cup design in 1937 under the name "Tassette", but rubber shortages during World War II and consumer preference for disposable products limited commercial success. Interest revived from the 1980s onward with silicone designs such as the Keeper and, later, brands such as DivaCup, Mooncup, and Lunette.

Safety and health

A 2019 systematic review in The Lancet Public Health found menstrual cups to be a safe option, with similar or lower leakage than pads and tampons and no increased risk of infection in most studies. Like other internal menstrual products, there is a small risk of toxic shock syndrome, and users are advised to follow cleaning instructions.
