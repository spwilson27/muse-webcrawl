Strike price

Payoff of a call option at expiry relative to the strike price K.

In finance, the strike price (or exercise price) of an option is a fixed price at which the owner of the option can buy (in the case of a call option), or sell (in the case of a put option), the underlying security or commodity. The strike price is a key variable of the option contract, set when the contract is written, and it — together with the underlying's price, time to expiry, volatility, interest rates, and dividends — determines the option's premium.

Moneyness

The relationship between the strike price K and the current spot price S of the underlying is called moneyness:

  • In the money (ITM) — a call with S > K, or a put with S < K; the option has intrinsic value.
  • At the money (ATM) — S approximately equals K.
  • Out of the money (OTM) — a call with S < K, or a put with S > K; the option has only time value.

At expiration, a call is worth max(S − K, 0) per share and a put is worth max(K − S, 0); the intrinsic value is measured directly against the strike.

Strike selection and contract terms

Exchanges list options in standardized strike intervals (for example every $2.50 or $5 for equities), creating a "strike chain" around the current price. Liquid markets trade many strikes per expiry. The strike remains fixed over the option's life but may be adjusted for corporate actions such as splits and special dividends.

Related concepts

In employee stock options, the strike is the price at which employees may later buy shares, typically set at the market price on the grant date. For warrants, the strike (exercise price) likewise fixes the price of acquiring the underlying shares. In "strike price" usage for futures-style contracts and binary options, the term similarly denotes the reference level that settles the contract.
