Pseudo-Aristotle

Pseudo-Aristotle is a general cognomen for authors of philosophical or medical treatises who attributed their work to the Greek philosopher Aristotle, or whose work was later attributed to him by others. The immense authority of Aristotle in the medieval world made his name a magnet for spurious works in Greek, Arabic, Latin and other languages.

Greek works

The Aristotelian corpus itself contains treatises now regarded as spurious or as products of Aristotle's school, the Peripatos. These include the Problemata (a vast collection of "why" questions, probably compiled over centuries), De Mundo (On the Universe), Mechanica, Physiognomonica, De Plantis, and On Marvellous Things Heard. Some, such as the Magna Moralia and parts of the Economics, are still debated.

Arabic and medieval works

In the Islamic world, pseudepigrapha flourished: the Theology of Aristotle was in fact a paraphrase of parts of Plotinus's Enneads, and the Liber de Causis derived from Proclus — both were for centuries treated as genuine and shaped medieval metaphysics. Other famous examples include the Secretum Secretorum (Secret of Secrets), presented as Aristotle's advice to Alexander the Great and hugely popular in Arabic and Latin versions, and the alchemical and lapidary treatises that bore his name.

Significance

Pseudo-Aristotelian works were not mere curiosities: they transmitted Neoplatonic ideas into Islamic and Christian philosophy under Aristotle's authority, and the medieval "Aristotle" known to many readers was a composite figure. Modern scholarship distinguishes the genuine corpus from these accretions, but studies the pseudo-Aristotelian literature as an important channel in the history of science, medicine, occult thought and political advice literature.
