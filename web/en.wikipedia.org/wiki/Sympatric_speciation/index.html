Sympatric speciation

Sympatric speciation is the evolution of a new species from a surviving ancestral species while both continue to inhabit the same geographic region. In evolutionary biology and biogeography, sympatric and sympatry are terms referring to organisms whose ranges overlap or are identical, so that they occur together at least in some places. If these organisms are closely related, such a distribution may be the result of sympatric speciation.

Sympatric speciation is one of four main modes of speciation, alongside allopatric, peripatric, and parapatric speciation. Unlike allopatric speciation, in which a population is split by a geographic barrier, sympatric speciation requires the emergence of reproductive isolation within a population that is not divided by any spatial barrier to gene flow.

Evidence and examples

Sympatric speciation was long regarded as controversial because gene flow between diverging subpopulations tends to homogenize them. However, strong disruptive selection combined with assortative mating can overcome this. Well-studied candidate examples include:

  • The apple maggot fly Rhagoletis pomonella, which shifted from hawthorn to introduced apple trees in North America in the 19th century, with host-associated mating promoting divergence.
  • Cichlid fishes in small crater lakes in Africa and Nicaragua, where species flocks appear to have diverged within a single lake.
  • The palms Howea forsteriana and Howea belmoreana on Lord Howe Island, which diverged in flowering time associated with different soil types.

Mechanisms

Polyploidy

In plants, sympatric speciation is often driven by polyploidy, particularly allopolyploidy, in which a hybrid between two species produces offspring with doubled chromosomes that cannot backcross with either parent, instantly creating a reproductively isolated lineage. A large fraction of flowering plant species are thought to have arisen this way.

Disruptive selection and niche divergence

When individuals exploiting distinct resources or habitats mate preferentially with similar individuals, selection can split a population. Host shifts in phytophagous insects are a classic setting.

Sexual selection

Divergence in mating signals and preferences — such as the color patterns and female preferences of African cichlids — can generate isolation without geographic separation.
