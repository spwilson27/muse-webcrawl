Kōan

A kōan (Japanese: 公案; Chinese: gōng'àn; Korean: 공안, gongan) is a story, dialogue, question, or statement that is used in Zen practice to provoke the "great doubt" (大疑) and to test a student's progress in Zen. Kōans are often paradoxical or inaccessible to conventional reasoning, and are given by a Zen master to a student as an object of meditation. The student works with the kōan, presenting responses to the teacher in private interviews (dokusan / sanzen), until an intuitive breakthrough is achieved.

Etymology

The Japanese term kōan is the Sino-Japanese reading of the Chinese gōng'àn (公案), meaning a "public case" — originally a legal term for the case records of a magistrate, a precedent that settles principles of justice. In Chan (Zen) usage it came to denote an authoritative account of an encounter between a master and a student, used as a model or test for later practitioners.

History

Kōan practice developed in China during the Song dynasty within the Linji (Japanese: Rinzai) school of Chan Buddhism. Teachers such as Dahui Zonggao (1089–1163) systematised the use of the huatou — the "critical phrase" of a kōan, such as the single word Mu — as the focus of meditation. In Japan, the practice was further organised by masters such as Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769), who revived the Rinzai school and arranged kōans into a curriculum of successive stages. The Sōtō school, following Dōgen, traditionally placed more emphasis on shikantaza ("just sitting"), though it also uses kōans.

Famous kōans

Among the best-known kōans:

A monk asked Jōshū, "Has a dog the Buddha-nature or not?" Jōshū answered, "Mu" (無, "no" / "not").
Two hands clap and there is a sound. What is the sound of one hand? — attributed to Hakuin.
What was your original face, before your parents were born?
A monk asked Dongshan, "What is Buddha?" Dongshan said, "Three pounds of flax."
When Yunmen was asked "What is Buddha?", he replied "Dried excrement" (kanshiketsu).

Kōan practice

In the Rinzai tradition, a student is assigned a first kōan — often Jōshū's Mu — and instructed to concentrate on it continuously, in sitting meditation and daily activity alike. The student periodically meets the teacher in private to present their understanding; responses are accepted or rejected, sometimes with a comment, a shout, or a blow of the stick. The process is intended to exhaust the student's conceptual resources until the kōan is "seen through" in a moment of awakening (kenshō or satori). Curricula may involve hundreds of kōans, followed by studies of poetry and the "five ranks" of Dongshan.

Collections

The classic collections of kōans with commentary include:

  • The Blue Cliff Record (碧巖錄, Biyan Lu), 100 cases, compiled in the 12th century.
  • The Gateless Barrier (無門關, Wumenguan / Mumonkan), 48 cases compiled by Wumen Huikai in 1228.
  • The Book of Equanimity (從容錄, Congrong Lu), 100 cases.
