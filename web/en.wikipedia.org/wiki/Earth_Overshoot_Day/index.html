Earth Overshoot Day is the calculated illustrative calendar date on which humanity's resource consumption for the year exceeds Earth's capacity to regenerate those resources that year. It is computed by the Global Footprint Network by dividing the planet's biocapacity by humanity's ecological footprint and multiplying by the number of days in the year. When the date falls before 31 December, humanity is said to be in "overshoot", using more ecological resources and services than nature can renew, and accumulating an ecological debt.

History

The concept is rooted in the ecological footprint accounting developed in the 1990s by Mathis Wackernagel and William Rees. According to the Global Footprint Network's historical reconstructions, global overshoot began in the early 1970s, when the day fell in late December. It has crept steadily earlier: by the late 1990s it fell in October, by around 2010 in late August, and in recent years it has fallen in late July or early August (for example, 28 July in 2022 and around 2 August in 2023), implying that humanity uses the equivalent of about 1.7 Earths per year. In 2020 the date moved later by several weeks, to 22 August, because of reduced economic activity during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Country overshoot days

The campaign also publishes "country overshoot days" — the date on which Earth Overshoot Day would fall if all humanity lived like the population of a given country. High-consumption countries such as Qatar, Luxembourg, the United States and Australia have overshoot days in February or March, while lower-consumption countries fall late in the year or do not overshoot at all.

Criticism

The ecological footprint methodology has been criticised by some economists and scientists for oversimplifying resource use, particularly for the large weight it gives to the hypothetical forest land needed to absorb carbon emissions. Supporters respond that it is a communication tool that usefully expresses the imbalance between demand on nature and its regenerative capacity.
