Thermal noise

Thermal noise (also called Johnson–Nyquist noise) is electronic noise generated by the thermal agitation of charge carriers (usually electrons) inside an electrical conductor at equilibrium, regardless of any applied voltage. It is present in all resistive elements and sets a fundamental limit on the sensitivity of amplifiers, radio receivers, and measurement instruments.

The phenomenon was first measured by John B. Johnson of Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1928 and explained theoretically by Harry Nyquist, whose formula gives the mean-square noise voltage as proportional to resistance, absolute temperature, and bandwidth: V² = 4kBTRΔf, where kB is Boltzmann's constant.

Characteristics

Thermal noise is approximately white, meaning its power spectral density is nearly constant across the frequency spectrum, though at very high frequencies quantum effects cause a roll-off. The available noise power of a resistor is kBTΔf watts, independent of the resistance value. At room temperature this corresponds to about −174 dBm per hertz of bandwidth, a figure widely used in receiver design.

Noise figure and applications

Engineers quantify the added noise of amplifiers and receivers using the noise figure. Cooling components, as in radio astronomy receivers and superconducting detectors, reduces thermal noise. Thermal noise is also exploited as a source of true randomness in random number generators and in Johnson noise thermometry, which measures temperature from the noise voltage of a resistor.
