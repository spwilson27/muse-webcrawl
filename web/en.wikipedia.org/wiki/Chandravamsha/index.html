Chandravamsha

Chandra, the moon god from whom the dynasty claims descent

Chandravamsha (Sanskrit: चन्द्रवंश, IAST: Candravaṃśa), also called the Lunar dynasty, is one of the principal lineages of Hindu mythology and of the Kshatriya (warrior-ruler) varna, alongside the Suryavamsha (solar dynasty). According to Hindu texts such as the Mahabharata, the Harivamsa, and the Puranas, the dynasty descends from Chandra, the moon god, through his son Budha (the planet Mercury) and Budha's son Pururavas.

Genealogy

The traditional lineage begins with the creator god Brahma, whose son Atri fathered Chandra (Soma), the moon. Chandra's son Budha married Ila, the daughter of the solar-dynasty founder Vaivasvata Manu, and their son Pururavas became the first king of the lunar line, ruling from Pratishthana. His descendants include Ayus, Nahusha, and Yayati.

King Yayati's five sons — Yadu, Turvasu, Druhyu, Anu, and Puru — founded the five main branches of the dynasty. From Yadu descended the Yadavas, the clan of Krishna; from Puru descended the Pauravas, including Bharata, after whom India is traditionally named Bhārata, and the Kuru line of the Pandavas and Kauravas, the central figures of the Mahabharata.

Dynasties claiming descent

Numerous historical ruling houses claimed Chandravamsha descent to legitimate their rule, including:

  • The Chandelas of Bundelkhand
  • The Yadava (Seuna) dynasty of Devagiri
  • The Somavamshi dynasty of Odisha (whose name itself means "lunar lineage")
  • The Lunar dynasty of Tripura
  • Various Rajput clans, including the Bhattis, Jadejas, and Tomaras
  • The royal house of Manipur, according to its chronicles

In literature

The history of the lunar dynasty forms the narrative framework of much of Sanskrit epic and Puranic literature. The ninth book of the Bhagavata Purana gives an extended genealogy from Chandra through Krishna and the Kurus. Stories of individual kings — Pururavas and Urvashi, Nahusha's fall, Yayati's exchange of old age with his son Puru, and Shakuntala and Bharata — became enduring subjects of classical Sanskrit drama and poetry.
