A hallucinogen is a psychoactive substance that alters perception, mood, and cognitive processes, producing experiences — including hallucinations, intensified sensory perception, and altered sense of time — that differ markedly from ordinary consciousness. The term encompasses three broad classes: psychedelics, dissociatives, and deliriants.

Classes and mechanisms

Classic psychedelics such as LSD, psilocybin (from "magic mushrooms"), mescaline (from peyote), and DMT act primarily as agonists at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. Dissociatives such as ketamine and phencyclidine (PCP) work mainly by antagonising NMDA receptors, producing detachment from the body and environment. Deliriants, such as scopolamine and compounds from nightshade plants, block acetylcholine receptors and produce true delirium with vivid hallucinations that users often cannot distinguish from reality.

History

Plant and fungal hallucinogens have been used for millennia in shamanic and religious rituals — including peyote in Indigenous American ceremonies and ayahuasca in Amazonian traditions. The modern era began with the isolation of mescaline in the 1890s and Albert Hofmann's synthesis of LSD in 1938 and discovery of its effects in 1943. Psychedelics became culturally prominent in the 1960s, were largely prohibited under laws such as the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances, and have seen renewed clinical interest since the 2000s for treating depression, PTSD, and addiction.

Effects and risks

Effects vary widely by substance, dose, and setting. Classic psychedelics are not considered physiologically addictive and have low toxicity, though they can provoke anxiety, panic, and, rarely, persistent perceptual changes (HPPD) or trigger psychosis in vulnerable individuals. Deliriants and dissociatives carry substantially higher physical risks.
