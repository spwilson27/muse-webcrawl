Brain mapping
FieldNeuroscience, neuroimaging
TechniquesfMRI, EEG, MEG, PET, diffusion imaging, cortical stimulation
PurposesLocalising function; connectomics; pre-surgical planning
Key projectsHuman Connectome Project; BRAIN Initiative

Brain mapping is a set of neuroscience techniques predicated on the mapping of biological quantities or properties onto spatial representations of the brain, resulting in maps. It studies the anatomy and function of the brain and spinal cord through imaging, electrophysiology, stimulation and lesion analysis, aiming to relate specific structures to specific functions — from the motor cortex to language areas and large-scale networks.

History

The localisation of brain function was established in the 19th century by the clinical cases of Broca and Wernicke and by Fritsch, Hitzig and Ferrier's electrical stimulation experiments; Korbinian Brodmann's cytoarchitectonic map of 1909 still names cortical areas used today. Penfield's stimulation maps of the motor and sensory "homunculi" followed in the 20th century, before CT, PET and MRI made non-invasive mapping routine.

Techniques

Functional MRI measures blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals during tasks or at rest; EEG and MEG record electrical and magnetic activity with millisecond resolution; diffusion MRI traces white-matter pathways (tractography) for structural connectomics; PET images metabolism and neurotransmitter systems. In neurosurgery, direct cortical stimulation mapping identifies eloquent cortex to preserve during tumour or epilepsy operations.

Atlases and connectomes

Standardised coordinate spaces (Talairach; MNI) and atlases allow results to be compared across studies. Large collaborative efforts — the Human Connectome Project, the Allen Brain Atlas and the BRAIN Initiative — aim at comprehensive structural and functional maps of human and model-organism brains. Brain mapping is distinct from, and unrelated to, the "brane" concept of string cosmology.
