Common cuckoo

The common cuckoo (Cuculus canorus) is a member of the cuckoo order of birds, Cuculiformes, which includes the roadrunners, the anis and the coucals. This species is a widespread summer migrant to Europe and Asia, and winters in Africa. It is a brood parasite, which means it lays eggs in the nests of other bird species, particularly dunnocks, meadow pipits, and reed warblers.

Description

The common cuckoo is 32–34 centimetres (13–13.5 in) long from bill to tail, with a tail of 13–15 centimetres and a wingspan of 55–60 centimetres. The plumage is slate grey above and barred below, resembling a sparrowhawk in flight — a resemblance thought to frighten host birds away from their nests. The male gives the familiar two-note "cu-coo" call, while the female has a rich bubbling chuckle.

Brood parasitism

Female cuckoos lay their eggs in the nests of other species, each female specialising in a particular host species and laying eggs that closely mimic those of the host. A female may lay up to 25 eggs in a season, one per nest. The cuckoo egg hatches earlier than the host's, and the blind, naked chick instinctively ejects the host's eggs or chicks from the nest, monopolising the foster parents' care.

Migration

The species breeds across Europe and Asia as far east as Japan, and migrates to sub-Saharan Africa for the winter. Adult birds leave their breeding grounds as early as July, while juveniles migrate later, navigating to Africa without ever having made the journey before.

In culture

The cuckoo's arrival has long been taken as a sign of spring in Europe, and its call gives its name to the cuckoo clock. The phrase "cuckoo in the nest" derives from its parasitic behaviour. Numbers have declined in parts of western Europe in recent decades, possibly owing to declines in host species and changes along migration routes.
