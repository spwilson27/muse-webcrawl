The Tonawanda Band of Seneca Indians (Seneca: Ta:nöwöde') is a federally recognized tribe of Seneca people in western New York State. Its members are citizens of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) and reside primarily on the Tonawanda Reservation, which spans parts of Erie, Genesee, and Niagara counties near Akron, New York.

History

The Tonawanda Seneca separated politically from the Seneca Nation of Indians in the nineteenth century. Under the disputed Treaty of Buffalo Creek (1838), the Seneca were to be removed west and their New York reservations sold; the Tonawanda band refused to leave and challenged the treaty, since their chiefs had not signed. In the Treaty of 1857 with the United States, the Tonawanda Band was recognized as a distinct political entity and used money from the sale of Kansas lands to buy back part of their reservation—about 7,549 acres—from the Ogden Land Company.

Government and culture

Unlike the Seneca Nation, which adopted an elected republican government in 1848, the Tonawanda Band retained the traditional Haudenosaunee system of governance: eight sachem chiefs selected by clan mothers of the eight Seneca clans. The band maintains the longhouse religion associated with the teachings of Handsome Lake, celebrates traditional ceremonies, and has worked to revitalize the Seneca language through community programs. The tribe operates the Tonawanda Seneca Heritage Center and cultural programs; in 2023 the band acquired additional ancestral land adjacent to the Iroquois National Wildlife Refuge for conservation and cultural use.

Legal significance

The Tonawanda Band's nineteenth-century legal struggle to remain on its land is a landmark in federal Indian law, notably involving the band's secretary, Ely S. Parker (Donehogawa)—a Tonawanda Seneca who became a Union general's aide, helped draft the surrender terms at Appomattox, and served as the first Native American Commissioner of Indian Affairs.
