Catastrophe theory

Catastrophe theory
FieldMathematics (dynamical systems, singularity theory)
Key figuresRené Thom, Christopher Zeeman, Vladimir Arnold
Central resultClassification of elementary catastrophes (Thom's theorem)

In mathematics, catastrophe theory is a branch of bifurcation theory in the study of dynamical systems; it is also a particular special case of more general singularity theory in geometry. It studies and classifies phenomena characterised by sudden shifts in behaviour arising from small changes in circumstances. The theory originated with the work of the French mathematician René Thom in the 1960s and was popularised by Christopher Zeeman in the 1970s.

Elementary catastrophes

Thom proved that, for systems governed by a potential function and depending smoothly on no more than four control parameters, all discontinuous changes of state can be modelled by one of seven universal geometric forms, the elementary catastrophes: the fold, cusp, swallowtail, butterfly, hyperbolic umbilic, elliptic umbilic and parabolic umbilic. The best known is the cusp catastrophe, in which a surface folded over a "pleat" makes a system jump abruptly from one state to another as parameters vary, producing hysteresis — different behaviour depending on the direction of change.

Applications and criticism

Catastrophe theory has been applied to optics (caustics), elasticity (buckling), phase transitions and embryological development, which was Thom's original motivation. Attempts in the 1970s to apply it qualitatively to prison riots, stock market crashes and animal behaviour drew sharp criticism — notably from Hector Sussmann and Raphael Zahler — for making unfalsifiable claims, and its popularity in the social sciences waned. Within mathematics, however, its concepts were absorbed into the rigorous fields of singularity and bifurcation theory.
