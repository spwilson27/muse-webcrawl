Crime prevention through environmental design

CPTED uses lighting, sight lines and territorial definition to discourage offending.

Crime prevention through environmental design (CPTED) is a multidisciplinary approach to deterring crime that focuses on changing how places are designed, laid out and managed so as to reduce opportunities for offending and increase the perceived risk of being caught. The term was coined by criminologist C. Ray Jeffery in his 1971 book of the same name, and was popularised in practical form by architect Oscar Newman, whose concept of "defensible space" (1972) influenced housing design.

Core strategies

First-generation CPTED is conventionally organised around four strategies: natural surveillance (arranging windows, lighting and sight lines so people can observe public space), natural access control (using entrances, fences and landscaping to guide movement and deny easy entry or escape), territorial reinforcement (design cues that signal ownership and encourage residents to look after an area), and maintenance (prompt repair and upkeep, related to "broken windows" theory). Later "second-generation" CPTED adds social factors such as community cohesion, connectivity and local culture.

Application and criticism

CPTED principles are embedded in planning guidance, building codes and police "Secured by Design" schemes in countries including the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada and the Netherlands, and are applied to housing estates, schools, car parks and transport interchanges. Evaluations suggest modest crime reductions in some settings, though critics warn against overclaiming, displacement of crime to other places, and the risk of creating exclusionary or fortress-like environments that stigmatise residents.
