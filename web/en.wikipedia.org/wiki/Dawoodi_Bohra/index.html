The Dawoodi Bohras are a religious denomination within the Tayyibi branch of Musta'li Ismaili Shia Islam. Numbering around one million, they trace their heritage to the Fatimid caliphate of Egypt and to missionaries who carried the faith to western India in the 11th century. The community is led by the Da'i al-Mutlaq, the absolute missionary who represents the hidden Imam in his seclusion.

History

After the death of the Fatimid imam-caliph al-Mustansir in 1094, a succession dispute split the Ismailis; the Musta'li line recognised by the Bohras' ancestors passed to the infant imam al-Tayyib, who entered concealment (satr) in Yemen around 1130. Authority devolved on a line of da'is in Yemen, and from the 16th century, as Yemen became unsafe, the da'iship moved to India. The community's name derives from the Gujarati voharvu, "to trade", reflecting the Bohras' historic calling as merchants.

A succession dispute in the late 16th century split the Indian Tayyibis: the majority followed Da'ud ibn Qutb Shah as 27th Da'i — hence "Dawoodi" — while minorities formed the Sulaymani and Alavi Bohra communities. Persecuted intermittently under Aurangzeb, the Dawoodis later prospered as traders across the Indian Ocean, and under the 51st Da'i, Taher Saifuddin (1915–1965), and his son Mohammed Burhanuddin (1965–2014), the community was centrally reorganised and globalised.

Leadership and succession

The Da'i al-Mutlaq wields comprehensive religious and communal authority, appointing local officials (amils), administering community trusts, and issuing guidance on matters from dress to business ethics. Succession is by nass, designation by the incumbent. The death of the 52nd Da'i, Mohammed Burhanuddin, in 2014 was followed by a contested succession: his son Mufaddal Saifuddin is recognised by the overwhelming majority and by Indian courts' dismissal of the rival claim of the late Da'i's half-brother Khuzaima Qutbuddin (and later his son Taher Fakhruddin), which was pressed in the Bombay High Court until judgment for Saifuddin in 2024.

Beliefs and practice

Dawoodi Bohras affirm the seven pillars of Ismaili practice, pray in a distinctive manner combining Shia observances, fast in Ramadan, and centre communal life on the mosque and the jamatkhana. Distinctive features include the rida (colourful embroidered cloak) worn by women, the white kurta-saya and gold-edged cap worn by men, communal meals served on a shared thaal, reverence for the tombs of da'is and saints (notably at the Raudat Tahera mausoleum in Mumbai), and the use of Lisan al-Dawat. The community is noted for high literacy, a strong trading ethos, and extensive charitable and educational institutions run through the Dawoodi Bohra organisation worldwide.
