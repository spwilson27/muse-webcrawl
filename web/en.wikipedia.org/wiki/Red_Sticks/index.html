Red Sticks

The Red Sticks (also Redsticks) were a traditionalist faction of the Creek (Muscogee) Indians who resisted assimilation and United States expansion in the early 19th century. Their name derived from their red-painted war clubs and the red sticks used by their prophets. Their conflict with the "Lower Creek" faction and the United States became the Creek War of 1813–1814, fought as part of the wider War of 1812.

Background

Inspired in part by the Shawnee leader Tecumseh's call in 1811 for Native peoples to reject American ways and return to traditional lifeways, Red Stick prophets preached resistance among the Upper Towns of the Creek Nation. Tensions turned to civil war within the Creek Nation in 1813, when Red Sticks attacked accommodationist chiefs and their property.

Creek War

The war widened after Red Stick warriors attacked Fort Mims in August 1813, killing several hundred settlers and enslaved people. American forces under Andrew Jackson, aided by Lower Creek and Cherokee allies, campaigned into Creek country, winning battles at Tallushatchee, Talladega, and Emuckfaw before the decisive victory at the Battle of Horseshoe Bend on 27 March 1814, where roughly 800 of 1,000 Red Stick warriors were killed.

Aftermath

The Treaty of Fort Jackson (August 1814) forced the Creek Nation — including Jackson's Creek allies — to cede some 23 million acres (about half of Creek land, in present-day Alabama and Georgia) to the United States. Many surviving Red Sticks fled to Spanish Florida, where they joined the Seminoles.
