Grand opéra

Grand opéra is a genre of 19th-century opera associated with Paris, characterised by large-scale casts, lavish spectacle, historical subjects, elaborate stage effects, and a ballet. It flourished at the Paris Opéra (the Salle Le Peletier) from the late 1820s through the mid-century.

Grand opéra
OriginParis, 1820s–1850s
Typical structureFive acts, with ballet
Key composersAuber, Rossini, Meyerbeer, Halévy, Verdi
LibrettistEugène Scribe (dominant figure)

Characteristics

Grand opéras typically ran to five acts on libretti by Eugène Scribe, dramatising historical conflicts of religion, politics, and love, with crowd scenes and dramatic coups de théâtre exploiting the Opéra's gas lighting and machinery. Landmark works include Auber's La muette de Portici (1828), Rossini's Guillaume Tell (1829), Meyerbeer's Les Huguenots (1836), and Halévy's La Juive (1835).

Influence

The genre shaped opera across Europe: Verdi's Don Carlos and Les vêpres siciliennes were written for Paris in the grand manner, and Wagner both admired and reacted against it. Its scale fell from favour in the later 19th century, but grand opéra remains a byword for operatic spectacle.
