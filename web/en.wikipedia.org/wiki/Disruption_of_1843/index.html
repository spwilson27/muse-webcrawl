The Disruption of 1843, also known as the Great Disruption, was a schism in 1843 in which 450 evangelical ministers broke away from the Church of Scotland to form the Free Church of Scotland. The main conflict was over whether the Church of Scotland or the British Parliament had the final authority over clerical appointments and the church's spiritual independence.

The Disruption came after ten years of conflict within the Church — the "Ten Years' Conflict" — in which the evangelical party sought to abolish lay patronage, the right of landowners to appoint parish ministers against the wishes of congregations. The Veto Act of 1834 allowed congregations to reject a patron's nominee, but the civil courts and ultimately the House of Lords ruled the Act ultra vires, culminating in cases such as the Auchterarder case and the Strathbogie controversy.

The Disruption Assembly

At the General Assembly meeting in St Andrew's Church, Edinburgh, on 18 May 1843, the retiring Moderator, David Welsh, read a protest asserting the church's spiritual independence and the "crown rights of the Redeemer", then led a procession of ministers out of the Assembly to Tanfield Hall in Canonmills, where they constituted the first Assembly of the Free Church with Thomas Chalmers as Moderator. In all, some 450 ministers — over a third of the ministry — signed the Deed of Demission, surrendering their churches, manses, and stipends.

Consequences

The seceding ministers and their congregations had to build an entirely new infrastructure of churches, schools, colleges and a sustentation fund, a task largely organised by Chalmers. The Disruption shaped Scottish religious, educational, and political life for the rest of the nineteenth century. Most of the Free Church reunited with the United Presbyterian Church in 1900 to form the United Free Church, and in 1929 the majority of that body rejoined the Church of Scotland after Parliament removed the causes of dispute.
