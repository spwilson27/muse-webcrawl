Carnot heat engine

A Carnot heat engine is a theoretical heat engine that operates on the Carnot cycle, the most efficient cycle possible for converting a given amount of thermal energy into work, or conversely for using work to create a temperature difference. The model was developed by French physicist Sadi Carnot in his 1824 work Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire.

The cycle

The Carnot cycle consists of four reversible stages: (1) reversible isothermal expansion of the working gas at the hot temperature TH, absorbing heat; (2) reversible adiabatic (isentropic) expansion, cooling the gas to TC; (3) reversible isothermal compression at TC, rejecting heat; and (4) reversible adiabatic compression back to the starting state. Because every step is reversible, the cycle can be run backwards as a refrigerator or heat pump.

Efficiency

By Carnot's theorem, the engine's efficiency equals 1 − TC/TH and is the maximum attainable by any engine between the same reservoirs — see Carnot efficiency. Carnot's analysis laid the groundwork for the second law of thermodynamics and the concept of entropy, later formalised by Clausius and Kelvin.
