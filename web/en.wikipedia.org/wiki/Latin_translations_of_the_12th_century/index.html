The Latin translations of the 12th century were a major intellectual phenomenon of the European Middle Ages: scholars across Latin Christendom sought out and translated scientific and philosophical works from Arabic and Greek into Latin, in what is sometimes called the "translation movement" of the Renaissance of the 12th century. The recovered texts — Aristotle above all, but also Euclid, Ptolemy, Galen, Avicenna, and Averroes — transformed medieval learning and laid the foundations of scholasticism.

Causes and centres

Several frontier zones between Latin, Greek, and Arabic cultures served as translation centres. In Spain, following the Christian conquest of Toledo in 1085, scholars gained access to the great Arabic libraries there; the city drew translators from across Europe, often working in teams pairing a speaker of Arabic with a Latin scholar. In Norman and Hohenstaufen Sicily, Greek and Arabic scholarship coexisted at court in Palermo. Other translators worked in Antioch, southern France, and Constantinople itself, where James of Venice and Burgundio of Pisa translated Greek texts directly.

Major translators and works

The most prolific translator was Gerard of Cremona (c. 1114–1187), who in Toledo rendered more than seventy works from Arabic, including Ptolemy's Almagest, al-Khwarizmi's algebra, and the medical encyclopedias of Rhazes and Avicenna's Canon of Medicine. Adelard of Bath translated Euclid's Elements from Arabic; Plato of Tivoli and Robert of Chester translated mathematics and astronomy; and Michael Scot, at the court of Frederick II, translated Aristotle's zoological works and Averroes' commentaries. James of Venice's Greek-to-Latin Aristotle — the Posterior Analytics and other logical works — supplied the scholastic curriculum.

Impact

The influx of translated knowledge reshaped the universities. The "new Aristotle" forced theologians to reconcile faith with a powerful natural philosophy, producing the scholastic synthesis of the following century. Latin vocabulary expanded with transliterated Arabic terms — algebra, algorithm, alcohol, zenith, nadir — many of which survive today.
