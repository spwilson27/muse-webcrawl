Ibn Arabi

Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240), full name Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn al-ʿArabī al-Ḥātimī aṭ-Ṭāʾī, was an Andalusian Muslim scholar, mystic, poet, and philosopher whose works have profoundly influenced Islamic thought and Sufism. He is known by the honorifics al-Shaykh al-Akbar ("the Greatest Master") and Muḥyī al-Dīn ("the Reviver of Religion").

Life

Ibn Arabi was born in Murcia in al-Andalus (Islamic Spain) in 1165 and raised in Seville. In his youth he received a visionary calling to the spiritual path and met many Sufi masters of the Maghreb and Andalusia; he also famously encountered the philosopher Ibn Rushd (Averroes). Around 1200 he left Spain for the east, performing the pilgrimage to Mecca, where he began his magnum opus. He travelled through Anatolia, Iraq, and Syria, settling in Damascus in 1223, where he died in 1240 and was buried on the slopes of Mount Qasioun.

Works and thought

His enormous output of more than 300 works includes Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya ("The Meccan Illuminations"), a vast encyclopedia of spiritual knowledge, and Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam ("The Bezels of Wisdom"), considered his most concentrated statement of doctrine, in which each chapter is associated with a prophet embodying a particular divine wisdom.

Ibn Arabi is associated above all with the doctrine later called waḥdat al-wujūd, the "unity of being," holding that existence is fundamentally one and that God alone truly exists, the universe being His self-disclosure (tajallī). His concepts of the Perfect Human (al-insān al-kāmil) and the creative imagination have influenced thinkers from the Ottoman world to Persia and India. His views have also been controversial: critics such as Ibn Taymiyya attacked them as monism incompatible with orthodoxy, while defenders maintain his teaching preserves divine transcendence.
