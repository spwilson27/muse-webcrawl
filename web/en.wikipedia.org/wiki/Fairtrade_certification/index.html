Fairtrade certification

The Fairtrade certification system, administered by Fairtrade International (FLO) and its national organisations, allows products that meet agreed social, economic and environmental standards to carry the blue-and-green FAIRTRADE Mark. The system aims to give farmers and workers in developing countries fairer prices, decent working conditions, and a Fairtrade Premium — an additional sum invested in community or business projects chosen democratically by producers.

How it works

Standards are set by Fairtrade International for small producer organisations, hired labour situations, and traders. Certification and auditing are carried out by FLOCERT, an independent body. Key guarantees include a Fairtrade Minimum Price for many products, intended to cover the average costs of sustainable production and act as a safety net when market prices fall. Certified products include coffee, cocoa, bananas, tea, sugar, cotton, flowers and gold.

History

The first Fairtrade label, "Max Havelaar", was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for coffee from Mexican cooperatives. National labelling initiatives spread through Europe and North America in the 1990s and were unified under Fairtrade Labelling Organizations International (founded 1997, later Fairtrade International), with a single international Mark introduced in 2002. In 2011 the U.S. organisation Fair Trade USA left the international system to pursue its own certification model, a split that drew debate within the movement.

Criticism

Critics argue certification costs can burden small producers, that benefits to hired labourers may be limited, and that only a portion of certified output is sold on Fairtrade terms. Supporters point to studies showing income stability and community investment benefits for certified cooperatives.
