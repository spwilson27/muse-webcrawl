The socialist-oriented market economy (Vietnamese: kinh tế thị trường định hướng xã hội chủ nghĩa) is the official designation of the economic system of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam. It is a multi-sector market economy in which the state sector plays a leading role, operating under the management of the socialist state and the leadership of the Communist Party of Vietnam, with the stated long-term goal of transitioning toward socialism.

Origins: Đổi Mới

The system emerged from the Đổi Mới ("renovation") reforms launched at the Sixth National Congress of the Communist Party of Vietnam in December 1986. Facing hyperinflation, food shortages, and the collapse of Soviet-bloc aid, Vietnam dismantled central planning and price controls, legalised private enterprise, decollectivised agriculture through the household contract system, and opened the economy to foreign investment with the 1987 Foreign Investment Law. The term "socialist-oriented market economy" was formally adopted in the platform of the Ninth Party Congress in 2001.

Characteristics

According to official doctrine, the model is a market economy because it recognises multiple forms of ownership and economic sectors — state, collective, private, and foreign-invested — allows supply and demand to allocate most goods, and integrates Vietnam into global markets. It is "socialist-oriented" because the state retains ownership of land on behalf of the people, state-owned enterprises dominate strategic sectors such as energy, telecommunications, and banking, and macroeconomic policy is directed toward social equity and poverty reduction. Vietnam joined the World Trade Organization in 2007 and is party to major trade agreements including the CPTPP and the EU–Vietnam Free Trade Agreement.

Performance

Reforms transformed Vietnam from one of the world's poorest countries into a lower-middle-income economy. GDP growth averaged around 6–7 percent annually for decades; the poverty rate fell from over 70 percent in the early 1990s to low single digits by World Bank extreme-poverty measures; and Vietnam became a major exporter of electronics, textiles, footwear, and agricultural products, attracting large investments from firms such as Samsung and Intel.

Debates and criticism

Domestic and foreign analysts debate whether the model represents a transitional stage toward socialism, as the Party maintains, or a form of state capitalism. Criticisms include persistent inefficiency and corruption in state-owned enterprises, land-rights disputes arising from public land ownership, and dependence on foreign-invested firms for high-value exports. The United States long treated Vietnam as a non-market economy for trade-remedy purposes, a designation Vietnam has sought to have removed.

Comparison

The model is frequently compared with China's "socialist market economy", sharing features such as one-party rule, a leading state sector, and gradualist reform, though Vietnam's private sector and political pluralism within the Party's consultative bodies are sometimes said to be comparatively more open.
