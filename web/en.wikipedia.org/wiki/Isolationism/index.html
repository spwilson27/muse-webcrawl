Isolationism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Isolationism is a political philosophy advocating a national foreign policy that opposes involvement in the political affairs, and especially the wars, of other countries. The term is most associated with the United States, whose Farewell Address tradition (Washington, 1796: avoid "permanent alliances") and Monroe Doctrine shaped a 19th-century pattern of hemispheric dominance combined with abstention from European conflicts — an abstention twice interrupted (1917, 1941) before the US became the architect of the post-1945 alliance system.

History and usage

Interwar isolationism was a mass movement (the America First Committee, 1940–41) defeated by Pearl Harbor. "Isolationist" survives mostly as an epithet: positions favouring retrenchment, restraint or "America First" trade and alliance scepticism are labelled isolationist by critics, while their holders usually describe themselves as prioritising domestic renewal or avoiding overextension. Analogues elsewhere include Switzerland's permanent neutrality (a related but distinct doctrine), Japan's sakoku seclusion (1630s–1853), and China's and Albania's 20th-century autarkic episodes. Scholars distinguish isolationism proper (broad withdrawal from political-military engagement, historically compatible with active trade) from non-interventionism and neutrality.
