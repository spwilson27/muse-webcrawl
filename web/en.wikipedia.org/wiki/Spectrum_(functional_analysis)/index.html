Spectrum (functional analysis)

In mathematics, particularly in functional analysis, the spectrum of a bounded linear operator (or, more generally, an element of a Banach algebra) is the set of complex numbers for which the operator fails to have a bounded inverse defined on the whole space. It generalizes the notion of eigenvalues of a matrix to operators on infinite-dimensional spaces.

Definition

Let T be a bounded linear operator on a Banach space X. For a complex number λ, consider the operator T − λI. The resolvent set ρ(T) consists of those λ for which T − λI is bijective with bounded inverse; the spectrum σ(T) is the complement of the resolvent set in the complex plane. The spectrum is always nonempty (for operators on complex Banach spaces), closed, and contained in the disc of radius equal to the operator norm; the radius of the smallest such disc is the spectral radius, given by Gelfand's formula.

Classification

The spectrum is commonly decomposed into three disjoint parts:

  • Point spectrum — λ such that T − λI is not injective; these are the eigenvalues.
  • Continuous spectrum — λ such that T − λI is injective with dense but not surjective range, the inverse being unbounded.
  • Residual spectrum — λ such that T − λI is injective but its range is not dense.

Significance

Spectral theory underlies the spectral theorem for self-adjoint and normal operators, central to quantum mechanics where observables are represented by operators and measurement outcomes by their spectra. The concept extends to unbounded operators, C*-algebras, and commutative Banach algebras, where the spectrum of an element is tied to the Gelfand representation.
