The July Crisis was a series of interrelated diplomatic and military escalations among the major powers of Europe in the summer of 1914, following the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary by the Bosnian Serb nationalist Gavrilo Princip on 28 June 1914. Within five weeks the crisis produced declarations of war between the great powers and thus the outbreak of the First World War.

The "blank cheque"

In early July, Germany assured Austria-Hungary of its full support even if action against Serbia led to war with Russia — the so-called "blank cheque" of 5–6 July 1914. Emboldened, Vienna decided to use the assassination to crush Serbian power.

The ultimatum

On 23 July Austria-Hungary delivered an ultimatum to Serbia with demands so harsh they were designed to be rejected, including a requirement that Austro-Hungarian officials investigate on Serbian soil. Serbia accepted nearly all demands within the 48-hour deadline but reserved one point for arbitration at The Hague. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July 1914 and shelled Belgrade the next day.

Escalation

Russia, Serbia's patron, ordered partial and then general mobilisation (30 July). Germany issued ultimata to Russia and France, declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August, and implemented the Schlieffen Plan by invading Luxembourg and Belgium. Britain, guaranteeing Belgian neutrality, declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914. Attempts at mediation, including Kaiser Wilhelm II's exchanges with Tsar Nicholas II (the "Willy–Nicky" telegrams), failed.

Historiography

Responsibility for the crisis has been debated ever since: the Versailles Treaty's "war guilt" clause blamed Germany and its allies, while later historians, notably Fritz Fischer, stressed German and Austro-Hungarian initiative; more recent accounts, such as Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers, distribute responsibility among all the chancelleries.
