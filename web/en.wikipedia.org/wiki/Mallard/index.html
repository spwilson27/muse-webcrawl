The mallard (Anas platyrhynchos) is a dabbling duck that breeds throughout the temperate and subtropical Americas, Eurasia and North Africa. It is probably the best-known and most recognisable of all ducks, and is the ancestor of most breeds of domestic duck.

Description

The mallard is a medium-sized waterfowl species, measuring 50–65 cm (20–26 in) in length with a wingspan of 81–98 cm (32–39 in). The species is strongly sexually dimorphic. Breeding males have a glossy bottle-green head, a white collar, a chestnut-brown breast, grey-brown wings and a pale grey belly. Females are predominantly mottled brown, providing camouflage while nesting. Both sexes have an iridescent blue-purple speculum patch edged with white on the wing, and a yellow-orange bill in the male.

Distribution and habitat

Mallards inhabit a wide range of wetlands, including lakes, ponds, rivers, marshes and estuaries, as well as urban parks. Northern populations migrate south in winter, while birds in milder regions are largely resident. The species has also been introduced to Australia, New Zealand and parts of South America and southern Africa.

Behaviour and diet

Mallards feed mainly by dabbling at the surface for plant material, seeds, grasses and aquatic invertebrates, and also graze on land. The female builds a nest on the ground near water, lined with down, and incubates a clutch of 8–13 eggs alone. The familiar loud "quack" is produced by the female; males give quieter, rasping calls.

Relationship with humans

The mallard is one of the most common game birds and is widely hunted. Nearly all domestic duck breeds descend from the mallard, the main exception being breeds derived from the Muscovy duck. Because it readily interbreeds with closely related species and domestic ducks, hybridisation is a conservation concern for some wild duck populations.
