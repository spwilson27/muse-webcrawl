Detection theory

Detection theory or signal detection theory is a means to measure the ability to differentiate between information-bearing patterns (called stimulus in living organisms, signal in machines) and random patterns that distract from the information (called noise, consisting of background stimuli and random activity of the detection machine and of the nervous system of the operator).

Outcomes

In any detection task there are four possible outcomes: a hit (signal present and reported present), a miss (signal present but reported absent), a false alarm (signal absent but reported present), and a correct rejection (signal absent and reported absent).

Sensitivity and bias

Signal detection theory separates an observer's sensitivity (d′, "d-prime") — how distinguishable the signal is from noise — from their response bias or criterion, the threshold of evidence they require before reporting the signal. Changing the payoffs or base rates shifts the criterion without necessarily changing sensitivity.

Applications

The theory, developed from radar-operator research during the Second World War and formalised by Peterson, Birdsall, Fox, Tanner and Green in the 1950s, is now used in psychology, medical diagnosis, radiology, quality control, weather forecasting, lie detection and machine learning evaluation.
