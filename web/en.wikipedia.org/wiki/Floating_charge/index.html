Floating charge

A floating charge is a security interest over a fund of changing assets (such as stock in trade) of a company or other artificial person. Unlike a fixed charge, which attaches to a specific asset, a floating charge "floats" or "hovers" over a class of present and future assets, allowing the chargor to deal with those assets in the ordinary course of business — buying, selling and replacing stock — without the charge holder's consent. Upon the occurrence of certain events, typically default or insolvency, the floating charge crystallises into a fixed charge over the assets then held.

Floating charges commonly cover circulating assets such as trading stock.

Nature and history

The floating charge is a creation of English equity, developed by the courts in the nineteenth century — the concept was recognised in cases such as Holroyd v Marshall (1862) and Panama, New Zealand and Australian Royal Mail Co (1870) — and it became the standard security taken by banks lending to limited companies. The classic description of its characteristics was given by Romer LJ in Re Yorkshire Woolcombers Association [1903]: a charge over a class of assets present and future, which changes from time to time, and where the company may carry on its business in the ordinary way with that class of assets.

Crystallisation

Crystallisation occurs on events such as the winding up of the company, the appointment of a receiver or administrator, cessation of business, or an express crystallisation event defined in the debenture. Once crystallised, the company can no longer dispose of the charged assets free of the charge.

Priority and insolvency

A floating charge generally ranks behind fixed charges and, in insolvency, behind preferential creditors and the "prescribed part" set aside for unsecured creditors under English law. The distinction between fixed and floating charges over book debts was settled by the House of Lords in Re Spectrum Plus [2005], which held that the label given by the parties is not decisive — the court examines the degree of control actually exercised by the charge holder.

The floating charge is widely used in common-law jurisdictions such as England and Wales, Ireland and Hong Kong, but is not recognised in most civil-law systems; Scots law historically restricted its use, though it has been available to companies since 1961.
