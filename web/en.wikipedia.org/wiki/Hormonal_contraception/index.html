Hormonal contraception

Hormonal contraception refers to birth control methods that act on the endocrine system. Almost all methods consist of steroid hormones, although in India one selective estrogen receptor modulator is marketed as a contraceptive. The original hormonal method — the combined oral contraceptive pill — was first marketed as a contraceptive in 1960. In the ensuing decades many other delivery methods have been developed.

Methods

Hormonal methods include:

  • Combined estrogen–progestin methods: the combined oral contraceptive pill, the contraceptive patch, and the vaginal ring.
  • Progestin-only methods: progestin-only pills, implants such as etonogestrel and levonorgestrel rods, injectables such as depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (Depo-Provera), and hormonal intrauterine devices.
  • Emergency contraception: levonorgestrel and ulipristal acetate pills used after unprotected intercourse.

Mechanism

The primary mechanism is prevention of ovulation through suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis: exogenous estrogen and progestin exert negative feedback, reducing gonadotropin-releasing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, and the mid-cycle luteinizing hormone surge. Secondary effects include thickening of cervical mucus, which impedes sperm passage, and changes to the endometrial lining.

Effectiveness and effects

With perfect use, most hormonal methods exceed 99% effectiveness per year; typical-use failure rates are higher for user-dependent methods such as pills. Benefits beyond contraception include regulation of menstrual cycles, reduced menstrual pain and blood loss, and decreased risk of ovarian and endometrial cancers. Risks include a small increased risk of venous thromboembolism, particularly with estrogen-containing methods, and possible mood and libido effects. Hormonal contraception does not protect against sexually transmitted infections.

History

The development of the pill in the 1950s by Gregory Pincus, John Rock, and Margaret Sanger's advocacy, with funding from Katharine McCormick, transformed social and sexual life in much of the world. Enovid was approved for contraceptive use in the United States in 1960.
