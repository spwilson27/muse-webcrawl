Vickrey–Clarke–Groves auction

A Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) auction is a type of sealed-bid auction of multiple items, generalising the single-item Vickrey (second-price) auction. Bidders submit bids on bundles of items; the auctioneer chooses the allocation that maximises the total declared value, and each winner pays not their own bid but the externality they impose on others — the loss in total value to the rest of the bidders caused by their presence.

In a VCG auction, each winner pays the harm their winning causes others

Mechanism

Formally, if bidder i receives allocation x and the reported valuations are v, then i's payment is the total value the other bidders would have achieved in the best allocation without i, minus the total value the others actually achieve with i present. Because a bidder's own bid affects only whether they win, and not the price they pay (except through the outcome), truthful bidding is a dominant strategy: no bidder can gain by misreporting their valuation, whatever others do.

Properties and applications

The VCG auction simultaneously achieves an efficient (value-maximising) allocation and incentive compatibility, and it is the flagship example of the broader Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism in mechanism design theory, named after William Vickrey, Edward H. Clarke and Theodore Groves. VCG ideas underlie the pricing of sponsored search advertising (in modified form as the generalised second-price auction actually used by early search engines) and have been proposed for spectrum allocation, airport slots and allocating resources in networks.

Drawbacks include potentially low or zero revenue (sometimes far below other auction formats), vulnerability to collusion and false-name bids, and the computational difficulty of finding the optimal allocation in large combinatorial settings. These concerns explain why pure VCG auctions are less common in practice than the theory might suggest.
