A disc herniation (also called a slipped or ruptured disc) is a condition affecting the spine in which a tear in the outer fibrous ring (annulus fibrosus) of an intervertebral disc allows the soft central portion (nucleus pulposus) to bulge out and potentially compress nearby spinal nerves. It most often occurs in the lower back, where it is a common cause of sciatica — pain radiating down the leg along the sciatic nerve.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on the level and position of the herniation. Lumbar herniations typically cause back pain and radicular leg pain, sometimes with numbness, tingling or weakness in a dermatomal distribution. Cervical herniations cause neck and arm symptoms. Most herniations are asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging. A rare but serious complication, cauda equina syndrome, involves bladder or bowel dysfunction and saddle anaesthesia and requires emergency surgery.

Causes and diagnosis

Herniation is usually the result of age-related disc degeneration combined with mechanical loading; a single traumatic event is a less common cause. Diagnosis is based on the clinical picture, with MRI the imaging modality of choice when confirmation is needed.

Treatment

Most cases resolve with conservative management — staying active, analgesics and anti-inflammatory medication, and physiotherapy — within several weeks. Epidural steroid injections may give short-term relief. Surgery, most commonly discectomy or microdiscectomy to remove the herniated fragment, is reserved for persistent disabling pain, progressive neurological deficit, or cauda equina syndrome.
