The Lockheed L-1011 TriStar is an American medium-to-long-range, wide-body trijet airliner built by Lockheed Corporation. It was the third wide-body airliner to enter service, after the Boeing 747 and the McDonnell Douglas DC-10, with which it competed directly. The TriStar is distinguished by its centre engine mounted in the tail with an S-shaped intake duct, and it was the first wide-body airliner certified for fully automatic "autoland" landings (Category IIIc).

Development

Lockheed developed the L-1011 in response to American Airlines' requirement for a wide-body aircraft smaller than the 747. The design used three Rolls-Royce RB211 turbofans. Development was badly delayed when Rolls-Royce collapsed into bankruptcy in 1971 over RB211 costs and had to be nationalised by the British government; the delay cost the TriStar sales against the DC-10, which reached the market first. Lockheed sold 250 aircraft — far short of the roughly 500 needed to break even — and the programme's heavy losses contributed to Lockheed's withdrawal from the commercial airliner market.

Design

The TriStar was technologically advanced: it featured a full flying-tail (stabilator), direct lift control for smooth approaches, and an autoland system that allowed landings in zero visibility. Capacity was up to about 400 passengers in high-density layout, with a typical three-class load of around 250–300, and range of up to roughly 7,410 km (4,600 mi) in the -500 long-range variant. Variants included the -1, -100, -200, shortened long-range -500, and conversions to freighters and RAF tanker/transports (TriStar KC1/K1/C2).

Operators and legacy

Launch and major customers included Eastern Air Lines, TWA, Delta Air Lines (the largest operator), Pan Am, All Nippon Airways, Cathay Pacific and British Airways. The type was involved in notable accidents, including Eastern Air Lines Flight 401 (1972), which crashed in the Florida Everglades while the crew was distracted by a faulty gear indicator light — the origin of the famous "ghost of Flight 401" legend — and Delta Air Lines Flight 191 (1985), destroyed by a microburst at Dallas/Fort Worth. The last passenger services ended in the 2000s; a single L-1011, Northrop Grumman's Stargazer, remains in use as an air-launch mothership for Pegasus rockets.
