János Bolyai (15 December 1802 – 27 January 1860) was a Hungarian mathematician from Transylvania, one of the founders of non-Euclidean geometry. In 1832 he published the first systematic account of hyperbolic geometry — a geometry in which Euclid's parallel postulate does not hold — developed independently of Nikolai Lobachevsky, who had published similar results in Russia a few years earlier.

Life

Bolyai was the son of Farkas Bolyai, a professor of mathematics and friend of Carl Friedrich Gauss. A child prodigy who mastered calculus by thirteen and played the violin to a high standard, János entered the Imperial Engineering Academy in Vienna and became an officer in the Austrian army, serving from 1823 to 1833, when fever forced his retirement. He spent the rest of his life in relative obscurity in Transylvania, working on mathematics, music theory and a universal philosophical system.

The "Appendix"

Farkas Bolyai had spent years trying to prove Euclid's parallel postulate and warned his son away from the problem, writing that he must not waste an hour on it. Instead János, by 1823, had grasped that a consistent geometry could be built by denying the postulate; he wrote to his father that "out of nothing I have created a strange new universe". The work appeared in 1832 as an appendix — the Appendix Scientiam Spatii Absolute Veram Exhibens — to Farkas's textbook Tentamen. Gauss, reading it, wrote that he regarded the younger Bolyai as "a genius of the first order" but also claimed to have reached the same conclusions himself decades earlier, which deeply disappointed Bolyai.

Recognition

Bolyai's work was little read in his lifetime and he published nothing further of substance. Only later in the nineteenth century, as the papers of Gauss and the work of Lobachevsky became known, was Bolyai recognised as a founder of modern geometry. The Bolyai crater on the Moon and the János Bolyai Mathematical Institute at Szeged commemorate him.
