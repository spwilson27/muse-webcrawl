Content Scramble System

The Content Scramble System (CSS) is a digital rights management (DRM) and encryption system employed on many commercially produced DVD-Video discs. Developed by Matsushita (Panasonic) and Toshiba and licensed by the DVD Copy Control Association, it uses a proprietary 40-bit stream cipher. CSS was introduced in 1996 alongside the DVD format and was intended to prevent unauthorised copying and to support region playback control.

How it works

Each disc carries a set of "disc keys" encrypted with hundreds of licensed "player keys". A licensed player uses its player key to recover the disc key, which in turn decrypts a title key for each video title; the video data is scrambled with the title key using CSS's linear-feedback shift register-based cipher. Licensed players must also implement restrictions on outputs and respect the disc's region code.

Cracking

The system was compromised in October 1999, when the teenage programmer Jon Lech Johansen and collaborators released DeCSS, software that descrambled DVD content. The break exploited a player (the software player XingDVD) whose key had been poorly protected, together with weaknesses of the 40-bit cipher — a key length constrained by then-current United States export controls on cryptography — which made brute-force and known-plaintext attacks practical.

Legal issues

The motion picture industry sued to suppress DeCSS under the anti-circumvention provisions of the U.S. Digital Millennium Copyright Act. In Universal City Studios v. Corley (2001), the Second Circuit upheld an injunction against distributing DeCSS. In California, the trade-secret case DVD CCA v. Bunner ultimately failed because the information had become widely known. Johansen was prosecuted in Norway and acquitted in 2003. The episode became a celebrated cause in debates over free speech and code, with the DeCSS algorithm printed on T-shirts and recast in verse.

Successors

CSS's weaknesses led to stronger systems on later formats: AACS for HD DVD and Blu-ray, and BD+ as an additional layer. CSS itself remains in use on standard DVDs.
