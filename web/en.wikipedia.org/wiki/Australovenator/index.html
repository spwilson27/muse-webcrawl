Australovenator (meaning "southern hunter") is a genus of megaraptoran theropod dinosaur from the Late Cretaceous (Cenomanian, about 95 million years ago) Winton Formation of Queensland, Australia. The type and only species is Australovenator wintonensis, named in honour of the nearby town of Winton. It is the most complete predatory dinosaur skeleton found in Australia.

Discovery and naming

The holotype specimen (AODF 85) was nicknamed "Banjo" after the Australian poet Banjo Paterson, author of "Waltzing Matilda", which was written near Winton. The remains were excavated in 2006 and 2007 from the "Matilda site" by the Australian Age of Dinosaurs Museum, working in partnership with the Queensland Museum. The discovery site also yielded the sauropods Diamantinasaurus matildae and Wintonotitan wattsi; the three dinosaurs were formally described together in 2009 by Scott Hocknull and colleagues.

Description

Australovenator was a medium-sized, lightly built predator, estimated at about 5 to 6 metres long and roughly 500 kilograms in weight. It had long, powerful forearms ending in three fingers tipped with large, curved claws — the claw on the inner finger was particularly large and may have been used to slash at prey. Its hind limbs were adapted for running, and its teeth were small, sharp and serrated, suited to slicing flesh. The animal has been described as the "cheetah" of its time and is often called the Australian equivalent of Velociraptor, though considerably larger.

Classification and palaeoecology

The genus has been placed among the megaraptorans, a group of allosauroid theropods noted for their enlarged hand claws; some analyses instead place megaraptorans within Coelurosauria, possibly close to tyrannosauroids. Australovenator inhabited a lush floodplain environment of rivers and lakes near Winton, sharing it with giant sauropods, crocodile-like predators and early birds.
