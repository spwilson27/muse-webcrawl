Metaphony

In historical linguistics, metaphony is a type of sound change in which a vowel in a word is altered in anticipation of a vowel in a following syllable. It is a form of vowel harmony operating at a distance, closely related to the Germanic phenomenon known as umlaut (i-mutation). The term derives from Greek meta- ("change") and phōnē ("sound").

Romance metaphony

The term is most often used for metaphony in the Romance languages, particularly in southern Italian dialects and in some varieties of Spanish and Portuguese. In many Neapolitan and other south Italian dialects, stressed mid vowels raise or diphthongise when the final vowel of the word is high (/i/ or /u/), even though those final vowels were later reduced to schwa. Because the triggering vowel is no longer distinct, metaphony has become a morphological marker: differences in the stem vowel alone can distinguish, for example, masculine from feminine or singular from plural forms.

Examples

  • In Asturian, final /u/ triggers raising of stressed vowels in masculine singular nouns, contrasting with feminine forms in /a/.
  • In the dialects of Salento and Cilento, metaphony of /ɛ/ and /ɔ/ yields diphthongs before high final vowels.
  • In Cantabrian (Pasiego) dialects, metaphony affects vowels throughout the word, not only stressed vowels — a feature often called vowel harmony in that context.

Relation to umlaut

Germanic umlaut and Romance metaphony are parallel developments: both began as phonetic assimilation to a following high or front vowel, and both were phonologised — becoming grammatical markers — when the conditioning vowels weakened or disappeared. Some scholars use "metaphony" as the general term and "umlaut" for the Germanic case specifically.
