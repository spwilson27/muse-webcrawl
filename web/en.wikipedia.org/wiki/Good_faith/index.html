Good faith

Good faith (Latin: bona fides) is a general legal and moral concept denoting sincere intention to deal fairly and honestly with others, to abstain from deception or taking unconscientious advantage, and to honor the reasonable expectations created by one's commitments. It appears throughout contract law, commercial law, property law, labor relations, and public international law, though its precise content varies between legal systems.

In contract law

In civil law systems, good faith is a pervasive governing principle: the German Civil Code (BGB §242) requires performance according to Treu und Glauben ("good faith and fair dealing"), and the French Civil Code requires that agreements be performed in good faith. Judges in these systems use the doctrine to supplement gaps in contracts, restrain abusive exercises of rights, and police unfair terms.

Common law systems historically resisted a general doctrine, emphasizing freedom of contract and the parties' written terms. English law still lacks a general duty of good faith in commercial contracts, though good faith duties arise in specific contexts such as insurance (the doctrine of uberrima fides, utmost good faith), fiduciary relations, and some long-term "relational" contracts. Other common law jurisdictions have moved further: Canada's Supreme Court recognized an organizing principle of good faith in Bhasin v. Hrynew (2014), including a duty of honest performance.

In the United States, the Uniform Commercial Code imposes an obligation of good faith in the performance and enforcement of contracts within its scope, generally non-disclaimable, and the common law of most states recognizes the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in every contract.

In international law

Good faith is a foundational principle of international law. The UN Charter obliges members to fulfill their obligations in good faith, and the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties requires that treaties be performed in good faith (pacta sunt servanda, Article 26) and interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning of their terms (Article 31). The International Court of Justice has described good faith as "one of the basic principles governing the creation and performance of legal obligations" (Nuclear Tests, 1974), grounding doctrines such as estoppel and the binding force of unilateral declarations.

Bona fide purchaser

In property law, a bona fide purchaser (BFP) is one who buys property for value, in good faith, and without notice of a prior claim or defect in title. Such a purchaser may take the property free of undisclosed equitable interests — a doctrine sometimes called the "darling of equity". Recording statutes in property systems protect BFPs who record their interests first under the applicable race, notice, or race-notice scheme.

Negotiation and labor law

Statutes governing collective bargaining, such as the U.S. National Labor Relations Act, require employers and unions to bargain in good faith: a genuine effort to reach agreement, evidenced by meeting, exchanging proposals, and refraining from "surface bargaining", though the law does not compel either side to accept any particular term. Similar duties of good-faith negotiation appear in insurance settlement practice, franchise law, and pre-contractual liability (culpa in contrahendo) in civil law systems.

Bad faith

The converse, bad faith (mala fides), denotes dishonest intent, fraud, or deliberate evasion of one's obligations. It carries distinct legal consequences in several fields: bad-faith breach by an insurer can expose it to extra-contractual and punitive damages in many U.S. states; bad-faith patent or trademark applications may be invalid; and bad faith in litigation can lead to sanctions.
