Cotton Mather (February 12, 1663 – February 13, 1728) was a New England Puritan minister, prolific author and pamphleteer. The son of Increase Mather and grandson of two founding Puritan divines, he ministered at Boston's North Church for more than four decades, published some 450 books and pamphlets, and remains a controversial figure for his association with the Salem witch trials and, more favourably, for championing smallpox inoculation.

Life and ministry

Mather graduated from Harvard College at fifteen and, after overcoming a stammer, became assistant pastor to his father at the Second (North) Church in 1685, serving there until his death. He was among the most influential voices in colonial Massachusetts, involved in the overthrow of Governor Edmund Andros in 1689 and in the colony's religious and political controversies for decades.

Salem witch trials

During the Salem witchcraft crisis of 1692–93, Mather supported the proceedings and wrote The Wonders of the Invisible World (1693), defending the trials even as he privately cautioned against reliance on "spectral evidence". His association with Salem permanently damaged his reputation, though historians debate the degree of his responsibility.

Science and inoculation

Mather was a Fellow of the Royal Society and an early American man of science. His Christian Philosopher (1721) reconciled natural philosophy with religion, and he experimented with plant hybridisation. Most consequentially, during Boston's 1721 smallpox epidemic he promoted variolation — learned from Onesimus, an African man enslaved in his household — and with physician Zabdiel Boylston persuaded a handful of Bostonians to be inoculated despite violent public opposition; the procedure's far lower mortality helped establish inoculation in the West.

Works

His masterpiece, Magnalia Christi Americana (1702), is an ecclesiastical history of New England. His diary and his late work Biblia Americana, an enormous unpublished biblical commentary, round out the output of one of colonial America's most learned and driven figures.
