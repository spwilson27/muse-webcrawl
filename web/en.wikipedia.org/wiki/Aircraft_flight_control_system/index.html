An aircraft flight control system comprises the cockpit controls, linkages, actuators, and movable surfaces by which a pilot controls the direction and attitude of an aircraft in flight. The primary flight controls — ailerons for roll, the elevator for pitch, and the rudder for yaw — produce moments about the aircraft's three axes, while secondary controls such as flaps, leading-edge slats, spoilers, and trim systems adjust lift, drag, and control forces.

Mechanical and powered systems

In early and light aircraft, the pilot's stick (or yoke) and rudder pedals connect to the surfaces by cables, pulleys, pushrods, and bell cranks, and the pilot feels aerodynamic forces directly, sometimes reduced by trim tabs and servo tabs. As aircraft grew faster and heavier, the forces became too great for muscle power, leading to hydraulically powered controls with artificial feel, first on large aircraft of the 1940s and 1950s. Such systems typically use multiple independent hydraulic circuits, irreversible actuators, and yaw dampers and stability augmentation to give acceptable handling.

Fly-by-wire

In fly-by-wire systems, electrical signals from the cockpit controls — often a sidestick — are processed by flight control computers that command hydraulic or electric actuators. Fly-by-wire first flew on military aircraft such as the F-16 (with quadruplex redundancy and relaxed static stability) and entered airliner service with the Airbus A320 in 1988, complete with flight-envelope protection laws that prevent the aircraft from exceeding structural or aerodynamic limits. Boeing's later designs, such as the 777 and 787, use fly-by-wire while retaining more conventional control philosophy. Fly-by-light and power-by-wire electro-hydrostatic actuators represent further developments aimed at weight and reliability gains.

Secondary controls and failure safety

High-lift devices — trailing-edge flaps and leading-edge slats — are deployed for takeoff and landing; spoilers assist roll control, dump lift after touchdown, and act as speed brakes; trimmable stabilizers and rudder and aileron trims relieve sustained loads. Because flight controls are safety-critical, they are designed with multiple redundancy: split surfaces, independent power supplies, jam-tolerant linkages, and, in fly-by-wire, multiple dissimilar computers, so that no single failure can cause loss of control.
