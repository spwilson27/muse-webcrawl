Iterative and incremental development

Iterative and incremental development is a combination of iterative design and incremental build methods used in software development. A system is built through repeated cycles (iterations) and in smaller portions at a time (increments), allowing developers to take advantage of what was learned during the development of earlier parts of the system. It stands in contrast to the linear waterfall model.

Overview

Learning comes from both the development and the use of the system: key steps begin with a simple implementation of a subset of requirements and iteratively enhance the evolving versions until the full system is implemented. At each iteration, design modifications are made and new functional capabilities added. The method has roots in 1950s and 1960s engineering practice, including work at NASA's Project Mercury, and was articulated by practitioners such as Tom Gilb and later flows into methodologies such as the Rational Unified Process and agile software development.

Characteristics

  • Early, partial working software that users can evaluate;
  • Frequent feedback that reduces the risk of building the wrong system;
  • Requirements and architecture refined over time rather than fully specified up front;
  • Each increment delivers additional, tested functionality.
