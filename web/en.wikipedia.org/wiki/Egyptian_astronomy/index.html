Egyptian astronomy

Egyptian astronomy began in prehistoric times and was closely intertwined with religion, agriculture and the state calendar. The Egyptians were careful observers of the sky: they tracked the motions of the Sun and stars to regulate the calendar, time the annual Nile flood and align temples and pyramids.

Astronomical ceiling from the tomb of Senenmut, showing decans and constellations.

The calendar

The Egyptians developed a civil calendar of 365 days — twelve months of thirty days plus five epagomenal days — the first known calendar with a fixed 365-day year. Because it lacked a leap year, it drifted slowly against the seasons, completing a full "Sothic cycle" of about 1,460 years. The heliacal rising of Sirius (Sopdet) heralded the Nile inundation and anchored the religious year.

Decans and timekeeping

Priest-astronomers divided the night sky into 36 groups of stars called decans, whose successive risings marked the hours of the night; diagonal star tables depicting them were painted inside coffin lids. Water clocks (clepsydrae) and shadow clocks supplemented stellar timekeeping. The day was divided into 24 hours — twelve of day and twelve of night — a convention transmitted through Hellenistic astronomy to the modern world.

Cosmology and alignments

Egyptian cosmology pictured the sky goddess Nut arched over the earth god Geb, with the Sun god Ra travelling by boat across the sky. The circumpolar "imperishable stars" held religious significance, and the Great Pyramid of Giza was aligned to true north with remarkable precision, probably by stellar observation. In the Ptolemaic period, Egyptian traditions merged with Babylonian and Greek astronomy in Alexandria.
