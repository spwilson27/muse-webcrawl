The foreign policy of the United States is its interactions with foreign nations and the standards it sets for its organisations, corporations and individual citizens. It is shaped by the President, advised by the Secretary of State, with Congress exercising constitutional roles in declaring war, ratifying treaties (through the Senate), and funding. Stated aims traditionally include national security, the prosperity of the American economy, and the promotion of democracy and human rights.

Historical outline

George Washington's Farewell Address counselled avoiding permanent alliances, and the Monroe Doctrine (1823) declared the Americas closed to new European colonisation. The 19th century saw continental expansion and, in 1898, war with Spain that brought overseas possessions. The United States intervened decisively in both World Wars, and President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points advanced self-determination and a League of Nations.

After 1945 the US built a network of alliances and institutions — the United Nations, the Bretton Woods system, NATO and bilateral pacts in East Asia — while pursuing containment of the Soviet Union through the Cold War, expressed in the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan. Following the Cold War, US policy confronted regional conflicts, terrorism after the September 11 attacks, the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, and, more recently, strategic competition with China and Russia.

Institutions and instruments

Policy is executed through the State Department and embassies worldwide, foreign aid administered by USAID, the armed forces of the Department of Defense, intelligence agencies, and economic tools including trade agreements, sanctions and export controls. The United States maintains one of the world's largest diplomatic networks and military presence, with bases in allied countries across Europe and Asia.
