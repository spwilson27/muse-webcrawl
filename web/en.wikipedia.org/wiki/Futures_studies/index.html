Futures studies

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Futures studies, also called futurology or futuristics, is the systematic study of possible, probable and preferable futures. Rather than predicting a single future, futurists map a range of alternative futures and the worldviews and myths that underlie them, to help organisations and societies make better decisions under uncertainty. Its practitioners generally reject the label of fortune-telling: as the field's textbooks put it, the future is not predictable, but alternative futures can be explored and preferred futures can be influenced.

The "futures cone": possible futures widen with time, narrowing toward the probable and the preferable.

Methods

Core techniques include environmental scanning for weak signals, the Delphi method of iterative expert judgement, trend extrapolation, cross-impact analysis, and especially scenario planning — building internally consistent stories of several contrasting futures, a method developed at RAND and famously applied at Royal Dutch Shell in the 1970s. Other approaches include causal layered analysis, backcasting from a desired future, futures wheels, and participatory workshops. A common framework distinguishes probable, possible, plausible and preferable futures.

History

Systematic futures thinking grew out of Second World War operations research and Cold War strategic studies, notably at the RAND Corporation, where Herman Kahn pioneered scenario methods. The Club of Rome's The Limits to Growth (1972) brought computer world-modelling to public attention. Institutions such as the World Futures Studies Federation (founded 1973), the Institute for the Future in California, the journal Futures (1968–), and graduate programmes at the University of Houston and Tamkang University professionalised the field. Governments including Singapore, Finland and the United Arab Emirates now maintain dedicated foresight units, and strategic foresight is a standard corporate planning function.

Criticism

Futurology is criticised for vague claims, poor predictive records and susceptibility to hype; academic futurists respond that the field's goal is preparedness and better present choices, not prophecy, and point to its formal methods and peer-reviewed literature.
