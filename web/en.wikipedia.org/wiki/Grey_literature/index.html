Grey literature

Grey literature
Technical reports, a classic grey literature form
DefinitionResearch material produced outside commercial publishing channels, not controlled by commercial publishers
ExamplesReports, working papers, theses, conference proceedings, preprints, government documents
Key bodyGreyNet International

Grey literature (or gray literature) is material and research produced by organisations, governments, academics and industry outside the control of commercial publishers — that is, where publishing is not the primary activity of the producing body. A widely used definition from the 1997 Luxembourg convention describes it as information produced "on all levels of government, academics, business and industry in print and electronic formats, but which is not controlled by commercial publishers".

Types and importance

Grey literature includes technical and research reports, working papers, doctoral theses, conference proceedings, preprints, patents, standards, policy documents, newsletters and much government publishing. It is especially important in fast-moving scientific and technical fields, where reports may appear years before journal articles, and in policy research and systematic reviews: evidence syntheses that ignore grey literature risk publication bias, since studies with negative or null results are more likely to remain in report form.

Access and preservation

Because it is not distributed through bookshops or standard journal channels, grey literature is often hard to identify, locate and preserve — it has been called "fugitive" material. Libraries and repositories respond with institutional repositories, subject gateways and services such as OpenGrey (successor to SIGLE), the NTIS database of US government reports, and preprint servers such as arXiv. The growth of open-access repositories and the web has blurred the boundary between grey and formally published literature.
