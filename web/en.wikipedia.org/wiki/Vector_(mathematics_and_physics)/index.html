Vector (mathematics and physics)

In mathematics, physics, and engineering, a vector or Euclidean vector (sometimes called a geometric vector or spatial vector) is a geometric object that has magnitude (or length) and direction. Euclid's Elements already treated line segments with direction in geometry, but the modern concept emerged in the 19th century in the work of Bellavitis, Hamilton, Grassmann, and Gibbs.

Vectors can be added to other vectors according to vector algebra. A Euclidean vector is frequently represented by a directed line segment or graphically as an arrow connecting an initial point to a terminal point. In physics, vectors describe quantities such as displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, and momentum, in contrast to scalars, which have magnitude only.

Notation

Vectors are commonly denoted by boldface type (v), by a letter with an arrow above it (v⃗), or by the endpoints of the representing segment. In a Cartesian coordinate system a vector is given by its components, for example v = (vx, vy, vz).

Basic concepts

The magnitude of a vector v is denoted |v| and computed from its components by the Pythagorean theorem. A unit vector has magnitude one and indicates direction only. Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and direction, regardless of their position.

Operations

  • Addition and subtraction — componentwise, geometrically by the parallelogram or triangle law.
  • Scalar multiplication — scales the magnitude and may reverse the direction.
  • Dot product — a scalar equal to |a||b| cos θ, giving projections and work.
  • Cross product — a vector perpendicular to both factors, with magnitude |a||b| sin θ.

Generalizations

In linear algebra, a vector is any element of a vector space, which may consist of functions, polynomials, or matrices. In physics, the fundamental notion is refined further: a (contravariant) vector is defined by how its components transform under changes of coordinates, distinguishing vectors from pseudovectors and tensors.
