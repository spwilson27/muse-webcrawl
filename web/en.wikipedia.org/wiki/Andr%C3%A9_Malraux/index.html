Georges André Malraux (3 November 1901 – 23 November 1976) was a French novelist, art theorist, and statesman. He served as France's first Minister of Cultural Affairs under President Charles de Gaulle from 1959 to 1969.

Early life

Malraux was born in Paris into a middle-class family. Largely self-educated, he studied Oriental languages and became fascinated by Asian art and archaeology. In the 1920s he travelled to Indochina, where he was arrested for removing Khmer bas-reliefs from the temple of Banteay Srei, an episode that fed his early fiction.

Literary career

His novel La Condition humaine (1933), translated as Man's Fate, set during the Chinese civil war, won the Prix Goncourt and established his reputation. L'Espoir (1937) drew on his experiences organising an air squadron for the Republican side in the Spanish Civil War. His later works on art, including The Voices of Silence (1951), developed the idea of the "museum without walls".

Political career

Malraux joined the French Resistance during the Second World War and later became a close ally of de Gaulle. As Minister of Cultural Affairs he founded the maisons de la culture, promoted the cleaning of Paris landmarks, and championed access to culture across France.

Legacy

Malraux's ashes were transferred to the Panthéon in 1996 in recognition of his contributions to French letters and public life.
