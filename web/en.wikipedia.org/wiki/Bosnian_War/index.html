Bosnian War

The Bosnian War (Serbo-Croatian: Rat u Bosni i Hercegovini) was an international armed conflict that took place in Bosnia and Herzegovina between 1992 and 1995. The war is commonly seen as having started on 6 April 1992, following a number of earlier violent incidents and the international recognition of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina, and ended on 14 December 1995 with the signing of the Dayton Agreement in Paris. The main belligerents were the forces of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina, those of the self-proclaimed Bosnian Serb entity Republika Srpska, and those of the self-proclaimed Croat entity Herzeg-Bosnia, supported respectively from abroad in varying degrees. As Yugoslavia dissolved, Bosnia's three constituent peoples — Bosniaks (Muslims), Serbs and Croats — were drawn into a war over the state's future that became notorious for ethnic cleansing, concentration camps, the 44-month siege of Sarajevo, and the Srebrenica massacre of July 1995, in which more than 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were murdered — an act the international courts have ruled genocide. Roughly 100,000 people were killed (older estimates of 200,000–250,000 have been revised downward by demographic research) and over two million displaced, more than half the pre-war population.

Background

Bosnia and Herzegovina was the most ethnically mixed republic of socialist Yugoslavia — in the 1991 census about 43.5% Muslim (Bosniak), 31% Serb and 17% Croat — with no group holding a majority and centuries of shared life alongside sharp memories of Second World War communal massacres. As Yugoslavia unraveled after Tito's death (1980), republic-level nationalist parties won the first multi-party elections of 1990 and formed an uneasy coalition that collapsed over the question of independence. Slovenia and Croatia seceded in 1991; the Bosnian Serb leadership under Radovan Karadžić, backed by Slobodan Milošević's Serbia, proclaimed Serb autonomous regions and then a Serb republic, and made clear it would not accept an independent Bosnia in which Serbs would be a minority. A referendum on independence (29 February–1 March 1992), boycotted by most Serbs, passed overwhelmingly; the European Community and the United States recognised Bosnia on 6–7 April 1992, by which time Serb forces, built around the local units and heavy weapons of the Yugoslav People's Army, were already seizing territory.

Course of the war

1992: conquest and ethnic cleansing

In the war's first months Bosnian Serb forces, with JNA support, overran some two-thirds of the country, besieged Sarajevo, and expelled or killed the non-Serb population of eastern and northern Bosnia — the towns of Foča, Višegrad, Zvornik and Prijedor became bywords for massacre and camp atrocities; images of emaciated prisoners at Omarska camp in August 1992 shocked the world. The Bosnian government army (ARBiH), hampered by an international arms embargo that froze the Serbs' heavy-weapons advantage, held the cities and enclaves. Croatian forces simultaneously carved out Herzeg-Bosnia in the west and south.

1993–94: the Croat–Bosniak war

In 1993 the Bosniak–Croat alliance collapsed into a second war within the war, fought bitterly in central Bosnia and Mostar, whose Old Bridge was destroyed by Croat shelling in November 1993. Successive international peace plans (Vance–Owen, Owen–Stoltenberg) failed. The February 1994 Markale marketplace massacre in Sarajevo brought NATO's first ultimatums and air strikes; in March 1994 the Washington Agreement ended the Croat–Bosniak war and created the Bosniak–Croat Federation.

1995: Srebrenica, NATO intervention and Dayton

In July 1995 Bosnian Serb forces under Ratko Mladić overran the UN "safe area" of Srebrenica, expelled its women and children, and systematically murdered more than 8,000 captured men and boys — the worst massacre in Europe since 1945. Weeks later Žepa fell too. Croatia's Operation Storm (August 1995) retook the Krajina, Bosnian and Croat offensives rolled back Serb lines in western Bosnia, and NATO's Operation Deliberate Force bombed Bosnian Serb positions after a second Markale massacre. With the map approaching the 51:49 split the Contact Group had proposed, the parties were brought to Dayton, Ohio, where presidents Izetbegović, Milošević and Tuđman initialled a peace on 21 November 1995, signed in Paris on 14 December.

Aftermath

The Dayton constitution preserved Bosnia as one state composed of two entities — the Federation and Republika Srpska — with a tripartite presidency, and entrenched ethnic power-sharing that still structures (and deadlocks) Bosnian politics. A NATO-led force of 60,000 (IFOR, then SFOR, then the EU's EUFOR) kept the peace; refugees returned in large numbers to some areas but ethnic cleansing's demographic results largely endured. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) in The Hague tried the war's leadership: Karadžić and Mladić were eventually arrested (2008 and 2011) and convicted of genocide and crimes against humanity, as Milošević died during his trial in 2006. The war's legacy — Srebrenica above all, commemorated every 11 July — remains the central fact of Bosnia's public life and the defining European failure of the post-Cold-War decade.
