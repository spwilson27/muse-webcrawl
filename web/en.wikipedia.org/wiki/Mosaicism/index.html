Mosaicism

Mosaicism (or genetic mosaicism) is a condition arising from a genetic mutation present in only some of the cells of a multicellular organism. A mosaic individual is composed of two or more genetically distinct cell populations derived from a single zygote, in contrast to a chimera, whose cell lines originate from more than one zygote. Depending on when the mutation occurs in development, mosaicism may affect somatic cells, germ cells, or both.

Types

Somatic mosaicism

Somatic mosaicism arises from a post-zygotic mutation and is transmitted only to the daughter cells of the affected cell. It can produce partial or localised manifestations of a genetic disorder, as in mosaic forms of McCune–Albright syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1 presenting segmentally, and mosaic pigmentary disorders following the lines of Blaschko. Post-zygotic mutations are also a common driver of cancer: many tumours are, in effect, expanding mosaics of a mutated clone within normal tissue.

Germline mosaicism

In germline (gonadal) mosaicism, the mutation is confined to the egg or sperm precursor cells. A parent who is not affected by a genetic condition can nevertheless have more than one affected child, because some of the parent's gametes carry the mutation.

Chromosomal mosaicism

Mosaic aneuploidies — for example mosaic trisomy 21 in a proportion of people with Down syndrome, or mosaic Turner syndrome (45,X/46,XX) — often produce milder phenotypes than the full chromosomal abnormality. Confined placental mosaicism, detected at chorionic villus sampling, involves discrepancy between the chromosome constitution of the placenta and the fetus.

Detection

Mosaicism may be detected by karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridisation, chromosomal microarray, or next-generation sequencing, though low-level mosaicism can be missed when the sampled tissue does not contain the affected cell line.
