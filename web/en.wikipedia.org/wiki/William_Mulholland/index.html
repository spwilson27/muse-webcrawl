William Mulholland (11 September 1855 – 22 July 1935) was an Irish-American self-taught civil engineer who was responsible for building the infrastructure to provide water to Los Angeles. As the head of the city's Department of Water and Power he designed and supervised construction of the Los Angeles Aqueduct, a 233-mile (375 km) system that carries water from the Owens Valley to Los Angeles, completed in 1913.

Mulholland's career effectively ended with the catastrophic failure of his St. Francis Dam in 1928, twelve hours after he had personally inspected it; the flood killed at least 431 people and remains the second-deadliest disaster in California history.

Early life

Born in Belfast, Mulholland emigrated to New York in 1872 and arrived in San Pedro, California in 1877, taking work as a ditch-tender for the private Los Angeles City Water Company. Largely self-educated in engineering and mathematics, he rose to superintendent of the company and, when the city took the water system public in 1902, became chief engineer of the new Bureau of Water Works.

Los Angeles water

Mulholland foresaw that the Los Angeles River could not supply a growing city. With the support of Mayor Fred Eaton, who had scouted the Owens River in the Sierra Nevada, he proposed a gravity-fed aqueduct over 200 miles long — then the largest municipal water project in the United States.

The aqueduct and the Owens Valley

Construction ran from 1908 to 1913 with a workforce of some 5,000. At the opening on 5 November 1913 Mulholland famously told the crowd, "There it is. Take it!" The diversion of the Owens River desiccated Owens Lake and embittered valley farmers, whose resistance in the 1920s — the California Water Wars — included dynamiting the aqueduct. The episode inspired the 1974 film Chinatown.

St. Francis Dam disaster

The St. Francis Dam in San Francisquito Canyon, completed in 1926, failed just before midnight on 12 March 1928. A jury of inquiry found an unforeseeable geological weakness, but Mulholland accepted full responsibility, telling investigators he envied the dead. He retired later that year, a broken man.

Legacy

Mulholland Drive and Mulholland Highway in Los Angeles bear his name. His aqueduct made modern Los Angeles possible; his name remains inseparable from both the city's growth and its worst engineering disaster.
