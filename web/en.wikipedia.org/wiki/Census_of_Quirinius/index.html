Census of Quirinius

Registration for a Roman census, as depicted in later art.

The Census of Quirinius was a Roman census of the province of Judaea taken in 6 CE by Publius Sulpicius Quirinius, the governor of Syria, after Judaea came under direct Roman rule following the deposition of Herod Archelaus. It is mentioned by the historian Josephus and in the Gospel of Luke, which associates the census with the birth of Jesus.

Historical background

The census served taxation purposes and provoked resentment; Judas of Galilee led a revolt against it, an episode Josephus credits with seeding the movement that culminated in the First Jewish–Roman War. Josephus dates the census to about 37 years after the Battle of Actium, corresponding to 6 CE.

The dating problem

Luke 2 places Jesus's birth "when Quirinius was governor of Syria" and during the reign of Herod the Great, who died in 4 BCE — roughly a decade before Quirinius's census. This discrepancy, sometimes called the "Quirinius problem," has generated extensive scholarly debate. Proposed resolutions include that Quirinius held an earlier command in Syria, that Luke's Greek could mean a census "before" Quirinius's governorship, or that Luke simply erred. The mainstream historical view treats the two accounts as difficult to reconcile.
