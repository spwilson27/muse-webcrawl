Functional finance

Functional finance is a heterodox macroeconomic theory developed by Russian-born economist Abba P. Lerner in the 1940s. It holds that government fiscal policy — spending, taxation, borrowing, and money creation — should be judged solely by its effects ("functions") on the economy, principally maintaining full employment and price stability, and not by whether the budget is balanced in any given period.

Principles

Lerner's rules state that the government should: (1) adjust total spending — by spending more or taxing less when demand is too low, and doing the reverse when demand threatens inflation — so as to keep aggregate demand at the level that buys all output producible at current prices; (2) borrow or repay (lend) so as to set the interest rate that produces the most desirable level of investment; and (3) print, hold, or destroy money as needed to carry out the first two rules. On this view, taxes exist not to "pay for" spending but to withdraw purchasing power and control inflation, and the national debt is not comparable to household debt for a currency-issuing state.

Influence

Functional finance grew out of Lerner's reading of Keynes and influenced post-Keynesian economics and, later, modern monetary theory, which adopts many of its propositions. Mainstream critics object that the approach understates inflation dynamics, expectations, and political constraints on timely fiscal adjustment.
