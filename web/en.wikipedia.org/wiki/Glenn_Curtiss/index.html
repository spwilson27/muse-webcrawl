Glenn Curtiss

Glenn Hammond Curtiss (May 21, 1878 – July 23, 1930) was an American aviation and motorcycling pioneer, and the founder of the U.S. aircraft industry. A rival of the Wright brothers, he made the first officially witnessed flight in North America of a heavier-than-air machine in public view, won the Scientific American trophy in 1908, pioneered naval aviation with the first take-off from a warship (flown by Eugene Ely in an aircraft Curtiss built), and designed the famous Curtiss JN-4 "Jenny" trainer of the First World War.

Early life and motorcycles

Curtiss was born in Hammondsport, New York. He began his career as a bicycle racer and builder, then turned to motorcycles, founding a company that produced some of the fastest machines of the era; in 1907 he set an unofficial world speed record of 136.36 mph on a V-8-powered motorcycle at Ormond Beach, Florida, earning the title "fastest man on Earth". His lightweight V-8 engine attracted the attention of balloonist Thomas Scott Baldwin and led Curtiss into aeronautics.

Aviation career

In 1907 Curtiss joined Alexander Graham Bell's Aerial Experiment Association, where he helped design the June Bug, which he flew on July 4, 1908 to win the Scientific American trophy for the first public kilometre flight in America. The Wright brothers sued him for patent infringement over wing-warping; Curtiss used ailerons instead, and the resulting litigation dominated his career until the patents were pooled during the First World War.

Curtiss won the first Gordon Bennett Cup race at Reims in 1909 and made the first long-distance flight between Albany and New York City in 1910. He developed the flying boat (the Model F and the large H-series), sold the first aircraft to the U.S. Navy, and trained many early naval aviators. His Curtiss Aeroplane and Motor Company became one of the largest aircraft manufacturers of the war; in 1929 it merged with Wright Aeronautical to form Curtiss-Wright.

Later life

In the 1920s Curtiss turned to Florida real estate, developing the cities of Miami Springs and Hialeah and the resort of Opa-locka. He died in 1930 in Buffalo, New York, following an operation for appendicitis.
