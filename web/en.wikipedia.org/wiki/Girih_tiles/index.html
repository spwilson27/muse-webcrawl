Girih tiles are a set of five tiles used in Islamic geometric tiling to create intricate patterns for the decoration of buildings. The name comes from the Persian word gereh (گره), meaning "knot". The five tiles — a regular decagon, an elongated hexagon, a bowtie, a rhombus and a regular pentagon — all have angles that are multiples of 36°, and each is decorated with strapwork lines that cross the edges at standard positions, allowing the straps to continue unbroken across tile boundaries.

Use and history

Girih patterns were constructed with compass and straightedge in earlier centuries, but by about 1200 the tile-based method appeared, enabling far more complex designs. Outstanding examples survive in Persia and Anatolia, including the Darb-e Imam shrine in Isfahan (1453), whose patterns display near-perfect quasicrystalline order.

Quasicrystal connection

In 2007 physicists Peter J. Steinhardt and Paul Lu showed that girih tilings on medieval Islamic buildings embody the same mathematics as modern quasicrystals: by subdividing the tiles into smaller copies of themselves, artisans could generate self-similar patterns equivalent to Penrose tiling, some five centuries before those ideas were formalised in the West.
