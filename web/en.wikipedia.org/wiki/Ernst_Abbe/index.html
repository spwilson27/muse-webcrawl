Ernst Abbe

Ernst Karl Abbe
Born: 23 January 1840, Eisenach, Germany
Died: 14 January 1905 (aged 64), Jena, Germany
Fields: Physics, optics, astronomy
Known for: Abbe number; Abbe sine condition; Zeiss foundation reforms

Ernst Karl Abbe (23 January 1840 – 14 January 1905) was a German physicist, optical scientist, entrepreneur, and social reformer. Together with Otto Schott and Carl Zeiss, he laid the foundation of modern optics and developed the microscope to its theoretical limits. As Zeiss's successor he created the Carl Zeiss Foundation, an early model of socially responsible industrial ownership.

Optics

Abbe joined Zeiss's Jena workshop in 1866 and put microscope design on a scientific basis, calculating lens systems mathematically rather than by trial and error. In 1873 he published his theory of image formation in the microscope, deriving the diffraction limit on resolution — the Abbe limit, d = λ/(2·NA) — and the Abbe sine condition for aplanatic imaging. He introduced the Abbe condenser, the apochromatic objective, and the Abbe number (V-number), which measures a glass's dispersion and remains a standard specification for optical materials. With glassmaker Otto Schott, he developed new optical glasses that made these designs manufacturable. Abbe also invented the Abbe refractometer, still used for measuring refractive index.

Industrial and social reform

After Carl Zeiss's death, Abbe transferred ownership of the Zeiss and Schott works to the Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung (Carl Zeiss Foundation) in 1889–1891, endowing research and workers' welfare. The foundation's statutes, drafted by Abbe, guaranteed employees rights far ahead of their time, including an eight-hour day (introduced in 1900), paid holidays, pensions, and profit-sharing. A crater on the Moon and the asteroid 5224 Abbe are named in his honour.
