Phyllosilicate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Muscovite mica, a sheet silicate.

Phyllosilicates, or sheet silicates, are a subclass of silicate minerals characterised by parallel sheets of silicate tetrahedra. The name comes from the Greek phyllon, "leaf". Each tetrahedron shares three of its four oxygen atoms with neighbouring tetrahedra, giving a sheet structure with a silicon-to-oxygen ratio of 2:5. The sheets are stacked and held together by cations and weaker interlayer forces, which is why phyllosilicates tend to have one direction of perfect cleavage and flaky or platy habits.

Principal groups

  • Micas — muscovite, biotite, phlogopite, lepidolite.
  • Clay minerals — kaolinite, smectites (such as montmorillonite), illite, vermiculite.
  • Serpentine group — antigorite, chrysotile, lizardite.
  • Chlorite group and others such as talc and pyrophyllite.

Structurally, phyllosilicates combine tetrahedral sheets (T) with octahedral sheets (O) of aluminium or magnesium hydroxides, in 1:1 (T-O, e.g. kaolinite) or 2:1 (T-O-T, e.g. micas and smectites) arrangements. Clay minerals are of enormous practical importance: they dominate soils and sediments, determine soil plasticity and cation exchange, and are used in ceramics, drilling muds, cat litter, and industry. Swelling clays such as bentonite can absorb many times their weight in water.
