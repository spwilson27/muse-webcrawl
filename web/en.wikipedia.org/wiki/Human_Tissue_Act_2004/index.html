The Human Tissue Act 2004 (c. 30) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that regulates the removal, storage, use and disposal of human bodies, organs and tissue. It created the Human Tissue Authority (HTA) to license and inspect organisations that store and use human tissue, and it made appropriate consent the fundamental legal requirement for such activities.

Background

The Act was a direct response to the organ-retention scandals of the late 1990s, above all the revelations at Alder Hey Children's Hospital in Liverpool and Bristol Royal Infirmary, where organs and tissue from deceased children had been retained for research and teaching without parents' knowledge. The Kennedy and Redfern inquiries, and the census of retained organs led by the Chief Medical Officer, recommended a comprehensive statutory framework, replacing the patchy Human Tissue Act 1961, Anatomy Act 1984 and Human Organ Transplants Act 1989 (the latter two the 2004 Act repealed in its extent).

Provisions

The Act applies to "relevant material" — material consisting of or including human cells, with exceptions including gametes and embryos (regulated separately), hair and nails from the living, and cell lines. Its main elements include:

  • Consent: scheduled purposes — including post-mortem examination, anatomical examination, research, transplantation, education and public display — require "appropriate consent" from the person concerned, a nominated representative, or a person in a qualifying relationship.
  • Licensing: storage and use of relevant material for scheduled purposes must take place on HTA-licensed premises (with exceptions, e.g. specific research arrangements), under a designated individual.
  • Offences: DNA analysis without consent ("DNA theft") is an offence under section 45; trafficking in human organs and live-donor transplants without HTA approval are also offences.
  • Coroners and museums: special provisions govern tissue retained after coroners' post-mortems and allow national museums to de-accession human remains.

Effect and later changes

The Act largely extends to England, Wales and Northern Ireland; Scotland has its own Human Tissue (Scotland) Act 2006. Wales later moved organ donation to a deemed-consent ("opt-out") system under the Human Transplantation (Wales) Act 2013, followed by England in 2020 under the Organ Donation (Deemed Consent) Act 2019, which amended the 2004 Act's consent rules for transplantation. The 2004 Act remains the core statute governing human tissue research, pathology archives and post-mortem practice.
