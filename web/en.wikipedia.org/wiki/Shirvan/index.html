Shirvan

Shirvan (Persian: شروان, Azerbaijani: Şirvan) is a historical region in the eastern Caucasus, on the western shore of the Caspian Sea, in what is now the Republic of Azerbaijan. In its narrower sense it denotes the territory between the Kura River to the south and the city of Derbent to the north, encompassing the Shirvan plain and the southeastern slopes of the Greater Caucasus. Historically it was also known in part as part of Caucasian Albania in antiquity.

History

Shirvan emerged as a distinct polity under the Shirvanshahs, a dynasty of Arab origin that became Persianized and ruled the region, with interruptions, from the 9th century until 1538. The Shirvanshahs maintained their capital first at Yazidiyya (later Shamakhi) and later at Baku, and their state became a notable center of Persian culture in the Caucasus; the poets Khaqani and Nizami Ganjavi were associated with its court milieu. The Palace of the Shirvanshahs in Baku's Old City is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The region endured repeated invasions — by Rus' raiders from the Caspian, Seljuks, Mongols, and Timur — yet the Shirvanshah state survived through diplomacy until Shah Tahmasp I of Safavid Persia annexed it in 1538. Thereafter Shirvan was a Safavid province (beylerbeylik) contested with the Ottoman Empire, and its silk production and trade were economically significant. A semi-independent Shirvan Khanate existed in the 18th century before the territory was ceded to the Russian Empire by the Treaty of Gulistan (1813) and Treaty of Turkmenchay (1828).

Modern usage

In modern Azerbaijan the name survives in the city of Shirvan (formerly Ali Bayramli) and the Shirvan National Park, known for its goitered gazelles. Shirvan is also renowned for its carpet-weaving tradition, part of the Azerbaijani school of carpet making.
