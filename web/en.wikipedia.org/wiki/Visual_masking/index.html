Visual masking

In backward masking a briefly presented target is rendered hard to see by a following mask

Visual masking is a phenomenon in visual perception in which the visibility of one stimulus, the target, is reduced by the presence of another stimulus, the mask. Masking is studied by measuring a subject's ability to detect or identify the target as the spatial and temporal relationship between target and mask is varied. It has been an important tool for probing how quickly visual information is processed and for distinguishing feedforward from recurrent processing in the visual system.

Types

In simultaneous masking the target and mask appear together, while in forward masking the mask precedes the target and in backward masking the mask follows it. Backward masking is the most striking: a target flashed for a few tens of milliseconds can be rendered almost invisible by a mask presented up to about 100 ms later. The timing between stimuli is called the stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA); visibility typically follows a U-shaped function of SOA. Masks may share the target's location (pattern masking, using a structured pattern or noise) or surround it (metacontrast), where suppression is strongest when the mask's contours abut, but do not overlap, the target's.

Mechanisms and uses

Explanations involve interruption of the target's neural processing, integration of target and mask signals, and inhibition between channels in early visual cortex and beyond. Masking paradigms underpin research on unconscious perception and the neural correlates of consciousness, since a masked target may fail to reach awareness while still influencing behaviour (priming). Clinical and applied uses include studying dyslexia and schizophrenia, and the tachistoscopic assessment of recognition speed.
