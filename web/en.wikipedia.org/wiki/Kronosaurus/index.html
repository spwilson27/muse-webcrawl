Kronosaurus (meaning "lizard of Kronos") is an extinct genus of short-necked pliosaurid plesiosaur that lived during the Early Cretaceous period (Aptian to Albian stages) in what is now Australia and South America. It was one of the largest pliosaurs ever discovered and one of the apex marine predators of its time.

Discovery and species

The genus was named in 1924 by Albert Heber Longman based on a fragmentary mandible from the Toolebuc Formation near Hughenden, Queensland. The species name queenslandicus references its source. The most famous specimen is the near-complete skeleton recovered by a Harvard University expedition in 1931–32 near Richmond, Queensland; after years of preparation it was mounted at the Harvard Museum of Comparative Zoology in 1959, funded in part by the philanthropist Godfrey Lowell Cabot. The mounted skeleton's length was long overstated because the reconstruction included too many vertebrae; modern estimates are lower.

A second species, K. boyacensis, was described in 1992 from the Paja Formation of Villa de Leyva, Colombia (originally as a distinct form, and its assignment to Kronosaurus has been debated). Fossil material also comes from the Wallumbilla Formation of New South Wales.

Description

Kronosaurus was a large pliosaurid with a massive, robust skull reaching over 2.4 metres in the largest individuals, armed with large conical teeth. Body length estimates for mature individuals range from about 9 to 10.9 metres (30–36 ft), with weight estimates up to roughly 11–20 tonnes. Like other pliosaurs, it had four large paddle-like flippers, a short tail and a short neck of around 12–13 vertebrae. The Harvard skeleton is about 12.8 m long as mounted, but this includes an excessive number of vertebral casts.

Palaeobiology

The inland Eromanga Sea that covered much of Cretaceous Australia was home to Kronosaurus, which preyed on fish, squid-like cephalopods, turtles, ichthyosaurs and smaller plesiosaurs; stomach contents and bite-marked fossils attest to this diet. Tooth marks on the skull of the Australian elasmosaur Woolungasaurus and a juvenile Kronosaurus skull bearing bite marks suggest it both hunted large marine reptiles and faced attack from its own kind.
