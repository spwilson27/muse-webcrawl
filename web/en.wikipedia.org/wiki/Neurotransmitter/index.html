Neurotransmitter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A neurotransmitter is a signalling molecule secreted by a neuron that carries a message across a synapse to another neuron, a muscle cell or a gland cell. The concept dates to the 1920s, when Otto Loewi demonstrated chemical transmission in the frog heart (his "Vagusstoff" was acetylcholine) and Henry Dale mapped acetylcholine's actions.

Major neurotransmitters

The principal excitatory transmitter of the brain is glutamate; the principal inhibitory one is GABA. The monoamines — dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline and histamine — modulate mood, arousal, reward and sleep. Acetylcholine drives muscle contraction and attention; its loss is a feature of Alzheimer's disease. Neuropeptides (endorphins, oxytocin, substance P) and even gases (nitric oxide) also serve as transmitters, blurring the classic criteria.

After release, transmitters act on receptors — fast ion channels or slower G-protein-coupled receptors — and are cleared by reuptake pumps or enzymes, the very steps targeted by most psychiatric and neurological drugs, from SSRIs (serotonin reuptake) to Parkinson's therapies. The electrical counterpart of this chemical signalling is the action potential that triggers release.
