Golden Age

The term Golden Age comes from Greek mythology and Roman literature, where it described the first and best of the ages of man — a primordial era of peace, harmony, stability and abundance in which humans lived without labour or sorrow. The concept subsequently became a general metaphor for any period of exceptional flourishing in a civilisation, art form, industry or field of endeavour.

Mythological origin

The Greek poet Hesiod, in Works and Days (c. 700 BC), described five successive ages of humanity — Gold, Silver, Bronze, Heroic and Iron — degenerating from an original golden race that lived under Cronus "like gods, with carefree hearts". The Roman poet Ovid retold the scheme in the Metamorphoses, adding that in the Golden Age the earth produced food without cultivation, spring was eternal, and there were no laws, wars or seafaring. The myth is often associated with the Garden of Eden and other paradise traditions.

Historical and cultural uses

Historians and critics have applied the label to many periods, including the Golden Age of Athens under Pericles (5th century BC), the Dutch Golden Age of the 17th century, the Spanish Golden Age (Siglo de Oro) of arts and literature, the Islamic Golden Age of science and scholarship, the Golden Age of Piracy (c. 1650–1730), the Golden Age of Hollywood cinema (c. 1910s–1960s), the Golden Age of Comic Books (c. 1938–1956), and the Golden Age of Television. The term is generally retrospective and often idealised, contrasting a past peak with a presumed present decline.
