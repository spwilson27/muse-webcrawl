Date10 October 1911 – 12 February 1912

ResultFall of the Qing dynasty; establishment of the Republic of China

The Xinhai Revolution (Chinese: 辛亥革命), also known as the Chinese Revolution of 1911, was the revolution that overthrew China's last imperial dynasty, the Qing, and established the Republic of China. It is named after the Xinhai year (1911) in the traditional Chinese sexagenary calendar.

Background

By 1900 the Qing dynasty was weakened by military defeats, indemnities, internal rebellion and a widely perceived failure to modernise. Revolutionary groups, notably Sun Yat-sen's Tongmenghui, agitated for a republic, while the court's own reforms — including the New Army and the nationalisation of railways in 1911, which provoked the Railway Protection Movement in Sichuan — created the conditions for revolt.

Wuchang Uprising

On 10 October 1911, New Army soldiers in Wuchang (now part of Wuhan) mutinied after a bomb accidentally exploded at a revolutionary safe house, exposing their plans. The uprising seized the city within days, and over the following weeks province after province declared independence from the Qing.

Founding of the Republic

Sun Yat-sen, returning from abroad, was elected provisional president of the Republic of China in Nanjing on 1 January 1912. To secure national unity and the allegiance of the Beiyang Army, Sun soon yielded the presidency to the strongman Yuan Shikai, who negotiated the abdication of the last emperor, Puyi, on 12 February 1912, ending more than two thousand years of imperial rule in China.

Legacy

The revolution is commemorated in both the People's Republic of China and the Republic of China on Taiwan; 10 October is celebrated as Double Ten Day, the national day of the Republic of China.
