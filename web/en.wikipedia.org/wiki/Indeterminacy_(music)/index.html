Indeterminacy (music)

Indeterminacy is a composing approach in which some aspects of a musical work are left open to chance or to the interpreter's free choice. The term is most closely associated with the American composer John Cage, who used chance operations — notably the I Ching — to compose works such as Music of Changes (1951), and who defined an experimental action as one "the outcome of which is not foreseen". Indeterminacy is related to, but distinct from, aleatoric music (from Latin alea, dice), a term favoured by European composers such as Pierre Boulez.

History

Early precedents include the Musikalisches Würfelspiel (musical dice games) of the 18th century attributed to composers including Mozart, in which pre-written measures were assembled by dice rolls, and the mobile-form experiments of Charles Ives. The decisive development came in the early 1950s with Cage and the circle sometimes called the New York School: Cage, Morton Feldman, Earle Brown and Christian Wolff. Feldman's Projection series (1950–51) used graph notation specifying register and duration but not exact pitches; Brown's December 1952 is an entirely abstract graphic score.

Types of indeterminacy

Cage distinguished indeterminacy in composition (using chance procedures to fix a score, as in Music of Changes) from indeterminacy in performance, where the work's identity is not fixed between performances — as in his Variations series or Wolff's prose scores, which specify activities rather than sounds. Cage's most famous work, 4′33″ (1952), frames whatever ambient sounds occur during its duration, pushing indeterminacy toward the sounds of the environment itself.

Open form and European aleatoricism

In Europe, Boulez's Third Piano Sonata (1955–57) allowed the performer to choose the order of movements ("mobile form"), and Karlheinz Stockhausen's Klavierstück XI (1956) presents fragments the pianist navigates freely. Witold Lutosławski developed "limited aleatorism", in which performers repeat short cells unsynchronised within otherwise fully composed structures.
