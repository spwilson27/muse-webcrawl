Crème brûlée

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A crème brûlée with a glossy amber caramel crust being cracked by a spoon]

Crème brûlée is a dessert of baked vanilla custard chilled beneath a thin shell of caramelised sugar, served in a shallow ramekin so that every spoonful carries the maximum of its defining contrast: cold, silken cream against warm, bitter, shattering caramel. French for "burnt cream", it has at least three homelands — France claims the first printed recipe (François Massialot, 1691); Trinity College, Cambridge claims "Trinity cream", made there by the 17th century and branded with the college arms; and Catalonia claims the older crema catalana, flavoured with citrus and cinnamon for St Joseph's Day.

History and revival

Burnt creams simmered in great-house kitchens for centuries before restaurants claimed them. The modern crème brûlée is a creature of the 1980s: New York's Le Cirque put it on the menu — chef Dieter Schorner's version, championed by owner Sirio Maccioni — and it became the decade's obligatory dessert, spreading to every ambitious restaurant in the Western world. Ubiquity bred parodies (the Amélie heroine's small pleasure of cracking the crust with a spoon is the affectionate version), but the dish survives fashion because the contrast is genuinely perfect.

Method

The custard — cream, yolks, sugar, vanilla — bakes in a water bath until just set with a wobble, then chills. The crust is made at the last moment: a dusting of sugar, melted to amber with a blowtorch (the modern tool), a salamander iron or a fierce grill. Too thin and it cracks like ice; too thick and it eats like toffee. Flavoured variants ( coffee, lavender, matcha, chocolate) fill bistro menus, but the plain vanilla original, ideally speckled with real vanilla seed, remains the test piece.
