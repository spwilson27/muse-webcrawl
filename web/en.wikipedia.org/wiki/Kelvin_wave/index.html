Kelvin wave

An equatorial Kelvin wave travels eastward, trapped near the equator.

A Kelvin wave is a large-scale wave in the ocean or atmosphere that balances the Coriolis force against a topographic boundary such as a coastline, or against a waveguide such as the equator. Named after Lord Kelvin, who first described coastal Kelvin waves, these waves are non-dispersive and propagate with the boundary on their right in the Northern Hemisphere (left in the Southern Hemisphere).

Coastal Kelvin waves

Coastal Kelvin waves are trapped against shorelines, decaying exponentially away from the coast over the Rossby radius of deformation. They propagate cyclonically around ocean basins and play a role in tidal dynamics and in the adjustment of currents to wind changes.

Equatorial Kelvin waves

At the equator, where the Coriolis parameter changes sign, eastward-propagating Kelvin waves are trapped in an equatorial waveguide. In the Pacific Ocean they travel along the thermocline in a matter of weeks to months and are a central mechanism of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation: downwelling Kelvin waves deepen the thermocline off South America and help trigger El Niño warming, while upwelling waves contribute to La Niña. Atmospheric Kelvin waves are likewise important in tropical convection and the Madden–Julian oscillation.
