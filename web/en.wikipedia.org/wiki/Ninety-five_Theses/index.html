The Ninety-five Theses, formally the Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences, is a list of propositions for academic debate written in 1517 by Martin Luther, a professor of theology at the University of Wittenberg. They are conventionally regarded as the starting point of the Protestant Reformation and the beginning of Protestantism.

Background

Luther was protesting against the sale of indulgences — certificates said to reduce punishment for sins — preached in neighbouring territories by the Dominican friar Johann Tetzel to raise funds for rebuilding St. Peter's Basilica in Rome and for Archbishop Albert of Mainz's debts. Luther objected particularly to the slogan attributed to Tetzel, "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs", and to claims that indulgences freed souls from purgatory without repentance.

Publication

On 31 October 1517 Luther sent the theses, written in Latin, to Archbishop Albert with a letter of protest. Tradition holds that he also nailed them to the door of All Saints' Church (the Castle Church) in Wittenberg, which served as a university notice-board; historians debate whether the famous nailing actually occurred. Thanks to the printing press, the theses were reprinted and translated into German, spreading across Europe within weeks — a landmark early use of print for mass religious controversy.

Content

The theses open: "When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said 'Repent,' he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance." They deny that the Pope can remit guilt (as opposed to penalties he himself imposed), question the existence of a "treasury of merit" at the Pope's disposal, and urge Christians to follow Christ through suffering rather than seeking false security through indulgences. Luther did not yet deny papal authority outright; the document was framed as an invitation to debate.

Consequences

The dispute escalated through the Heidelberg Disputation (1518), the Leipzig Debate (1519) and Luther's 1520 treatises, culminating in his excommunication (1521) and defence at the Diet of Worms. Reformation Day is still commemorated on 31 October, and the 500th anniversary in 2017 was marked across Germany and the Protestant world.
