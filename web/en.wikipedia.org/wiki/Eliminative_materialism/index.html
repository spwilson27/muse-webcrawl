Eliminative materialism

Eliminative materialism (or eliminativism) is a materialist position in the philosophy of mind arguing that the common-sense understanding of the mind—"folk psychology"—is a false theory that will be entirely replaced by neuroscience as the science of the brain matures. On this view, mental-state concepts such as belief, desire, and pain are not reduced to neural states but eliminated, much as the concepts of phlogiston, caloric fluid, and demonic possession were eliminated from science rather than identified with anything real.

Arguments

Paul Feyerabend and Richard Rorty raised early versions of the view, and it was developed most fully by Paul and Patricia Churchland. Eliminativists argue that folk psychology is a stagnant research program: it has made little progress in explaining sleep, memory, mental illness, or learning, and shows no sign of integrating with the rest of science. Patricia Churchland's Neurophilosophy (1986) and Paul Churchland's Scientific Realism and the Plasticity of Mind (1979) and A Neurocomputational Perspective (1989) contend that a completed neuroscience would give a more accurate taxonomy of cognition, in terms of activation vectors and neural networks, than the sentence-like "propositional attitudes" of everyday psychology.

Criticism

Critics object that eliminativism is self-refuting—asserting that there are no beliefs seems itself to express a belief—and that folk psychology is far too predictively successful in daily life to be a false theory. Defenders reply that the objection begs the question, since the vocabulary in which the criticism is framed is precisely what is in dispute, and that once-successful frameworks (such as the Ptolemaic astronomy of everyday appearances) have been abandoned before. Jerry Fodor and others have defended the autonomy and reality of belief–desire explanation, while philosophers such as Terence Horgan and John Tienson have sought middle positions.
