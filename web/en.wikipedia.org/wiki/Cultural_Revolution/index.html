The Cultural Revolution, formally the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution, was a sociopolitical movement in the People's Republic of China launched by Mao Zedong in May 1966 and lasting until his death in 1976. Its stated goal was to preserve Chinese communism by purging remnants of capitalist and traditional elements from society and to reassert Mao's authority within the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) after the failures of the Great Leap Forward.

Course of events

Mao mobilised China's youth into Red Guard groups, who attacked teachers, officials, and the "Four Olds"—old ideas, culture, customs, and habits—destroying countless historical and cultural artefacts. Party leaders such as Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping were purged; Liu died in detention in 1969. By 1967–1968 factional fighting among Red Guards and widespread chaos forced Mao to deploy the People's Liberation Army to restore order, and millions of urban youth were "sent down" to the countryside. The Ninth Party Congress in 1969 named Lin Biao as Mao's successor, but Lin died in a plane crash in 1971 while allegedly fleeing after a failed coup attempt. The radical Gang of Four, led by Mao's wife Jiang Qing, rose in influence in the final years.

Human and cultural cost

Estimates of deaths from violence, persecution, and famine-adjacent hardship range from hundreds of thousands to around two million, and tens of millions of people were persecuted. Schools and universities were closed or disrupted for years, and a generation's education was lost. Economic output suffered, and China's cultural heritage sustained severe losses.

Aftermath

After Mao's death in September 1976, Hua Guofeng had the Gang of Four arrested, effectively ending the Cultural Revolution. In 1981 the CCP's official resolution judged it "a grave blunder" that had caused "the most severe setback and the heaviest losses" since 1949, while stopping short of fully repudiating Mao. Under Deng Xiaoping, China turned toward the reform and opening up policies.
