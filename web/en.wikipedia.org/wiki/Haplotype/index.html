Haplotype

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A haplotype is a set of alleles at linked loci inherited together on one chromosome.

A haplotype (from Greek haploos, "single, simple") is a group of alleles in an organism that are inherited together from a single parent: specifically, a set of DNA variations, or polymorphisms, that tend to be inherited together along a single chromosome. The term was coined by the immunogeneticist Ruggero Ceppellini in 1967 to describe combinations of HLA alleles. A haplogroup is a group of similar haplotypes sharing a common ancestor.

Definition and use

Because recombination between homologous chromosomes is relatively rare over short distances, alleles at neighbouring loci are usually transmitted as a block; the statistical association between them is called linkage disequilibrium. The haplotype of an individual at a region can be written as the sequence of alleles on one of the two homologous chromosomes, for example A–B–c–d.

Haplotypes are fundamental to human genetics. The International HapMap Project (2002–2010) catalogued common haplotype patterns across human populations, enabling genome-wide association studies in which a few "tag SNPs" capture the variation of an entire block. Haplotype phasing — determining which variants lie on the same chromosome — is a standard step in modern sequence analysis.

Applications

  • Ancestry and forensics: Haplotypes of the non-recombining Y chromosome and of mitochondrial DNA are inherited from one parent only and are used to trace paternal and maternal lineages and define haplogroups.
  • Medicine: HLA haplotypes determine tissue compatibility in transplantation and are associated with autoimmune diseases.
  • Population genetics: Shared haplotype tracts (identity by descent) reveal relatedness, migration and selection, including selective sweeps around advantageous mutations.
  • Agriculture: Haplotype maps guide marker-assisted breeding in crops and livestock.

Haplotype block and resolution

In humans, the genome is organised into "haplotype blocks" of high linkage disequilibrium separated by recombination hotspots. Statistical methods such as the EM algorithm estimate population haplotype frequencies from unphased genotype data, while laboratory methods (cloning, long-read sequencing, or family trios) can resolve phase directly.
