Hertz

The hertz (symbol: Hz) is the unit of frequency in the International System of Units (SI), defined as one event (or cycle) per second. It is named after the German physicist Heinrich Hertz, who in the 1880s provided conclusive experimental proof of the existence of electromagnetic waves. The hertz is an SI derived unit; in SI base units it is equal to one per second (s−1).

Definition

The hertz is defined as one cycle per second. If an event repeats with a period T seconds, its frequency is 1/T hertz. The unit may be used with any SI prefix; common multiples include the kilohertz (kHz, 103 Hz), megahertz (MHz, 106 Hz), gigahertz (GHz, 109 Hz), and terahertz (THz, 1012 Hz).

History

The name was established by the International Electrotechnical Commission in 1930 in honour of Heinrich Hertz, and the unit was adopted by the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) in 1960, replacing the previous name "cycles per second" and its multiples. The term "cycles per second" remained common in electronics into the 1970s.

Applications

The hertz is used to describe the frequencies of sound waves — human hearing spans roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz — the frequencies of radio and television transmissions, the wavelengths of visible light (on the order of hundreds of terahertz), and the clock rates of computer processors, which since the early 2000s have typically run at several gigahertz. Alternating current for domestic electricity is supplied at 50 Hz in most of the world and 60 Hz in the Americas. Higher rates of periodic events that are not sinusoidal oscillations, such as processor instructions or frames drawn by a display, are also commonly expressed in hertz.
