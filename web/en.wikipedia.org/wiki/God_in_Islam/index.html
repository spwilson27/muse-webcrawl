God in Islam

In Islam, God (Arabic: الله, Allāh) is the one, absolute and incomparable creator and sustainer of the universe. The central doctrine of Islam is tawhid — the absolute oneness and uniqueness of God — and the gravest sin is shirk, associating partners with God. The shahada, the Muslim declaration of faith, begins "There is no god but God". Islam teaches that God is the same God worshipped in Judaism and Christianity (the God of Abraham), though the Islamic conception rejects the Trinity and any anthropomorphic likeness: as the famous chapter of the Qur'an, Surah Al-Ikhlas (112), puts it, "Say: He is Allah, the One... He neither begets nor is born, nor is there to Him any equivalent."

Names and attributes

Muslim tradition speaks of the 99 Names of God (asmāʾ Allāh al-ḥusnā, "the most beautiful names"), each expressing an attribute: al-Raḥmān (the Most Merciful), al-Raḥīm (the Most Compassionate), al-Malik (the King), al-Khāliq (the Creator), al-ʿAlīm (the All-Knowing), and so on. The Qur'an opens nearly every chapter with the invocation of God's mercy (bismillāh al-raḥmān al-raḥīm). Classical theology (kalām) debated how to understand the Qur'an's statements that seem to attribute a hand, a face or a throne to God, with schools such as the Ashʿarites affirming the attributes "without asking how" (bilā kayf) and the Muʿtazilites interpreting them metaphorically.

Oneness, creation and providence

God in Islam is eternal, self-sufficient, omnipotent and omniscient, the creator of everything "from nothing" by His command ("Be, and it is"). He is both transcendent — "there is nothing like unto Him" (Qur'an 42:11) — and immanently near, "closer to [man] than his jugular vein" (50:16). Angels, prophets, scriptures and the Last Day mediating no partners to His sovereignty: intercession is possible only by His permission. Human beings are His servants and vicegerents (khalīfa) on earth, and the purpose of creation is worship and moral testing, culminating in resurrection and judgement.

Relation to other Abrahamic faiths

Because "Allah" is simply the Arabic word for God, Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews also use it. Islam holds that the God who revealed the Torah and the Gospel is the same God who revealed the Qur'an, Islam's scripture being the final, uncorrupted revelation confirming and correcting earlier ones. Sufi tradition deepens the doctrine with themes of divine love and the unity of being (waḥdat al-wujūd), stressing God's immanence and the believer's intimate knowledge (maʿrifa) of Him.
