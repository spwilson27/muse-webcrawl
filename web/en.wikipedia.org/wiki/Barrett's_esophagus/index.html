Barrett's esophagus

Barrett's esophagus is a condition in which the flat pink squamous lining of the lower esophagus is replaced by a salmon-colored columnar lining with intestinal-type cells (intestinal metaplasia), as a result of chronic damage from gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). It is named after the British thoracic surgeon Norman Barrett, who described the condition in 1950. Barrett's esophagus is a precancerous lesion that increases the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Diagnosis and classification

Diagnosis requires endoscopy showing columnar mucosa extending at least 1 cm above the gastroesophageal junction, confirmed by biopsy showing intestinal metaplasia. Segments are described by the Prague C & M criteria (circumferential and maximal extent). Biopsies are graded as no dysplasia, indefinite for dysplasia, low-grade dysplasia, or high-grade dysplasia, which determines management.

Management

Management includes long-term acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors, weight management, and endoscopic surveillance at intervals based on dysplasia status. Confirmed dysplasia is treated with endoscopic eradication therapy, principally radiofrequency ablation and endoscopic mucosal resection, which has largely replaced esophagectomy for early neoplasia. Annual progression to adenocarcinoma in non-dysplastic Barrett's is roughly 0.2–0.3 percent, but the risk rises substantially with dysplasia.
