Strategy of tension

The strategy of tension (Italian: strategia della tensione) is a political policy in which violent struggle is encouraged rather than suppressed. The purpose is to create a general feeling of insecurity in the population and make people seek security in a strong government, hoping to make people believe the central or current government is the only institution capable of maintaining social order. The strategy of tension is most closely identified with the Years of Lead in Italy from 1968 to 1982, wherein far-right and far-left extremists, and possibly some agents of the state itself, carried out bombings and assassinations.

The expression strategia della tensione was first used in an article in the British newspaper The Observer on 7 December 1969, following the Piazza Fontana bombing in Milan.

Italy

During Italy's Years of Lead, a series of bombings and assassinations were carried out by far-right and neo-fascist groups, sometimes with the support or tolerance of elements within the Italian secret services. Major incidents associated with the strategy include the Piazza Fontana bombing (1969), the Peteano bombing (1972), the Brescia bombing (1974), the Italicus Express bombing (1974), and the Bologna massacre (1980), the deadliest terrorist attack of the period, which killed 85 people.

Investigations and trials later established that some of these attacks were intended to falsely attribute responsibility to left-wing groups and communists, thereby discrediting them and fostering public demand for an authoritarian response. The existence of Operation Gladio, a clandestine NATO "stay-behind" network in Italy, became public in 1990 and intensified debate about the involvement of state and foreign intelligence actors.
