Miami bass

Placeholder image: Car-audio culture was central to the Miami bass scene

Miami bass (also called booty bass or booty music) is a subgenre of hip hop music that became popular in Miami and across the southeastern United States in the 1980s and early 1990s. It is characterised by extremely deep, sustained bass — typically the kick drum sound of the Roland TR-808 drum machine — fast tempos suitable for dancing, and lyrics that are often sexually explicit and delivered in a party-oriented call-and-response style.

History

The genre grew out of electro-funk and local party culture, with early landmarks including MC Ade's "Bass Rock Express" (1985) and the work of producer Amos Larkins. Its defining act was 2 Live Crew, whose albums The 2 Live Crew Is What We Are (1986) and As Nasty As They Wanna Be (1989), led by Luther "Luke Skyywalker" Campbell, achieved national sales and national controversy: the latter was ruled obscene by a federal judge in Florida in 1990, a ruling overturned on appeal in a significant First Amendment case. Other central figures included DJ Magic Mike, whose virtuoso scratching and superhuman low-end records such as "Feel the Bass" made him a hero of the car-audio scene, as well as 69 Boyz ("Tootsee Roll"), Tag Team ("Whoomp! (There It Is)"), and Freak Nasty ("Da' Dip").

Characteristics and legacy

Miami bass tempos typically run between about 120 and 135 beats per minute, with the 808 kick pitched and sustained to produce sub-bass frequencies felt physically in cars and clubs; "bass check" records were made specifically to demonstrate sound systems. Though its commercial peak passed by the mid-1990s, Miami bass shaped Southern hip hop broadly — influencing crunk, bounce, and trap production — and its emphasis on low-frequency weight remains foundational to club music.
