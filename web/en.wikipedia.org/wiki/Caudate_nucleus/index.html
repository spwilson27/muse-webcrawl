Caudate nucleus

The caudate nucleus is one of the structures that make up the corpus striatum, which is a component of the basal ganglia in the human brain. The caudate is a C-shaped subcortical structure that curves along the lateral ventricle and plays important roles in procedural learning, associative learning, inhibitory control of action, and reward processing.

Anatomy

The caudate is divided into a large head, a body, and a slender tail that ends near the amygdala in the temporal lobe. Together with the putamen, it forms the dorsal striatum and is separated from the putamen by fibers of the internal capsule, except where the two merge ventrally at the nucleus accumbens. It receives major dopaminergic input from the substantia nigra pars compacta.

Function

The caudate is heavily involved in learning and memory, particularly in processing feedback. It contributes to goal-directed behavior, habit formation, language comprehension, and threshold control in the visual system. It works in parallel circuits with the putamen, globus pallidus, and thalamus.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the caudate is implicated in Huntington's disease, in which degeneration of caudate neurons produces involuntary movements and cognitive decline; in Parkinson's disease through dopaminergic loss; in obsessive–compulsive disorder; and in schizophrenia and ADHD.
