Anaximander (c. 610 – c. 546 BC) was a Pre-Socratic Greek philosopher from Miletus, a pupil and younger contemporary of Thales. He is credited with the earliest surviving attempt at a systematic, naturalistic account of the world: the first prose treatise on nature, the first map of the known world, and a cosmology in which the Earth floats unsupported at the centre of the universe. He proposed that the origin (archē) of all things is the apeiron — the boundless or indefinite — rather than any particular element.

Philosophy and cosmology

Anaximander argued that no single known element could be the source of all things, since the opposites (hot and cold, wet and dry) war against one another; the source must therefore be an indefinite, ageless mass, the apeiron, from which opposites separate out and to which they return, "paying penalty and retribution to one another for their injustice, according to the assessment of Time" — the one verbatim fragment of his work that survives. His Earth was a short cylinder, three times as wide as it is deep, held in place by its indifference — an early argument from equilibrium. Around it revolved rings of fire enclosed in mist, whose apertures are the Sun, Moon, and stars.

Science and speculation

Anaximander is credited with introducing the gnomon (sundial) to Greece, drawing a world map later improved by Hecataeus, and explaining rain, thunder, and earthquakes naturalistically. He also speculated that life arose from moisture warmed by the Sun, and that human beings must originally have been born from other animals, since human infants are helpless for so long — an idea often cited as a distant anticipation of evolution.

Legacy

Anaximander's work is known only through later reports, chiefly Aristotle and Theophrastus. The lunar crater Anaximander and the asteroid 6006 Anaximandros commemorate him. Karl Popper praised his cosmology as one of the boldest conjectures in the history of thought.
