Vestment

Vestments are the distinctive garments worn by clergy and other ministers in Christian liturgical services. Derived largely from the ordinary dress of the Greco-Roman world of late antiquity, vestments were retained and elaborated as secular fashion changed, acquiring symbolic meanings and canonical regulation. The principal Western vestments include the alb, stole, chasuble, dalmatic, cope, and humeral veil; Eastern traditions use corresponding garments such as the sticharion, epitrachelion, and phelonion.

History

Early Christian ministers celebrated in the formal everyday clothing of the late Roman world — the long tunic and the cloak — and as fashions shortened and changed, the church garments fossilised into distinctive dress. By the Carolingian period the main vestments were established, and medieval writers such as Amalarius of Metz and later Innocent III assigned them elaborate allegorical interpretations. The Council of Trent era codified Roman usage, while the Reformation churches varied: Lutherans and Anglicans retained much traditional dress, and many Reformed traditions adopted the black Geneva gown instead.

Principal garments

  • Alb — the long white under-tunic worn by all ministers.
  • Stole — a long band of cloth worn by deacons, priests and bishops as a sign of office.
  • Chasuble — the outer Eucharistic vestment of the celebrant.
  • Dalmatic — the outer vestment of the deacon.
  • Cope — a semicircular processional cloak, also called a pluvial.
  • Humeral veil — a shoulder veil used when handling the Blessed Sacrament.
  • Surplice and cassock — choir dress and everyday clerical dress.

Liturgical colours

Vestments are made in a sequence of colours — principally white, red, green, violet, and (rarely) black, rose, and blue — assigned to seasons and feasts of the liturgical year, a system standardised in the Roman Rite in the 16th century and widely imitated in other Western churches.
