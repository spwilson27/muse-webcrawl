Pluvial

Pluvial is another name for the cope, the long liturgical cloak worn by clergy in processions and at services other than the celebration of the Eucharist. The word derives from the Latin pluvia, "rain", recalling the garment's origin as a hooded weatherproof cloak: the cope began as a practical rain cape (cappa pluvialis) worn outdoors and in processions, and developed into a semicircular mantle of rich fabric fastened at the breast with a clasp called a morse.

Development

By the Middle Ages the pluvial had become a ceremonial vestment of silk or cloth of gold, its original hood reduced to a flat decorative shield on the back, and its front edges trimmed with a broad band of embroidery, the orphrey. Surviving medieval copes, such as those preserved in museum collections, are among the masterpieces of ecclesiastical embroidery (opus anglicanum).

Use

The pluvial is worn by bishops, priests, deacons and, in some traditions, lay ministers at processions, solemn Vespers, Benediction (together with the humeral veil), blessings, and other non-Eucharistic rites. It is never substituted for the chasuble at Mass. Its colour follows the liturgical season or feast.
