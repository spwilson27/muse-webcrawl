Chasuble

The chasuble is the outermost liturgical vestment worn by clergy for the celebration of the Eucharist in Western-tradition Christian churches that use full vestments, primarily in Roman Catholic, Anglican, and Lutheran churches. It is also used in some Methodist churches. In the Eastern tradition the equivalent vestment is the phelonion. The chasuble originated as a sewn-over poncho-like garment, the casula ("little house") of late antiquity, worn as ordinary dress and adapted to liturgical use.

Forms

Historically the chasuble was a generous conical or bell-shaped garment reaching almost to the ground on all sides, cut from a near-circular piece of cloth with a hole for the head. Over the centuries, especially from the 16th century, it was cut away at the sides to free the arms, producing the stiffened "fiddleback" shape of the Tridentine period; the fuller Gothic form returned to favour in the 19th and 20th centuries. Chasubles are made in the liturgical colours of the season — white, red, green, violet, and others — and are often decorated with an orphrey, a band of embroidery forming a cross or column down the back.

Use

The chasuble is worn by the celebrating priest (and by bishops) over the alb and stole. In the traditional Roman Rite the deacon assisted in vesting the celebrant, and prayers accompanied the putting on of the garment. A related garment, the chasuble-like dalmatic, is worn by deacons; the cope, by contrast, is a processional cloak never used as the Eucharistic vestment of the celebrant.
