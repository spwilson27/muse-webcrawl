Open-source-software movement

Type: Social and technical movement

Key figures: Richard Stallman, Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds

Key organizations: Free Software Foundation, Open Source Initiative

The open-source-software movement is a movement that supports the use of open-source licenses for some or all software, as part of the broader notion of open collaboration. The movement emerged to differentiate the philosophy of the free software movement from its pragmatic, business-oriented aspects, and to promote software whose source code is available for anyone to view, modify, and redistribute.

The open-source label was coined in 1998, shortly after the announcement that Netscape Communications would release the source code of its web browser. Christine Peterson of the Foresight Institute proposed the term, and a strategy session in Palo Alto, California, involving figures such as Eric S. Raymond, Todd Anderson, and Larry Augustin, led to the founding of the Open Source Initiative (OSI) by Raymond and Bruce Perens.

History

The roots of the movement lie in the hacker culture of the 1960s and 1970s, where programmers at institutions such as MIT freely shared software. In the 1980s, Richard Stallman founded the Free Software Foundation and launched the GNU Project in response to the increasing proprietarization of software, developing the concept of copyleft through the GNU General Public License.

The release of the Linux kernel by Linus Torvalds in 1991, and its relicensing under the GNU GPL in 1992, provided a catalyst for growth. Eric Raymond's 1997 essay The Cathedral and the Bazaar analyzed the open development model and influenced Netscape's decision to open-source its browser, which became the Mozilla project.

Philosophy

The Open Source Initiative maintains the Open Source Definition, derived from the Debian Free Software Guidelines written by Bruce Perens. Licenses that comply with the definition permit free redistribution, access to source code, derived works, and prohibit discrimination against persons, groups, or fields of endeavor. Prominent licenses include the Apache License, the MIT License, the BSD licenses, and the GNU General Public License.

Advocates argue that open-source development produces more reliable and secure software through peer review — a principle summarized by Raymond as "given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow," known as Linus's Law.

Impact

Open-source software underpins much of the modern internet: the Apache HTTP Server and Nginx serve a large share of websites, the Linux kernel powers the majority of servers and all of the world's top supercomputers, and languages and frameworks such as Python, PHP, and Node.js dominate web development. Major technology companies, including Microsoft, Google, and IBM, have become significant contributors; Microsoft acquired the code-hosting platform GitHub in 2018.

Criticism

Critics within the free software movement, including Stallman, argue that the term "open source" obscures the ethical and freedom-centered goals of free software. Others have raised concerns about corporate exploitation of volunteer labor, the sustainability of maintainer funding, and license choices that do not guarantee reciprocity.
