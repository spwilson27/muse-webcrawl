CMAS most commonly denotes calcium–magnesium–aluminium silicate (calcia-magnesia-alumina-silicate), a class of molten silicate deposits that attack the thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) of gas turbine engines. CMAS deposits form when airborne dust, sand, volcanic ash, and runway debris are ingested by a jet engine, melt at operating temperatures, and infiltrate the porous ceramic top coat of turbine blades.

CMAS melt infiltration is a leading cause of thermal barrier coating failure in aero engines.

Mechanism of attack

The standard TBC material, yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), is deposited with a columnar or porous microstructure that gives it strain tolerance. Above about 1200 °C, silicate debris melts into a glass that wicks into these pores by capillary action. On cooling, the infiltrated layer stiffens, losing strain tolerance, and the reaction between the melt and YSZ depletes yttria, destabilizing the zirconia phase and causing cracking and spallation of the coating.

Mitigation

Research into CMAS-resistant coatings includes alternative top-coat chemistries such as gadolinium zirconate (Gd2Zr2O7), which reacts with CMAS to form a dense apatite sealing layer, as well as sacrificial and alumina-forming overlayers that block melt infiltration. The problem has grown in importance as engines run hotter for efficiency and operate in dusty environments and after volcanic eruptions.

Other uses of the abbreviation

CMAS is also an acronym for systems such as the Commercial Mobile Alert System (now Wireless Emergency Alerts) in the United States and various professional bodies; context determines the intended meaning.
