Gordium

Location: Yassıhöyük, Polatlı, Ankara Province, Turkey
Region: Anatolia
Type: Settlement
History: Occupied from the Early Bronze Age; capital of Phrygia

Gordium (Greek: Γόρδιον, Górdion; Turkish: Gordion or Gordiyon) was the capital city of ancient Phrygia. It was located at the site of modern Yassıhöyük, about 70–80 km (43–50 mi) southwest of Ankara (ancient Ancyra), in the immediate vicinity of Polatlı district. Gordium's strategic location on the Sangarius River (modern Sakarya) and on the Persian Royal Road made it an important commercial centre.

The site is famous as the location of the Gordian Knot, the intricate knot that, according to legend, Alexander the Great cut (or untied) in 333 BC after hearing a prophecy that whoever loosed it would rule Asia.

History

Occupation at Gordium extends back to the Early Bronze Age (c. 3000 BC). In the Iron Age it became the capital of the Phrygian kingdom, reaching its height in the 9th and 8th centuries BC under kings such as Gordias and Midas. The city was destroyed by the Cimmerians around 700 BC — a destruction layer long associated with the legendary death of Midas — and later rebuilt. After the conquests of Cyrus the Great it became part of the Persian Empire, and Alexander the Great visited in 333 BC. The city continued to be inhabited through Hellenistic, Roman, and medieval times before being abandoned.

Archaeology

The site was excavated by Gustav and Alfred Körte in 1900 and by the University of Pennsylvania Museum under Rodney Young from 1950 to 1973, with excavations continuing under later directors. The Citadel Mound contains the remains of monumental Phrygian gate complexes, megaron buildings, and the Terrace Buildings, a row of industrial structures destroyed by fire.

Over one hundred burial mounds (tumuli) surround the site. The largest, Tumulus MM (the "Midas Mound," c. 740 BC), stands 53 metres high and contained the burial of a man in his sixties, accompanied by drinking vessels, bronze cauldrons and furniture inlaid with geometric designs — possibly the tomb of Midas's father, Gordias. Analysis of the burial goods revealed residues of a funerary feast, including a spicy lentil and sheep stew and a mixed fermented drink of wine, beer and mead.

In 2023 Gordium was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
