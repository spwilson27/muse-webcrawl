Roman Republic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Roman Forum, civic heart of the Republic, with the ruins of temples and basilicas]

The Roman Republic (509–27 BC) was the era of ancient Roman civilisation characterised by a republican form of government, beginning with the overthrow of the Roman Kingdom in 509 BC and ending with the establishment of the Roman Empire under Augustus in 27 BC. Governed by annually elected magistrates, above all two consuls, and advised by the Senate, the Republic conquered Italy, defeated Carthage in the Punic Wars, and by the 1st century BC ruled the whole Mediterranean world — even as civil wars between its own generals tore the republican system apart.

Institutions and expansion

Republican politics balanced the consuls, Senate and popular assemblies; the Conflict of the Orders gradually won plebeians access to office, the tribunate and the written law of the Twelve Tables. Roman armies — citizen legions organised in maniples — absorbed Samnites, Greeks and Gauls in Italy, then Carthaginians, Macedonians and Seleucids abroad. Conquest brought immense wealth, slaves and provincial taxation, enriching the senatorial class and displacing the small farmers who had manned the legions.

Crisis and fall

Reformers Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus (133, 123–121 BC) were killed by senatorial violence, opening a century of escalating civil conflict: Marius and Sulla marched armies on Rome; Pompey, Crassus and Caesar formed the First Triumvirate; Caesar crossed the Rubicon (49 BC), won the civil war and was assassinated (44 BC); the Second Triumvirate proscribed its enemies and defeated the assassins, then split, until Octavian's victory at Actium (31 BC) left him master of the Roman world. In 27 BC, as Augustus, he founded the principate — the Empire in republican dress.
