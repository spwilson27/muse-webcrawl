Umberto Eco (5 January 1932 – 19 February 2016) was an Italian medievalist, philosopher, semiotician, novelist and literary critic. He is best known internationally for his 1980 novel The Name of the Rose (Il nome della rosa), a murder mystery set in a fourteenth-century Italian monastery, which combines semiotics, biblical analysis and medieval studies, and has sold tens of millions of copies worldwide.

Life and academic career

Eco was born in Alessandria in Piedmont. He studied medieval philosophy and literature at the University of Turin, writing his thesis on the aesthetics of Thomas Aquinas. After working as a cultural editor for the broadcaster RAI and as an editor at the Bompiani publishing house, he taught at several universities and in 1975 became professor of semiotics at the University of Bologna, the first chair of its kind in Italy. His scholarly works include The Open Work (1962), which analysed modernist art and literature, and A Theory of Semiotics (1976).

Fiction

The Name of the Rose, published when Eco was 48, was followed by Foucault's Pendulum (1988), a labyrinthine novel about conspiracy theories; The Island of the Day Before (1994); Baudolino (2000); The Mysterious Flame of Queen Loana (2004); The Prague Cemetery (2010), about the forgery of the Protocols of the Elders of Zion; and Numero Zero (2015), a satire of journalism. His novels characteristically erode the boundary between high and popular culture and demand active, learned readers.

Essays and public role

Eco was a prolific essayist and newspaper columnist, famous for essays such as "Ur-Fascism" (1995), which proposed fourteen properties of eternal fascism. He wrote on everything from Superman and James Bond to translation, lists, and the internet, and his personal library in Milan held some 30,000 volumes. He died of pancreatic cancer in Milan in 2016.
