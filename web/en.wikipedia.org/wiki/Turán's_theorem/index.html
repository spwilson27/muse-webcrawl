Turán's theorem

Turán's theorem is a fundamental result in extremal graph theory, proved by the Hungarian mathematician Pál Turán in 1941. It determines the maximum number of edges in a graph on n vertices that does not contain a complete graph Kr+1 as a subgraph.

Statement

The unique extremal graph is the Turán graph T(n, r): a complete r-partite graph whose parts are as equal in size as possible. Turán's theorem states that any Kr+1-free graph on n vertices has at most as many edges as T(n, r) — asymptotically at most (1 − 1/r) · n²/2 edges. The case r = 2 is Mantel's theorem (1907): a triangle-free graph has at most n²/4 edges.

Significance

Turán's theorem is often regarded as the founding result of extremal graph theory. It led to the Turán density concept, the Erdős–Stone theorem, and stability results showing that near-extremal graphs resemble Turán graphs.
