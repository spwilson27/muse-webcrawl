The Social Democratic Party (SDP) was a centrist to centre-left political party in the United Kingdom. The party was founded in March 1981 by a group of dissident moderate Labour politicians, known as the "Gang of Four": Roy Jenkins, David Owen, Bill Rodgers, and Shirley Williams.

The SDP formed the SDP–Liberal Alliance with the Liberal Party and fought the 1983 and 1987 general elections. In 1988, the two parties merged to form the Social and Liberal Democrats, later renamed the Liberal Democrats, although a minority, led by Owen, continued with a separate "continuing" SDP, which was dissolved in 1990.

History

The origins of the SDP lay in the perceived move to the left of the Labour Party under Michael Foot, together with disputes over Europe, unilateral nuclear disarmament, and reselection of MPs. The Council for Social Democracy was formed in January 1981, and the Limehouse Declaration, issued on 25 January 1981, announced the intent to form a new party.

Elections

At its peak the party had 28 Members of Parliament, most of them defectors from Labour. Early by-election victories, including Shirley Williams at Crosby in 1981 and Roy Jenkins at Glasgow Hillhead in 1982, suggested the party might "break the mould" of British politics, but the Falklands War and the first-past-the-post electoral system limited its success; in the 1983 general election the Alliance won 25.4% of the vote but only 23 seats.
