Convergence tests

In mathematics, convergence tests are methods of testing for the convergence, conditional convergence, absolute convergence, interval of convergence, or divergence of an infinite series. Because the sum of an infinite series is defined as the limit of its sequence of partial sums, these tests provide practical criteria for deciding whether that limit exists and is finite.

List of tests

  • Limit of the summand test (nth-term test) — if the terms of the series do not tend to zero, the series diverges.
  • Ratio test — examines the limit of the ratio of successive terms; limits below 1 imply absolute convergence, above 1 divergence.
  • Root test — uses the limit superior of the nth root of the absolute value of the terms.
  • Integral test — compares the series with an improper integral of a related monotone function.
  • Direct comparison test and limit comparison test — compare the given series term-by-term with a series of known behavior.
  • Alternating series test (Leibniz criterion) — an alternating series whose terms decrease in absolute value to zero converges.
  • Cauchy condensation test — converts a monotone series into a related "condensed" series with the same convergence behavior.
  • Dirichlet's test and Abel's test — handle products of sequences where one has bounded partial sums and the other decreases to zero.
  • Raabe's test and Gauss's test — refinements of the ratio test for borderline cases.

Conditional convergence

A series that converges but does not converge absolutely is called conditionally convergent; the alternating harmonic series is the standard example. By the Riemann series theorem, a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any real value, or to diverge, underscoring why tests distinguishing absolute from conditional convergence matter.
