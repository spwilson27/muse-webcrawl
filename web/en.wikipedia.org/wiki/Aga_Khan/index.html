Aga Khan (Persian: Āqā Khān) is the hereditary title of the Imam of the Nizari branch of Ismaili Shia Islam — a community of some 15 million people in more than 25 countries, mainly in Central and South Asia, East Africa and the West. The title, combining the Turkic agha ("chief, lord") and Persian khan, was first bestowed on Hasan Ali Shah, the 46th Imam, by the Qajar Shah of Persia in 1817/18. The Aga Khans claim direct descent from the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima and cousin Ali, and from the Fatimid caliphs of Egypt.

History of the title

Aga Khan I (1804–1881), governor of Kerman in Persia, fell out with the Qajar court, fled via Afghanistan to British India in the 1840s, and settled in Bombay, where the British recognised him as leader of the Ismailis and a prince. His great-grandson Aga Khan III (1877–1957), Sir Sultan Muhammed Shah, held the office for 72 years: he was a founding figure and first president of the All-India Muslim League, represented India at the League of Nations (which he served as president in 1937), and famously raced Thoroughbred horses, winning the Epsom Derby five times; at his jubilees his followers weighed him against gold, diamonds and platinum, the proceeds funding development work. He was succeeded not by his son but by his grandson, Aga Khan IV (1936–2025), Prince Karim, a Harvard graduate who became the 49th Imam in 1957 aged 20.

The modern Imamate

Aga Khan IV built the Aga Khan Development Network (AKDN), a group of agencies in health, education, culture and rural development — including the Aga Khan University, the University of Central Asia, the Aga Khan Foundation and the Aga Khan Award for Architecture — operating largely in Africa and Asia and employing tens of thousands. He was also one of the world's most successful Thoroughbred owner-breeders. On his death in Lisbon in February 2025 he was succeeded by his eldest son, Prince Rahim, as Aga Khan V, the 50th Imam.
