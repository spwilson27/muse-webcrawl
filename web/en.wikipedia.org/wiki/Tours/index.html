Tours (French pronunciation: [tuʁ]) is a city in west-central France, the prefecture of the department of Indre-et-Loire and the largest city in the Centre-Val de Loire region. It stands on the lower reaches of the Loire river, between Orléans and the Atlantic coast, and is known as "Le Jardin de la France" ("The Garden of France").

History

The area was the territory of the Gaulish Turones tribe, from whom the city takes its name. Tours became an important Christian centre under Saint Martin of Tours, bishop in the 4th century, whose tomb made the city a major pilgrimage site. In 732 the Battle of Tours (also called the Battle of Poitiers) was fought nearby, in which Charles Martel halted the Umayyad advance into Francia. During the Hundred Years' War, Tours served at times as a seat of the French court, and Joan of Arc came here in 1429. The city was famous for its silk industry in the early modern period.

Geography and culture

Tours lies between the Loire and the Cher rivers. Its old town features well-preserved half-timbered houses around the Place Plumereau, and the Gothic cathedral of Saint-Gatien has a notable Flamboyant façade. The region around Tours is part of the Loire Valley UNESCO World Heritage Site, famous for its châteaux. The local dialect is traditionally reputed to be the purest form of spoken French.

Transport

Tours is a major railway junction, served by TGV trains to Paris (about one hour) and Bordeaux. A tramway line opened in 2013.
