Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection

SpecialtyInfectious disease, hematology
SymptomsFever, chills, vomiting, diarrhea, shock
ComplicationsSepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, death
CausesEncapsulated bacteria following splenectomy
PreventionVaccination, prophylactic antibiotics, patient education

An overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) is a rare but rapidly fatal complication following removal of the spleen (splenectomy) or in conditions of functional asplenia. The infection typically begins with mild, flu-like symptoms but progresses within hours to septic shock, and historically has carried a mortality rate of 50–70% once fully established.

Mechanism

The spleen is the principal organ for filtering blood-borne encapsulated bacteria and for producing opsonizing antibodies, particularly IgM memory B cells. Without a spleen, organisms such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and Neisseria meningitidis can multiply unchecked in the bloodstream, because their polysaccharide capsules resist phagocytosis without adequate opsonization.

Signs and symptoms

OPSI often begins with nonspecific features — fever, chills, myalgia, vomiting, or diarrhea — resembling a viral illness. This prodrome may last only a few hours before the onset of septicemia, hypotension, disseminated intravascular coagulation, adrenal hemorrhage, coma, and death. The speed of deterioration is the hallmark of the syndrome.

Risk

The risk of OPSI is highest in the first two years after splenectomy and in children, but it persists for life. It is also elevated in patients splenectomized for hematologic disease compared with those splenectomized for trauma. Other organisms involved include Capnocytophaga canimorsus (after dog bites), Babesia species, and malaria parasites.

Prevention

Preventive measures include vaccination against pneumococcus, H. influenzae type b, and meningococcus, ideally given at least two weeks before elective splenectomy or shortly after emergency surgery; prophylactic antibiotics (often penicillin) in selected patients; standby antibiotics for immediate self-administration at the first sign of fever; and education of patients to seek urgent medical attention for any febrile illness and to carry an alert card documenting their asplenic status.

Treatment

Suspected OPSI is a medical emergency. Immediate empiric intravenous antibiotics — typically a third-generation cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone, with vancomycin added where resistant pneumococcus is a concern — should be given without waiting for culture results, together with full supportive care for sepsis.
