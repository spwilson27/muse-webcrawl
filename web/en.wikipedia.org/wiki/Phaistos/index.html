Phaistos

Location: Heraklion regional unit, Crete, Greece

Civilization: Minoan

Occupied: Neolithic through Hellenistic periods; palace c. 2000–1450 BC

Phaistos (Greek: Φαιστός), also transliterated as Phaestos, Festus, or Faistos, is an archaeological site in southern Crete, Greece, famous for its palace of the Bronze Age Minoan civilization. Overlooking the fertile Messara Plain from a low hill near the Libyan Sea, Phaistos was one of the principal Minoan palatial centres, second in importance only to Knossos.

History

The site was inhabited from the Neolithic period. The first palace was built around 2000 BC in the Middle Minoan period and was destroyed, like the other palaces, by an earthquake around 1700 BC. A grander second palace was erected on its ruins, featuring a large central court, a monumental west court and grand staircase, royal apartments, storerooms (magazines) with giant pithoi, and early theatrical areas. Phaistos declined after the destructions of about 1450 BC, though settlement continued in later periods; a Greek city of Phaistos flourished in the archaic and Hellenistic eras and minted its own coins before being destroyed by Gortyn around the mid-second century BC.

Excavations and finds

The site was excavated by the Italian School of Archaeology under Federico Halbherr and Luigi Pernier beginning in 1900. The most famous find is the Phaistos Disc, a unique fired-clay disc stamped with pictorial signs in a spiral arrangement, discovered in 1908 in a basement room of the palace complex and still undeciphered. Other finds include Kamares ware pottery, sealings, and Linear A tablets, testifying to the site's administrative role.

In Greek myth, Phaistos was associated with Rhadamanthus, brother of Minos, said to have ruled there.
