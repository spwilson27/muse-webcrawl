Active learning

Active learning is a form of learning in which students are actively or experientially involved in the learning process, rather than passively receiving information from lectures. It encompasses a wide range of instructional strategies — discussion, problem-solving, case studies, role playing, peer teaching and collaborative projects — that engage students in doing things and thinking about what they are doing, in Charles Bonwell and James Eison's widely cited formulation.

[Image: Students working collaboratively in class]

History

Although the term became popular in the 1980s and 1990s, its roots run through the work of John Dewey, Jean Piaget and Lev Vygotsky, who emphasised learning through experience and social interaction. Bonwell and Eison's 1991 report helped popularise the term in higher education.

Evidence

A large meta-analysis by Freeman and colleagues (2014), covering 225 studies of undergraduate science, engineering and mathematics courses, found that average examination scores improved by about 6 per cent under active learning and that students in traditional lectures were 1.5 times more likely to fail. Such findings have driven the adoption of active learning techniques in university teaching, including flipped classrooms, clickers and think-pair-share exercises.

Techniques

Common techniques include minute papers, think-pair-share, peer instruction, jigsaw activities, problem-based learning, and classroom response systems. The level of engagement ranges from brief pauses for discussion within a lecture to fully student-centred course designs.
