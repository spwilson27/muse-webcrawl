Peace of Nicias

ConcludedMarch 421 BC
PartiesAthens and its allies; Sparta and its allies
Named forNicias, Athenian negotiator
DurationNominally 50 years; effectively collapsed within a few years
ContextPeloponnesian War, end of the Archidamian War phase

The Peace of Nicias was a peace treaty signed between Athens and Sparta in March 421 BC that ended the first decade of the Peloponnesian War — the phase modern historians call the Archidamian War. Named after the cautious Athenian general and statesman Nicias, its chief Athenian sponsor, the treaty promised fifty years of peace but was undermined from the start by dissatisfied allies on both sides and by incomplete implementation; fighting resumed within a few years, first by proxy and then openly.

Background

After ten years of war — annual Spartan invasions of Attica, Athenian naval raids, the plague that devastated Athens, and the Athenian successes at Pylos and Sphacteria (425 BC), where Sparta lost captured hoplites held hostage in Athens — both powers had reason to settle. Sparta wanted its prisoners back and feared revolt among the helots; Athens, checked by its defeat at Delium (424 BC) and by the loss of Amphipolis to the Spartan general Brasidas, wanted to consolidate its empire. The deaths in 422 BC at Amphipolis of both Brasidas and the Athenian war-party leader Cleon — the two most uncompromising opponents of peace, as Thucydides pointedly notes — cleared the way for negotiators Nicias for Athens and King Pleistoanax for Sparta.

Terms

The treaty, quoted in full by Thucydides (Book 5), provided essentially for the status quo ante bellum: each side was to restore most wartime conquests. Athens was to return Pylos, Cythera and other places; Sparta was to return Amphipolis and other cities in Thrace; prisoners were to be exchanged; the cities of the Chalcidice were to be autonomous and pay the old tribute; and the sanctuary of Delphi was to be independent. Arbitration clauses were to settle disputes, and the peace was sworn for fifty years, accompanied by a separate defensive alliance between Athens and Sparta.

Failure

Key allies — Corinth, Boeotia, Elis and Megara — refused to accept the treaty, and its central exchanges were never completed: Sparta could not compel Amphipolis to return to Athens, and Athens consequently retained Pylos. Argos, Sparta's Peloponnesian rival, built a counter-alliance with Athens, brokered by the young Alcibiades; Athenian and Spartan troops then fought on opposite sides at the Battle of Mantinea (418 BC), in all but name a resumption of the war. Athens' catastrophic Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BC) — which Nicias himself, ironically, was chosen to command — destroyed the peace entirely, ushering in the war's final and most destructive phase.

Assessment

Thucydides treated the so-called peace as an interlude rather than a settlement, counting the whole 27 years as one war. The Peace of Nicias remains a classic case study of a negotiated settlement undermined by spoilers, non-compliance, and the unresolved ambitions that had caused the war.
