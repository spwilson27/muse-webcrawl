Valve audio amplifier

A valve audio amplifier (in North America, a tube amplifier) is an electronic amplifier that uses vacuum tubes (thermionic valves) to increase the power or amplitude of an audio signal. Valve amplifiers dominated audio from the 1920s until transistors displaced them in most applications during the 1960s and 1970s.

Survival and revival

Valves persisted in guitar amplifiers — where their soft clipping when overdriven is a defining part of electric guitar tone, as in classic Marshall, Fender and Vox designs — and in high-end hi-fi, where enthusiasts value their sound, their predominantly even-order harmonic distortion and gentle overload behaviour. Specialist manufacturers continue to produce valve amplifiers, and classic circuits such as the Williamson amplifier (1947) and the Mullard 5-10 remain influential.

Characteristics

Valve amplifiers require output transformers to match high valve impedances to loudspeakers, are heavier and less efficient than transistor designs, and need periodic valve replacement. Their input stages use small-signal triodes and pentodes, while output stages commonly employ beam tetrodes or pentodes in push-pull or single-ended configurations.
