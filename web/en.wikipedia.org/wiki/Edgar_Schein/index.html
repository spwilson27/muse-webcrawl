Edgar Henry Schein (March 5, 1928 – January 26, 2023) was a Swiss-born American psychologist and a foundational figure in the field of organisational development. A longtime professor at the MIT Sloan School of Management, he is best known for his model of organisational culture and for popularising the very term "corporate culture" as an object of study.

Contributions

Organisational culture

In Organizational Culture and Leadership (1985), Schein defined culture as a pattern of shared basic assumptions learned by a group, and described it at three levels: visible artefacts, espoused beliefs and values, and deeper, taken-for-granted basic underlying assumptions. The model remains one of the most cited frameworks in management studies.

Other concepts

From studies of prisoners of war in Korea and of management trainees, Schein developed the ideas of "coercive persuasion," the "psychological contract" between employee and organisation, "career anchors" describing individuals' self-perceived talents and values, and "process consultation," an approach in which a consultant helps clients diagnose and solve their own problems. His later work promoted "humble inquiry" — leading through questions rather than telling.

Life

Born in Zurich, Schein emigrated to the United States as a child. He studied at the University of Chicago, Stanford and Harvard, where he earned his doctorate in social psychology in 1952. He joined MIT in 1956 and remained associated with Sloan for the rest of his career.
