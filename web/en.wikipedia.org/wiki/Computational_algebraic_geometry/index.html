Computational algebraic geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Computational algebraic geometry is a branch of mathematics at the intersection of algebraic geometry and computer algebra, concerned with the design and analysis of algorithms for solving systems of polynomial equations and for studying the geometric objects — algebraic varieties — defined by them. The field emerged in the second half of the twentieth century as computers made it possible to carry out symbolic computations far beyond the reach of hand calculation.

Gröbner bases

The foundational tool of the subject is the Gröbner basis, introduced by Bruno Buchberger in his 1965 doctoral dissertation. A Gröbner basis is a special generating set for an ideal in a polynomial ring with properties analogous to a row-echelon form for linear systems or the greatest common divisor for univariate polynomials. With respect to a fixed monomial ordering, Buchberger's algorithm computes such a basis, allowing one to decide ideal membership, solve polynomial systems (in favourable cases), compute elimination ideals, and determine dimension and degree of varieties. Later algorithms such as Faugère's F4 and F5 improved substantially on the original method.

Other methods and applications

Beyond Gröbner bases, the field includes triangular decompositions and characteristic sets (in the tradition of Wu Wenjun), resultant and elimination methods, numerical algebraic geometry using homotopy continuation, and real algebraic geometry, including cylindrical algebraic decomposition. Computational algebraic geometry has found applications in robotics (inverse kinematics), computer-aided design, coding theory, cryptography, statistics (algebraic statistics), chemical reaction networks, and optimisation. General-purpose systems implementing these algorithms include Macaulay2, Singular, CoCoA, Magma, and SageMath, with Macaulay2 and Singular especially associated with research in the field.
