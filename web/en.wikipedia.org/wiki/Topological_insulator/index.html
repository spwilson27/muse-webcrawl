Topological insulator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of a topological insulator: an insulating block with conducting states flowing around its surface]
Insulating inside, conducting on the surface — protected by topology.

A topological insulator is a material whose interior behaves as an electrical insulator while its surface conducts electricity via special states protected by topology and time-reversal symmetry: surface electrons cannot be localised by ordinary disorder because backscattering would require flipping their spin, which is locked to their momentum (spin–momentum locking).

The concept descends from the Quantum Hall effect: after Haldane's 1988 model showed the effect could occur without a magnetic field, Kane and Mele (2005) proposed the quantum spin Hall effect in graphene, and Bernevig, Hughes and Zhang (2006) predicted it in HgTe quantum wells — confirmed experimentally in 2007. Three-dimensional topological insulators (Bi₂Se₃ and relatives, from 2008–09) made the physics robust and room-temperature relevant. The field has since widened to topological superconductors (hosting hypothetical Majorana modes for fault-tolerant Quantum computing), topological semimetals, and photonic/acoustic analogues — the broad programme of topological phases that won the 2016 Nobel Prize (Thouless, Haldane, Kosterlitz).

Why topology

The surface states exist because the band structure's topology (a Z₂ invariant) differs from vacuum's; at the boundary the invariant must change, forcing gapless states — the same bulk–boundary logic as the Hall effect's edge channels. Protection is not absolute (magnetic impurities break it), but it is of a different character from ordinary conduction: robust to the nonmagnetic disorder that plagues nanoscale electronics, a property engineers hope to exploit in spintronics and quantum devices.
