Heart murmur

Heart murmurs are unusual sounds produced by turbulent blood flow through the heart, loud enough to be heard with a stethoscope. They are distinct from the normal "lub-dub" heart sounds caused by valve closure. Murmurs may be innocent (physiological) — very common in children and harmless — or may indicate a structural problem of the heart valves or chambers.

Classification

Murmurs are described by their timing within the cardiac cycle, location, intensity, pitch, quality and radiation. Systolic murmurs occur during ventricular contraction and include ejection murmurs from aortic or pulmonary stenosis and regurgitant murmurs from mitral or tricuspid insufficiency. Diastolic murmurs, heard during ventricular relaxation, are almost always pathological and classically arise from aortic or pulmonary regurgitation or mitral stenosis. Continuous murmurs, such as that of a patent ductus arteriosus, span both phases.

Intensity is graded on the Levine scale from I (barely audible) to VI (audible with the stethoscope off the chest); a palpable vibration, or thrill, accompanies grades IV and above.

Causes

Turbulence may result from narrowed (stenotic) valves, leaking (regurgitant) valves, abnormal connections such as ventricular septal defects, or simply from increased flow across normal valves — as in fever, anaemia, pregnancy or childhood. Infective endocarditis, rheumatic heart disease and congenital heart defects are important pathological causes.

Diagnosis and management

Detection is usually by auscultation during a physical examination. The character of the murmur, together with symptoms and history, guides investigation; echocardiography is the definitive test for identifying the underlying lesion. Innocent murmurs need no treatment or restrictions. Murmurs due to valve disease may require monitoring, medicines, or valve repair or replacement, and some patients need antibiotic prophylaxis considerations and follow-up for endocarditis risk.

In children

Innocent murmurs are detected in a large proportion of children at some point. Classic examples include Still's murmur, a musical vibratory systolic murmur of early childhood, and the venous hum. Most resolve with age and have no effect on health, growth or exercise.
