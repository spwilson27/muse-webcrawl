Pirate radio is radio broadcasting that is unlicensed by the relevant regulatory authorities. The term is most famously associated with the offshore stations of the 1960s, which broadcast pop music to European audiences from ships and disused sea forts in international waters, outside the reach of national broadcast monopolies.

Offshore radio in Europe

The first offshore station was Radio Mercur, which began broadcasting to Denmark from a ship in 1958, followed by Radio Veronica in the Netherlands (1960) and Radio Nord in Sweden. In the United Kingdom, where the BBC held a monopoly and played little pop music, stations such as Radio Caroline (founded 1964) and Radio London attracted audiences of millions. The British government responded with the Marine Broadcasting Offences Act 1967, which cut off their supply and advertising revenue and forced most to close; in their wake the BBC launched Radio 1, hiring many former pirate DJs including John Peel and Tony Blackburn.

Land-based and later pirates

Land-based unlicensed stations have operated in many countries, serving political, religious, or community audiences ignored by licensed media. In London, rooftop FM pirates were crucial to the development of jungle, garage, and grime music from the 1990s onward, with stations such as Deja Vu and Rinse FM (later licensed). In the United States, low-power "microbroadcasters" and shortwave pirates continue despite FCC enforcement.

Regulation

Unlicensed broadcasting is generally illegal because it can interfere with licensed services and emergency communications; penalties range from fines and equipment seizure to imprisonment. Nevertheless, pirate radio persists wherever legal channels are costly or unavailable, and has repeatedly reshaped the music and formats later adopted by legal broadcasters.
