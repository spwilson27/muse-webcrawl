Architecture of Paris

Haussmann buildings — stone façades, zinc roofs, and continuous balconies — define the Paris streetscape.

The architecture of Paris reflects the city's continuous history from Roman Lutetia to the present, and is most famous for the uniform limestone streetscapes created during the renovation of Paris by Baron Haussmann under Napoleon III in the 19th century. Successive eras — medieval Gothic, Renaissance classicism, Baroque, Neoclassicism, Art Nouveau, Art Deco, and modernism — have each left celebrated monuments.

Ancient and medieval Paris

Roman remains include the Arènes de Lutèce amphitheatre and the baths whose ruins stand at the Musée de Cluny. The supreme monument of medieval Paris is Notre-Dame Cathedral (begun 1163), a masterpiece of French Gothic architecture, together with the jewel-like Sainte-Chapelle (1248), built by Louis IX to house relics of the Passion.

Renaissance and classical eras

The Louvre was transformed from a fortress into a Renaissance and then classical palace; its east façade (1667–1670) by Claude Perrault is a landmark of French classicism. The 17th century added the Luxembourg Palace, the domed churches of Val-de-Grâce and Les Invalides (with its great golden dome over Napoleon's tomb), and the planned squares Place des Vosges and Place Vendôme. Under Napoleon I, neoclassical monuments such as the Arc de Triomphe and the church of La Madeleine reshaped the city.

Haussmann's transformation

Between 1853 and 1870, prefect Georges-Eugène Haussmann demolished much of medieval Paris and built wide tree-lined boulevards, sewers, parks, and thousands of apartment buildings. Strict rules governed the Haussmann building: a ground floor and mezzanine, continuous balcony floors, a 45-degree zinc "mansard" roof, and façades aligned to a common cornice height — producing the harmonious, instantly recognisable Parisian streetscape.

Belle Époque to modernism

The Eiffel Tower (1889), built for the Exposition Universelle, became the city's emblem. Art Nouveau flourished in Hector Guimard's Métro entrances and apartment houses; after 1945, modern landmarks included the UNESCO headquarters, the Centre Pompidou (1977) by Piano and Rogers, and François Mitterrand's Grands Projets: the Louvre Pyramid by I. M. Pei, the Grande Arche de la Défense, the Opéra Bastille, and the Bibliothèque nationale's François-Mitterrand site. The business district of La Défense, outside the historic centre, concentrates Paris's skyscrapers, since tall buildings within the city remain tightly regulated.
