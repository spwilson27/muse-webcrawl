Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay (Argentina v. Uruguay)

Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay (Argentina v. Uruguay) was a case decided by the International Court of Justice (ICJ) concerning a dispute between Argentina and Uruguay over the construction of cellulose pulp mills on the Uruguayan bank of the Uruguay River, which forms part of the border between the two countries. The dispute triggered years of protest, including blockades of international bridges by Argentine environmental activists.

Background

In the early 2000s, two companies — the Spanish firm ENCE and the Finnish firm Metsä-Botnia (later UPM) — announced plans to build large pulp mills near Fray Bentos, Uruguay, to process eucalyptus grown in Uruguayan plantations. Residents of the Argentine city of Gualeguaychú, across the river, feared pollution would damage tourism, fishing and health, and organized sustained protests, blockading the General San Martín Bridge for extended periods. Argentina argued that Uruguay had breached the 1975 Statute of the River Uruguay by authorizing the mills without the required prior notification and consultation through the binational commission CARU.

Proceedings

Argentina filed its application with the ICJ in May 2006, asking the Court to declare that Uruguay had violated its procedural and substantive obligations under the 1975 Statute and to order the mills dismantled. ENCE abandoned its project and relocated in 2006, leaving the Botnia (Orion) mill, which began operating in November 2007, as the focus of the case. The Court delivered its judgment on 20 April 2010.

Judgment

The Court held that Uruguay had breached its procedural obligations to inform, notify and negotiate under Articles 7 to 12 of the 1975 Statute. However, it found that Uruguay had not breached its substantive environmental obligations: Argentina had not proven that the Orion mill's discharges harmed the river's water quality or ecosystem. The Court therefore declined to order the mill's closure or dismantling, holding that its declaration of procedural wrongfulness was itself appropriate satisfaction. The judgment is noted for recognizing an environmental impact assessment as a requirement of general international law where a proposed activity risks significant transboundary harm.

Aftermath

Following the judgment, the two governments agreed in 2010 to joint monitoring of the river and the mill through CARU, and the bridge blockades were lifted. Diplomatic tension flared again in 2013 when Uruguay authorized an increase in the mill's production, but the dispute remained within diplomatic channels.
