Kokugaku

RegionJapan
EraEdo period
Main interestsPhilology, ancient Japanese texts, Shinto
Notable figuresKada no Azumamaro, Kamo no Mabuchi, Motoori Norinaga, Hirata Atsutane

Kokugaku (国学, "national study") was an academic movement and school of Japanese philology and philosophy during the Tokugawa period. Kokugaku scholars sought to distinguish a purely Japanese tradition and culture from foreign influences, particularly Chinese and Buddhist thought, by returning to the study of Japan's earliest classical texts.

History

The movement began in the 17th century with figures such as Keichū, a Buddhist monk whose philological work on the Man'yōshū laid groundwork for later scholarship. Kada no Azumamaro, Kamo no Mabuchi, Motoori Norinaga, and Hirata Atsutane are traditionally regarded as the four great men of kokugaku. Norinaga devoted decades to his Kojiki-den, a monumental commentary on the Kojiki, arguing that its texts preserved an ancient "Way of the kami" (Shinto) uncorrupted by Chinese rationalism, and he formulated the aesthetic concept of mono no aware, the pathos of things.

Influence

Hirata Atsutane expanded kokugaku into a populist religious movement, and its emphasis on the imperial institution and a native Japanese essence contributed intellectual foundations to the Sonnō jōi movement and ultimately the Meiji Restoration. In the Meiji period the movement fed into State Shinto and later ultranationalist thought, though its philological achievements — critical editions and analysis of the Kojiki, Nihon Shoki, and Man'yōshū — remain central to Japanese classical studies.
