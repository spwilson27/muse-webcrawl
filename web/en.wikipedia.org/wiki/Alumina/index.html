Alumina is the common name for aluminium oxide, an inorganic compound with the formula Al2O3. It is the most commonly occurring oxide of aluminium and is the principal component of the ore bauxite. In its crystalline form, called corundum, alumina is extremely hard — second only to diamond among common natural materials — and its gem-quality varieties are ruby and sapphire. Alumina is an electrical insulator but a relatively good thermal conductor, and it is amphoteric, reacting with both acids and bases.

Production

Most alumina is refined from bauxite by the Bayer process, in which the ore is digested in hot sodium hydroxide; the aluminium oxides dissolve, impurities are removed as "red mud", and aluminium hydroxide is precipitated and calcined to alumina. The great majority of this "smelter-grade" alumina is reduced to aluminium metal by the Hall–Héroult electrolytic process. A smaller share is made into specialty aluminas for ceramics and other applications.

Uses

Besides aluminium production, alumina is used as an abrasive (emery and grinding media), as a refractory material lining furnaces and kilns, in technical ceramics for spark plugs, cutting tools, and medical implants, as a catalyst and catalyst support in chemical industry, as a filler for plastics, and in water treatment and chromatography. Transparent polycrystalline alumina serves in high-pressure sodium lamp tubes.
