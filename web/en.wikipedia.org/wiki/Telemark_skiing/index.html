Telemark skiing

Telemark skiing is a skiing technique and discipline that combines elements of Alpine and Nordic skiing. Telemark skiers use bindings that attach the boot only at the toe, leaving the heel free, and turn by dropping into a lunge-like position — the telemark turn — with the trailing knee bent toward the ski. The technique is named after the Telemark region of Norway, where it was popularized in the nineteenth century.

History

The telemark turn was developed and demonstrated by Sondre Norheim of Morgedal, Telemark, who is often called the father of modern skiing. Norheim also pioneered the Christiania turn and early ski jumping, and designed bindings that gave the free-heel style its control. Telemark technique dominated skiing until fixed-heel Alpine methods displaced it in the early twentieth century.

The style was revived in the 1970s in the United States, particularly in Colorado, where backcountry skiers adapted it for descending ungroomed terrain on lightweight Nordic equipment. The revival spread internationally, and the FIS has sanctioned a Telemark World Cup circuit since the 1990s.

Technique and equipment

In a telemark turn, the skier advances the outside ski and sinks into a lunge, weighting both skis while the rear heel lifts off the ski. Modern telemark equipment includes stiff plastic boots, cable or NTN (New Telemark Norm) bindings, and Alpine-width skis, a far cry from the narrow cross-country gear of the revival era. Because heels are free, telemark gear also serves for ski touring, usually with climbing skins for ascent.

Competition

FIS telemark racing combines giant-slalom gates with a jump, a 360-degree banked turn (reitur), and a skating section, penalizing skiers who fail to maintain proper telemark form through the gates. The discipline has repeatedly sought, so far unsuccessfully, inclusion in the Winter Olympic Games.
