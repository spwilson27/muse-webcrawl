Compact disc

The compact disc (CD) is a digital optical disc data storage format that was co-developed by Philips and Sony to store and play digital audio recordings. Released in October 1982, it was the first format in the CD family and inaugurated the digital era of consumer audio; the format was later adapted for general data storage as the CD-ROM and for recordable and rewritable media (CD-R and CD-RW). A standard CD measures 120 mm in diameter and holds up to about 74–80 minutes of uncompressed stereo audio, or roughly 650–700 megabytes of data.

Technical standard

The Compact Disc Digital Audio standard, defined in the 1980 "Red Book," specifies audio sampled at 44.1 kHz with 16-bit linear PCM in two channels. Data is encoded with eight-to-fourteen modulation (EFM) and protected by Cross-Interleaved Reed–Solomon Coding (CIRC), which allows small scratches and defects to be corrected or concealed. Information is stored as a spiral of microscopic pits and lands in a reflective aluminum layer, read by a 780 nm infrared laser focused through the polycarbonate substrate.

The 74-minute capacity is famously said to have been chosen so that Beethoven's Ninth Symphony could fit on a single disc — a claim associated with Sony executive Norio Ohga, though its exact origins are debated.

History and decline

Philips and Sony demonstrated prototypes in 1979–1980; the first commercial players and discs appeared in Japan in 1982 and worldwide in 1983, with ABBA's The Visitors among the first titles pressed. CDs overtook vinyl LPs in sales by 1988 and cassettes in the early 1990s, dominating recorded music for two decades. Sales peaked around 2000, then declined with the rise of MP3 file sharing, downloads, and streaming; however, CDs retained niches in some markets (notably Japan) and saw a modest revival alongside the vinyl resurgence.
