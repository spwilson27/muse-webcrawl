Vasa (ship)


Vasa in the Vasa Museum, Stockholm
History
Builder:Stockholm navy yard
Laid down:1626
Launched / maiden voyage:10 August 1628
Fate:Sank on maiden voyage; salvaged 1961

Vasa (often spelled Wasa) is a Swedish warship built between 1626 and 1628. The ship sank after sailing roughly 1,300 m (1,400 yd) into her maiden voyage on 10 August 1628. She fell into obscurity after most of her valuable bronze cannons were salvaged in the 17th century, until she was located again in the late 1950s in a busy shipping lane just off Stockholm harbour. The ship was salvaged with a largely intact hull in 1961 and is now displayed at the Vasa Museum in Stockholm, one of Sweden's most popular tourist attractions.

Construction and sinking

Vasa was ordered by King Gustavus Adolphus as the most powerfully armed ship in the Baltic, carrying 64 bronze cannon on two gun decks. During construction the king pressed for extra guns and an additional deck, while successive changes of shipwright—including the death of the original master, Henrik Hybertsson—left the ship dangerously top-heavy, with too little ballast for her high, narrow hull. On her maiden voyage a gust of wind heeled her over; water rushed in through the open lower gunports and she sank in about 30 metres of water, with the loss of perhaps 30 of the roughly 200 people aboard.

Salvage and preservation

Amateur researcher Anders Franzén located the wreck in 1956, and on 24 April 1961 Vasa was raised in an internationally publicized operation, the hull lifted by cables passed beneath it by divers using water jets. Thousands of artefacts—including sculptures, clothing, weapons, coins and six sails—were recovered, offering an unmatched window into 17th-century life. The ship was sprayed with polyethylene glycol for 17 years to prevent the waterlogged oak from cracking as it dried.

Legacy

Vasa has become an iconic cautionary tale about unstable design, political pressure on engineering, and the absence of proper stability testing: a pre-launch test, in which 30 men ran from side to side to rock the ship, was stopped early because the ship threatened to capsize—yet she was allowed to sail anyway.
