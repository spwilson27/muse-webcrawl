Envy-free division

Envy-free division, also called envy-free cake-cutting, is a kind of fair division problem in which participants with different tastes must divide a resource such that every person believes they have received a fair share — specifically, that no participant prefers another participant's piece to their own. The term was introduced by George Gamow and Marvin Stern in 1958.

Definitions

A division is envy-free if each recipient feels that, by their own measure, no other recipient got more than they did. Envy-freeness is stronger than mere proportionality, in which each of n people is guaranteed at least 1/n of the resource by their own valuation. With two players, the simple "divide and choose" procedure is envy-free; for three or more players the problem becomes substantially harder.

Procedures

For three players, the Selfridge–Conway discrete procedure (developed independently by John Selfridge and John Horton Conway around 1960) achieves an envy-free division of a cake. For four or more players, envy-free procedures were long known only for special cases; a finite but extremely complex protocol for any number of players was published by Aziz and Mackenzie in 2016, following their bounded envy-free protocol for four players.

Applications and related notions

Envy-freeness is a central criterion in fair division theory, applied to inheritance, divorce settlements, allocation of chores and rent division. Related concepts include exact division, equitable division, and "envy-freeness up to one item" (EF1) for indivisible goods, where envy can always be eliminated by removing a single item from the envied bundle.
