Expendable launch system

An expendable launch system (or expendable launch vehicle, ELV) is a launch vehicle that is used only once to carry a payload into space: its stages and components are discarded during flight or destroyed on re-entry, rather than being recovered and flown again. Historically, nearly all orbital launchers — derived from ballistic missiles — were expendable, including the Atlas, Delta, Titan, Ariane, Proton, and Soyuz families.

Characteristics

Expendable vehicles are typically multistage rockets whose boosters and lower stages fall away as their propellant is exhausted, burning up in the atmosphere or falling into the ocean. Because the vehicle need not carry recovery hardware or survive re-entry, expendable designs can maximise payload performance for a given structure, and each launch uses newly manufactured hardware.

Comparison with reusable systems

The principal disadvantage is cost: the entire vehicle is lost on each flight. Reusable launch systems, pioneered operationally by the Space Shuttle (which was partially reusable) and later by SpaceX's Falcon 9 first stages, aim to reduce costs by recovering and reflying major components. Since the 2010s, recoverable first stages have become common in commercial launch, although the upper stages of such vehicles remain expendable. Fully expendable designs continue in wide use for many government and commercial missions.
