Antagonist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An antagonist is a character in a story who is presented as the main enemy or rival of the protagonist and is often their foil. Derived from the Greek antagonistēs ("opponent, competitor"), the term refers to a function in the plot rather than to morality: the antagonist creates or embodies the principal obstacle the protagonist must overcome, generating the story's central conflict.

Role in narrative

The antagonist may be a single character (a villain such as Iago in Othello or Moriarty in the Sherlock Holmes stories), a group or institution, a force of nature, or even an aspect of the protagonist themselves. Not every antagonist is evil, and not every villain is the antagonist: an antagonist simply stands in opposition to the protagonist's goal. In classical literature the hero's adversary often mirrors the hero's qualities, as Satan does for God — and, in some readings, for Adam — in Milton's Paradise Lost.

Types

Common types include the villainous mastermind, the rival with a legitimate claim, the obstructive authority figure, and impersonal antagonists such as disease, society or environment ("man against nature" and "man against society" conflicts). Modern storytelling frequently gives antagonists sympathetic motivations, blurring the line between antagonist and antihero.
