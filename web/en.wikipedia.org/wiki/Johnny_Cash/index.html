Johnny Cash

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Johnny Cash performing at Folsom Prison in 1968: the Man in Black with his guitar before the inmates, in Jim Marshall's era]

John R. Cash (26 February 1932 – 12 September 2003) was an American singer-songwriter, the 'Man in Black', whose deep baritone and spare 'boom-chicka-boom' sound made him an icon far beyond country music. His best-known songs include 'I Walk the Line' (1956), 'Folsom Prison Blues' (1955), 'Ring of Fire' (1963) and 'Man in Black' (1971); his live albums recorded at Folsom Prison (1968) and San Quentin (1969) are landmarks of American music.

Born to poor cotton farmers in Kingsland, Arkansas, and raised in the Dyess Colony, Cash served in the US Air Force in Germany (where he bought his first guitar and intercepted Soviet Morse code, by his account the first American to learn of Stalin's death), then auditioned at Sun Studio in Memphis in 1954.

Sun, Columbia and prison concerts

At Sun, alongside Elvis Presley, Carl Perkins and Jerry Lee Lewis (the 'Million Dollar Quartet' of a famous 1956 jam session), Cash's first records established the outlaw sympathy — Folsom Prison Blues imagines shooting a man in Reno just to watch him die (the song's most infamous line, noted here in summary only) — that his prison concerts made real. Amphetamine addiction wrecked his first marriage and nearly his career in the sixties; June Carter, who co-wrote 'Ring of Fire' about their forbidden love, married him in 1968 and, by his account, saved his life.

The American recordings

Dropped by labels in the eighties, Cash was resurrected by producer Rick Rubin: the American Recordings series (1994–2010) stripped him to voice and guitar, covering songs from Nine Inch Nails ('Hurt', whose video is routinely called one of the greatest ever made) to standards, and won him a young audience. He died four months after June, in 2003; the biopic Walk the Line (2005) carried the story worldwide.
