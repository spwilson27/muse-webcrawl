Twin-lens reflex camera

A twin-lens reflex camera (TLR) is a type of camera with two objective lenses of the same focal length. One lens forms the image on the film ("taking" lens); the other serves the viewfinder system, reflecting its image via a mirror onto a ground-glass focusing screen viewed from above. The two lenses are coupled so that the focus shown on the screen is the same as that on the film.

History

The design was popularized by the German Rolleiflex of 1929, whose quality and handling made the TLR the standard professional medium format camera for decades. Inexpensive TLRs such as the Lubitel were produced in the Soviet Union, and Japanese makers including Yashica and Minolta built well-regarded models. Most TLRs use 120 roll film, producing 6×6 cm images.

Characteristics

TLRs offer a large, bright waist-level viewing image, quiet operation (no mirror slap), and continuous viewing during exposure, since the viewing lens never blacks out. Their disadvantages include parallax error at close range, a laterally reversed finder image, bulk, and — in most models — fixed, non-interchangeable lenses. The design was largely displaced by single-lens reflex and rangefinder cameras but retains a following among medium-format film enthusiasts.
