Dithering

Dithering is an intentionally applied form of noise used to randomize quantization error, preventing large-scale patterns such as color banding in images. The term dither was published in books on analog computation and hydraulically controlled guns in World War II. Though he did not use the term dither, the concept of adding noise to reduce errors in ADCs can be traced back to a 1961 paper by Lawrence G. Roberts, in which he described a technique he called picture coding.

Digital audio

In digital audio, dither is added before any quantization or re-quantization process, in order to de-correlate the quantization noise from the input signal and to prevent non-linear behavior such as distortion. The amount of dither required depends on the depth of the final bit depth: roughly 1 LSB of triangular probability density function (TPDF) dither is standard practice for word-length reduction.

Digital images

Dithering is used in computer graphics to create the illusion of color depth in images displaying a limited color palette. In a dithered image, colors not available in the palette are approximated by a diffusion of colored pixels from within the available palette. The human eye perceives the diffusion as a mixture of the colors within it. Common algorithms include the Floyd–Steinberg algorithm, which diffuses error to neighboring pixels using error diffusion, and ordered dithering, which applies a fixed threshold map or Bayer matrix.
