Adhesive

Adhesives bond materials through adhesion to their surfaces and internal cohesion.

Adhesive, also known as glue, cement, mucilage or paste, is any non-metallic substance applied to one or both surfaces of two separate items that binds them together and resists their separation. Adhesives offer advantages over mechanical fasteners, including distributing stress over a joint, joining dissimilar or thin materials, and sealing against fluids.

Mechanisms

Bonding is explained by adhesion — the attraction between the adhesive and the substrate, involving wetting of the surface, van der Waals forces, chemical bonding and mechanical interlocking with surface pores — and cohesion, the internal strength of the adhesive itself.

Types

Adhesives are classified by chemistry (epoxy, polyurethane, cyanoacrylate "superglue", polyvinyl acetate wood glue, silicone), by setting mechanism (drying, heat, pressure-sensitive, UV or anaerobic curing, and hot-melt), and by origin. Natural adhesives such as starch, casein, animal glues and bitumen have been used since prehistory — Neanderthals produced birch-bark tar — while modern synthetic adhesives date from the early 20th century. Pressure-sensitive adhesives are used in tapes and sticky notes; structural adhesives are used in aircraft, automobiles and construction.
