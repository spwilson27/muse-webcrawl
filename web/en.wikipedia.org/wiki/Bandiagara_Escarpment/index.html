The Bandiagara Escarpment is a sandstone cliff formation in the Mopti region of Mali, West Africa, running for roughly 150 kilometres (about 90 miles) and rising in places some 500 metres above the sandy plain below. It is the heartland of the Dogon people and one of the most striking landscapes in West Africa.

Geography and geology

The escarpment stretches from the south near the town of Bandiagara northeastwards toward Douentza. It is composed of ancient sandstones resting on a basement of older rock, part of the Taoudeni basin. Erosion has carved the cliff into ledges, caves and debris slopes (talus) at its base, creating hundreds of distinct settlement sites.

History and culture

The cliffs were inhabited by the Tellem people from roughly the eleventh to the sixteenth centuries; their dwellings and granaries, built high into cliff faces, still survive. The Dogon migrated into the area from the fourteenth century onward, settling both on the plateau above and in villages at the base of the cliff, partly for defence. Dogon culture is renowned for its elaborate cosmology, masked dances (dama), distinctive architecture of mud-brick granaries with conical thatched roofs, and the toguna (men's meeting houses) found in every village.

World Heritage status

In 1989 UNESCO inscribed the "Cliff of Bandiagara (Land of the Dogons)" as a mixed cultural and natural World Heritage Site, recognising both the landscape and the living traditions of the Dogon and the earlier Tellem. Tourism to the region flourished in the 1990s and 2000s but declined sharply after insecurity spread across central Mali in the 2010s.
