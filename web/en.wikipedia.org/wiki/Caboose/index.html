Caboose

TypeRailroad car
RoleCrew accommodation and monitoring
Era of widespread useLate 19th to late 20th century

A caboose is a crewed railroad car that was traditionally carried at the end of a freight train. It provided accommodation for the conductor and brakemen, who monitored the train for problems such as dragging equipment, shifting loads, and overheating axle bearings (hot boxes).

Cabooses were typically painted in bright colours — often the red that made them an iconic image of North American railroading — and featured a raised cupola or bay windows for observing the train.

History

The caboose emerged in the mid-19th century from converted boxcars and tool cars. By the early 20th century the purpose-built cupola caboose was standard on North American freight trains, serving as an office, bunkhouse, and lookout.

Decline

From the 1980s, electronic end-of-train devices (EOTDs or FREDs) replaced the caboose's monitoring functions, defect detectors lineside automated inspections, and crew sizes shrank. Most railroads ended routine caboose use by the end of the 1980s, though some survive in local and yard service or as shoving platforms.

Preservation

Retired cabooses are widely preserved in museums, parks, and as novelty lodging, and remain a popular subject for model railroaders.
