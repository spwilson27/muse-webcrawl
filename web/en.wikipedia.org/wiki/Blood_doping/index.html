Blood doping

Blood doping is a form of doping in which the number of red blood cells in the bloodstream is boosted in order to enhance athletic performance. Because red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the muscles, a higher concentration in the blood can improve an athlete's aerobic capacity and stamina. Blood doping is prohibited by the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA).

Methods

The three most common methods are blood transfusions, injections of erythropoietin (EPO), and injections of synthetic oxygen carriers. In autologous transfusion, an athlete's own blood is drawn, stored (often frozen), and re-infused before competition; in homologous transfusion, blood from a matched donor is used. EPO, a hormone naturally produced by the kidneys, stimulates red-cell production and became widespread in endurance sports in the 1990s before a urine test was introduced at the 2000 Sydney Olympics.

Detection and risks

Detection relies on tests for recombinant EPO, markers of stored blood, and the longitudinal Athlete Biological Passport, which tracks blood variables over time to flag abnormal changes. Risks include blood clots, stroke and heart attack from thickened blood, infections from transfusions, and immune reactions to donor blood.

Notable cases

Blood doping scandals have centred on professional cycling — including the Festina affair of 1998, Operación Puerto in 2006, and the USADA case against Lance Armstrong — as well as on cross-country skiing and distance running.
