Transshipment (sometimes also trans-shipment) is the shipment of goods or containers to an intermediate destination, where they are unloaded and transferred to another vessel or vehicle, before travelling on to their final destination. It is a central feature of modern container shipping, in which large vessels serve major hub ports and smaller feeder vessels distribute cargo to regional ports.

Transshipment at sea

In container shipping, transshipment allows carriers to consolidate cargo from many origins onto high-capacity "trunk" routes between hub ports. Ports such as Singapore, Rotterdam and Busan handle enormous transshipment volumes — in Singapore's case, the large majority of containers handled are transshipped rather than originating or terminating locally. Hub-and-spoke networks improve vessel utilisation but add handling time and make cargo vulnerable to delays at the hub.

Transshipment at land borders

Transshipment also occurs where a change of rail gauge, vehicle, or legal regime interrupts a journey — for example where freight moving between China and Europe is transferred between standard-gauge and broad-gauge wagons at border terminals. Customs-bonded transshipment allows cargo to pass through an intermediate country without clearing customs there.

Illegal transshipment

The term is also used for the transfer of goods at sea — including oil, fish catches and sanctioned commodities — from one vessel to another ("ship-to-ship transfer") to obscure their origin. Transshipment of fish at sea is a particular concern in fisheries enforcement, as it can conceal illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing.
