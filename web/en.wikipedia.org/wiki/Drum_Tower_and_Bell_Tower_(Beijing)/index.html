Drum Tower and Bell Tower (Beijing)

The Drum Tower (Chinese: 鼓楼; pinyin: Gǔlóu) and Bell Tower (Chinese: 钟楼; pinyin: Zhōnglóu) of Beijing stand at the northern end of the central axis of the city's Inner City, north of Di'anmen Street. The two towers originally served as the official timepieces of the city, announcing the hours with drumbeats and bell chimes, and were central to the rhythm of daily life in the capital during the Yuan, Ming, and Qing dynasties.

History

The first Drum Tower was built in 1272, during the Yuan dynasty, when the city was known as Khanbaliq. The Bell Tower was constructed nearby soon after. Both buildings were destroyed and rebuilt several times; the present Drum Tower dates largely to the Ming dynasty, while the Bell Tower was rebuilt in brick in the Qing dynasty after a fire destroyed its wooden predecessor in 1747.

The towers marked the beginning and end of the day's watches. The bell was struck and the drums beaten at set intervals through the night, with the final drum performance at dawn signalling the opening of the city gates. The practice of official timekeeping from the towers continued until 1924, when the last Qing emperor, Puyi, left the Forbidden City.

Architecture

The Drum Tower is a two-storey timber and masonry structure about 47 metres high, its upper floor once holding 25 drums, of which only one original survives. The Bell Tower, about 48 metres high, is built entirely of brick and stone and houses a huge bronze bell weighing roughly 63 tonnes, cast in the Ming dynasty; its sound was said to carry for kilometres across the flat city.
