The automotive industry crisis of 2008–2009 was a collapse of global car sales and manufacturer solvency during the Great Recession. In the United States, light-vehicle sales fell from about 16 million a year to an annualised rate below 9 million by early 2009. General Motors and Chrysler entered Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2009 and were restructured with tens of billions of dollars of government support, while Ford survived without a bailout. The crisis accelerated plant closures, brand terminations and a long-term shift in the industry.

Causes

The Detroit Three had become dependent on high-margin trucks and SUVs; the 2008 spike in fuel prices abruptly shifted demand to smaller cars they could not supply competitively. The September 2008 financial crisis then froze consumer credit and dealer floorplan financing, and sales collapsed across all manufacturers. Years of high legacy costs — pensions and retiree health care for a large retired workforce — and loss-making finance arms compounded the American firms' weakness, while European and Asian manufacturers faced their own demand shocks.

Government responses

In December 2008 the US Treasury extended bridge loans to GM and Chrysler from the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) after Congress declined to pass an auto-specific bill. The Obama administration's Automotive Task Force, led by Steven Rattner, imposed managed restructurings: Chrysler filed for bankruptcy on 30 April 2009 and emerged in June in partnership with Fiat, and GM filed on 1 June 2009, emerging in July as a new company majority-owned by the US Treasury. Brands including Pontiac, Saturn, Hummer and Saab were discontinued or sold, thousands of dealerships were terminated, and UAW contracts were renegotiated.

Other governments introduced scrappage schemes paying consumers to replace old cars, such as Germany's Abwrackprämie, France's prime à la casse and the US "Cash for Clunkers" programme (CARS) of mid-2009, which briefly revived sales. Japan, South Korea, China and other states provided loans, tax breaks or purchase incentives; Chinese sales surged, making China the world's largest car market in 2009.

Aftermath

US sales recovered gradually to over 17 million by 2015–2016. The Treasury ultimately recovered most of its outlay, with a net loss commonly estimated at about $9–12 billion against roughly $80 billion disbursed to automakers and suppliers; supporters argued the rescue saved over a million jobs. The crisis reshaped the industry toward smaller, more fuel-efficient vehicles, electrification commitments and consolidated global platforms.
