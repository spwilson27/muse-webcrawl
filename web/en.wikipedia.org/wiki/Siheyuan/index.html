Siheyuan

A siheyuan (Chinese: 四合院; pinyin: sìhéyuàn) is a traditional Chinese courtyard residence, most closely associated with Beijing and northern China, in which single-storey buildings or rooms on four sides enclose a central courtyard. Siheyuan range from modest single-courtyard compounds to the multi-courtyard mansions of officials and merchants; together with the hutong lanes between them, they formed the historic urban fabric of old Beijing.

Layout

The classic siheyuan is arranged on a north–south axis, entered from the south-east corner through a gatehouse (often with a screening wall, yingbi, inside). The principal building on the north side (zhengfang), facing south across the courtyard, housed the head of the family and ancestral rites; the eastern and western wings (xiangfang) accommodated younger generations, and the southern range (daozuofang) contained reception rooms, studies and servants' quarters. The plan embodied Confucian principles of hierarchy, privacy and orientation, and feng shui considerations: rooms faced inward, presenting a blank wall to the street.

History and decline

The form descends from courtyard houses of the Western Zhou and Han dynasties and reached its mature form under the Ming and Qing dynasties, when Beijing's residential districts were laid out in hutong grids. Famous surviving examples include the Prince Gong Mansion (Gong Wang Fu), the former residences of Song Qingling and of the writer Lao She's birthplace quarter, and the great mansion compounds of Shanxi merchants such as the Qiao family compound in Pingyao.

After 1949 many siheyuan were subdivided among multiple households as dazayuan ("large miscellaneous courtyards"), with accretion of makeshift structures in the yards. From the 1990s, large-scale redevelopment demolished most of old Beijing's siheyuan districts; surviving courtyard houses have become highly valuable real estate, and some districts—notably around Shichahai—are protected, with renovated siheyuan serving as homes, hotels and cultural venues.

Regional variants

Analogous courtyard houses exist throughout China: the narrower "sky-well" (tianjing) courtyards of the south, the courtyard compounds of Shanxi with their high decorated walls, Hakka enclosed houses, and the tulou of Fujian represent regional adaptations of the courtyard principle to climate and defence.
