Soil acidity (soil pH)

Soil acidity is measured by soil pH, the negative logarithm of the hydrogen ion activity in a soil suspension. Soil pH is considered a master variable in soil science because it affects the chemical, physical, and biological properties of soil and the availability of plant nutrients. Most soils have pH values between 3.5 and 10; rainforest soils tend to be acidic, while soils in arid regions are often alkaline.

Causes of soil acidity

Soils become acidic through several processes. Rainfall and leaching remove basic cations (calcium, magnesium, potassium, and sodium) over time. Decomposition of organic matter produces carbon dioxide and organic acids. Nitrification of ammonium-based fertilisers releases hydrogen ions, and the removal of harvested plant material exports alkalinity from the field. Acid rain and the oxidation of sulfidic materials (as in acid sulfate soils) can acidify soils further.

Effects on plants

At low pH, aluminium and manganese can reach concentrations toxic to plant roots, while essential nutrients such as phosphorus, calcium, molybdenum, and magnesium become less available. Most agricultural crops grow best between pH 6.0 and 7.0, though some plants, such as blueberries, azaleas, and potatoes, prefer moderately acidic soils. Soil pH also influences microbial communities, earthworm activity, and the efficacy of certain herbicides.

Amending soil pH

Acidic soils are commonly amended with lime — ground limestone (calcium carbonate) or dolomitic lime (calcium magnesium carbonate) — which neutralises acidity. The amount required depends on the soil's buffering capacity, measured by a buffer pH test. Alkaline soils may be acidified with elemental sulfur, which soil bacteria oxidise to sulfuric acid, or with acidifying fertilisers.

Measurement

Soil pH is measured in a suspension of soil in water or in a salt solution such as calcium chloride, using a pH electrode or indicator dyes. Field test kits give approximate values; laboratory electrode measurement is more precise.
