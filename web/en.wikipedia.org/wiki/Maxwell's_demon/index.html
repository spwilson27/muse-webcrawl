Maxwell's demon is a thought experiment that would hypothetically violate the second law of thermodynamics. It was proposed by the physicist James Clerk Maxwell in 1867. In his first letter proposing the argument, Maxwell referred to the hypothetical being as a "finite being" — William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) would later call it a "demon" in the journal Nature.

In the thought experiment, a demon controls a door between two chambers of gas. As individual gas molecules (or atoms) approach the door, the demon quickly opens and closes the door to allow only fast-moving molecules to pass through in one direction, and only slow-moving molecules to pass through in the other. As faster-than-average molecules are selectively allowed to pass into one chamber while slower molecules are allowed into the other, the temperature difference between the chambers increases, reducing the overall entropy of the gas while expending no work.

Origin and history

The thought experiment first appeared in a letter Maxwell wrote to Peter Guthrie Tait on 11 December 1867. It appeared again in Maxwell's book Theory of Heat (1871) in a chapter on the "Limitations of the Second Law of Thermodynamics". Maxwell intended to show that the second law has only a statistical certainty.

Criticism and developments

Several physicists have presented calculations that show the second law is not actually violated by a physical demon. In 1929 Leó Szilárd designed the "Szilard engine", a one-molecule version of the experiment, and argued the demon must expend energy to measure molecular speeds. In 1961 Rolf Landauer argued that erasing information has an unavoidable thermodynamic cost (Landauer's principle); in 1982 Charles Bennett showed that the demon must eventually erase the information it gathers, paying the entropy cost and saving the second law.

Real-life applications and experiments

Experimental realisations of information-powered devices, sometimes called information engines, have been built using colloidal particles in optical traps, single-electron boxes, and feedback-controlled quantum dots. In 2010 researchers at Chuo University in Japan demonstrated an information-to-energy conversion consistent with the generalised Jarzynski equality.
