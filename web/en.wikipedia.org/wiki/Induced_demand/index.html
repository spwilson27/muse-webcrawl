Induced demand

Induced demand
FieldTransport economics, urban planning
Related conceptDowns–Thomson paradox; Braess's paradox

Induced demand — or, in transport planning, induced traffic — is the phenomenon whereby an increase in supply of a good or service (such as road capacity) leads to an increase in its consumption, partly or wholly offsetting the intended benefit. In the case of roads, expanding a congested highway lowers the time cost of driving, which encourages people to drive more, take longer trips, switch routes and modes, and promotes car-dependent development, so congestion often returns toward its previous level.

Evidence

The idea was articulated by planners from the 1930s onward and formalised by Anthony Downs in his 1962 "law of peak-hour expressway convergence." A major 1994 report by the UK government's Standing Advisory Committee on Trunk Road Assessment (SACTRA) confirmed that new road capacity generates additional traffic. Econometric studies, notably Duranton and Turner's "fundamental law of road congestion" (2011), found that vehicle-kilometres travelled increase roughly in proportion to lane-kilometres in US metropolitan areas — an elasticity near one.

Implications

Induced demand is a central argument in debates over highway expansion, and conversely underlies "traffic evaporation" observed when road space is removed. The concept also appears in other domains: more efficient lighting can increase energy use for lighting (a form of Jevons paradox), and added hospital or broadband capacity can reveal latent demand. Critics note that induced travel is not necessarily welfare-reducing, since the new trips themselves have value; the dispute is over whether expansion is an effective remedy for congestion.
