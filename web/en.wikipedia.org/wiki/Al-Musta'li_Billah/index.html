Al-Mustaʿlī bi-llāh (Arabic: المستعلي بالله, lit. "the one raised up by God"), born Abū al-Qāsim Aḥmad (1074–1101), was the ninth Fatimid caliph, reigning in Egypt from 1094 to 1101, and the nineteenth Imam according to Musta'li Isma'ili tradition. His accession precipitated the Nizari–Musta'li schism, the most consequential split in Isma'ili history, whose two branches survive to the present day.

Accession and the Nizari schism

Al-Musta'li was the youngest son of the long-reigning caliph al-Mustansir Billah. When his father died in 1094, the all-powerful vizier al-Afdal Shahanshah — whose sister was married to Ahmad — enthroned him in Cairo on the same day, setting aside the claims of the elder brother Nizar, whom many considered the designated heir (naṣṣ). Nizar revolted from Alexandria in 1095 but was captured and, according to most accounts, immured. His adherents in Persia, led by Hasan-i Sabbah from the fortress of Alamut, refused to recognise al-Musta'li, becoming the Nizari Isma'ilis; the Fatimid establishment and the da'wa in Yemen and India upheld al-Musta'li, becoming the Musta'lis.

Rule under al-Afdal

Like his later Fatimid contemporaries, al-Musta'li was largely a figurehead. Government was conducted by al-Afdal, who controlled the army, finances and foreign policy. Internally, Egypt remained comparatively prosperous and administratively stable under al-Afdal's management, despite the severing of the Persian da'wa and mounting disorder in Syria, where Seljuk princes and local rulers fragmented.

The First Crusade

The arrival of the First Crusade transformed the politics of his reign. Fatimid forces under al-Afdal took Jerusalem from its Seljuk-aligned Artuqid rulers in August 1098. Fatimid envoys, who appear to have regarded the Crusaders as potential Byzantine-style mercenaries or allies against the Seljuks, negotiated with them during the siege of Antioch. The misunderstanding collapsed: the Crusaders stormed Jerusalem in July 1099, and in August al-Afdal's relief army was routed at the Battle of Ascalon. The Fatimids retained Ascalon and parts of the coast for another half-century, launching repeated expeditions, but the interior of Palestine was lost.

Death and succession

Al-Musta'li died in December 1101, aged only twenty-seven; rumours of poisoning by al-Afdal were reported by later chroniclers but are unverifiable. He was succeeded by his young son al-Amir bi-Ahkam Allah, born in 1096, under al-Afdal's continued regency. The Musta'li imamate continued through al-Amir and, in Bohra belief, passed into occultation with his infant son al-Tayyib, represented on earth by the Da'i al-Mutlaq.
