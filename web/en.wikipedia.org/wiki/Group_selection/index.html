Group selection

Group selection is a proposed mechanism of evolution in which natural selection acts at the level of the group, rather than at the level of the individual organism or gene. Traits that are harmful to an individual may spread if they benefit the group to which the individual belongs—for example, through increased group survival or reproduction.

History

Charles Darwin suggested group selection as an explanation for human moral traits in The Descent of Man (1871). The idea was popularized in the 20th century by V. C. Wynne-Edwards, who argued in Animal Dispersion in Relation to Social Behaviour (1962) that animals restrain their reproduction for the good of the population. In the 1960s and 1970s the concept was widely criticized by George C. Williams, John Maynard Smith, and Richard Dawkins, who showed that most supposed cases could be explained by individual selection or kin selection.

Modern debate

Interest revived with multilevel selection theory, developed by David Sloan Wilson and Elliott Sober, which formalizes selection acting simultaneously at multiple levels. Whether group selection is a significant force—particularly in human cultural evolution and the evolution of eusociality—remains an active scientific debate.
