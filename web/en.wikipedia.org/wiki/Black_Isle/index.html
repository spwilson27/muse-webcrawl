The Black Isle (Scottish Gaelic: An t-Eilean Dubh) is a peninsula in the Highlands of Scotland, lying between the Moray Firth to the east, the Cromarty Firth to the north, and the Beauly Firth to the south. Despite its name it is neither black nor an island: the "black" is traditionally said to come from its dark, fertile soil and its appearance—free of snow while surrounding hills are white—or simply from uncultivated black moorland, and it is joined to the mainland at its western end.

Geography and settlements

The peninsula is about 25 kilometres long. Its main settlements are the former cathedral town of Fortrose, neighbouring Rosemarkie on the east coast, the fishing village of Avoch, and the historic port of Cromarty at the northern tip, a well-preserved example of an 18th–19th-century burgh. Chanonry Point, near Fortrose, is famous as one of the best places in Britain to watch bottlenose dolphins from shore. The Kessock Bridge (1982) links the Black Isle to Inverness across the Beauly Firth.

History

Fortrose Cathedral, founded in the 13th century, was the seat of the bishops of Ross. The Black Isle is rich in Pictish carved stones, including examples at Rosemarkie. The geologist and writer Hugh Miller was born in Cromarty in 1802; his cottage is now a museum. Agriculture remains prominent—some of Scotland's most fertile arable land—and the peninsula is known for its barley, much of it destined for whisky distilling.
