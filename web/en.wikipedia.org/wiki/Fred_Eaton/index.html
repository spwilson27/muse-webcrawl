Frederick Eaton (1856 – March 11, 1934) was an American engineer, rancher and politician, the 11th mayor of Los Angeles, California, serving from 1898 to 1900. He is best remembered as the political promoter of the Los Angeles Aqueduct and as the man who introduced the engineer William Mulholland to the Owens Valley water project — and later quarrelled with both Mulholland and the city over the profits of his land and water rights.


Fred Eaton

Early career

Eaton was born in Los Angeles in 1856, the son of a judge. Largely self-taught in engineering, he rose to become superintendent of the private Los Angeles City Water Company, where he hired and mentored the young William Mulholland. As mayor, he championed the municipal takeover of the water system, completed shortly after his term.

The Owens Valley project

Eaton envisioned Los Angeles supplied by the Owens River, some 200 miles away. Posing as a rancher (and at times as a federal reclamation agent), he quietly bought land and water rights in the Owens Valley, intending to sell the crucial rights — especially his Long Valley reservoir site — to the city at a large profit. He brought Mulholland to see the river in 1904, persuading him of the scheme, and the city built the Los Angeles Aqueduct (1908–1913).

Eaton expected to become wealthy from the Long Valley deal, but the city refused his price. The feud embittered both men. Eaton held onto Long Valley for decades, blocking the city's plans for a large storage reservoir there. In 1928 the collapse of the St. Francis Dam destroyed Mulholland's career; Eaton went bankrupt in the 1930s when his valley properties were finally sold under pressure, and he died in 1934.
