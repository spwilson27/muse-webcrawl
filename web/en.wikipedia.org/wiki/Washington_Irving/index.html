Washington Irving (April 3, 1783 – November 28, 1859) was an American short-story writer, essayist, biographer, historian, and diplomat of the early 19th century. He wrote the short stories "Rip Van Winkle" (1819) and "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow" (1820), both of which appear in his collection The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent. His historical works include biographies of Oliver Goldsmith, Muhammad, and George Washington, as well as histories of 15th-century Spain dealing with subjects such as the Alhambra, Christopher Columbus, and the Moors. Irving served as American ambassador to Spain in the 1840s.

Life

Irving was born in New York City on April 3, 1783, the same week the city learned of the ceasefire ending the American Revolutionary War, and was named after George Washington. Trained as a lawyer, he preferred writing, producing satirical essays for his brother's newspaper and, in 1809, the comic A History of New York under the pseudonym Diedrich Knickerbocker. From 1815 to 1832 he lived in Europe, where The Sketch Book (1819–20) made him the first American writer to achieve international literary fame, admired by authors including Walter Scott, Lord Byron, and Charles Dickens.

Later career

Returning to the United States, Irving settled at Sunnyside, his estate in Tarrytown, New York. He wrote histories of Columbus, the conquest of Granada, and a five-volume biography of George Washington, and encouraged younger American writers such as Nathaniel Hawthorne and Edgar Allan Poe. He served as U.S. minister to Spain from 1842 to 1846. Irving died of a heart attack at Sunnyside on November 28, 1859, and was buried in Sleepy Hollow Cemetery.

Legacy

Irving is regarded as one of the founders of American literature and a pioneer of the short story as a literary form. His fictional Dutch New York helped create enduring elements of American folklore and popular culture, including the figure of Santa Claus ("Saint Nicholas") and the nickname "Gotham" for New York City.
