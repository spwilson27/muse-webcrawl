Battle of Beda Fomm

The Battle of Beda Fomm took place between 6 and 7 February 1941, during Operation Compass, the first great British offensive of the Western Desert campaign in the Second World War. A small, fast-moving British force — "Combe Force" of the 7th Armoured Division — cut across the desert to block the coast road at Beda Fomm, south of Benghazi, and intercepted the retreating Italian Tenth Army, which was destroyed after desperate attempts to break through.

Background

Operation Compass, launched in December 1940 by General Richard O'Connor's Western Desert Force, routed the Italian Tenth Army in Egypt and captured Bardia and Tobruk. As the Italians retreated along the Libyan coast toward Tripoli, O'Connor gambled on sending the 7th Armoured Division across the waterless interior of the Jebel Akhdar to cut the coast road south of Benghazi, while the Australian 6th Division pursued along the coast.

Battle

Combe Force — about 2,000–3,000 men with armored cars, anti-tank guns and artillery, commanded by Lt. Col. John Combe — reached the coast road near Beda Fomm on 5 February and established a blocking position. Over the next two days the head of the Italian retreat, including the Babini armored brigade with around 100 tanks, made repeated, costly attacks to break through. Supported as the rest of the 7th Armoured Division arrived, the British held; on the morning of 7 February the Italians surrendered. Some 25,000 prisoners were taken at Beda Fomm alone.

Significance

Beda Fomm completed the destruction of the Italian Tenth Army. In ten weeks Operation Compass had advanced 800 km and captured about 130,000 prisoners at a cost of some 2,000 British casualties. The victory was soon overshadowed by the diversion of British forces to Greece and the arrival of Rommel's Afrika Korps, which reversed the gains in the spring of 1941.
