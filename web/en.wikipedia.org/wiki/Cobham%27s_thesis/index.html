Cobham's thesis

Cobham's thesis, also known as the Cobham–Edmonds thesis, is a principle in computational complexity theory stating that a problem is computationally feasible if and only if it can be solved in polynomial time. It is named after Alan Cobham, who discussed the idea in 1965, and is associated with Jack Edmonds's work on polynomial-time algorithms.

Significance

The thesis underlies the identification of the complexity class P with "efficiently solvable" problems and motivates the study of NP-completeness: problems in NP not known to be in P are regarded as intractable in practice. Critics note exceptions, such as polynomial algorithms with huge exponents or constants, quantum algorithms like Shor's algorithm, and problems easy on average despite bad worst cases.
