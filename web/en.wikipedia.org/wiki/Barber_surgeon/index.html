Barber surgeon

The barber surgeon was one of the most common medical practitioners of medieval and early modern Europe, generally charged with caring for soldiers during or after battle, and for the routine surgical needs of civilians. Barber surgeons were expert in the use of the razor and lancet, and their duties extended from haircuts and shaving to bloodletting, leeching, cupping, tooth extraction, lancing abscesses, and treating wounds, fractures, and skin diseases.

Origins

The combination of trades arose partly from ecclesiastical rules: clergy and monks, who long provided much medical care, were forbidden by Church councils from shedding blood, and monastic tonsures required regular shaving, so monastic servants and barbers took over both grooming and minor surgery. University-trained physicians considered manual surgery beneath their dignity and referred such work to the barbers' guilds. Ambroise Paré, the celebrated French surgeon of the sixteenth century and a founder of modern surgery, began his career as a barber surgeon.

Guilds and separation

In London, barbers and surgeons were joined in a single company by an Act of Parliament in 1540—the United Barber-Surgeons' Company, famously depicted with Henry VIII in a painting attributed to Holbein's circle. As surgery professionalized, the union dissolved: in 1745 British surgeons split to form what became the Royal College of Surgeons, and barbers were thereafter restricted to grooming. Similar separations occurred across Europe through the eighteenth century, though barber surgeons continued in armies and rural areas for decades.

Legacy

The red-and-white barber's pole, commonly explained as representing blood and bandages (with the pole itself the staff gripped during bloodletting), is the most enduring vestige of the trade.
