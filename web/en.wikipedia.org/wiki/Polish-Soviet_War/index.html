The Polish–Soviet War (1919–1921) was an armed conflict between the Second Polish Republic and Soviet Russia (RSFSR) over control of the borderlands of Ukraine and Belarus following the collapse of the Russian, German, and Austro-Hungarian empires after the First World War. Polish head of state Józef Piłsudski sought to rebuild a Polish-led federation in Eastern Europe (the Międzymorze concept), while the Bolshevik leadership sought both to recover former imperial territories and to carry communist revolution westward into Germany.

Course of the war

Fighting began in 1919 as Polish and Red Army units advanced into the vacuum left by withdrawing German forces. In 1920 Poland, allied with Symon Petliura's Ukrainian People's Republic, launched the Kyiv offensive (April–May 1920) and briefly occupied Kyiv. The Soviet counteroffensive under Mikhail Tukhachevsky drove the Poles back across hundreds of kilometres to the gates of Warsaw, while a second Soviet army advanced through Ukraine toward Lwów.

Battle of Warsaw

In August 1920 the tide turned at the Battle of Warsaw, celebrated in Poland as the "Miracle on the Vistula". A Polish counterstroke from the Wieprz River, planned by Piłsudski, struck the flank of the overextended Soviet Western Front and routed it; thousands of Red Army soldiers were killed, captured, or interned in East Prussia. Polish forces followed up with victory at the Battle of the Niemen River in September. Lord D'Abernon called Warsaw the eighteenth decisive battle of world history, judging that it halted the westward spread of Bolshevism into a Europe in turmoil.

Treaty of Riga

An armistice took effect in October 1920, and the Treaty of Riga (18 March 1921) fixed the Polish–Soviet border roughly 250 km east of the Curzon Line, dividing Ukrainian and Belarusian lands between Poland and the Soviet republics. The settlement left large ethnic minorities within Poland and contributed to the tensions that shaped interwar Eastern Europe.
