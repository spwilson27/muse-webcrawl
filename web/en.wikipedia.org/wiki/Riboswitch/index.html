Riboswitch

TypeCis-regulatory RNA element
LocationUsually the 5′ untranslated region of mRNA
Typical ligandsMetabolites, coenzymes, amino acids, ions, nucleotides
Found inMainly bacteria; also some archaea, fungi and plants

In molecular biology, a riboswitch is a regulatory segment of a messenger RNA molecule that binds a small molecule, resulting in a change in production of the proteins encoded by the mRNA. In this way, an mRNA that contains a riboswitch is directly involved in regulating its own activity, in response to the concentration of its effector molecule. The discovery that modern organisms use RNA to bind small molecules — a capacity once thought to belong solely to proteins — was an important part of the evidence that RNA-based regulation is ancient.

Structure and mechanism

A riboswitch consists of two functional domains. The aptamer domain folds into a precise three-dimensional pocket that binds the target ligand with high specificity — often distinguishing between molecules that differ by a single atom. The expression platform changes shape depending on whether the aptamer is occupied, and this conformational switch alters gene expression. In bacteria the usual outcomes are premature termination of transcription (formation of an intrinsic terminator hairpin) or sequestration of the Shine–Dalgarno sequence to block translation initiation. Some riboswitches act as ribozymes, cleaving themselves when bound to their ligand.

Classes

Dozens of riboswitch classes are known, sensing ligands that include coenzymes (thiamine pyrophosphate, flavin mononucleotide, cobalamin, S-adenosylmethionine), amino acids (lysine, glycine), nucleobases and nucleotides (guanine, adenine, cyclic di-GMP, ppGpp), metal ions (magnesium, manganese, fluoride) and others. The thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) riboswitch is the only one so far found in eukaryotes, where it regulates alternative splicing in fungi, algae and plants.

Discovery

Although unexplained observations of metabolite-dependent mRNA regulation accumulated during the 1990s, riboswitches were first definitively demonstrated in 2002 by Ronald Breaker's laboratory at Yale and by other groups, which showed that conserved RNA elements in the leaders of vitamin biosynthesis genes bound thiamine pyrophosphate, FMN and cobalamin directly, without any protein intermediary.

Applications

Because many riboswitches control essential metabolic genes in pathogenic bacteria and are absent from humans, they are attractive targets for new antibacterial drugs; the compound ribocil was developed to target the FMN riboswitch. Engineered riboswitches and related aptamer-based switches are also used in synthetic biology as genetically encoded sensors and gene-control devices.
