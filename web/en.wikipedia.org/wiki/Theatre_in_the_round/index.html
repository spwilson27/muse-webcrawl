Theatre in the round

Also called: Arena theatre, arena stage, central staging
Stage forms: vs. proscenium, thrust stage, traverse

Theatre in the round, also known as arena theatre, is a form of theatrical staging in which the acting area is completely surrounded by the audience. The stage is usually circular, square or rectangular and is typically at the same level as, or slightly below, the first rows of seats, which rise around it. Actors enter and exit through gaps (vomitoria) in the seating.

Characteristics

Because there is no "fourth wall" side to the stage, scenery must be minimal so as not to block sightlines, and furniture and props are kept low. Lighting is hung above the stage in a grid. Performers must play to all sides, and staging and choreography traditionally rotate movement so that no section of the audience sees only an actor's back for long. The form produces an unusually intimate and communal atmosphere in which audience members can see each other across the stage.

History

Central staging recalls ancient and medieval performance — market-place pageants, bullrings and circus rings — but the modern theatre-in-the-round movement began in the United States. Glenn Hughes established the Penthouse Theatre at the University of Washington in 1940, often cited as the first purpose-built modern arena theatre; Margo Jones's Theatre '47 in Dallas and Zelda Fichandler's Arena Stage in Washington, D.C. (founded 1950) popularised the form. In Britain, Stephen Joseph championed in-the-round performance from the 1950s, leading to the Stephen Joseph Theatre in Scarborough, and the Royal Exchange in Manchester (opened 1976) became the largest theatre in the round in the UK. The New Vic Theatre in Newcastle-under-Lyme (1986) was Europe's first purpose-built theatre in the round.
