Quality management

Quality management ensures that an organisation, product or service consistently meets its requirements and satisfies customers. It has four main components: quality planning, quality assurance, quality control and quality improvement. Quality management is focused not only on product and service quality, but also on the means to achieve it; it therefore uses quality assurance and control of processes as well as products to achieve more consistent quality and, essentially, is a business philosophy.

History

Quality moved from simple inspection of finished goods to a management discipline during the 20th century. Walter A. Shewhart introduced statistical process control and the Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) cycle at Bell Laboratories in the 1920s–30s. After the Second World War, W. Edwards Deming and Joseph M. Juran carried these ideas to Japan, where Kaoru Ishikawa's quality circles and Toyota's production system made "Japanese quality" a global benchmark. Philip Crosby popularised "zero defects" and "quality is free"; Armand Feigenbaum coined Total Quality Control.

Principles and approaches

The ISO 9000 family defines seven quality management principles: customer focus; leadership; engagement of people; process approach; improvement; evidence-based decision making; and relationship management. Organisations can be certified against ISO 9001, the world's most widely used quality management system standard.

Major approaches include:

  • Total Quality Management (TQM) — organisation-wide, continuous improvement involving every employee.
  • Six Sigma — a data-driven methodology (DMAIC) originating at Motorola in 1986 that seeks to reduce process variation and defects to 3.4 per million opportunities.
  • Lean management — elimination of waste (muda) derived from the Toyota Production System; often combined as Lean Six Sigma.
  • Kaizen — gradual, continuous improvement through small changes.
  • Benchmarking and quality function deployment — learning from best-in-class performers and translating customer requirements into design characteristics.

Tools

Classical quality tools include Ishikawa's seven basic tools: cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagrams, check sheets, control charts, histograms, Pareto charts, scatter diagrams and stratification/flowcharts — supplemented by failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), root-cause techniques such as the "five whys", and cost-of-quality accounting.
