Red giant

A red giant is a luminous giant star of low or intermediate mass (roughly 0.3–8 solar masses) in a late phase of stellar evolution. The outer atmosphere is inflated and tenuous, making the radius immense and the surface temperature low — below about 5,000 K — giving the star a reddish-orange appearance. Red giants lie on the red-giant branch (RGB) and the asymptotic giant branch (AGB) of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram and are of spectral types K and M, occasionally as early as G.

Formation and evolution

A star becomes a red giant after exhausting the hydrogen fuel in its core. For stars below about 2 solar masses the inert helium core contracts and becomes degenerate while hydrogen continues to burn in a shell around it; the envelope expands and cools, and the star climbs the red-giant branch, growing until the core reaches about 108 K and helium ignites abruptly in the helium flash. After core helium burning (the horizontal branch or red clump), the star ascends the asymptotic giant branch with alternating hydrogen and helium shell burning, thermal pulses, and heavy mass loss. Stars of up to roughly 8 solar masses end by ejecting their envelopes as planetary nebulae, leaving white dwarfs; more massive stars become red supergiants and end as supernovae.

Characteristics

Because the surface gravity is low and the envelope deeply convective, red giants show strong stellar winds, pulsation (as in the Mira variables), and dredge-up of fusion products that alter their surface chemistry — a key diagnostic of evolutionary state. Their huge radii mean that a red giant in the Solar System would engulf the inner planets; the Sun is expected to become a red giant in about five billion years, likely engulfing Mercury, Venus and possibly Earth.

Red giants as distance and age indicators

The abrupt tip of the red-giant branch (TRGB), set by the helium flash, has a nearly constant luminosity in the I band and serves as a standard candle for measuring distances to nearby galaxies. Red-giant asteroseismology, using missions such as Kepler, reveals interior rotation and precise ages of field stars.
