Arithmetic progression

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An arithmetic progression or arithmetic sequence is a sequence of numbers such that the difference between consecutive terms is constant, called the common difference. For example, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, … is an arithmetic progression with common difference 2.

Formulas

If the first term is a1 and the common difference is d, the n-th term is

an = a1 + (n − 1)d.

The sum of the first n terms (an arithmetic series) is

Sn = n/2 · (a1 + an) = n/2 · (2a1 + (n − 1)d),

a result famously rediscovered by the young Carl Friedrich Gauss, who summed 1 to 100 by pairing terms to give 50 × 101 = 5050. The arithmetic mean of the terms equals the average of the first and last terms.

Related results

Long arithmetic progressions in the prime numbers are the subject of the Green–Tao theorem (2004), which proves that the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. Van der Waerden's theorem likewise guarantees monochromatic arithmetic progressions in any finite colouring of the integers. Arithmetic progressions generalise to arithmetic progressions in groups and to the concept of an arithmetic progression in a sequence of sets studied in additive combinatorics (Szemerédi's theorem).
