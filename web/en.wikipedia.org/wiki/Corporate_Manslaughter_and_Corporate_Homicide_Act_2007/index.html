Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007

The Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007 (c. 19) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that seeks to broaden the law on corporate manslaughter in the United Kingdom. The Act, which came into force on 6 April 2008, created a new offence under which companies and other organisations can be prosecuted where there has been a gross failure in the way an organisation manages its activities, resulting in a person's death.

Background

Under the previous common law, a company could only be convicted of manslaughter if a single individual identifiable as the "directing mind" of the company was also guilty of gross negligence manslaughter. This "identification principle" made successful prosecutions of large organisations rare: prosecutions arising from disasters such as the Herald of Free Enterprise sinking (1987) and the Southall rail crash (1997) failed despite extensive criticism of the companies involved.

The offence

Under section 1 of the Act, an organisation is guilty of corporate manslaughter (corporate homicide in Scotland) if the way in which its activities are managed or organised by its senior management causes a person's death and amounts to a gross breach of a relevant duty of care owed by the organisation to the deceased. The penalty is an unlimited fine; courts may also impose remedial orders and publicity orders. The offence applies to corporations, police forces, government departments and certain partnerships and trade unions.

First convictions

The first conviction under the Act was that of Cotswold Geotechnical (Holdings) Ltd in 2011, following the death of a geologist in a collapsed trial pit. Subsequent convictions have mostly involved small and medium-sized companies.
