A Clockwork Orange (novel)

A Clockwork Orange is a dystopian satirical novella by English writer Anthony Burgess, published in 1962. It is narrated in the first person by Alex, a fifteen-year-old gang leader in a near-future England, who recounts his career of "ultra-violence" in an invented teen slang called Nadsat, flavoured with Russian-derived words. The novel explores free will, morality and the use of aversion therapy by the state to suppress criminal behaviour.

Plot

Alex and his three "droogs" (Dim, Georgie and Pete) spend their nights committing assaults, robbery and rape. After the gang turns on him, Alex is captured by police during a burglary in which an elderly woman dies. Convicted of murder, he volunteers for the experimental "Ludovico technique", a form of aversion therapy that conditions him to become violently ill at the thought of violence or at hearing Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, which was played during the treatment. Released as supposedly cured, he is defenceless against his former victims and enemies and attempts suicide. The state, embarrassed by the scandal, reverses his conditioning, and Alex forms a new gang — though in the final chapter he begins to tire of violence and imagines a settled adult life.

The last chapter

The British edition contains 21 chapters, but the original American edition omitted the final chapter at the publisher's insistence, ending with Alex unrepentant. Burgess later disliked this truncation, and the restored chapter was reinstated in later American editions. Stanley Kubrick's 1971 film adaptation was based on the American edition and likewise ends without the redemptive final chapter.

Language and themes

The Nadsat argot — from Russian words such as droog (friend), moloko (milk) and horrorshow (from khorosho, "good") — forces the reader to learn Alex's language, implicating them in his worldview. The novel's central question is whether goodness chosen freely is worth more than goodness imposed: as the prison chaplain observes, a man who cannot choose ceases to be a man. The title is usually explained as deriving from the Cockney expression "as queer as a clockwork orange", something superficially natural but internally mechanical.
