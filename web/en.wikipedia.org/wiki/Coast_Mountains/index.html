Coast Mountains

The Coast Mountains of British Columbia, Canada.

The Coast Mountains (French: La chaîne Côtière) are a major mountain range in southwestern British Columbia and southeastern Alaska, forming the westernmost cordillera of the North American Pacific coast. They extend roughly 1,600 kilometres (990 miles) from the Fraser River in the south to the Kelsall and Tatshenshini rivers in the Yukon in the north, and they are part of the larger Pacific mountain system sometimes called the Pacific Coast Ranges.

Geography

The range is divided into three main subdivisions: the Pacific Ranges in the south, the Kitimat Ranges in the centre, and the Boundary Ranges in the north. The highest summit is Mount Waddington, at 4,019 metres, the highest peak entirely within British Columbia. The mountains are heavily glaciated, with some of the largest temperate-latitude ice fields in the world, and their western slopes are cut by deep fjords such as Knight Inlet, Bute Inlet, and Douglas Channel.

Geology

The Coast Mountains are mostly composed of the granitic rocks of the Coast Plutonic Complex, one of the largest expanses of intrusive igneous rock on Earth, formed by subduction-related magmatism along the western margin of North America during the Jurassic, Cretaceous, and early Tertiary periods. Glaciers of the Pleistocene ice ages sculpted the range into its present rugged form.

Climate and ecology

The western slopes intercept moist Pacific air and receive some of the heaviest precipitation in North America, supporting dense temperate rainforest of Sitka spruce, western hemlock, and western red cedar. The eastern slopes lie in a rain shadow and are markedly drier. Because of the terrain, almost the entire range is wilderness with very few settlements and only a handful of roads crossing it.
