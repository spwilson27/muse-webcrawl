Aircraft hijacking

Aircraft hijacking (also known as skyjacking, sky hijacking, sky piracy, air piracy, or, in the United States, aircraft piracy) is the unlawful seizure of an aircraft by an individual or a group. In most cases the pilot is forced to fly according to the orders of the hijackers; occasionally hijackers fly the aircraft themselves, as in the September 11 attacks of 2001. Unlike carjacking or sea piracy, a hijacking is not usually committed for robbery or cargo theft — most hijackers seek to use the passengers as hostages, to obtain transport to a destination, or to make a political statement.

Security measures introduced since the 1970s have sharply reduced hijackings.

History

The first recorded hijacking took place in 1931, when Peruvian revolutionaries seized a Ford Tri-Motor. Hijackings became epidemic between 1968 and 1972, when more than 300 incidents were recorded worldwide, many of them diversions of American airliners to Cuba. The wave prompted the introduction of passenger screening in the United States in 1973 and international conventions — Tokyo (1963), The Hague (1970) and Montreal (1971) — obliging states to prosecute or extradite hijackers.

Later decades saw hijackings employed by militant groups, notably the 1970 Dawson's Field hijackings, the 1976 Air France flight diverted to Entebbe and the 1985 hijacking of TWA Flight 847. The September 11 attacks of 2001, in which four airliners were seized and flown into buildings, transformed aviation security worldwide, leading to hardened cockpit doors, expanded screening and air marshal programmes. Countermeasures

Modern defences include reinforced, locked cockpit doors; behavioural and document screening; no-fly lists; armed sky marshals on selected flights; and crew protocols that discourage compliance-based strategies in favour of keeping the aircraft on the ground. Shoot-down policies for hijacked aircraft that threaten populated areas have been adopted in several countries since 2001.
