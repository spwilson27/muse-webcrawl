Thermophile

Thermophile
Definition: an organism thriving at relatively high temperatures
Typical growth range: 41–122 °C (upper record held by Methanopyrus kandleri, 122 °C)
Habitats: hot springs, geysers, hydrothermal vents, compost, heated soils
Extremes: hyperthermophiles grow best above 80 °C
Famous example: Thermus aquaticus, source of Taq polymerase

A thermophile is an organism — a type of extremophile — that thrives at relatively high temperatures, between about 41 and 122 °C. Thermophiles are found in geothermally heated environments such as hot springs, geysers, fumaroles, and deep-sea hydrothermal vents, as well as in human-made hot environments like compost heaps and water heaters. Most are bacteria or archaea, though thermophilic fungi and algae also exist, and some multicellular organisms tolerate surprising heat: the Pompeii worm of hydrothermal vents endures gradients up to about 80 °C.

Classification

Thermophiles are commonly divided by their temperature optima: facultative thermophiles can grow at mesophilic temperatures too; obligate thermophiles require heat; and hyperthermophiles grow best above 80 °C, with some unable to grow below that. The current upper temperature limit for known life is held by the archaeon Methanopyrus kandleri, shown to reproduce at 122 °C under pressure.

Adaptations

Life at high temperature demands proteins that do not denature, achieved through more ionic bonds and salt bridges, compact hydrophobic cores, and the help of chaperone proteins. Membranes are adjusted to remain stable — in archaea, with ether-linked lipids that may form monolayers — and some hyperthermophiles use the enzyme reverse gyrase to introduce positive supercoils that stabilise DNA. Genomes often show elevated GC content in structural RNAs, whose melting temperatures must exceed the growth temperature.

Scientific and industrial importance

Thermophiles transformed biotechnology: the heat-stable DNA polymerase of Thermus aquaticus (Taq polymerase), isolated from a Yellowstone hot spring by Thomas Brock's group, made the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) practical and earned a Nobel Prize for its developer, Kary Mullis. Thermostable enzymes are now used in detergents, food and paper processing, and biofuel production. Thermophile communities also serve as models in the search for life on other worlds and as windows into the hot conditions of the early Earth, since many deeply branching lineages in the tree of life are thermophilic — a fact sometimes taken to suggest that life itself began in hot environments.
