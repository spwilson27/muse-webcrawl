Franco-Mexican War

The term Franco-Mexican War may refer to either of two conflicts between France and Mexico in the nineteenth century:

The Pastry War (1838–1839)

The Pastry War (Guerra de los Pasteles) was the first French intervention in Mexico. France demanded compensation for losses suffered by French citizens during Mexican unrest — famously including a pastry cook whose shop had been looted. When Mexico refused to pay, a French fleet blockaded Veracruz and bombarded the fortress of San Juan de Ulúa in November 1838. Under British mediation, Mexico agreed to pay 600,000 pesos, and the French withdrew in March 1839. During the fighting, General Antonio López de Santa Anna lost a leg, an event he later used to restore his political standing.

Second French Intervention (1861–1867)

The Second French intervention in Mexico began after President Benito Juárez suspended payment of Mexico's foreign debts in 1861. France, Britain, and Spain initially acted jointly, but Emperor Napoleon III of France pursued his own ambition of establishing a client empire in the Americas. French troops suffered a famous defeat at the Battle of Puebla on 5 May 1862 — commemorated as Cinco de Mayo — but advanced the following year and occupied Mexico City.

In 1864 France installed the Austrian archduke Maximilian as Emperor of Mexico, backed by Mexican conservatives. Republican forces under Juárez continued guerrilla resistance. With the end of the American Civil War, the United States pressured France to withdraw under the Monroe Doctrine, and Napoleon III recalled his troops in 1866–1867. Maximilian refused to abdicate, was captured at Querétaro, and was executed by firing squad on 19 June 1867, restoring the Mexican Republic.
