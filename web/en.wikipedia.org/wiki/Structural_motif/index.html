Structural motif

The helix-turn-helix, a common DNA-binding structural motif

In a chain-like biological molecule, such as a protein or nucleic acid, a structural motif is a common three-dimensional structure that appears in a variety of different, often functionally unrelated molecules. A structural motif need not have a fixed sequence pattern: the same spatial arrangement can be formed by different amino-acid sequences, in contrast to a sequence motif, which is defined by the sequence itself.

Protein motifs

Well-known protein structural motifs include the helix-turn-helix and zinc finger in DNA-binding proteins, the β-α-β motif, the Greek key motif in β-sheets, the Rossmann fold in nucleotide-binding proteins, and the leucine zipper in dimerisation domains. Motifs are the middle level of the protein structure hierarchy, between secondary structure and domains: a domain is generally assembled from several motifs. Databases such as SCOP and CATH classify folds and motifs to support structure comparison and function prediction.

Nucleic acid motifs

RNA and DNA also form recurring motifs, including hairpin loops, bulges, pseudoknots, the G-quadruplex, kink-turns in ribosomal RNA, and the ribose zipper. These motifs mediate folding, molecular recognition and catalysis in ribozymes and riboswitches.

Significance

Because structure is more conserved than sequence, recognizing a structural motif can reveal homology or function where sequence comparison fails. Motif templates are used in protein design and in docking and annotation pipelines.
