Islamic banking and finance is banking and financial activity that complies with sharia (Islamic law) and its practical application through Islamic economics. The central prohibitions are riba (interest or usury), gharar (excessive uncertainty or ambiguity in contracts) and maysir (gambling). Islamic finance instead emphasises risk-sharing between financiers and entrepreneurs, and requires transactions to be backed by real assets or services.

History

Classical Islamic commerce used partnerships such as the mudaraba (a trustee partnership in which one party supplies capital and the other labour) for centuries. The first modern experiment in interest-free banking is usually dated to 1963, when Ahmad Elnaggar founded a savings bank in Mit Ghamr, Egypt, operating on profit-sharing principles. The 1970s oil boom provided the capital for commercial-scale institutions: the Islamic Development Bank was founded in 1975 in Jeddah, and Dubai Islamic Bank, the first private commercial Islamic bank, opened the same year. The industry expanded rapidly in Malaysia, the Gulf states, and later in non-Muslim-majority financial centres such as London.

Principles

Prohibition of riba

The Qur'an prohibits riba, conventionally interpreted as any guaranteed interest charged on a loan. Money itself is treated as a medium of exchange rather than a commodity, so profit must arise from trade, leasing or partnership rather than from lending money at interest.

Risk sharing and asset-backing

Because a lender may not earn a predetermined return, Islamic finance substitutes contracts in which returns depend on the performance of an underlying asset or venture. Speculative derivatives and short-selling are generally restricted because of gharar and maysir.

Major products

  • Murabaha — cost-plus financing: the bank buys an asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, payable in instalments. It is the most widely used Islamic financing mode.
  • Ijara — leasing, analogous to an operating or finance lease.
  • Mudaraba — profit-sharing partnership between a capital provider and an entrepreneur; losses fall on the capital provider.
  • Musharaka — joint venture in which all partners contribute capital and share profit and loss.
  • Sukuk — certificates representing ownership in an asset or pool of assets, often compared to bonds but structured to avoid interest.
  • Takaful — cooperative insurance based on mutual guarantee.

Criticism

Critics, including some Islamic scholars and economists such as Timur Kuran, argue that many murabaha and sukuk structures replicate the economics of conventional interest-bearing debt while differing mainly in legal form. Defenders respond that the asset-backing and risk-sharing requirements impose substantive constraints, particularly in project finance and ethical screening of investments (for example, excluding alcohol, gambling and conventional finance stocks).
