Mridangam

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The mridangam, the principal percussion instrument of Carnatic music.

The mridangam is a double-headed drum that is the primary rhythmic accompaniment (pakka vadyam) in Carnatic music, the classical music of South India. It is a barrel-shaped drum, traditionally carved from a single piece of jackfruit wood, played horizontally across the player's lap with the fingers and palms of both hands. The name derives from the Sanskrit mrid (clay or earth) and anga (body), recalling that the earliest versions were made of clay.

Construction

The shell, about 60 cm long, tapers asymmetrically: the right head is smaller in diameter than the left. Both heads are made of layered goat skin held in place by braided leather straps tightened with wooden blocks. The right (treble) head carries a permanent black tuning spot, the soru or karanai, built up from many thin layers of a paste of cooked rice, manganese or iron filings, and stone powder; this gives the drum its characteristic pitched, bell-like overtones and allows it to be tuned precisely to the tonic of the concert. Before performance, a temporary paste of rice flour and water is applied to the inside of the left (bass) head to lower and modulate its pitch.

Playing technique

The mridangam is played with an intricate vocabulary of finger and palm strokes, each with a spoken solfa syllable (solkattu) such as tha, thi, thom, nam. Players render the rhythmic cycle (tala) and improvise complex patterns, cross-rhythms, and mathematical cadences (korvai), engaging in rhythmic dialogue with the soloist. A solo passage, the tani avartanam, is a standard feature of Carnatic concerts.

History and related instruments

Depictions of barrel drums appear in ancient Indian art and literature; the mridangam is closely related to the Hindustani pakhawaj and the mridanga used in Odissi music and Gaudiya Vaishnava kirtan, and it is one of the instruments of the god Shiva's dance and of the celestial musician Tumbaru in mythology. Celebrated 20th-century masters such as Palghat Mani Iyer, Palani Subramaniam Pillai, and Umayalpuram K. Sivaraman transformed the instrument's role from accompanist to virtuoso partner.
