Tit for tat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In game theory, tit for tat is a strategy for the iterated prisoner's dilemma. The player cooperates on the first move and thereafter copies the opponent's previous move — cooperating if the opponent cooperated last time, and defecting if the opponent defected. The strategy was famously submitted by Anatol Rapoport to Robert Axelrod's tournaments in 1980.

Why it works

Axelrod's tournaments pitted programs against one another in repeated prisoner's dilemma games, and Rapoport's simple tit-for-tat program won both tournaments. Axelrod identified four properties behind its success: it is nice (it never defects first), retaliatory (it punishes defection immediately), forgiving (it returns to cooperation once the opponent does), and clear (its behaviour is easy for an opponent to read). These qualities foster mutual cooperation while resisting exploitation.

Limitations

In a noisy environment, a single mistaken defection can trigger an echo of alternating retaliation between two tit-for-tat players. Variants such as generous tit-for-tat (which occasionally forgives a defection) or "tit for two tats" (which retaliates only after two consecutive defections) perform better under noise. The concept has influenced biology (reciprocal altruism), arms-control theory and the study of conflict resolution, and the phrase has entered ordinary English to mean equivalent retaliation.
