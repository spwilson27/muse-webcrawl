Atlantic mackerel
Scientific nameScomber scombrus
FamilyScombridae
Length30–47 cm typical
RangeNorth Atlantic, Mediterranean, Black Sea

The Atlantic mackerel (Scomber scombrus) is a pelagic schooling fish of the tuna and mackerel family Scombridae, found on both sides of the North Atlantic Ocean, as well as in the Mediterranean and Black Seas. It is recognisable by its iridescent blue-green back crossed by wavy dark vertical stripes, a silvery-white belly, and a deeply forked tail.

Biology

Atlantic mackerel are fast, active swimmers that lack a swim bladder and must swim continuously. They form large schools and migrate seasonally, moving inshore to spawn in spring and summer and retreating to deeper water in winter. They feed on zooplankton, small crustaceans and young fish, and are themselves prey for tuna, sharks, dolphins and seabirds.

Fisheries

It is one of the most commercially important fish in the North Atlantic, caught mainly with purse seines and midwater trawls, largely by Norway, the United Kingdom, Iceland and the Faroe Islands. Disputes over quota allocation — the so-called "mackerel wars" — arose when warming waters shifted the stock's distribution northward. The oily flesh is eaten fresh, smoked and canned.
