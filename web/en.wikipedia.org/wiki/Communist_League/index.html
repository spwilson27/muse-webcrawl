The Communist League (German: Bund der Kommunisten) was an international political party established on 1 June 1847 in London, formed by the merger of the League of the Just, headed by Karl Schapper, and the Communist Correspondence Committee of Brussels, in which Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels were the dominant figures. The League is commonly regarded as the first Marxist political party, and it was for the League that Marx and Engels wrote the Manifesto of the Communist Party, published in February 1848.

Origins

The League of the Just (Bund der Gerechten) had been founded in Paris in 1836 by German émigré artisans influenced by the ideas of Gracchus Babeuf and Wilhelm Weitling; its motto was "All Men are Brothers". By the mid-1840s its London and Paris branches had come into contact with Marx and Engels, whose "scientific" communism they found increasingly persuasive. At a congress in London in June 1847 — attended by Engels, with Marx kept away by lack of funds — the League renamed itself the Communist League, adopted democratic rules, abandoned its conspiratorial character in principle, and took the new motto: "Working Men of All Countries, Unite!"

A second congress, held in London from late November to December 1847 with both Marx and Engels present, adopted the organisation's programme and commissioned Marx and Engels to draft a public manifesto. The result, the Manifesto of the Communist Party, was published in German in London in February 1848, just as revolutions were erupting across Europe.

The revolutions of 1848–49

During the Revolutions of 1848, League members returned to Germany, where the organisation established its centre at Cologne and its members worked through democratic associations and the newspaper Neue Rheinische Zeitung, edited by Marx. The League's "Demands of the Communist Party in Germany" called for a united German republic, universal male suffrage, and arming of the people. Following the defeat of the revolutions, leading members were arrested; the organisation, which had transferred its Central Authority back to London, was torn by disputes between the majority around Marx and Engels and the adventurist Willich–Schapper faction, which favoured immediate revolutionary action.

Decline and trial

The split of September 1850 effectively ended the League as a unified body. In 1851 the Prussian police arrested members of the Cologne group; after a prolonged police operation involving forged documents and a provocateur, eleven defendants were tried at the Cologne Communist Trial in October–November 1852, and seven were convicted of treasonable conspiracy. On Marx's proposal, the London organisation formally dissolved itself on 17 November 1852.

Legacy

Though small — its membership probably never exceeded a few hundred, largely German émigré workers and intellectuals in London, Paris, Brussels, and Switzerland — the Communist League holds an outsized place in history as the first international communist organisation and the commissioner of the Communist Manifesto, one of the most influential political pamphlets ever written. Its members later formed part of the milieu from which the International Workingmen's Association (the First International) emerged in 1864.
