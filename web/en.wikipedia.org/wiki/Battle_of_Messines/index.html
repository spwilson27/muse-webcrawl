The Battle of Messines was a battle of the Western Front of the First World War, fought by the British Second Army under General Herbert Plumer against the German Fourth Army in June 1917. It is famous for the detonation of nineteen enormous mines beneath the German positions on the Messines–Wytschaete ridge, among the largest non-nuclear explosions of the war.

Background

The Messines ridge, south of Ypres, had been held by the Germans since 1914 and overlooked the British positions in the Ypres Salient. Capturing it was a necessary preliminary to the planned Flanders offensive (the Third Battle of Ypres). For over a year, British, Canadian, Australian and New Zealand tunnelling companies had dug deep galleries under the ridge and packed them with hundreds of tons of ammonal explosive.

The attack

After a week-long artillery bombardment, at 03:10 on 7 June 1917 the mines were fired simultaneously. The explosions devastated the German forward defences, killing thousands instantly; the shock was reportedly felt in London. Nine infantry divisions advanced behind a creeping barrage and, supported by tanks and aircraft, captured the ridge within hours. German counterattacks over the following week failed to regain the position.

Aftermath

Messines was one of the most complete British successes of the war to that date, a model of the limited-objective "bite and hold" method Plumer favoured. Combined British and Dominion casualties were roughly 24,000–25,000, comparable to German losses. The victory straightened the line south of Ypres and set the stage for the far bloodier Battle of Passchendaele that followed in July.
