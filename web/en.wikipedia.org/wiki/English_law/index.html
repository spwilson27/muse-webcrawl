English law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

English law is the legal system of England and Wales, one of the three legal jurisdictions of the United Kingdom (with Scots law and Northern Ireland law). It is the origin of the common law tradition adopted, in whole or part, by most former British territories — including the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and India — and remains influential through London's role in international commercial law and arbitration.

Sources and structure

English law's sources are case law (precedent), statute (Acts of Parliament at Westminster, and Welsh legislation from the Senedd), retained and assimilated EU-derived law after Brexit, and custom. Courts are headed by the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom (since 2009), with the Court of Appeal and High Court below, organised by divisions (King's Bench, Chancery, Family). Equity, developed by the Lord Chancellor's Court of Chancery to mitigate common-law rigidity, was administratively fused with law by the Judicature Acts 1873–75 while its doctrines (trusts, injunctions, fiduciary duties) remain distinct. The profession is split between solicitors and barristers (with fused rights expanding), and juries survive mainly in serious criminal trials.

Character

Uncodified and incremental, English law prizes precedent, adversarial procedure and judicial independence; it lacks a single constitutional text, relying on statutes, conventions and common-law constitutional principles whose strength is debated in cases on parliamentary sovereignty and the rule of law.
