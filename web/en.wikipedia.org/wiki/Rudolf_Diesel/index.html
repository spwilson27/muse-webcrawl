Rudolf Diesel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Rudolf Diesel in the 1890s, portrait photograph]
Rudolf Diesel.

Rudolf Christian Karl Diesel (1858–1913) was a German inventor and mechanical engineer, famous for the diesel engine that bears his name. Working at MAN in Augsburg, he sought an engine far more efficient than steam; his 1897 engine achieved roughly 26% efficiency and ran eventually on a range of fuels (he experimented with peanut oil). Diesel died in mysterious circumstances in 1913, disappearing from the steamer Dresden in the English Channel — accident, suicide amid financial troubles, and murder have all been proposed.

Path to the engine

Born in Paris to German parents and raised partly in London, Diesel studied in Munich under refrigeration pioneer Carl von Linde, whose ammonia compressors he later sold. His 1892 patent described a \'rational heat motor\' compressing air until injected fuel ignited — early trials blew cylinder heads before the successful 1897 demonstration.

Success and troubles

Licences sold worldwide made Diesel wealthy, but engine teething problems, patent disputes and bad investments eroded his fortune and health. He promoted his engine for navies and small industry against the steam establishment, and demonstrated a vehicle engine at the 1900 Paris Exposition running on peanut oil — an early biofuel moment he publicised.

Death

On 29 September 1913 Diesel boarded the Dresden at Antwerp for Harwich and vanished overnight; his body was found in the sea days later by a Dutch pilot boat (effects identified by his son). The official verdict was suicide by drowning; conspiracy theories involve agents fearing his engines\' sale to Britain. His engines nonetheless conquered shipping, rail and road freight within decades.
