Argon oxygen decarburization

[Image: Argon oxygen decarburization — An AOD converter vessel during refining of stainless steel]

Argon oxygen decarburization (AOD) is a process primarily used in stainless steel making and other high grade alloys with oxidizable elements such as chromium and aluminium. After initial melting, the metal is then transferred into an AOD vessel where it will be subjected to three steps of refining: decarburization, reduction and desulfurization.

The AOD process was invented in 1954 by the Lindé Division of the Union Carbide Corporation (which became known as Praxair in 1992).

Process

The AOD process is usually divided in three main steps: decarburization, reduction, and desulfurization.

Decarburization

Before the decarburization step, one more step should be taken into consideration: de-siliconization, which is a very important factor for refractory lining and heating of the metal. In the decarburization step, a mixture of oxygen and argon (or nitrogen) is blown into the melt through submerged tuyères. The argon dilutes the carbon monoxide formed by oxidation of carbon, lowering its partial pressure and allowing carbon to be removed preferentially while limiting the oxidation of valuable chromium. The oxygen-to-argon ratio is progressively shifted toward argon as carbon falls.

Reduction

After decarburization, ferrosilicon or aluminium is added together with lime to reduce chromium oxide from the slag back into the metal and to form a fluid slag.

Desulfurization

With a basic, low-oxygen slag in place, sulfur is removed from the steel; argon stirring continues to promote contact between metal and slag. Final composition adjustments and temperature homogenisation follow before tapping.

Advantages

AOD refining made it economical to produce stainless steel from less expensive, high-carbon ferrochrome and scrap, sharply lowering costs relative to earlier processes. It remains the dominant refining route for stainless steels worldwide, often paired with the electric arc furnace in a "duplex" process.
