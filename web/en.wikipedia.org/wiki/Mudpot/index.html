Mudpot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mudpot: a pool of hot, acidic, bubbling mud formed where water is scarce.

A mudpot, or mud pool, is a type of geothermal feature — an acidic hot spring with limited water in which heated water, volcanic gases, and decomposed rock combine to form a pool of bubbling, viscous mud. Mudpots occur in hydrothermal areas around the world, including Yellowstone National Park in the United States, the Hverir field in Iceland, and the Wai-O-Tapu and Rotorua areas of New Zealand.

Formation

Mudpots form where rising hydrogen sulfide gas oxidizes near the surface to sulfuric acid, which attacks the surrounding rock and alters it to clay. Because the water supply is limited — unlike a clear hot spring — the clay cannot be flushed away and instead mixes with the acidic water to form opalescent, grey mud. Escaping steam and gas bubbles rise through the mud, causing it to blip, plop, and occasionally throw up small cones and spatters; the consistency varies from soupy in wet seasons to cracked and dry in summer. Some mudpots are brightly coloured by iron oxides (the "paint pots" of Yellowstone's Fountain Paint Pot and Artist's Paint Pots) or tinted by thermophilic microorganisms.

Behaviour and hazards

Mudpot activity fluctuates with rainfall, groundwater level, and seismic activity; new mudpots can appear after earthquakes shift underground plumbing. The mud is near boiling and strongly acidic, and the ground around geothermal fields is often a thin crust over scalding fluid, so visitors are required to stay on boardwalks. Gases such as hydrogen sulfide give mudpots a characteristic rotten-egg smell.
