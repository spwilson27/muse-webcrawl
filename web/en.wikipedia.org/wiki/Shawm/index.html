Shawm

The shawm is a conical bore, double-reed woodwind instrument made in Europe from the 13th century to the present day. It achieved its peak of popularity during the medieval and Renaissance periods, after which it was gradually eclipsed by the cornett and then the oboe, of which it is the direct ancestor. The shawm's body is usually turned from a single piece of wood, ending in a flared bell, and its piercing, trumpet-like sound made it the principal loud wind instrument of the alta capella, the outdoor wind band of civic and court music.

A shawm, characterised by its flared bell and pirouette.

Description

The shawm has a conical bore and seven finger holes plus thumb hole, with a range of roughly an octave and a half. The double reed is mounted on a staple and, in most traditions, controlled by the player's lips supported on a disc called a pirouette. Instruments were built in a family of sizes from the high soprano to the great bass shawm, which could exceed 3 metres in length and required keys to reach the lowest holes.

History

The shawm derives from Near Eastern double-reed instruments such as the zurna, transmitted to Europe through contact with the Islamic world during the Crusades and trade with the Ottoman sphere. By the 14th century it was central to dance music and ceremonial music across Europe. Related folk instruments survive today, including the Catalan tenora and tible, the Breton bombarde, the Valencian dolçaina, and the Turkish zurna. The shawm was refined during the Baroque era into the hautbois, ancestor of the modern oboe, whose narrower bore and direct lip control of the reed produced a softer sound suitable for indoor music.

Use in performance

Since the early music revival of the 20th century, shawms have again been built by specialist makers, usually after museum originals, and the instrument features in performances of medieval and Renaissance dance music, civic waits music, and composers such as Praetorius, Susato, and Attaingnant.
