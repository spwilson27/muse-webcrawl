Inline engine (aeronautics)

In aviation, an inline engine is a reciprocating engine with a single bank of cylinders, or more broadly any piston engine whose cylinders are arranged in banks aligned with the crankshaft — including V, inverted-V and H configurations — as opposed to a radial engine, whose cylinders radiate around the crankshaft. In common usage the term usually denotes liquid-cooled V-block engines such as the Rolls-Royce Merlin and the Daimler-Benz DB 601.

Advantages and disadvantages

Inline engines present a small frontal area, allowing a streamlined, low-drag fuselage — a decisive advantage for high-performance fighters of the Second World War such as the Spitfire, Bf 109 and P-51 Mustang. Their disadvantages are a long, heavy crankshaft and crankcase, vulnerability of the liquid cooling system to battle damage (a single leak could seize the engine), and more complex maintenance than air-cooled radials, whose cylinders were individually exposed to the slipstream.

History

Early aircraft overwhelmingly used inline and rotary engines. By the 1930s the liquid-cooled V-12 reached its zenith in racing and military service. Jet propulsion displaced piston engines in front-line combat aircraft after 1945, but small air-cooled flat-four and flat-six engines — loosely descended from inline practice — remain the standard for light general aviation aircraft.
