Abraham Flexner

Abraham Flexner (November 13, 1866 – September 21, 1959) was an American educator and reformer best known for the Flexner Report (1910), a landmark study of medical education in the United States and Canada that transformed how physicians are trained. He later helped found the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, New Jersey, and served as its first director.

Early life

Flexner was born in Louisville, Kentucky, to German-Jewish immigrant parents. He graduated from Johns Hopkins University in two years and ran a preparatory school in Louisville before studying education in Europe. His 1908 book The American College criticised American higher education and brought him to the attention of the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching.

The Flexner Report

Commissioned by the Carnegie Foundation, Flexner visited all 155 medical schools then operating in the United States and Canada. His 1910 report exposed widespread inadequacies — proprietary schools with no laboratories, untrained faculty and no admission standards — and recommended university-based education, full-time faculty, laboratory science and clinical training modelled on Johns Hopkins. In its wake, roughly half of American medical schools closed or merged, admissions standards rose sharply, and medical training was standardised; critics note the closures also sharply reduced the number of schools training Black physicians and women.

Later career

Flexner subsequently worked for the Rockefeller Foundation's General Education Board, channelling funds to reform medical schools. In 1930, with funding from Louis Bamberger and Caroline Bamberger Fuld, he founded the Institute for Advanced Study and recruited scholars including Albert Einstein and John von Neumann. His 1939 essay "The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge" defended pure research pursued out of curiosity.
