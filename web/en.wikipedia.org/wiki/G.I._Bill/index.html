G.I. Bill

The G.I. Bill, officially the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, was a law that provided a range of benefits for some of the returning World War II veterans (commonly referred to as G.I.s). The original Act, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 22, 1944, expired in 1956, but the term "G.I. Bill" is still used to refer to programs created to assist American military veterans.

Benefits

The Act provided payments of tuition and living expenses to attend high school, college, or vocational school; low-cost mortgages; low-interest loans to start a business or farm; and one year of unemployment compensation. Millions of veterans used the education benefits: by 1956, roughly 7.8 million veterans had participated in an education or training program. The mortgage provisions are credited with fuelling postwar suburbanisation and the expansion of the American middle class.

Later versions

Successor programs include the Korean War and Vietnam-era G.I. Bills, the Montgomery G.I. Bill (1984), and the Post-9/11 G.I. Bill (2008), which expanded tuition coverage and added a housing allowance and transferability to dependents. The Forever GI Bill (2017) removed the 15-year use-it-or-lose-it limit for newer veterans.

Impact and criticism

Historians credit the G.I. Bill with averting a postwar economic crisis and transforming American higher education, though its administration through local and state agencies meant Black veterans were often systematically denied equal access to its benefits, particularly in the Jim Crow South — a disparity scholars link to the racial wealth gap.
