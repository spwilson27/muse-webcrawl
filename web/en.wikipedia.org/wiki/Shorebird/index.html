Shorebird

Shorebirds (called waders in most of the world outside North America) are birds of the order Charadriiformes commonly found wading along shorelines, mudflats, estuaries and wetlands in search of food. The group includes sandpipers, plovers, oystercatchers, avocets, stilts, curlews, snipes and phalaropes — roughly 390 species in some 20 families (counts vary by classification).

Ecology

Shorebirds feed mainly on invertebrates — worms, molluscs, crustaceans and insects — detected by sight or by sensitive, nerve-rich bill tips that probe mud and sand. Bill shapes are highly specialised: oystercatchers prise open shellfish, avocets sweep curved bills through water, and curlews probe deeply with long downcurved bills.

Migration

Many shorebirds are among the world's champion migrants, breeding in the Arctic and wintering on southern continents. The bar-tailed godwit makes non-stop trans-Pacific flights exceeding 11,000 km. Migratory shorebirds depend on chains of stopover wetlands — such as the Yellow Sea mudflats on the East Asian–Australasian Flyway — making them highly vulnerable to habitat loss. Many populations have declined steeply, and conservation efforts focus on protecting key staging sites.
