Punitive damages

Punitive damages, or exemplary damages, are damages assessed in order to punish the defendant for outrageous conduct and/or to reform or deter the defendant and others from engaging in conduct similar to that which formed the basis of the lawsuit. Although the purpose of punitive damages is not to compensate the plaintiff, the plaintiff will receive all or some of the punitive damages award.

Availability by jurisdiction

Punitive damages are principally a feature of the common law world, and are most prominent in the United States, where they may be awarded in tort cases involving fraud, malice, or gross negligence. In England and Wales, exemplary damages are confined to narrow categories following the decision in Rookes v Barnard (1964). Most civil law jurisdictions do not award punitive damages, regarding punishment as a function of criminal law, and some refuse to enforce foreign punitive damages awards as contrary to public policy.

United States constitutional limits

The Supreme Court of the United States has held that excessive punitive damages may violate the Due Process Clause. In BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore (1996) the Court set out guideposts for reviewing awards, and in State Farm v. Campbell (2003) it indicated that few awards exceeding a single-digit ratio of punitive to compensatory damages would satisfy due process. The Court applied similar reasoning to maritime law in Exxon Shipping Co. v. Baker (2008).
