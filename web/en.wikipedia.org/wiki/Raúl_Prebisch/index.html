Raúl Prebisch (April 17, 1901 – April 29, 1986) was an Argentine economist known for his contributions to structuralist economics and the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis, which holds that the terms of trade for primary commodity exporters tend to decline over time relative to manufactured goods. He served as executive director of the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLA) and was the founding secretary-general of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD).

Raúl Prebisch, one of the most influential development economists of the 20th century.

Early career in Argentina

Prebisch studied at the University of Buenos Aires and worked in Argentina's national statistics and finance administration. During the 1930s he helped design Argentina's response to the Great Depression, playing a key role in creating the Central Bank of the Argentine Republic (1935), of which he became the first general manager. He was forced from office in 1943 amid political changes and worked as an independent consultant and lecturer.

ECLA and structuralism

In 1949 Prebisch authored ECLA's landmark study, The Economic Development of Latin America and Its Principal Problems, sometimes called the "ECLA manifesto." It argued that the world economy was divided into an industrial "centre" and a primary-producing "periphery," and that trade between them systematically disadvantaged the periphery — the analytical basis of Latin American structuralism and a foundation of dependency theory. With Hans Singer, who reached similar conclusions independently, this became the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis.

UNCTAD and later work

Prebisch was the driving force behind the first UNCTAD conference in Geneva in 1964 and served as the organisation's first secretary-general (1964–1969), championing the Group of 77 and demands for a fairer international trading system for developing countries. He remained an influential voice in development economics until his death in Santiago in 1986. His ideas underpinned import-substitution industrialisation policies across Latin America.
