Chorda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chorda (plural chordae, from Greek χορδή, "gut, string of a musical instrument") is a term with several uses in anatomy, music history and zoology, generally referring to a cord or string-like structure.

In music

In ancient Greek and Roman music, a chorda was a string of a lyre or kithara, typically made of gut. The number of chordae determined the range of the instrument; the classical lyre grew from four to seven strings. The word is the origin of the modern term "chord" and of "chordophone", the family of instruments whose sound is produced by vibrating strings.

In anatomy

In anatomy, chordae appear in several structures: the chordae tendineae, fibrous cords that connect the papillary muscles to the tricuspid and mitral valves of the heart; the chorda tympani, a branch of the facial nerve carrying taste sensation from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue; and the chorda dorsalis, an older name for the notochord of chordate embryos.

In zoology

Chorda is also the name of a genus of brown algae in the family Chordaceae, whose long, cord-like thalli are found in northern seas.
