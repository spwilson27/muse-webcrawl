Western astrology is the system of astrology most popular in Western countries. It is historically based on Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos (2nd century CE), which in turn was a continuation of Hellenistic and Babylonian traditions. Western astrology uses the tropical zodiac, in which the twelve signs are fixed to the cycle of the seasons — beginning with Aries at the March equinox — rather than to the constellations, from which they have drifted due to the precession of the equinoxes.

The zodiac and the chart

The twelve signs — Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius, Capricorn, Aquarius, and Pisces — are each 30° of the ecliptic, grouped by element (fire, earth, air, water) and quality (cardinal, fixed, mutable). An astrologer casts a horoscope, or natal chart, mapping the positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets in the signs and in twelve "houses" at the moment and place of a person's birth. The point rising on the eastern horizon, the Ascendant, anchors the house system.

History

Astrology emerged in Mesopotamia by the 2nd millennium BCE; the Babylonians developed the zodiac of twelve signs by the 5th century BCE. In the Hellenistic world, particularly Alexandria, Babylonian techniques merged with Greek astronomy and philosophy, producing the personal horoscopic astrology codified by Ptolemy. Transmitted through Arabic scholars during the medieval period, it flourished in Renaissance Europe, where figures such as William Lilly practised it alongside the emerging astronomy from which it had not yet fully separated.

Astrology declined among the educated from the late 17th century but was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries, when popular "sun-sign" columns and psychological interpretations (influenced by Carl Jung's work) made it a mass cultural phenomenon.

Criticism

Astrology has no scientific validity: controlled studies, such as Shawn Carlson's 1985 double-blind test published in Nature, have found astrologers unable to match natal charts to personality profiles better than chance. It is generally classified as a pseudoscience, though it retains wide popular and cultural influence.
