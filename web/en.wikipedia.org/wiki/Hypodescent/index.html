Hypodescent

Under hypodescent, mixed-ancestry children are assigned to the subordinate group.

In societies that regard some races or ethnic groups as dominant and others as subordinate, hypodescent refers to the automatic assignment of children of a mixed union between members of different groups to the subordinate group. The opposite practice, hyperdescent, assigns such children to the dominant group.

The best-known example of hypodescent is the one-drop rule in the United States, under which a person with any known African ancestry, however small a fraction, was historically classified as Black. The rule was embedded in law in several states during the era of slavery and segregation and shaped American racial classification through much of the twentieth century.

History in the United States

During the period of slavery, the legal doctrine of partus sequitur ventrem provided that a child's status followed that of the mother, ensuring that children of enslaved women remained enslaved. After emancipation, hypodescent was reinforced through anti-miscegenation laws and census practice. Virginia's Racial Integrity Act of 1924 codified the one-drop rule in that state. Sociologist Marvin Harris coined the term "hypodescent" in 1964 to describe the pattern.

Comparisons

Other societies have organised mixed ancestry differently. In Brazil and much of Latin America, intermediate categories proliferated, and classification often rested on appearance, wealth and social status rather than ancestry alone. In apartheid-era South Africa, a separate "Coloured" category was maintained between white and Black. These comparisons show that racial assignment rules are social conventions rather than biological necessities.

Modern usage

Although the one-drop rule no longer has legal force, scholars argue that hypodescent persists in social perception: studies of racial categorisation find that perceivers more readily classify mixed-race faces into the minority group. Multiracial identity movements since the 1990s have challenged single-race assignment, and the United States Census has allowed respondents to mark more than one race since 2000.
