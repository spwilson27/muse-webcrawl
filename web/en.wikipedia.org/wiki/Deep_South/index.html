Deep South

[Image: Map of the Deep South states]
The Deep South, in dark red, within the Southern United States

The Deep South is a cultural and geographic subregion of the Southern United States. The term is generally applied to the states that were most dependent on plantations and slavery before the Civil War, and that formed the heart of the Confederacy. It is usually defined as comprising Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi and South Carolina, with northern Florida, eastern Texas and parts of Tennessee sometimes included.

History

The region's economy was built on the plantation system growing cotton and other cash crops with enslaved African-American labour; by 1860 the "Black Belt" of rich soil stretching across Alabama and Mississippi held some of the largest enslaved populations in the country. The Deep South states were the first seven to secede in 1860–61. After Reconstruction collapsed in 1877, the region was governed under Jim Crow segregation for nearly a century, and it was a central battleground of the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s — including the Montgomery bus boycott, the Birmingham campaign and Freedom Summer in Mississippi.

Culture and politics

The Deep South is distinguished by its own dialects of Southern American English, musical traditions (blues, gospel, jazz and country), soul food cuisine, and the strong role of evangelical Protestantism. Politically the region voted solidly Democratic (the "Solid South") from the end of Reconstruction until the 1960s, then shifted heavily Republican from the late twentieth century onward. It contrasts with the Upland or Upper South — Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, Kentucky and Arkansas — which had fewer plantations and stronger Unionist sentiment.
