Pennsylvania Dutch (Pennsilfaanisch Deitsch) is a variety of Palatine German spoken in North America by the Pennsylvania Dutch — descendants of German-speaking immigrants, many of them Anabaptists, who settled in Pennsylvania and neighbouring states in the 17th and 18th centuries. Despite the name, it is a German dialect: "Dutch" here reflects the older English use of the word for continental Germanic peoples and the German self-designation Deitsch (Deutsch).

Speakers

Among the "fancy Dutch" (non-Anabaptist descendants) the language largely died out during the 20th century under pressure from English and the stigma of the World Wars era. Among the "plain" communities — the Old Order Amish and conservative Mennonites — it remains a vigorous first language learned in childhood, and the rapid growth of those populations has made Pennsylvania Dutch one of the few immigrant languages in North America whose speaker numbers are increasing. Speakers are typically trilingual in practice: Pennsylvania Dutch at home, English for school and the outside world, and Standard German for Bible and hymnody.

Features

The language is based mainly on Palatine (Pfälzisch) dialects with admixtures from other German varieties and three centuries of English borrowing and influence. Two spelling systems compete: one modelled on Standard German orthography and one (the Buffington–Barba system) that represents sounds using English spelling conventions. A modest literature, dictionaries, and a Wikipedia edition exist in the language.
