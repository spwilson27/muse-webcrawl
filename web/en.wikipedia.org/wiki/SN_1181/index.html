SN 1181 was a supernova, recorded as a "guest star" by Chinese and Japanese astronomers in 1181 CE, that remained visible for 185 days. It appeared in the constellation Cassiopeia and is one of only a handful of supernovae in the Milky Way confirmed by pre-telescopic historical records, along with SN 1006, SN 1054, SN 1572 and SN 1604.

Historical records

Chinese texts of the Song dynasty and the Japanese chronicle Azuma Kagami describe a guest star appearing in early August 1181. Its long visibility and brightness made it one of the best documented of the historical supernovae, yet for centuries its remnant remained unidentified.

Identification of the remnant

For decades the pulsar 3C 58 was proposed as the remnant, but its estimated age appeared inconsistent with an event in 1181. In 2013 amateur astronomer Dana Patchick discovered the faint nebula Pa 30, and in 2021 astronomers identified it as the remnant of SN 1181: at its centre lies an extremely hot, luminous star (IRAS 00500+6713) surrounded by a nebula expanding at speeds consistent with an explosion roughly 840 years ago. The system is interpreted as the product of a merger of two white dwarfs that produced a low-luminosity Type Iax supernova, leaving behind an unusual "zombie star" that survived the explosion.
