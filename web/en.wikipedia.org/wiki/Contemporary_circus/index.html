Contemporary circus

Contemporary circus (also known by the French term nouveau cirque, "new circus") is a genre of performing art that developed in the late 20th century in which a story, theme or narrative is conveyed through traditional circus skills. It contrasts with classical circus — a succession of unconnected acts introduced by a ringmaster, often featuring trained animals — by borrowing heavily from theatre, dance and music, and by typically excluding animal acts.

Characteristics

  • Narrative and theme: shows are structured around a story or unifying concept rather than a variety bill.
  • Character: performers play characters within the drama instead of presenting acts as themselves.
  • Original music and design: productions use composed scores, theatrical lighting, and integrated costume and set design.
  • No animal acts: driven by both artistic aims and animal-welfare concerns.
  • Venues: performed in theatres as well as big tops, and at arts festivals.

History

The movement emerged in the 1970s in France, Australia, the West Coast of the United States and the United Kingdom, partly as a reaction against the economics and aesthetics of the traditional touring circus. Companies such as France's Cirque du Soleil-adjacent pioneers — Archaos, Cirque Plume — and Britain's Ra-Ra Zoo were early exemplars. The Montreal-based Cirque du Soleil, founded in 1984 by Guy Laliberté and Gilles Ste-Croix, became the genre's global commercial success, followed by companies including Cirque Éloize, Les 7 doigts de la main (The 7 Fingers), Compagnie XY, Gandini Juggling, and Australia's Circus Oz (founded 1978).

France institutionalised support through circus schools and the Centre national des arts du cirque at Châlons-en-Champagne; Montreal's École nationale de cirque and TOHU complex made Quebec a hub. Contemporary circus is now a fixture of international arts festivals and an object of academic study in performance studies.
