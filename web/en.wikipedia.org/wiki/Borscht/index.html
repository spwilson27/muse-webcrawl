Borscht

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bowl of ruby-red borscht with a swirl of sour cream and dill]

Borscht is a sour soup of Eastern Europe, most famously ruby-red from beetroot, cooked with meat or vegetables and finished with sour cream and dill. Its name preserves an older soup still: borshch derives from hogweed, whose fermented stems flavoured the original Slavic sour soup before beets took over in the early modern period. In 2022 UNESCO inscribed the culture of Ukrainian borscht cooking on its List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding — a recognition, made in wartime, of borscht as a Ukrainian dish at the heart of family and ritual life across the region it is shared in.

Varieties

Every household keeps its own ratio. Ukrainian borscht typically builds on a meat or mushroom broth with beetroot (often soured as kvas or balanced with tomato and vinegar), cabbage, potato, carrot and beans, served with garlic pampushky buns. Green borscht swaps beets for sorrel in spring; white borscht (Polish żurek's cousin) sours with fermented rye. Jewish delicatessens made a cold, sweet-sour beet borscht famous in America; Lithuanian šaltibarščiai is a different, pink, kefir-based summer soup that battles borscht for the region's soup crown.

A soup as identity

Borscht's ingredients were peasant staples — beets keep, cabbage keeps, souring preserves — and its meanings grew with its reach: fasting versions for Lent, wedding versions, funeral versions. The 2022 UNESCO listing drew global attention to how thoroughly food carries national identity, and how fiercely recipes are defended when identity is contested. Underneath the argument sits the same bowl: sweet earth, sour brightness, white cream.
