Phototropism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Seedlings exhibiting positive phototropism.

Phototropism is the growth of an organism in response to a light stimulus. It is most often observed in plants, but also occurs in fungi. Growth towards a light source is called positive phototropism, while growth away from light is negative phototropism. Most plant shoots exhibit positive phototropism, bending toward light to maximise photosynthesis, while roots generally exhibit negative phototropism, growing into the soil.

Mechanism

Blue light is detected by photoreceptor proteins called phototropins. According to the Cholodny–Went model, light causes the plant hormone auxin to redistribute to the shaded side of a shoot tip; the higher auxin concentration stimulates cell elongation on that side, so the shoot bends toward the light. (In roots, the same redistribution inhibits elongation, producing the opposite bend.) Experiments by Charles and Francis Darwin on grass coleoptiles in 1880 first showed that the light-sensing region is the tip while the bending occurs below it.

Other organisms

Fungal fruiting bodies such as those of Phycomyces show strong phototropism and were classic experimental subjects. Some animals, such as moths flying toward lamps, show analogous behaviour, though this is taxis (movement) rather than tropism (growth).
