Ungulates (from Latin ungula, "hoof") are members of the diverse clade Euungulata ("true ungulates"), which consists primarily of large mammals with hooves. Terrestrial ungulates are mostly herbivorous and bear their weight on the tips of their toes, which are encased in hooves. The two living orders are the even-toed ungulates (Artiodactyla) — cattle, pigs, deer, giraffes, hippos and their relatives — and the odd-toed ungulates (Perissodactyla) — horses, rhinoceroses and tapirs.

Characteristics

Ungulates typically have reduced numbers of toes, elongated limbs adapted for running, and teeth specialised for grinding plant material. Many even-toed ungulates are ruminants, fermenting food in a multi-chambered stomach. Most bear horns, antlers, ossicones or tusks. Molecular studies have shown that cetaceans (whales and dolphins) evolved from within the even-toed ungulates, making the combined group Cetartiodactyla; the hippopotamus is the closest living relative of whales.

Evolution

Ungulates appeared in the early Eocene, roughly 55 million years ago, and diversified into a dominant group of large terrestrial herbivores during the Cenozoic. Several extinct groups, such as the South American meridiungulates and the predatory mesonychians, are sometimes discussed in relation to ungulate evolution, though their exact placement is debated.

Domestication

Many ungulates were domesticated early in human history and remain central to agriculture: cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, donkeys, camels, water buffalo and reindeer provide meat, milk, wool, leather and draught power.
