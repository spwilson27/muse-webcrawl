Ponzo illusion

The Ponzo illusion is a geometrical-optical illusion that was first demonstrated by the Italian psychologist Mario Ponzo (1882–1960) in 1911. Ponzo suggested that the human mind judges an object's size based on its background. In the classic version, two identical horizontal lines are drawn inside a pair of converging lines resembling railway tracks; the upper line appears larger, though the two are exactly the same length.

Explanation

The most common explanation appeals to perspective: the converging lines are interpreted by the visual system as parallel lines receding into the distance, so the line placed "farther away" is interpreted as longer, since an object at a greater distance must be larger to produce the same retinal image size — an application of size constancy. This is sometimes called the misapplied size-constancy explanation. A competing account, the framing or assimilation theory, holds that the illusion is caused by the contrast between the lines and the surrounding frame: the upper line, being closer to the converging sides, is judged relative to a smaller gap and so appears larger.

The Ponzo illusion is related to the moon illusion, in which the Moon appears larger near the horizon, and to corridor illusions used in perception experiments. Versions of the illusion work in three-dimensional scenes and in other sensory modalities, and sensitivity to it varies with age and across cultures, which has been used as evidence for the role of experience with carpentered environments and pictorial depth cues in perceptual development.

History

Mario Ponzo published the figure in 1911 in the context of his studies of the perception of size and of railway passengers' judgements of distance. The illusion subsequently became a standard demonstration in psychology and a test case for theories of depth perception and size constancy.
