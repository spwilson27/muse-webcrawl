The Great Exhibition

The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations, known simply as the Great Exhibition or the Crystal Palace Exhibition, was an international exhibition held in Hyde Park, London, from 1 May to 15 October 1851. It was the first in the series of world's fairs that became a defining institution of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Organisation

The exhibition was organised by Henry Cole and Prince Albert, president of the Royal Society of Arts, who saw it as a celebration of modern industrial technology and design. Exhibits were housed in the Crystal Palace, a revolutionary prefabricated building of cast iron and glass designed by Joseph Paxton and erected in months. More than 13,000 exhibitors — roughly half from Britain and its empire — filled it with machinery, manufactures, raw materials and fine art.

Attendance and legacy

Over six million visitors attended, including Charles Darwin, Charlotte Brontë and Charles Dickens, with specially arranged excursion trains bringing working-class visitors. The exhibition made a profit of £186,000, which was used to buy land in South Kensington and found the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum and the Natural History Museum, as well as Imperial College and the Royal Albert Hall. The Crystal Palace itself was dismantled and re-erected in Sydenham, where it stood until destroyed by fire in 1936.
