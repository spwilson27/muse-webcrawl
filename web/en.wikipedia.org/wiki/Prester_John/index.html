Prester John (Latin: Presbyter Johannes) was a legendary Christian patriarch, presbyter and king. Stories popular in Europe between the 12th and the 17th centuries told of a Nestorian patriarch and king who ruled over a Christian nation lost amidst the pagans and Muslims of the Orient, in a kingdom of fabulous wealth filled with wonders. Tales of the Prester often mixed features from the Acts of the Apostle Thomas in India, the Alexander romances and accounts of Central Asian peoples.

Origins

The first report appears in the chronicle of Otto of Freising (1145), recording that Hugh, bishop of Jabala in Syria, told the papal court at Viterbo of a certain John, a Christian priest and king beyond Persia and Armenia, who had defeated the Persians in battle (an echo, probably, of the Battle of Qatwan in 1141, when the Qara Khitai under Yelü Dashi defeated the Seljuks) and intended to march to the aid of the Holy Land.

The Letter of Prester John

Around 1165 there appeared a forged letter, purportedly written by Prester John to the Byzantine emperor Manuel I Komnenos, describing in extravagant detail a utopian kingdom of immense riches, inhabited by exotic peoples and creatures, containing the Fountain of Youth, a sea of sand, and pepper forests, ruled by a priest-emperor served by kings and archbishops. The letter survives in more than a hundred manuscripts and many versions, and was widely believed for centuries. During the Fifth Crusade, reports that "King David", son or grandson of Prester John, was advancing against Islam (in reality Genghis Khan's campaigns) raised and then dashed crusader hopes at Damietta in 1221.

Search in Asia and Africa

As knowledge of Central Asia grew — through travellers such as William of Rubruck and the career of the Kerait khan Toghril ("Ong Khan"), sometimes identified with the Prester — the legendary kingdom was relocated. From the 14th century onward it was increasingly identified with Ethiopia, whose real Christian emperors corresponded to the figure. Portuguese missions of the 15th and 16th centuries, culminating in the embassy of Pêro da Covilhã (1487) and Francisco Álvares's detailed account (1540), sought and in a sense "found" Prester John in the Ethiopian negus, though the reality — a real but far poorer mountain kingdom — differed greatly from the legend. Interest faded through the 17th century as exploration mapped the regions where he was supposed to live.

Legacy

The Prester John legend influenced medieval cartography, crusading strategy and European exploration for nearly five hundred years, and it has continued to inspire fiction — from medieval romances to Umberto Eco's Baudolino and modern fantasy and comics.
