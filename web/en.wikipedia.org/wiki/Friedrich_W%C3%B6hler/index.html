Friedrich Wöhler, pioneer of organic chemistry

Friedrich Wöhler (31 July 1800 – 23 September 1882) was a German chemist, known for his groundbreaking work in organic and inorganic chemistry. He was a student of Jöns Jacob Berzelius and a close collaborator of Justus von Liebig.

Urea synthesis

In 1828 Wöhler reported that he could produce urea — an "organic" compound previously obtained only from living organisms — by heating the inorganic salt ammonium cyanate. The synthesis is traditionally credited with undermining the doctrine of vitalism, the belief that organic compounds required a "vital force" from life, and with founding modern organic chemistry. He wrote triumphantly to Berzelius that he could make urea "without needing a kidney or an animal of any kind".

Other work

Wöhler isolated the elements aluminium (1827) and beryllium (1828), prepared calcium carbide and acetylene, discovered silicon nitride and hydroquinone, and with Liebig developed the concept of chemical radicals through their study of benzoyl compounds. From 1836 he was professor at Göttingen, where his laboratory trained generations of chemists and his textbooks were translated across Europe.
