"Women and children first", also known as the Birkenhead drill, is a code of conduct whereby the lives of women and children are to be saved first in a life-threatening situation, typically during the evacuation of a ship. Although the principle is not codified in international maritime law, it became a celebrated norm of chivalry at sea in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Origins

The phrase is associated with the wreck of the troopship HMS Birkenhead off South Africa in 1852, when soldiers stood in ranks on deck while the women and children aboard were loaded into the ship's boats; most of the soldiers drowned. The disciplined conduct of the troops was widely reported and praised, including by Rudyard Kipling in his poem "Soldier an' Sailor Too". An earlier example occurred in the 1840 wreck of the Poland.

Titanic

The protocol is most famously associated with the sinking of RMS Titanic in April 1912, where officers — particularly Second Officer Charles Lightoller on the port side — enforced a strict women-and-children-first (in Lightoller's case, effectively women and children only) rule for the lifeboats. As a result, about 74% of the women and 52% of the children aboard survived, compared with roughly 20% of the men, though survival also varied sharply by class. The sinking of the Lusitania in 1915, by contrast, was so rapid that the protocol could not be systematically applied.

Modern practice

Contemporary maritime evacuation procedures are based on orderly muster and boarding rather than formal priority by sex or age, and studies of later disasters show that in practice survival advantages vary. The phrase survives as a cultural shorthand for self-sacrificing conduct in emergencies.
