Federal Reserve Board of Governors

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, commonly known as the Federal Reserve Board, is the main governing body of the United States' central bank. Its seven members (governors) are appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate to staggered fourteen-year terms; the chair and vice-chairs serve four-year terms in those roles. The Board, based in Washington, D.C., supervises the twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks, regulates bank holding companies and state member banks, issues consumer-protection and payments rules, and (with the regional Reserve Bank presidents) sets monetary policy through the Federal Open Market Committee, on which all seven governors sit as voting members alongside five of the twelve Reserve Bank presidents — a Board majority that was a deliberately centralising feature of the 1935 settlement.

Created by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 (as the Federal Reserve Board; renamed and strengthened by the Banking Act of 1935), the Board balances political appointment against structural independence: long terms, budgetary autonomy from congressional appropriation (funded by assessments and portfolio income, remitting profits to the Treasury) and the dual mandate (maximum employment and stable prices, formalised by 1977 amendments). Its accountability runs through the chair's semiannual congressional testimony and published minutes and transcripts.
