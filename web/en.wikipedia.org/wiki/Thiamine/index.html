Thiamine, also known as thiamin and vitamin B1, is a vitamin, an essential micronutrient for humans and animals. It is found in food and commercially synthesized to be a dietary supplement or medication. Phosphorylated forms of thiamine are required for some metabolic reactions, including the breakdown of glucose and amino acids.

Food sources of thiamine include whole grains, legumes, and some meats and fish. Grain processing removes much of the thiamine content, so in many countries cereals and flours are enriched with thiamine. Supplements and medications are available to treat and prevent thiamine deficiency and the disorders that result from it, including beriberi and Wernicke encephalopathy. Other uses include treatment of maple syrup urine disease and Leigh syndrome. They are typically taken by mouth, but may also be given by intravenous or intramuscular injection.

Medical uses

Thiamine supplements are generally well tolerated. Allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, may occur when given by injection. Thiamine was discovered in 1897, was the first B vitamin to be isolated in 1926, and was first made in 1936. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Chemistry

Thiamine is a colorless organosulfur compound with the formula C12H17N4OS. Its structure consists of an aminopyrimidine and a thiazolium ring linked by a methylene bridge. It is soluble in water, methanol, and glycerol.

Biological role

Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), the active coenzyme form, is a cofactor for several enzymes involved in carbohydrate and branched-chain amino acid metabolism, including pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and transketolase of the pentose phosphate pathway.

Deficiency

Thiamine deficiency causes beriberi, a disease affecting the peripheral nervous system and cardiovascular system, and in severe cases Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. Populations relying on polished rice as a staple are at particular risk, as milling removes the thiamine-rich bran.

History

The Dutch physician Christiaan Eijkman observed that chickens fed polished rice developed a beriberi-like polyneuritis that was cured by unpolished rice, work that contributed to the discovery of vitamins. Casimir Funk coined the term "vitamine" for the anti-beriberi factor in 1912.
