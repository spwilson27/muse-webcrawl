Lester Willis Young (27 August 1909 – 15 March 1959), nicknamed "Prez" (short for President), was an American jazz tenor saxophonist and clarinettist. Coming to prominence with Count Basie's orchestra, he developed a cool, light, flowing style — played with a relaxed tone and advanced harmonic ideas — that stood in contrast to the robust approach of Coleman Hawkins and profoundly influenced later generations, from bebop players to the "cool jazz" school.

Career

Young grew up in a musical family, playing in his father's travelling band. After stints with King Oliver and Fletcher Henderson, he joined Count Basie in 1936 and made classic recordings with Basie's Kansas City Seven and small groups, as well as the celebrated late-1930s sessions accompanying Billie Holiday, who gave him his nickname (he called her "Lady Day" in return). His solos on "Lady Be Good," "Lester Leaps In," and "Taxi War Dance" became models of melodic improvisation.

Drafted into the US Army in 1944, Young suffered a traumatic court-martial and imprisonment that marked him for life. His later years were clouded by alcoholism, though he continued to record and tour, including with Jazz at the Philharmonic. He died in New York in 1959, hours after returning from engagements in Paris.

Style and influence

Young played with a light vibrato, a laid-back sense of swing, and long, singing phrases that deliberately floated over bar lines. He was a major influence on Charlie Parker, Stan Getz, Zoot Sims, and the West Coast cool school, and his slang ("cool," "bread" for money) entered everyday language.
