Dehydration reaction

In chemistry, a dehydration reaction is a chemical reaction that involves the loss of a water molecule from the reacting molecule or ion. Dehydration reactions are a common type of condensation reaction. The reverse of a dehydration reaction is a hydration reaction, in which water is added to a molecule, often splitting it into two products in a process known as hydrolysis.

Dehydration reactions in organic chemistry

Many dehydration reactions are used to convert alcohols into alkenes. Heating an alcohol with a strong acid such as concentrated sulfuric acid or phosphoric acid removes the hydroxyl group and a neighbouring hydrogen atom, forming a carbon–carbon double bond and a molecule of water. The regiochemistry of such eliminations is commonly described by Zaitsev's rule, which predicts that the more substituted alkene is the major product.

Other important examples include:

  • The conversion of two alcohol molecules into an ether (intermolecular dehydration), as in the industrial production of diethyl ether from ethanol.
  • The formation of esters from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol (esterification), in which water is eliminated.
  • The formation of amides and peptide bonds from carboxylic acids and amines, which underlies the synthesis of proteins in living systems (carried out enzymatically on the ribosome).
  • The dehydration of sugars, such as the charring of sucrose by concentrated sulfuric acid, and the conversion of glucose to hydroxymethylfurfural.
  • The synthesis of acid anhydrides from two carboxylic acid molecules.

Dehydrating agents

Common dehydrating agents used to drive dehydration reactions include concentrated sulfuric acid, phosphorus pentoxide, alumina, hot ceramic wool, and reagents such as thionyl chloride and phosphoryl chloride. The removal of water can shift an equilibrium toward products, in accordance with Le Chatelier's principle.
