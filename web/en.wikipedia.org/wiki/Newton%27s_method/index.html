In numerical analysis, Newton's method (also called the Newton–Raphson method) is an iterative algorithm for finding successively better approximations to the roots (zeroes) of a real-valued function. Starting from a guess x₀, each step replaces the function by its tangent line and takes the x-intercept as the next guess: x₁ = x₀ − f(x₀)/f′(x₀).

Convergence

Near a simple root, and with a good starting guess, the method converges quadratically — the number of correct digits roughly doubles each iteration. It can, however, diverge or cycle if the derivative is near zero or the starting point is poorly chosen. The method generalises to systems of equations and to optimisation, where it underlies quasi-Newton methods.

History

The method descends from procedures described by Isaac Newton in the seventeenth century and was put in its modern iterative form by Joseph Raphson (1690), with earlier root-finding ideas in Babylonian and Greek mathematics.
