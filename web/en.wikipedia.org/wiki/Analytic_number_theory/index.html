Analytic number theory is a branch of number theory that uses methods from mathematical analysis — especially complex analysis — to solve problems about the integers. Its central objects are the prime numbers and the functions (such as the Riemann zeta function and Dirichlet L-functions) that encode their distribution.

History

The subject is often dated from Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet's 1837 proof that there are infinitely many primes in any arithmetic progression whose first term and difference are coprime, which introduced Dirichlet characters and L-series. Bernhard Riemann's 1859 memoir "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude" connected the zeta function ζ(s) to the prime-counting function and stated the Riemann hypothesis — that all non-trivial zeros of ζ(s) lie on the line Re(s) = 1/2 — which remains unproven and is one of the Millennium Prize Problems.

Main results

In 1896 Jacques Hadamard and Charles Jean de la Vallée Poussin independently proved the prime number theorem, showing that the number of primes below x is asymptotic to x / ln x. G. H. Hardy and John Edensor Littlewood developed the circle method, applied to Waring's problem and the Goldbach conjectures; with Ramanujan they also refined partition asymptotics. Atle Selberg and Paul Erdős gave an "elementary" proof of the prime number theorem in 1948–49. Sieve methods, from Viggo Brun's work on twin primes through the large sieve to the 2013 breakthrough of Yitang Zhang on bounded prime gaps (refined by James Maynard and the Polymath project), form another central strand. In 2004 Ben Green and Terence Tao proved that the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

Branches and methods

Analytic number theory is often divided into multiplicative number theory (primes, arithmetic functions, Dirichlet convolution) and additive number theory (Goldbach, Waring, partitions). Modern work draws on harmonic analysis, spectral theory of automorphic forms, and algebraic geometry; the Langlands program has deepened links between L-functions and representation theory.
