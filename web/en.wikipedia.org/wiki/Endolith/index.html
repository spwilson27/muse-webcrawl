Endolith

An endolith is an organism (an archaeon, bacterium, fungus, lichen, alga or amoeba) that lives inside the pore spaces, fissures or structural cavities of a rock or in the pores between mineral grains. The word derives from the Greek endon ("inside") and lithos ("stone"). Endoliths are extremophiles in many cases, colonising some of the most inhospitable environments on Earth, and they are studied by astrobiologists as models for life that might survive in the subsurface of Mars and other bodies.

Cryptoendolithic communities form coloured bands a few millimetres beneath rock surfaces in Antarctica's Dry Valleys.

Classification

Following Golubic and colleagues (1981), endoliths are classified by how they occupy the rock: chasmoendoliths inhabit natural cracks and fissures; cryptoendoliths live in structural cavities within porous rocks such as sandstone, behind a protective surface crust; and euendoliths actively bore into the rock, dissolving tunnels matched to their body shape — common among marine cyanobacteria and fungi that penetrate limestone and shells.

Habitats

Endolithic communities are famous from the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica, where lichens and cyanobacteria survive a few millimetres inside Beacon sandstone, sheltered from wind, ultraviolet radiation and extreme desiccation while receiving just enough light for photosynthesis. Endoliths also occur in hyper-arid hot deserts such as the Atacama and Negev, inside deep-sea and terrestrial subsurface rocks, and in hydrothermal settings. The terrestrial deep subsurface biosphere, largely endolithic, may contain a substantial fraction of Earth's total bacterial biomass.

Survival and astrobiology

Endoliths tolerate extremes of temperature, dryness and radiation by entering dormant states, in some cases surviving for thousands of years. Because rock interiors on Mars would shield organisms from surface radiation and oxidants, cryptoendolithic habitats are a prime analogue in the search for extraterrestrial life; endolithic microbes also produce distinctive textural and chemical biosignatures that could persist in the geological record.
