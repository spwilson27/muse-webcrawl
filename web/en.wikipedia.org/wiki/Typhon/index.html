Typhon (/ˈtaɪfɒn, -fən/; Greek: Τυφῶν, Typhôn), also Typhoeus (Τυφωεύς), Typhaon (Τυφάων) or Typhos (Τυφώς), was a monstrous serpentine giant and one of the deadliest creatures in Greek mythology. According to Hesiod, Typhon was the son of Gaia and Tartarus. He was the last son of Gaia, fathered by Tartarus, and is considered the most powerful and deadliest of all creatures in Greek mythology. His most notable features were his hundred dragon heads, serpentine lower body, and vast wings.

Battle with Zeus

Typhon attempted to overthrow Zeus for the supremacy of the cosmos. In the ensuing battle, described at length in Hesiod's Theogony and Apollodorus' Bibliotheca, Typhon initially overcame Zeus, tearing out his sinews and hiding them in a cave guarded by the she-dragon Delphyne; Hermes and Aegipan recovered them. Zeus finally defeated Typhon with his thunderbolts and cast him into Tartarus, or — in a common later tradition — imprisoned him beneath Mount Etna in Sicily, whose volcanic eruptions were said to be caused by the monster's struggles.

Offspring

By the half-woman, half-serpent Echidna, Typhon fathered many of the most famous monsters of Greek myth: the two-headed dog Orthrus, the three-headed hound Cerberus, the many-headed Lernaean Hydra, the fire-breathing Chimera, the Sphinx, the Nemean lion, the dragon Ladon, and the eagle that tormented Prometheus. For this he is known as the "father of all monsters".

Interpretations

Typhon's myth has been compared with the Near Eastern combat myths in which a storm god defeats a chaos monster, such as the Hittite myth of Illuyanka. The name is also the probable origin, via Persian transmission, of the Arabic ṭūfān, and ultimately of the word "typhoon". In Egyptian-influenced Greek writing Typhon was identified with the Egyptian god Set.
