Windlass

A windlass is a machine used on ships to let out and heave up equipment such as a ship's anchor or a fishing trawl. It consists of a horizontal cylinder (the barrel) which is turned so that the anchor chain or rope wraps around it, together with a chainwheel (or "gypsy") with pockets shaped to grip the links of the chain. On most modern vessels anchor windlasses are driven by electric motors or hydraulic power; historically they were worked by hand, steam or, for the largest ships of the age of sail, by the crew pushing on bars.

Terminology

The windlass is distinguished from the capstan by the orientation of its axis: a windlass drum rotates about a horizontal axis, while a capstan rotates about a vertical axis. The word "windlass" is thought to derive from a combination of Old Norse vinda (to wind) and áss (a pole or beam). The command to release the anchor so it falls freely is "let go", and a windlass typically includes a brake and clutch so the chain can run out under control or be released for free fall.

Types

Anchor windlasses are mounted on the forecastle and handle the anchor chain, with a gypsy wheel matched to the chain's calibre. Mooring winches or mooring windlasses handle wire or fibre mooring lines to hold a vessel to the quay, and combination machines handle both. On small pleasure craft, compact electric windlasses with a remote control are common, greatly reducing the labour of anchoring.

Operation

To weigh anchor, the operator engages the clutch between the motor and the gypsy and releases the brake; the windlass hauls the chain in, and the chain is guided down the chain pipe into the chain locker while the anchor is drawn up to the hawsepipe. Because a windlass is far less powerful than a ship underway, seamanship practice is to use the engine to move the ship over its anchor rather than relying on the windlass alone to break the anchor out of the seabed.
