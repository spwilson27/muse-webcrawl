Paul Wittgenstein (5 November 1887 – 3 March 1961) was an Austrian-born concert pianist who became famous after losing his right arm in the First World War and rebuilding his career playing with the left hand alone. Using his family's great wealth, he commissioned an extraordinary body of left-hand works from leading composers, most famously Maurice Ravel's Piano Concerto for the Left Hand.

Early life and injury

Wittgenstein was born in Vienna into one of Europe's richest industrial families; his father Karl Wittgenstein was a steel magnate, and the family's Palais Wittgenstein hosted Brahms, Mahler, Clara Schumann and other luminaries. Paul studied with Josef Labor, the blind organist-composer, and made his debut in 1913 to promising reviews. Called up in August 1914 as a cavalry officer on the Eastern Front, he was shot in the elbow and captured by the Russians in Poland; his right arm was amputated. As a prisoner in Siberia he resolved to continue as a pianist, practising on a wooden box and composing exercises for the left hand while awaiting repatriation in a prisoner exchange.

The left-hand commissions

Resuming his career in 1916, Wittgenstein found the existing left-hand literature thin and began commissioning on a grand scale. Composers who wrote for him include:

  • Maurice Ravel — Piano Concerto for the Left Hand in D major (1929–30), premiered by Wittgenstein in Vienna in 1932
  • Sergei Prokofiev — Piano Concerto No. 4 (1931), which Wittgenstein never performed, saying he did not understand it
  • Benjamin Britten — Diversions for piano left hand and orchestra (1940)
  • Richard Strauss — Parergon zur Symphonia Domestica and the Panathenäenzug
  • Paul Hindemith — Piano Music with Orchestra, Op. 29 (1923), whose score, missing for decades, was rediscovered among Wittgenstein's papers after his widow's death and finally premiered in 2004
  • Erich Wolfgang Korngold, Franz Schmidt, and his teacher Josef Labor

Wittgenstein paid handsomely but demanded exclusive performing rights for his lifetime and freely altered scores — his interventions in Ravel's concerto led to a celebrated quarrel, Ravel insisting performers were "slaves" to the score; the two were eventually reconciled in print, though Wittgenstein kept his cadenzas.

Later life

Wittgenstein taught in Vienna until Austria's annexation by Nazi Germany; of Jewish descent on his father's side, he emigrated to the United States in 1938–39, negotiated his family's assets under persecution, and became an American citizen in 1946. He continued to perform and teach, maintaining that "if I couldn't play with the left hand, I wouldn't play at all." He died in New York in 1961. His commissions permanently enlarged the left-hand repertoire, later taken up by pianists such as Leon Fleisher and Gary Graffman after their own right-hand injuries.
