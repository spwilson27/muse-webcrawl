STOVL (short take-off and vertical landing) describes an aircraft's ability to take off from a short runway — or a carrier deck, often with the aid of a ski-jump ramp — and to land vertically. It is a compromise between pure VTOL capability and conventional operation: taking off after a short ground run, assisted by wing lift, lets the aircraft carry far more fuel and weapons than a vertical take-off would permit, while still needing no catapult or long runway for recovery.

Operational use

The concept was pioneered operationally by the British Harrier family: Sea Harriers operated from the Royal Navy's Invincible-class carriers using a ski-jump, a configuration that proved itself in the 1982 Falklands War. The United States Marine Corps adopted the AV-8B Harrier II for the same reason — close support from amphibious assault ships and austere forward sites. The F-35B, the STOVL variant of the Joint Strike Fighter, uses a shaft-driven lift fan plus a swivelling tailpipe, and serves with the US Marine Corps, the Royal Navy/Royal Air Force, and the Italian, Japanese, South Korean and Singaporean forces, among others. The Soviet Yakovlev Yak-141 demonstrated the technique but never entered service.

Advantages and drawbacks

STOVL frees fast-jet operations from large catapult-equipped carriers and long runways, enabling smaller, cheaper ships. The penalties are substantial: vertical-landing weight limits can force aircraft to jettison fuel or weapons before recovery ("bring-back" limits), and the lift systems add weight, complexity and maintenance burden that reduce range and payload compared with conventional carrier aircraft.
