Macedonia (ancient kingdom)

Macedonia (Greek: Μακεδονία, Makedonía), also called Macedon, was an ancient kingdom on the periphery of Archaic and Classical Greece, later the dominant state of Hellenistic Greece. Emerging around the 7th century BCE under the Argead dynasty, it rose to supremacy under Philip II (359–336 BCE) and his son Alexander the Great, whose conquests created one of the largest empires of the ancient world. The kingdom was dismantled by Rome in 168 BCE after the Battle of Pydna.

Early history

According to Herodotus and Thucydides, the Macedonians were a Greek people whose royal house, the Temenids or Argeads, claimed descent from Temenus of Argos. The kingdom's early heartland lay in the Haliacmon valley and the plain around its first capital, Aegae (modern Vergina). For centuries Macedon remained a rough frontier kingdom, periodically subject to Persian, Athenian and Spartan pressure, and its kings engaged cautiously in the politics of the southern city-states.

Rise under Philip II

Philip II transformed Macedon after 359 BCE: he reformed the army around the sarissa phalanx and combined arms, secured the kingdom's borders, gained control of the gold mines of Mount Pangaeus, and steadily extended Macedonian influence over Thessaly, Thrace and Illyria. His victory over Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea in 338 BCE made Macedon hegemon of Greece through the League of Corinth. Philip was assassinated at Aegae in 336 BCE.

Alexander and the Hellenistic age

Alexander III succeeded at twenty and, between 334 and 323 BCE, conquered the Persian Empire, Egypt and lands as far east as the Indus, spreading Greek language and culture across the Near East. After his death at Babylon, his empire fragmented among the Diadochi; Macedon itself eventually came under the Antigonid dynasty, ruling a reduced kingdom in Greece.

Fall to Rome

Macedon's interference in Greek affairs brought it into conflict with the Roman Republic. Defeats at Cynoscephalae (197 BCE) and Pydna (168 BCE) ended the monarchy; the kingdom was divided into four republics and, in 148 BCE, annexed as the Roman province of Macedonia.
