Extraversion and introversion

Extraversion and introversion are a central dimension in some human personality theories. The terms were popularized by Carl Jung, though both the popular understanding and psychological usage differ. Extraversion tends to be manifested in outgoing, talkative, energetic behaviour, whereas introversion is manifested in more reflective and reserved behaviour.

Jung's theory

Jung introduced the concepts in his 1921 work Psychological Types, describing extraversion as the direction of psychic energy (libido) outward toward the external world and introversion as its direction inward toward the subjective experience. Most modern tests measure the traits on a continuum; people near the middle are sometimes called ambiverts.

Trait models

In the Big Five model, extraversion is one of five broad dimensions, with facets including warmth, gregariousness, assertiveness, activity, excitement-seeking, and positive emotions. Hans Eysenck linked extraversion to cortical arousal, proposing that introverts have higher baseline arousal and therefore avoid stimulation, while extraverts seek it. Jeffrey Gray's reinforcement sensitivity theory and dopaminergic reward accounts have refined this biology.

Behaviour and outcomes

Extraversion predicts social network size, leadership emergence, and positive affect, while introversion is associated with preference for solitary work and smaller but deeper social circles. Neither pole is considered pathological, and expression of the trait varies across cultures and situations.
