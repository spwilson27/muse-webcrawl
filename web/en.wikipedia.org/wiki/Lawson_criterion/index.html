Lawson criterion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In nuclear fusion research, the Lawson criterion is a figure of merit used to express the conditions under which a plasma will produce more fusion energy than the energy input required to heat it. It was formulated by the British engineer and physicist John D. Lawson in 1955 and published in 1957.

Formulation

Lawson showed that a fusion device must achieve a minimum product of plasma density and energy confinement time at a given temperature. In modern usage the criterion is commonly expressed through the fusion triple product:

n T τE ≥ 3 × 1021 keV·s·m−3

for deuterium–tritium fuel near its optimum temperature of roughly 15 keV (about 170 million kelvin). Here n is the particle density, T the temperature, and τE the energy confinement time. Reaching this product corresponds to ignition — a self-sustaining burn in which heating by fusion products alone maintains the plasma temperature — while lower thresholds correspond to breakeven (Q = 1) and to useful net energy gain.

Significance

The criterion frames fusion progress as simultaneously mastering density, temperature, and confinement. Magnetic-confinement devices such as tokamaks pursue long confinement at moderate density; inertial-confinement devices pursue extreme density for very short times. In December 2022 the National Ignition Facility reported ignition, a milestone measured against criteria descended from Lawson's analysis.
