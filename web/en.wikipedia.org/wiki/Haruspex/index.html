Haruspex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Piacenza liver, a bronze model of a sheep's liver inscribed with the names of Etruscan gods.

A haruspex (plural haruspices) was a religious specialist in ancient Rome and Etruria trained in haruspicy: divination by inspecting the entrails (exta) of sacrificed animals, above all the liver of a sheep. The practice formed part of the disciplina Etrusca, the body of Etruscan religious lore that Rome adopted wholesale.

Practice

After a sacrifice, the haruspex examined the colour, shape and markings of the liver, gall bladder, lungs and heart. A smooth, full liver was favourable; abnormalities — a missing lobe, spots, or the "head of the liver" (caput iecoris) being absent or displaced — portended disaster and might require the sacrifice to be repeated or expiatory victims offered. The bronze Piacenza liver (discovered in 1877) divides the organ into forty regions, each assigned to a deity of the Etruscan sky, serving as a template correlating the liver's surface with the heavens. Haruspices also interpreted lightning and prodigies, and the Roman Senate formally consulted them on ominous events.

History

The art derived ultimately from Mesopotamian liver divination (bārûtu), transmitted through Anatolia to the Etruscans. Rome institutionalised it: a college of sixty haruspices was maintained, though they stood outside the official priestly colleges of Roman citizens. The emperor Claudius, an antiquarian, proposed state support for the discipline in AD 47. Haruspices accompanied Roman armies into late antiquity — notoriously, they are said to have warned Julius Caesar, and to have attended Julian's Persian campaign in 363 — before Christian emperors suppressed the practice along with public sacrifice in the fourth century.
