Fluency

Fluency
FieldLinguistics, speech-language pathology, education
ComponentsAccuracy, rate, prosody, automaticity
OppositeDysfluency

Fluency refers to continuity, smoothness, rate, and effort in producing speech or language. In language learning, fluency is the ability to speak or write a language with ease, accuracy, and appropriate speed. In speech-language pathology, fluency describes the flow of speech sounds, syllables, and words; its disruption is called dysfluency, of which stuttering is the best-known example.

Language proficiency

In second-language acquisition, fluency is often distinguished from accuracy. A fluent speaker produces language rapidly and smoothly with few pauses or hesitations, even if occasional grammatical errors occur. Proficiency frameworks such as the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) and the ACTFL guidelines treat fluency as one dimension of overall communicative competence alongside accuracy, vocabulary range, and pronunciation.

Reading fluency

Reading fluency is the ability to read text accurately, quickly, and with proper expression (prosody). It is widely regarded as a bridge between word recognition and reading comprehension: readers who decode words automatically can devote more cognitive resources to understanding meaning. Educators commonly assess oral reading fluency by counting words read correctly per minute.

Speech fluency disorders

Fluency disorders include stuttering, characterized by repetitions, prolongations, and blocks in the flow of speech, and cluttering, characterized by a rapid or irregular speaking rate and excessive dysfluencies. Speech-language pathologists treat these conditions with techniques that modify speech patterns and reduce avoidance behaviors.
