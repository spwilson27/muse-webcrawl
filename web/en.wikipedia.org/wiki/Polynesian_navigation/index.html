Polynesian navigation

Polynesian navigation is the system of wayfinding used by Polynesian voyagers to cross thousands of kilometres of open Pacific Ocean in double-hulled canoes and settle the islands of the Polynesian Triangle — Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui (Easter Island), and Aotearoa (New Zealand) — without instruments, charts, or writing.

A traditional double-hulled voyaging canoe of the kind used in Polynesian exploration.

Techniques

Navigators memorised a star compass: the rising and setting points of key stars divided the horizon into named "houses" that gave bearings to destination islands. Course was held by steering to successive stars through the night and by the sun and ocean swells by day. Navigators read deep-ocean swells refracted and reflected by distant islands, observed land-based seabirds flying out to feed and returning at dusk, watched cloud formations that gather over islands, and detected lagoon reflections and bioluminescence patterns. In the Marshall Islands, stick charts recorded swell patterns.

Settlement of the Pacific

The ancestors of the Polynesians, the Lapita culture, spread from the Bismarck Archipelago into Remote Oceania by about 1000 BC; after a long pause in West Polynesia, voyagers settled East Polynesia, reaching Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui, and finally New Zealand by roughly 1200–1300 AD — one of the greatest feats of pre-modern exploration.

Revival

By the twentieth century the knowledge had largely been lost in Polynesia itself. The 1976 voyage of the canoe Hōkūleʻa from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti, navigated without instruments by the Micronesian master navigator Mau Piailug of Satawal, sparked a Pacific-wide cultural renaissance in traditional voyaging, carried forward by organisations such as the Polynesian Voyaging Society.
