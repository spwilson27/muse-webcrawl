Transformation (genetics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In molecular biology and genetics, transformation is the genetic alteration of a cell resulting from the direct uptake and incorporation of exogenous genetic material (DNA) from its surroundings through the cell membrane. It is one of three horizontal gene transfer processes in bacteria, alongside transduction (mediated by viruses) and conjugation (direct cell-to-cell contact).

History

Transformation was first demonstrated in 1928 by Frederick Griffith, who showed that a harmless strain of Streptococcus pneumoniae could be made virulent by exposure to heat-killed virulent bacteria — the "transforming principle". In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty showed the transforming principle was DNA, a landmark result indicating that DNA is the hereditary material.

Natural competence

Some bacteria — including species of Streptococcus, Bacillus, Neisseria, and Haemophilus — can enter a physiological state of competence in which they actively take up environmental DNA using dedicated membrane machinery, stimulated in some species by quorum-sensing signals. Incorporated DNA can be used for genetic diversity, DNA repair, or nutrition.

Artificial transformation

In the laboratory, transformation is a routine technique for introducing plasmid DNA into bacteria such as Escherichia coli. Cells are made competent chemically (e.g. with calcium chloride) and subjected to heat shock, or by electroporation, in which an electric pulse opens temporary pores in the membrane. Related methods deliver DNA into yeast, plant, and animal cells, where the process is usually termed transfection for animal cells and stable incorporation is used to create transgenic organisms.
