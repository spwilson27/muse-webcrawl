True north

True north (also called geodetic north) is the direction along the Earth's surface towards the geographic North Pole — the northern end of the axis about which the Earth rotates. It is the reference direction from which azimuths are measured in surveying and navigation, and it differs from magnetic north, the direction in which a compass needle points, by an angle known as magnetic declination.

Definition

Every point on the Earth's surface has a meridian — a great circle passing through both geographic poles. True north at that point is the direction along the local meridian toward the North Pole. Because meridians converge at the poles, the direction of true north changes from place to place. Stars, in particular Polaris in the Northern Hemisphere, can be used to determine true north astronomically, since the north celestial pole lies very close to that star.

True, magnetic and grid north

Three distinct "norths" are in common use:

  • True north — toward the geographic North Pole.
  • Magnetic north — toward the north magnetic pole, the direction a compass indicates. The magnetic pole wanders over time, so declination must be corrected and updated.
  • Grid north — the direction of the vertical grid lines on a map projection, which coincides with true north only along the projection's central meridian; the small difference is called grid convergence.

Navigators convert between compass bearings and true bearings by applying the local declination, which is printed on nautical and aeronautical charts and updated in geomagnetic models such as the World Magnetic Model.

Determination

True north can be found by observing the Sun's transit or shadow at local noon, by gyrocompass (which senses the Earth's rotation and is unaffected by magnetism), by GNSS positioning, or by astronomical observation of circumpolar stars. Modern surveying instruments and aircraft heading reference systems all ultimately report heading relative to true north.
