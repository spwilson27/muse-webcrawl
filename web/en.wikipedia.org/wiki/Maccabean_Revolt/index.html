The Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BCE) was a Jewish rebellion against the Seleucid Empire and the Hellenising policies of its king, Antiochus IV Epiphanes. Led initially by the priest Mattathias and then by his son Judas Maccabeus, the revolt rededicated the Temple in Jerusalem — an event commemorated by the Jewish festival of Hanukkah — and laid the groundwork for the independent Hasmonean kingdom.

Causes

Antiochus IV, eager to unify his empire culturally and raise funds, sold the high priesthood, and his officials outraged Jewish religious sensibilities. In 168 BCE he sacked Jerusalem and in 167 issued decrees forbidding the Sabbath, circumcision, and dietary laws on pain of death; the Temple was desecrated with pagan sacrifices — the "abomination of desolation" of the Book of Daniel. When a royal official ordered Mattathias to offer pagan sacrifice at Modein, Mattathias refused, killed the official and a compliant Jew, and fled to the hills with his sons, sparking a rural uprising.

The revolt

Judas Maccabeus, a brilliant guerrilla commander, won a string of victories over Seleucid generals at Emmaus, Beth Zur, and Beth Zechariah, exploiting mobility and knowledge of the terrain. In December 164 BCE his forces entered Jerusalem, cleansed the Temple, and rekindled the menorah — tradition holds the oil lasted eight days (Hanukkah). Judas fell in battle at Elasa in 160 BCE, but his brothers Jonathan and Simon continued the struggle, securing tribute, alliances with Rome, and ultimately full independence around 142 BCE.

Legacy

The revolt is recorded in the deuterocanonical Books of the Maccabees and in Josephus. It became a central symbol of Jewish religious and national self-assertion — from Hanukkah's celebration of religious freedom to modern Zionist iconography of the fighting Jew. Historian debate continues over how far the conflict was purely religious versus a struggle for political power among Judean factions.
