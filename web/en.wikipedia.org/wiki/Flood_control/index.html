Flood control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Thames Barrier raised against a storm surge in London
The Thames Barrier raised against a storm surge in London

Flood control (or flood mitigation) comprises the methods used to reduce or prevent the damaging effects of floodwaters — levees and flood walls, dams and reservoirs, channel works, floodplain zoning, wetlands restoration and forecasting-and-warning systems. Its history is a negotiation with rivers: from Mesopotamian and Chinese levee systems to the Netherlands' Delta Works and modern 'room for the river' strategies that retreat from hard defence to accommodation.

Contents1. Methods
2. Climate era

Methods

Structural measures (embankments, barriers such as the Thames Barrier and Maeslantkering, detention basins, pumps) restrain water; non-structural measures (land-use planning, flood insurance, building codes, early warning) reduce exposure. The 'levee effect' — protection encouraging development that magnifies eventual catastrophe — and failures like New Orleans in Hurricane Katrina (2005) taught that residual risk never vanishes.

Climate era

Rising seas and intensifying rainfall are driving a paradigm shift toward adaptive, layered strategies: managed retreat, sponge cities (China's programme), restored floodplains and nature-based defences, with megaprojects (Jakarta's sea wall, Venice's MOSE) racing the water in sinking deltas.
