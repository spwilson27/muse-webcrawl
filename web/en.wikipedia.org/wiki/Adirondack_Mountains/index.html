The Adirondack Mountains form a great circular massif in northeastern New York State, separated from Vermont by Lake Champlain. Unlike most eastern ranges, they are not part of the Appalachian chain; geologically they are a southern extension of the Laurentian (Canadian Shield) highlands, composed of billion-year-old metamorphic and igneous rocks that are still slowly rising. The mountains lie almost entirely within the Adirondack Park, the largest publicly protected area in the contiguous United States.

Geography

The range contains more than a hundred summits, including the 46 "High Peaks" traditionally listed as over 4,000 feet; the highest, Mount Marcy (5,344 ft), is the tallest mountain in New York. Thousands of lakes and ponds — among them Lake Placid, Saranac Lake and the Fulton Chain — dot the region, which is the source of the Hudson River. Forests are a transition mix of northern hardwoods, spruce and fir, with alpine tundra on the highest summits. Lake Placid hosted the Winter Olympic Games in 1932 and 1980.

History and conservation

The name is said to derive from a Mohawk word meaning "bark eaters", applied to neighbouring Algonquian peoples. Heavy logging and forest fires in the nineteenth century prompted New York to create a Forest Preserve in 1885 and the Adirondack Park in 1892; in 1894 the state constitution's "forever wild" clause (Article XIV) protected state lands from timber cutting and sale. The park today is a mosaic of public Forest Preserve and private land, overseen by the Adirondack Park Agency.
