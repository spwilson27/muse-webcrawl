Charles Frederick Worth

Charles Frederick Worth (13 October 1825 – 10 March 1895) was an English fashion designer who founded the House of Worth, one of the foremost fashion houses of the 19th and early 20th centuries. He is widely credited as the father of haute couture, transforming dressmaking from a craft trade into an art directed by the designer.

Early life

Worth was born in Bourne, Lincolnshire, and apprenticed as a draper in London, working at the firm of Swan & Edgar in Piccadilly. In 1846 he moved to Paris, then the centre of the silk and fashion trades, and worked his way up at the maison Gagelin.

House of Worth

In 1858 Worth opened his own house, Worth et Bobergh, at 7 Rue de la Paix. His fortunes rose when his designs attracted the attention of Princess Pauline von Metternich, who introduced him to Empress Eugénie, wife of Napoleon III. The Empress became his most famous client, and Worth soon dressed royalty and society figures across Europe and America.

Worth revolutionized fashion: he presented seasonal collections shown on live models, sewed his label into garments like an artist's signature, and expected clients to accept his designs rather than dictate them. He popularized the crinoline at its height and later the bustle, and created garments such as the princess line and elaborate court trains.

Legacy

After Worth's death in 1895 his sons Gaston-Lucien and Jean-Philippe continued the house. His model of the designer as creative author remains the basis of the modern fashion industry.
