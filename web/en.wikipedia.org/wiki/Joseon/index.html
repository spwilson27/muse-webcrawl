Joseon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Gyeongbokgung Palace in Seoul, the main palace of the Joseon dynasty]

Joseon (1392–1897, then the Korean Empire until 1910) was a Korean dynastic state founded by Yi Seong-gye (King Taejo) after the fall of Goryeo, and the longest-lived Confucian dynasty in East Asia. From its capital at Hanseong (modern Seoul) it ruled the Korean peninsula for five centuries, reshaping Korean society on Neo-Confucian lines: the yangban scholar-official class, the civil examinations, and under Sejong the Great the invention of the Hangul alphabet (1443/1446) — one of history's most deliberate creations of a writing system. Joseon survived the Imjin War against Japan (1592–1598) and Manchu invasions, endured as the 'Hermit Kingdom' of Western accounts, and ended amid the imperial rivalries that led to Japanese annexation in 1910.

A Confucian kingdom

Joseon's founders replaced Goryeo's Buddhism with Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucianism as state ideology, remaking landholding, ritual and family law. Sejong (r. 1418–1450) sponsored scholarship, science (rain gauges, astronomical instruments) and Hangul — promulgated in the Hunminjeongeum — despite aristocratic opposition to a script for commoners. Factional strife among scholar-officials (sarim) repeatedly convulsed the court in literati purges.

Invasions and late Joseon

Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasions (1592–1598) devastated the peninsula; Admiral Yi Sun-sin's turtle ships and Ming Chinese intervention repelled the Japanese, at ruinous cost. Manchu invasions (1627, 1636) forced Joseon to transfer allegiance from Ming to Qing. The 18th century brought a renaissance under Yeongjo and Jeongjo, but the 19th brought court corruption by in-law families, peasant rebellions, and foreign pressure; the dynasty became the Korean Empire in 1897, a Japanese protectorate in 1905, and was annexed in 1910.
