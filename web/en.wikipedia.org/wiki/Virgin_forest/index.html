An old-growth forest (also called primary forest, virgin forest, primeval forest, or late seral forest) is a forest that has developed over a long period of time without significant disturbance and thereby exhibits unique ecological features. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations defines a primary forest as a "naturally regenerated forest of native tree species, where there are no clearly visible indications of human activities and the ecological processes are not significantly disturbed."

More than one-third (34 percent) of the world's forests are primary forests, on more than 1.11 billion hectares, with the largest areas in the Amazon, the Congo Basin, boreal Canada and Russia, and parts of Southeast Asia and New Guinea.

Characteristics

Old-growth forests typically contain large, old trees of a range of ages, a multi-layered canopy with gaps created by falling trees, standing dead trees (snags), and abundant fallen logs (coarse woody debris) in all stages of decomposition. This structural complexity creates many niches, supporting high biodiversity, including species that depend on undisturbed forest interior. Tree ages in old growth can reach several hundred or, in species such as coast redwood and giant sequoia, more than a thousand years.

Ecological importance

Old-growth forests store large amounts of carbon in biomass and soils, regulate water cycles, and serve as refuges for species that cannot survive in managed or secondary forest, such as the northern spotted owl of the Pacific Northwest. Although young forests may absorb carbon faster per year, old-growth forests continue to accumulate carbon and hold far greater total stocks; their conversion to other land uses releases large quantities of greenhouse gases.

Distribution and loss

The largest remaining tracts of virgin forest are in the Amazon rainforest, the Congo Basin, Canada and Russia's boreal forests, and the temperate rainforests of the Pacific coast of North America, Chile, Tasmania, and New Zealand. Standalone famous remnants include Białowieża Forest on the Polish–Belarusian border, one of Europe's last large primeval lowland forests. Globally, primary forest has declined through logging, conversion to agriculture and plantations, mining, and infrastructure; between 1990 and 2020 the world lost tens of millions of hectares of primary forest.

Definitions and debate

Definitions of "old growth" vary by forest type and region, and the concept has been contested in forestry policy—particularly in North America, where designations affect logging on public lands. Some ecologists prefer the term "primary forest," reserving "old growth" for a developmental stage that a previously disturbed (secondary) forest can also eventually reach. Both differ from ancient woodland (in British usage) and from untouched wilderness generally.
