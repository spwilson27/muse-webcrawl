Die Wende (German for "the turn" or "the change") is a German term that has come to stand for the complete process of political change in the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) in 1989–1990, from the end of the domination of the Socialist Unity Party (SED) to the adoption of a democratic system and the reunification of Germany on 3 October 1990. The term was popularised publicly by SED leader Egon Krenz in October 1989, who promised a Wende in policy — but it soon came to denote the far deeper transformation driven by the citizens' movement.

Course of events

Through the summer of 1989, thousands of East Germans fled via Hungary after it opened its border with Austria, while Monday demonstrations in Leipzig and other cities grew from prayer meetings into mass protests chanting "Wir sind das Volk" ("We are the people"). Erich Honecker was replaced by Krenz in October 1989, but reforms came too late: on 9 November 1989, after a botched press conference by Günter Schabowski, the Berlin Wall was opened. Free elections in March 1990 brought a government committed to unification, and monetary union followed in July 1990.

Aftermath

The Wende encompassed not only reunification but also the Treuhand privatisation of the East German economy, the opening of the Stasi files, and profound social dislocation in the new federal states — experiences that continue to shape German politics and the memory culture of Ostalgie.
