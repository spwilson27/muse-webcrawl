René Noël Théophile Girard (25 December 1923 – 4 November 2015) was a French historian, literary critic, and philosopher of social science whose work belongs to the tradition of philosophical anthropology. He is best known for mimetic theory: the idea that human desire is fundamentally imitative ("mimetic"), which leads to rivalry and conflict, and that societies have historically resolved such crises through the scapegoat mechanism — the unanimous violence against a single victim, which he argued lies at the foundation of religion and culture.

Work

In Deceit, Desire and the Novel (1961) Girard analysed the works of Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Proust and Dostoevsky to develop his theory of triangular desire: we desire objects according to the desire of a model or mediator. In Violence and the Sacred (1972) and Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World (1978), he extended the theory to myth, ritual sacrifice, and the Bible, arguing that the Gospels uniquely expose and undermine the scapegoat mechanism by proclaiming the innocence of the victim.

Career and recognition

Girard emigrated to the United States in 1947 and taught at Duke, Bryn Mawr, Johns Hopkins, SUNY Buffalo and, from 1981, Stanford University. He was elected to the Académie française in 2005.
