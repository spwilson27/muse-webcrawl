Sandline affair

The Sandline affair was a political scandal that became public in 1997, when it was revealed that the government of Papua New Guinea, then led by Prime Minister Sir Julius Chan, had secretly contracted the private military company Sandline International to deploy mercenaries to fight in the Bougainville conflict, with the aim of retaking the Panguna copper mine. The affair triggered a military mutiny, brought down the Chan government, and helped precipitate a peace process on Bougainville.

Background

Since 1988, the Papua New Guinea Defence Force (PNGDF) had been fighting the Bougainville Revolutionary Army in a secessionist conflict that closed the Panguna mine and killed thousands. Frustrated after years of stalemate, Chan's government signed a contract worth about US$36 million with Sandline International, run by former British officer Tim Spicer, in January 1997. Around 40–70 Sandline personnel, including veterans of the South African firm Executive Outcomes, were deployed to Port Moresby with weapons and helicopters.

Crisis

When news of the contract leaked in March 1997, Brigadier-General Jerry Singirok, commander of the PNGDF, denounced the mercenaries, launched "Operation Rausim Kwik", and ordered them detained and deported, demanding Chan's resignation. Days of political crisis and protests followed; Chan stepped aside (and soon resigned), parliament was suspended amid military defiance, and the affair dominated the 1997 general election, in which Chan lost his seat. The Sandline personnel were expelled without fighting on Bougainville.

Aftermath

Spicer sued for the unpaid fee under the contract; an arbitral tribunal awarded Sandline several million dollars. In Papua New Guinea the crisis discredited military solutions on Bougainville and opened the way to the Burnham talks and the eventual Bougainville Peace Agreement of 2001. Internationally, the affair intensified debate over the regulation of private military companies.
