Ground proximity warning system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ground proximity warning system (GPWS) is a system designed to alert pilots if their aircraft is in immediate danger of flying into the ground or an obstacle. The United States Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) defines GPWS as a type of terrain awareness and warning system (TAWS). More advanced systems, introduced in 1996, are known as enhanced ground proximity warning systems (EGPWS), which add a terrain database and forward-looking capability.

History

In the late 1960s, a series of controlled flight into terrain (CFIT) accidents — in which a fully functional aircraft was flown into the ground under pilot control — killed hundreds of people. Studies by the National Transportation Safety Board found that many of these accidents could have been avoided if a warning device had alerted the pilots in time. Canadian engineer C. Donald Bateman is credited with developing the original GPWS at Honeywell in the early 1970s.

Beginning in 1974, the FAA required all large turbine and turbojet airplanes to install GPWS equipment. Following this mandate, there was a sharp decline in CFIT accidents in the United States. In 2000, the mandate was extended to smaller aircraft in the form of TAWS requirements.

Operation

The system monitors the aircraft's height above ground using a radio altimeter and compares the flight profile against a set of alerting modes, including excessive descent rate, excessive terrain closure rate, altitude loss after takeoff, unsafe terrain clearance when not in landing configuration, and excessive deviation below the glideslope. When a hazardous condition is detected, the system issues aural warnings such as "PULL UP", "TERRAIN", "SINK RATE", or "DON'T SINK", together with visual alerts.

Enhanced systems combine the classic modes with a worldwide terrain and obstacle database and the aircraft's GPS position, allowing alerts to be generated well before the aircraft reaches hazardous terrain — often 30 to 60 seconds ahead of a potential impact.

Effectiveness

Before the introduction of GPWS, CFIT accidents involving large aircraft occurred at a rate of roughly one per year in the United States. After the mandate, the rate dropped dramatically; by some estimates, CFIT accidents for equipped aircraft fell to near zero in U.S. airspace for extended periods. GPWS is widely regarded as one of the most successful safety innovations in aviation history.
