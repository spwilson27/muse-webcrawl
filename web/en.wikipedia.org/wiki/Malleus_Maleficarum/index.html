The Malleus Maleficarum, usually rendered The Hammer of Witches, is the best-known treatise on witchcraft. It was written by the German Catholic clergyman Heinrich Kramer (Henricus Institoris) and published in Speyer in 1486 or 1487; the name of fellow Dominican Jacob Sprenger was added as co-author, a claim modern scholars generally doubt. The book became the standard handbook for witch-hunters in secular and ecclesiastical courts for the following two centuries.

Contents

The work is divided into three parts. The first argues, with copious theological citation, that witchcraft is real and that to disbelieve in it is heresy. The second describes the practices of witches — pacts with the Devil, flight, harmful magic (maleficia), and the incubi and succubi — and how they may be countered. The third part is a legal manual: how to initiate proceedings, gather evidence, use torture, and pass sentence. The text is notorious for its virulent misogyny, asserting that women are more prone to witchcraft because of intellectual and moral weakness; the great majority of its accused are women.

Authority and reception

Kramer prefixed to the book the 1484 papal bull Summis desiderantes affectibus, in which Pope Innocent VIII authorised Kramer and Sprenger to prosecute witches in Germany, and an approbation purporting to come from the University of Cologne's theology faculty — the genuineness and scope of which are disputed. The Church itself was ambivalent: the Spanish Inquisition treated the book with suspicion, and some theologians condemned its procedures. Nevertheless, at least 28 editions appeared between 1486 and 1669, and the Malleus profoundly shaped the great European witch hunts, contributing legal reasoning that helped send tens of thousands — mostly women — to execution.

Legacy

Today the Malleus Maleficarum is studied as a primary document of early modern demonology and of institutionalised misogyny. Complete English translations were published by Montague Summers (1928, uncritical) and by Christopher S. Mackay (2006/2009, scholarly).
