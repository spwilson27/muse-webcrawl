Ménière's disease

Ménière's disease (MD) is a chronic disorder of the inner ear characterised by recurrent episodes of vertigo, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus (ringing in the ear) and a sensation of fullness or pressure in the affected ear. The condition is named after the French physician Prosper Ménière, who in 1861 first reported that vertigo could result from inner ear dysfunction rather than from cerebral causes.

Signs and symptoms

Attacks of spontaneous vertigo typically last from 20 minutes to several hours (by diagnostic criteria, 20 minutes to 12 hours), often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Hearing during attacks characteristically worsens in the low frequencies and usually recovers at first, but over years many patients develop permanent hearing loss. Tinnitus and aural fullness in the affected ear often accompany or precede attacks. The disease usually begins in one ear, though it may become bilateral in a substantial minority of patients. Sudden falls without loss of consciousness (Tumarkin otolithic crises, or "drop attacks") occur in a small number of cases.

Causes and pathophysiology

The underlying cause of Ménière's disease is unknown. It is strongly associated with endolymphatic hydrops — an excessive accumulation of endolymph fluid within the membranous labyrinth of the inner ear — though hydrops is also found in people without symptoms, so it may be a marker rather than the sole cause. Proposed contributing factors include genetic predisposition (familial cases occur), autoimmune processes, viral infection, allergy, migraine association and abnormal fluid regulation in the endolymphatic sac.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical and based on criteria such as those of the Bárány Society (2015): two or more spontaneous episodes of vertigo lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours; audiometrically documented low- to medium-frequency sensorineural hearing loss in the affected ear; fluctuating aural symptoms; and exclusion of other causes. Magnetic resonance imaging is used to rule out retrocochlear pathology such as vestibular schwannoma.

Treatment

There is no cure, and management aims to reduce the frequency and severity of vertigo attacks and preserve hearing. First-line measures include a low-salt diet and maintenance therapy with betahistine (widely used in Europe) or diuretics such as hydrochlorothiazide. Acute attacks may be treated with vestibular suppressants such as meclizine. For refractory cases, intratympanic injections of gentamicin or corticosteroids, endolymphatic sac surgery, or, as a last resort, chemical or surgical labyrinthectomy may be considered. Psychological support and vestibular rehabilitation are often helpful.

Epidemiology

Ménière's disease most commonly begins between the ages of 40 and 60 and affects women slightly more often than men. Prevalence estimates vary widely, from roughly 3.5 to over 500 per 100,000 people, partly because of differing diagnostic criteria.
