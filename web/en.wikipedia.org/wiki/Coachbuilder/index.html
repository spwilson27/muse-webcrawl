Coachbuilder

A coachbuilt body by an independent firm on a manufacturer's rolling chassis.

A coachbuilder is a company that manufactures bodies for passenger-carrying vehicles. The term derives from the makers of horse-drawn coaches, and it carried over to the early automobile industry, when many manufacturers sold only a rolling chassis — engine, transmission, and running gear — and wealthy customers commissioned an independent coachbuilder to design and build a bespoke body to their specifications.

History

In the first decades of the twentieth century, coachbuilding was the normal way of buying a luxury car. Renowned firms included Pininfarina, Zagato, and Touring in Italy; Figoni et Falaschi and Saoutchik in France; Hooper and Mulliner in Britain; and Brewster, LeBaron, and Fleetwood in the United States. Chassis from makers such as Rolls-Royce, Bugatti, Duesenberg, and Delahaye were routinely clothed in one-off coachwork.

The spread of mass-produced steel unit bodies in the 1930s, and the cost pressures of the Great Depression, destroyed most of the trade. Manufacturers brought body production in-house, and surviving coachbuilders either were absorbed, became design houses, or shifted to specialist and commercial work.

Modern coachbuilding

A small bespoke coachbuilding sector survives. Firms such as Zagato and Touring Superleggera produce limited-run bodies on modern supercar platforms, and manufacturers including Ferrari and Rolls-Royce have revived one-off coachbuilt commissions for individual clients. The term is also still used for builders of bus and motorhome bodies on separate chassis.
