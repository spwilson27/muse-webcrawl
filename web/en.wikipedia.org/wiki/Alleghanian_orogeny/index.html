The Alleghanian orogeny (also spelled Alleghenian) was a mountain-building event that created the Appalachian Mountains of eastern North America, beginning in the Carboniferous period about 325 million years ago and continuing into the early Permian. It was the third and final of the great Paleozoic orogenies to build the Appalachians, following the Taconic and Acadian orogenies.

Cause

The orogeny resulted from the collision of the supercontinent Gondwana — specifically its African margin — with Laurentia, the core of North America, closing the Rheic Ocean. The collision completed the assembly of the supercontinent Pangaea. The same continental collision produced the Variscan (Hercynian) mountains of Europe and the Ouachita Mountains of the south-central United States.

Geological effects

Compression thrust enormous sheets of rock northwestward over the North American margin, folding and faulting the sedimentary strata of the Appalachian basin into the long parallel ridges of today's Valley and Ridge province, and metamorphosing and intruding the Piedmont. Sediment shed westward from the rising mountains filled the Appalachian foreland basin, depositing the coal-bearing Pennsylvanian strata of Pennsylvania, West Virginia and Kentucky. At their height, the Alleghanian Appalachians are thought to have rivalled the modern Himalayas; nearly 300 million years of erosion have since reduced them to their present modest elevations.
