Social wasp

Social wasps live in colonies with a reproductive queen and sterile female workers.

Social wasps are wasps that live in colonies with a division of labour between a reproductive caste (queens) and a largely sterile worker caste. They belong mainly to the family Vespidae, in the subfamilies Vespinae (yellowjackets and hornets) and Polistinae (paper wasps); social behaviour also evolved independently in the Stenogastrinae. Social wasps contrast with the great majority of wasp species, which are solitary.

Colony cycle

In temperate species, a colony is founded in spring by a single mated queen that overwintered. She builds a small nest of paper — wood fibres chewed and mixed with saliva — lays eggs, and rears the first generation of workers herself. The workers then take over foraging, nest expansion, and brood care while the queen concentrates on egg-laying. Colonies may grow to thousands of individuals. In late summer the colony produces males and new queens; the old queen and workers die with the onset of cold, and only the newly mated queens survive the winter. Tropical species such as many Polybia may have perennial colonies and reproduce by swarming.

Behaviour and ecology

Workers are predators of other insects, including many agricultural pests, and also scavenge carrion and sugary substances; adults fuel themselves largely on nectar and other carbohydrates while feeding protein to the larvae, which in return produce a sugary secretion the adults consume. Social wasps are accomplished defenders: many species can sting repeatedly, and alarm pheromones recruit nestmates to attack a threat. Their conspicuous yellow-and-black patterning is a classic example of aposematic (warning) coloration and is mimicked by harmless insects such as hoverflies.

Relations with humans

Social wasps provide ecosystem services as predators and incidental pollinators, but colonies near homes and their attraction to sweet foods in late summer bring them into conflict with people, and their stings can trigger dangerous allergic reactions. Some species, such as the German yellowjacket (Vespula germanica), have become invasive pests in North America, Australia, and New Zealand.
