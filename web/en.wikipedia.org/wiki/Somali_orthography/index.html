Somali orthography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The orthography of the Somali language has been written in several different scripts. Since 1972 the official orthography has been a modified Latin alphabet devised by Shire Jama Ahmed. Before standardization, Somali was written in Arabic-based scripts and in several purpose-built indigenous alphabets, and the choice of script was one of the most contentious cultural questions in Somali public life for much of the 20th century.

Historical scripts

The oldest tradition is the Arabic-based Wadaad writing, used by religious scholars (wadaad) for poetry, correspondence, and religious texts from at least the 19th century and in informal use much earlier. In the 1920s Osman Yusuf Kenadid invented the Osmanya alphabet, a left-to-right script resembling Ethiopic in appearance, championed by the Majeerteen Sultanate and later by nationalists who wanted a unique Somali script. Other inventions included the Borama alphabet of Sheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Nuur (1933) and the Kaddare alphabet of Hussein Sheikh Ahmed Kaddare (1952).

The 1972 standardization

After independence in 1960, a Somali Language Commission examined ten scripts without reaching a decision acceptable to all factions. Following the 1969 revolution, the military government resolved the question by decree on 21 October 1972, adopting the Latin orthography. The decision was justified on grounds of printing technology, cost, and compatibility with education and international communication. The orthography is largely phonemic: consonants B T J X KH D R S SH DH C G F Q K L M N W H Y, five short vowels written a e i o u and five long vowels written double, with digraphs dh, sh, kh. The glottal stop is written with an apostrophe in careful writing but often omitted. Grammatical tone is generally unmarked.

The introduction of the orthography was paired with the rural literacy campaign of 1973–75 ("Bar Ama Baro", "Teach or Learn"), which spread the standard written language nationwide. Osmanya continued in limited use and has since been encoded in Unicode, and Arabic script remains in religious use.
