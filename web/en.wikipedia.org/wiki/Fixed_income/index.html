Fixed income

Fixed income refers to a type of investment security that pays investors fixed interest or dividend payments until its maturity date. At maturity, investors are repaid the principal amount they had invested. Government and corporate bonds are the most common types of fixed-income products, but the category also includes instruments such as treasury bills, notes, municipal bonds, certificates of deposit and preferred stock. Unlike variable-income securities such as equities, where payments change based on an underlying measure, fixed income payments are known in advance.

Types of issuers

Governments issue sovereign bonds (for example, U.S. Treasury securities, British gilts and German bunds) to finance deficits and refinance maturing debt. Municipalities issue municipal bonds, often tax-advantaged, to fund public projects. Corporations issue corporate bonds, which range from investment grade to high-yield ("junk") bonds depending on the issuer's creditworthiness as assessed by rating agencies such as Moody's, S&P and Fitch.

Risks

Fixed income investors face several risks. Interest rate risk: when market interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall, with longer-maturity and lower-coupon bonds (higher duration) most affected. Credit risk: the issuer may default. Inflation risk: fixed payments may lose purchasing power. Other considerations include reinvestment risk, liquidity risk and call risk where the issuer can redeem the bond early.

Role in portfolios

Because of their regular payments and (for high-quality issuers) relative stability, fixed income securities are a core holding for conservative investors, retirees seeking income, and pension funds matching future liabilities. Global fixed income markets are larger than global equity markets by market value outstanding.
