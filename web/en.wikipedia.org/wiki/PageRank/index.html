PageRank (PR) is an algorithm used by Google Search to rank web pages in search engine results. It is named both after the term "web page" and after its co-developer Larry Page. PageRank is a way of measuring the importance of website pages by counting the number and quality of links to a page, on the underlying assumption that more important websites are likely to receive more links from other websites.

Algorithm

PageRank models a "random surfer" who follows links at random but occasionally jumps to a random page; the probability that the surfer lands on a given page is its PageRank. Formally, each page distributes its rank equally among the pages it links to, scaled by a damping factor (often quoted as 0.85) representing the probability of continuing to follow links rather than teleporting. The resulting values are the principal eigenvector of the normalised link matrix, computed iteratively over the entire web graph.

History

PageRank was developed at Stanford University by Larry Page and Sergey Brin in 1996 as part of a research project about a new kind of search engine; their 1998 paper "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine" described the prototype of Google. The algorithm built on earlier work in citation analysis and on Robin Li's RankDex. A simplified 0–10 "Toolbar PageRank" score was publicly displayed by Google for many years before being retired from public view in 2016, though PageRank-like signals remain part of Google's ranking systems.

Manipulation and SEO

Because links confer rank, PageRank spawned an industry of search engine optimisation, including link farms and paid links; Google responded with measures such as the nofollow link attribute (2005) and algorithmic penalties for manipulative linking.
