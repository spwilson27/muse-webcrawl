William Childs Westmoreland (March 26, 1914 – July 18, 2005) was a United States Army general who commanded American military operations in the Vietnam War from 1964 to 1968, at the peak of U.S. involvement. He pursued a strategy of attrition — large-unit "search and destroy" operations measured by enemy body counts — and served thereafter as Chief of Staff of the Army (1968–1972).

Career

A graduate of West Point, Westmoreland fought as an artillery officer in North Africa and France in the Second World War and commanded troops in Korea. He was superintendent of West Point (1960–1963) before taking command of Military Assistance Command, Vietnam (MACV) in 1964. Under him, American troop strength in Vietnam rose to more than 535,000.

Vietnam and controversy

Westmoreland's optimistic public reports of progress — including his November 1967 assurance that the end was coming into view — were badly undercut by the Tet Offensive of early 1968, a military defeat for the attackers but a political turning point in the United States. Historians continue to debate his attrition strategy, the body-count metric, and a dispute over enemy order-of-battle estimates. In 1982 CBS broadcast the documentary The Uncounted Enemy, which alleged a conspiracy to suppress intelligence estimates; Westmoreland sued for libel, and the case was settled in 1985 without a verdict. His memoir, A Soldier Reports, appeared in 1976.
