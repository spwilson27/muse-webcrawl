Lake Kivu is one of the African Great Lakes, lying in the Albertine Rift on the border between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Rwanda. It drains southward through the Ruzizi River into Lake Tanganyika and contains Idjwi Island, one of the largest inland islands in Africa.

Dissolved gases

Kivu is one of only three known "exploding lakes" (with Lake Nyos and Lake Monoun in Cameroon): its deep waters hold enormous quantities of dissolved carbon dioxide and an estimated 55–60 billion cubic metres of methane, accumulated from volcanic CO₂ reacting with bacterial processes. A limnic eruption — a sudden overturn releasing the gas — could asphyxiate large numbers of the roughly two million people living on its shores, a risk scientists monitor but consider low in probability.

Methane extraction

The same methane is a resource: Rwanda's KivuWatt project and earlier pilot plants extract gas from the deep water to generate electricity, simultaneously reducing the gas inventory. The lake also supports an important fishery, particularly the introduced Tanganyika sardine (Limnothrissa miodon), known locally as sambaza.

History

The lake region was the scene of intense conflict during and after the 1994 Rwandan genocide and the Congo wars; the lakeside cities of Goma (DRC) and Gisenyi/Rubavu (Rwanda) face each other across the northern shore, near the active Nyiragongo volcano, whose lava reached the lake in 2002 and again threatened Goma in 2021.
