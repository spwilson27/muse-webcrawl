Papermaking

Papermaking is the manufacturing process of producing paper and paperboard from cellulose fibres, usually derived from wood pulp, rags, or grasses. Paper was invented in China — traditionally credited to the court official Cai Lun around 105 AD, though archaeological finds show earlier paper — and the craft spread westward through the Islamic world to Europe, where it was mechanized during the Industrial Revolution.

Process

Modern papermaking, whether by hand or machine, follows the same essential steps:

  • Pulping: Raw material is broken down into fibres, mechanically or chemically (as in the kraft process), to produce pulp.
  • Preparation: The pulp is washed, bleached if necessary, beaten to refine the fibres, and mixed with water and additives such as fillers, sizing, and dyes.
  • Sheet formation: The dilute slurry is spread onto a moving wire screen (in a Fourdrinier machine) or dipped with a mould by hand; water drains away, leaving a mat of interlaced fibres.
  • Pressing and drying: The wet sheet is pressed between rollers and dried over heated cylinders.
  • Finishing: The paper may be calendered for smoothness, coated, cut, and packaged.

History

Chinese papermakers used hemp, mulberry bark, and rags. Muslim papermakers learned the craft by the eighth century — tradition associates its transmission with the Battle of Talas in 751 — and mills spread through Spain and Italy by the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The Fourdrinier machine, patented in the early 1800s, enabled continuous production, and from the mid-nineteenth century wood pulp largely replaced rags as the raw material.

Environmental issues

The pulp and paper industry is a major consumer of water and energy, and historically a significant polluter; modern mills increasingly use recycled fibre, closed-loop water systems, and recovery of pulping chemicals to reduce their impact.
