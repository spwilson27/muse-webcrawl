McMahon Line

[Image: The McMahon Line along the crest of the eastern Himalayas]
The eastern sector of the China–India boundary

The McMahon Line is the boundary line agreed to by Britain and Tibet as part of the 1914 Simla Convention, negotiated at Shimla by British India's foreign secretary, Sir Henry McMahon, whose name it bears. It runs for about 890 kilometres (550 miles) along the crest of the Himalayas from Bhutan in the west to the great bend of the Brahmaputra River in the east, separating what is now the Indian state of Arunachal Pradesh from Tibet.

History

At the Simla conference of 1913–14, representatives of Britain, Tibet and the Republic of China negotiated Tibet's status and borders. The Tibetan and British plenipotentiaries initialled the convention and a bilateral note exchanging the boundary map, but the Chinese delegate repudiated the agreement and China never ratified it. The line placed the Tawang tract and other traditionally Tibetan-administered areas on the British (Indian) side. The agreement was not published until 1938, and India began administering the area south of the line — organised as the North-East Frontier Agency — only in the 1950s.

Dispute

China has never recognised the McMahon Line, regarding the Simla Convention as an unequal agreement signed by a local Tibetan authority without Chinese sovereignty over Tibet. The boundary dispute in this "eastern sector" was a principal cause of the Sino-Indian War of 1962, in which Chinese forces advanced south of the line before withdrawing to positions near it. India treats the line as its legal northeastern border; China claims some 90,000 km² south of it as "South Tibet". The frontier remains undemarcated on the ground and is patrolled along differing perceptions of the Line of Actual Control, with periodic stand-offs, most recently infrastructure competition and clashes in the Tawang sector in December 2022.
