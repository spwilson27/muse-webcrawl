Law of tangents

Law of tangents
FieldTrigonometry
Related resultsLaw of sines; law of cosines; Mollweide's formulas

In trigonometry, the law of tangents is a statement about the relationship between the tangents of two angles of a triangle and the lengths of the opposing sides. It is less well known than the law of sines and the law of cosines, but it can be useful when two sides and the included angle, or two angles and a side, are known and the remaining parts of the triangle are to be found.

Let a and b be two sides of a triangle, and let α and β be the angles opposite those sides respectively. The law of tangents states:

(a − b) / (a + b) = tan( (α − β)/2 ) / tan( (α + β)/2 )

History

The law of tangents for planar triangles was known to medieval Islamic mathematicians; Ibn Muʿādh al-Jayyānī described the underlying results in the 11th century, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi gave the law in its modern form in the 13th century. It was popular in the era of logarithmic computation, because the right-hand side converts easily to sums and differences suitable for log tables — unlike the law of cosines, which requires a product term. The spherical version of the law follows from Napier's analogies.

Proof

The law follows directly from the law of sines, a/sin α = b/sin β = 2R. Writing (a − b)/(a + b) as (sin α − sin β)/(sin α + sin β) and applying the sum-to-product identities gives 2 cos((α+β)/2) sin((α−β)/2) in the numerator and 2 sin((α+β)/2) cos((α−β)/2) in the denominator, whose quotient is tan((α−β)/2) / tan((α+β)/2).

Application

Given two sides a and b and the included angle γ, one computes α + β = 180° − γ, then uses the law of tangents to obtain α − β, from which both angles follow; the third side can then be found with the law of sines. Before electronic calculators this was the standard method in surveying and navigation.
