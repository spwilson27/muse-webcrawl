Kinetic art

Kinetic art is art from any medium that contains movement perceivable by the viewer or that depends on motion for its visual effect. It includes works that move through mechanical means, natural forces such as wind and water, or the movement of the viewer around the work.

History

The term emerged in the early 20th century, with early examples in the work of the Dada and Constructivist movements. Marcel Duchamp's Bicycle Wheel (1913) is often cited as a precursor. Alexander Calder became famous for his mobiles — hanging sculptures balanced to move with air currents — while artists such as Naum Gabo, who used the term "kinetic" in his Realistic Manifesto of 1920, explored motor-driven construction. In the 1950s and 1960s kinetic art became a major international movement, associated with Victor Vasarely, Jean Tinguely, Jesús Rafael Soto, and the op art movement.

Characteristics

Kinetic works may be powered by motors, clockwork, water, or the viewer's own motion. Jean Tinguely's self-destroying machines, such as Homage to New York (1960), pushed the idea to a theatrical extreme. Contemporary kinetic art continues in large-scale public installations and wind sculptures.
