Parapet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Parapet
TypeArchitectural / defensive barrier
EtymologyItalian parapetto: parare (to shield) + petto (chest)
LocationsRoof edges, balconies, bridges, fortification walls

A parapet is a barrier that is an upward extension of a wall at the edge of a roof, terrace, balcony, walkway or other structure. The name derives from the Italian parapetto, "chest-high wall" (from parare, to shield, and petto, chest), reflecting its origin as a breast-high defence behind which soldiers could shelter while fighting.

In fortification

In military architecture the parapet is the part of a fortification wall that rises above the rampart walk to protect defenders from enemy fire. Medieval parapets were often crowned with battlements — a pattern of solid blocks (merlons) alternating with gaps (crenels) through which defenders could shoot. In early-modern and trench warfare, parapets of earth, sandbags or masonry served the same purpose; in the First World War the sandbagged parapet of a trench, with its firestep and loopholes, was a defining feature of the Western Front, and soldiers risked sniper fire by looking "over the top".

In civil architecture

On buildings, parapets extend the external wall above the roofline. They serve to prevent falls, to conceal flat roofs and rooftop equipment, to act as a firebreak between attached buildings, and to define the architectural silhouette — essential, for example, to the look of Georgian terraces and of flat-roofed modernist buildings. Because parapets are exposed to weather on both faces and at the top, they are a common source of water ingress; modern detailing uses coping stones, flashing and damp-proof courses to protect them. Building codes in many jurisdictions prescribe minimum parapet heights and guardrail requirements where roofs are accessible.

On bridges

Bridge parapets combine pedestrian protection with, on road bridges, vehicle containment: they must be strong enough to redirect an errant vehicle back onto the carriageway while preventing falls from height, a requirement balanced against the visual lightness demanded of notable bridge designs.
