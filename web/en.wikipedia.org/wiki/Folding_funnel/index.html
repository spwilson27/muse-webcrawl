Folding funnel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The folding funnel hypothesis is a specific model of protein folding theory in which a protein's energy landscape is shaped like a funnel: the wide top represents the large number of conformations and high entropy of the unfolded chain, while the narrow bottom corresponds to the single, low-energy native structure. As folding proceeds, the chain's conformational entropy decreases while its energy also decreases, funnelling the protein toward its native state.

Background

The hypothesis, developed in the 1980s and 1990s by Ken Dill, José Onuchic, Peter Wolynes, Hue Sun Chan and others, resolves Levinthal's paradox: a protein cannot find its native structure by random search, but it does not need to — a biased, funnel-shaped landscape of possible interactions guides it downhill. The concept rests on the principle of minimal frustration: evolution has selected sequences whose native interactions are largely mutually consistent, smoothing the landscape, whereas random sequences would have rugged landscapes with many competing traps.

Energy landscapes

Real landscapes are not perfectly smooth funnels; they have roughness, local minima and intermediate states, which account for folding intermediates, kinetic traps and misfolding diseases. The funnel picture unified statistical-mechanical models (such as lattice models) with experimental kinetics and underlies modern methods for predicting folding pathways and, indirectly, structure prediction.
