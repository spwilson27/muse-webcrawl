Aerophone

Aerophone
Hornbostel–Sachs4 (aerophones)
Sound sourceVibrating column of air
SubclassesFree aerophones; wind instruments (lip-, reed-, flute-based)
ExamplesFlute, oboe, trumpet, didgeridoo, harmonica

An aerophone is any musical instrument that produces sound primarily by causing a body of air to vibrate, without the use of strings or membranes and without the vibration of the instrument itself adding significantly to the sound. It is one of the four main classes in the original Hornbostel–Sachs instrument classification system, published in 1914.

Classification

Hornbostel and Sachs divided aerophones into two groups:

  • Free aerophones, in which the vibrating air is not confined by the instrument — for example, the bullroarer, the harmonica (free reeds), and the siren.
  • Wind instruments proper, in which the air is confined within the instrument. These are further divided into lip-vibrated instruments (trumpets, horns, trombones), reed instruments (clarinets, saxophones, oboes, bassoons), and flutes (transverse flutes, recorders, panpipes, shakuhachi), in which the air stream is split at a sharp edge.

Acoustics

In wind instruments, the pitch is determined primarily by the length of the vibrating air column and by the pattern of its modes of vibration. Players change pitch by altering the effective length of the tube (through valves, slides, or finger holes) and by overblowing to excite higher harmonics. The bore shape — cylindrical versus conical — strongly influences timbre; for example, the conical bore of the oboe and saxophone yields a brighter tone than the cylindrical clarinet.

History

Aerophones are among humanity's oldest instruments: bone and ivory flutes from Swabian caves date to about 40,000 years ago. Lip-vibrated instruments such as the bronze lur appear in Scandinavian Bronze Age contexts, and reed instruments were central to ancient Egyptian, Greek, and Chinese music.
