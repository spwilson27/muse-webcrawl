Śakra (Buddhism)

Śakra (Sanskrit: शक्र; Pali: Sakka) is a Buddhist deity, the ruler of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, the "Heaven of the Thirty-Three [gods]", which stands on the summit of Mount Meru at the centre of the Buddhist cosmos. He is often identified with the Vedic god Indra, and in Buddhist texts he appears as a devout protector and supporter of the Buddha and his teaching.

Sanskrit: Śakra

Title: King of the devas; Devānām Indra, "Lord of the Gods"

Consort: Sujātā

In the early texts

In the Pali Canon, Sakka appears frequently in dialogue with the Buddha. The Sakkapañha Sutta of the Dīgha Nikāya records a long visit in which Sakka asks the Buddha about the causes of conflict among beings and is praised for the depth of his questions. Sakka is portrayed not as an omnipotent creator but as a long-lived yet mortal god, subject like all beings to rebirth, who has attained the fruit of stream-entry (sotāpatti) through his devotion. His previous life is described in Jātaka tales as that of a generous human, Magha, whose merit earned him his divine throne.

Role and character

Śakra presides over the assembly of the Thirty-Three in the Sudhammā hall and rides the elephant Airāvata (Erawan). Stories depict him intervening on earth to test monks, protect the virtuous, and assist the Buddha — for example, attending the Buddha during his retreat at Indasālaguhā cave, and assisting at key moments of the Buddha's life. Though powerful, Śakra remains within saṃsāra, and Buddhist texts use his anxieties about his own eventual death and fall from heaven to illustrate impermanence.

In Mahāyāna and later Buddhism

In Mahāyāna literature Śakra (often as Śakra Devānām Indra) appears among the Buddha's standard assembly and as a guardian of the Dharma, frequently paired with Brahmā, who together are said to have requested the Buddha to teach after his awakening. In East Asian Buddhism he is known as Dìshìtiān (帝釋天) and in Japanese as Taishakuten, one of the Twelve Devas protecting the Buddhist world, and he is commonly depicted in temple art holding a vajra.
