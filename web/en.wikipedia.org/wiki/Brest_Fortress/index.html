The Brest Fortress (Belarusian: Брэсцкая крэпасць; Russian: Брестская крепость) is a 19th-century fortress in Brest, Belarus, at the confluence of the Bug and Mukhavets rivers. Built by the Russian Empire between 1833 and 1842, it became famous for the defence of the fortress in June 1941, when its Soviet garrison resisted the opening assault of Operation Barbarossa far longer than German planners expected. In 1965 it became the only fortress awarded the title Hero Fortress by the Soviet Union.

Construction

The fortress was built on a site of the old town of Brest-Litovsk, which was demolished and rebuilt a kilometre to the east. The star-shaped fortification comprised a central citadel on an island formed by the two rivers and the moat, surrounded by three defended bridgeheads — the Kobrin, Volyn, and Terespol fortifications. A ring of earthworks and forts extended outward over the following decades.

First World War and interwar period

In 1915, Russian forces evacuated the fortress during the Great Retreat, partly demolishing it. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, ending Russia's participation in the First World War, was signed in the citadel's White Palace in March 1918. Between the wars the fortress belonged to Poland and served as barracks and a political prison. In September 1939 it was defended against German attack by Polish forces under General Konstanty Plisowski before the garrison withdrew; the territory was then transferred to the USSR under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact boundary.

Defence of June 1941

On 22 June 1941 the fortress was attacked by the German 45th Infantry Division. The Soviet garrison — some 9,000 troops of the Red Army and NKVD border guards, many caught by surprise — held out in isolated pockets. Organized resistance in most sectors ended by 29 June, though individual defenders reportedly fought on into July, and inscriptions left by dying soldiers ("I am dying, but I will not surrender!") became emblematic. The defence became one of the most celebrated episodes of Soviet resistance in the Great Patriotic War, popularized by Sergey Smirnov's research and the 1956 film Immortal Garrison.

Memorial

The Brest Hero Fortress memorial complex, opened in 1971, includes the monumental 33.5-metre "Courage" sculpture, an obelisk bayonet, an eternal flame, the preserved ruins of the citadel and Kholm Gate, and the Museum of the Defence of Brest Fortress. Excavations continue to recover the remains of defenders.
