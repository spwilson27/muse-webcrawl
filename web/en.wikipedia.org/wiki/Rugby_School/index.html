Rugby School is a public school (an independent fee-paying boarding school) in Rugby, Warwickshire, England. Founded in 1567 as a free grammar school for local boys by Lawrence Sheriff, a grocer who made his fortune supplying Queen Elizabeth I, it became one of England's most famous schools and is one of the original nine "Clarendon" public schools studied by the 1864 commission. Rugby gave its name to the sport of rugby football.

History

Sheriff's endowment grew valuable as London property values rose, allowing the school to attract boarders from across the country. Under headmaster Thomas Arnold (1828–1841), Rugby became the model of the reformed Victorian public school: Arnold emphasised moral and religious character, academic rigour, and a prefect system in which senior boys ("praepostors") exercised authority—an approach immortalised, with some license, in Thomas Hughes's novel Tom Brown's School Days (1857), set at the school.

Rugby football

According to the famous legend, in 1823 pupil William Webb Ellis "with a fine disregard for the rules of football as played in his time, first took the ball in his arms and ran with it" during a school football match, originating the handling game. The story is now considered apocryphal in its details, but Rugby School boys did develop their own distinctive football code, the first written rules of which were produced by pupils in 1845, and the school is honoured as the sport's birthplace; the Rugby World Cup trophy is named the Webb Ellis Cup.

Today

Rugby is now a fully co-educational boarding and day school for pupils aged 13–18, with around 800 students. Notable alumni include the poet Rupert Brooke, novelist Lewis Carroll (briefly), prime minister Neville Chamberlain, and Salman Rushdie. Its architecture includes the Victorian Gothic chapel and buildings by William Butterfield.
