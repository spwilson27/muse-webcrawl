King v. Burwell, 576 U.S. 473 (2015), was a decision of the Supreme Court of the United States interpreting the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA or "Obamacare"). The Court held, 6–3, that the federal tax credits used to subsidise health-insurance premiums are available to eligible Americans whether their state's insurance exchange was established by the state itself or by the federal government.

Background

The ACA grants premium tax credits for insurance purchased on an exchange "established by the State". When some 34 states declined to build their own exchanges, the federal government ran theirs through HealthCare.gov, and an IRS rule extended the credits to those states. Four Virginia plaintiffs, backed by the Competitive Enterprise Institute, argued that the statutory phrase limited credits to state-run exchanges; accepting their reading would have stripped subsidies from millions of people and, because the individual mandate's affordability exemption depended on those subsidies, risked unravelling the law's insurance markets. The Fourth Circuit upheld the IRS rule, while the D.C. Circuit in the parallel Halbig case had struck it down, creating a conflict the Supreme Court resolved.

Decision

Chief Justice John Roberts, joined by Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan, declined to apply the usual Chevron deference — calling the question too economically significant — but concluded on the statute's text read in context that Congress plainly intended credits on both kinds of exchange: "Congress passed the Affordable Care Act to improve health insurance markets, not to destroy them." Justice Scalia's dissent, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito, accused the majority of rewriting the statute, memorably describing its interpretive method as "pure applesauce" and the law's defenders' reading as "somersaults of statutory interpretation". The ruling was the second major Supreme Court decision preserving the ACA, after NFIB v. Sebelius (2012).
