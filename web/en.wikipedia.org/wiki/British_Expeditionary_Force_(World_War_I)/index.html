British Expeditionary Force (World War I)

Active1914–1918
CountryUnited Kingdom (British Empire)
CommandersField Marshal Sir John French (1914–1915); Field Marshal Sir Douglas Haig (1915–1919)
Size (1914)6 infantry divisions and 1 cavalry division (~120,000 men initially)

The British Expeditionary Force (BEF) was the British Army formation sent to France at the start of the First World War. Created as part of the pre-war planning between Secretary of State for War Richard Haldane and the War Office, the BEF was a small, professional force of regulars and reservists that formed the original British contribution to the Western Front in 1914. German Emperor Wilhelm II supposedly dismissed it as a "contemptible little army," a phrase its survivors proudly adopted as the "Old Contemptibles."

1914 campaign

Commanded by Field Marshal Sir John French, the BEF landed in France in August 1914 and met the German advance at the Battle of Mons on 23 August. Forced to retreat, it fought rearguard actions including Le Cateau, before joining the Allied counter-offensive at the First Battle of the Marne and then the First Battle of Ypres, where the original regular army was largely destroyed holding the line.

Expansion

The original BEF was succeeded on the Western Front by the mass volunteer armies raised by Lord Kitchener and, later, conscripts. The name "British Expeditionary Force" continued to denote the British forces in France and Flanders, which grew to comprise five armies and some two million men at their peak, commanded from December 1915 by Sir Douglas Haig. The BEF fought in the major battles of the war, including the Somme (1916), Passchendaele (1917) and the Hundred Days Offensive (1918) that led to the Armistice.

Legacy

Survivors of the 1914 BEF formed the Old Contemptibles' Association. A new force of the same name was sent to France in the Second World War and evacuated from Dunkirk in 1940.
