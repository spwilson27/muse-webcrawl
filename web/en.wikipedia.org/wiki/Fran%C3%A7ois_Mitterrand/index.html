François Maurice Adrien Marie Mitterrand (26 October 1916 – 8 January 1996) was a French politician and statesman who served as President of France from 1981 to 1995, the longest holder of the office in French history. Under his presidency, France abolished the death penalty, lowered the retirement age to 60, extended paid holidays, and nationalized major banks and industries before an economic policy turn ("tournant de la rigueur") in 1983.

Early life and career

Mitterrand served as a soldier in the Second World War, was taken prisoner by Germany, escaped, and later worked both in the Vichy administration and the French Resistance. After the war he held numerous ministerial posts under the Fourth Republic, including Minister of the Interior and Minister of Justice.

Presidency

Elected in 1981 as the first Socialist president of the Fifth Republic, Mitterrand was re-elected in 1988. His two terms included periods of "cohabitation" with right-wing governments. In Europe he worked closely with German Chancellor Helmut Kohl; they jointly drove the Maastricht Treaty and the creation of the European Union and are commemorated holding hands at Verdun in 1984. His presidency also saw the Grands Projets in Paris, including the Louvre Pyramid and the Bibliothèque nationale de France site that now bears his name.

Death and legacy

Mitterrand died of prostate cancer in January 1996, less than a year after leaving office; the illness had been concealed during much of his presidency.
