Copyright

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A copyright is a type of intellectual property that gives the creator of an original work — or another right holder — the exclusive right to copy, distribute, adapt, display and perform that work, usually for a limited time. Copyright protects the expression of ideas in literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works, films, broadcasts and software — not the underlying ideas, facts or methods. In most jurisdictions protection arises automatically on creation, without registration.

History and framework

Modern copyright begins with England's Statute of Anne (1710), the first statute to vest rights in authors. The Berne Convention (1886) harmonised protection internationally (no formalities, national treatment, minimum terms), and the US and EU standard term is now the author's life plus 70 years — extended historically from much shorter periods, a trend critics label and debate. Continental systems additionally recognise authors' moral rights of attribution and integrity.

Limits and debates

Copyright is balanced by exceptions: fair use (United States) and fair dealing (Commonwealth) for criticism, news, teaching and parody; first-sale/exhaustion doctrines; compulsory licences; and the public domain into which works fall on expiry. Digital reproduction collapsed enforcement costs and assumptions, generating controversies over file-sharing, streaming, orphan works, platform liability and the use of copyrighted works to train generative AI — among the most contested questions in current IP law and policy.
