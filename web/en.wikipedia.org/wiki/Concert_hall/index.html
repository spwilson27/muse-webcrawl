Concert hall

Concert hall
PurposeLive musical performance, principally classical music
Key featuresStage, raked or terraced seating, acoustic reflectors, reverberation control
Notable examplesMusikverein (Vienna), Concertgebouw (Amsterdam), Philharmonie (Berlin), Carnegie Hall (New York)

A concert hall is a cultural building with a stage and auditorium designed for live musical performance, principally of classical music. The term is most often applied to halls whose primary purpose is the performance of instrumental music by orchestras, chamber ensembles, choirs and soloists, as distinct from theatres designed for opera, ballet or spoken drama.

History

Public concerts emerged in the late 17th and 18th centuries as music-making moved out of aristocratic salons and churches. Early purpose-built rooms included the Holywell Music Room in Oxford (1748), among the oldest surviving concert rooms in Europe. The 19th century saw the construction of large halls in the industrial cities of Europe and North America, including the original Gewandhaus in Leipzig, the Musikverein in Vienna (1870) and the Concertgebouw in Amsterdam (1888), whose acoustics remain benchmarks.

Acoustics

The acoustic quality of a concert hall depends chiefly on reverberation time, clarity, envelopment and the balance between direct and reflected sound. The traditional "shoebox" plan — a tall, narrow rectangular room with a high ceiling — is prized for the strong lateral reflections it produces. Twentieth-century designs experimented with other geometries, notably the "vineyard" layout pioneered by Hans Scharoun's Berlin Philharmonie (1963), in which terraces of seating surround the stage on all sides.

Types

Concert halls range from small recital rooms seating a few hundred listeners to large symphony halls of around 2,000 seats. Many modern cultural complexes combine a concert hall with an opera house and recital rooms under one roof, sharing foyers and backstage facilities.
