Parental leave

Parental leave allows employed parents time away from work to care for a young child.

Parental leave, or family leave, is an employee benefit available in almost all countries. The term "parental leave" may include maternity, paternity, and adoption leave; or may be used distinctly from "maternity leave" and "paternity leave" to describe separate family leave available to either parent to care for small children.

Models

Countries differ widely in the length of leave, whether it is paid, and how the cost is shared among employers, social insurance and the state. Nordic countries are noted for generous, well-paid leave, often with a portion reserved for each parent on a use-it-or-lose-it basis ("father's quotas") to encourage fathers to take leave. The United States is unusual among developed countries in having no federal mandate for paid family leave, though the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 guarantees eligible employees up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave.

International standards

The International Labour Organization's Maternity Protection Convention recommends at least 14 weeks of maternity leave. The European Union's Work–Life Balance Directive (2019) requires at least ten working days of paternity leave and two months of non-transferable parental leave for each parent.

Effects

Research generally associates paid parental leave with improved infant health outcomes, higher rates of breastfeeding, greater maternal labour-force attachment in the longer term, and — where fathers take substantial leave — a more equal division of childcare. Critics raise concerns about costs to employers and possible statistical discrimination against women of childbearing age.
