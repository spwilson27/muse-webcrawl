Bombing of Hamburg in World War II

The bombing of Hamburg in World War II consisted of a series of Allied air raids on the German port city, by the RAF Bomber Command and the United States Army Air Forces. The heaviest attacks, codenamed Operation Gomorrah, took place in late July and early August 1943 and created one of the largest firestorms of the war.

Operation Gomorrah

Between 24 July and 3 August 1943, British and American bombers flew a coordinated series of day and night raids against Hamburg's shipyards, U-boat pens, oil refineries and working-class residential districts. On the night of 27–28 July, concentrated bombing in hot, dry weather ignited a firestorm: hurricane-force winds drew flames into a vortex, temperatures reached an estimated 800 °C, and roughly 37,000–40,000 people were killed, most in that single night. In total the Gomorrah raids killed more than 42,000 people and destroyed much of the city.

The raids made early operational use of "Window" — strips of aluminium foil dropped to blind German radar — which dramatically reduced bomber losses on the first night.

Later raids and aftermath

Hamburg was bombed repeatedly until April 1945. By the end of the war over half of the city's housing had been destroyed. Hamburg surrendered to British forces on 3 May 1945.
