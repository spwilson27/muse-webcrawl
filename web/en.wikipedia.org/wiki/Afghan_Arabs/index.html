Afghan Arabs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For the Afghan resistance fighters themselves, see Afghan mujahideen.


Arab volunteers in Afghanistan, 1980s (placeholder image).

The Afghan Arabs were Arab and other Muslim foreign volunteers who came to Afghanistan during and after the Soviet–Afghan War (1979–1989) to fight alongside the Afghan mujahideen against the Soviet-supported government, and later in the Afghan civil wars. Estimates of their total numbers vary widely, from several thousand to tens of thousands over the course of the conflict.

Recruitment and role

Recruitment flowed largely through Peshawar, Pakistan, where the Palestinian scholar Abdullah Yusuf Azzam and the wealthy Saudi Osama bin Laden established the Maktab al-Khidamat (Services Bureau) in 1984 to receive, fund and channel foreign volunteers. The Arabs' direct military contribution to the war against the Soviets was marginal compared with the Afghan factions, but the networks, fundraising channels and ideology formed in Peshawar proved historically decisive.

Aftermath

After the Soviet withdrawal in 1989, some Afghan Arabs remained to fight in the Afghan civil war, often aligned with hardline factions; others returned home, where veterans radicalised by the jihad joined insurgencies in Algeria, Egypt and elsewhere; still others moved on to conflicts in Bosnia, Chechnya and Kashmir. In 1988 bin Laden and Azzam's circle founded al-Qaeda, drawing its early cadre largely from Afghan Arab veterans — a lineage that led, via the 1996 and 1998 declarations of war on the United States, to the September 11 attacks and to the veterans returning to Afghanistan as the enemy in the war that began in 2001.
