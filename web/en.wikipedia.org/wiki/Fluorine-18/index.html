Fluorine-18

Fluorine-18 (18F) is a radioisotope of fluorine that is one of the most important radionuclides in nuclear medicine. It decays by positron emission (97%) and electron capture (3%) with a half-life of 109.8 minutes to stable oxygen-18. The emitted positrons annihilate with electrons in tissue, producing pairs of 511 keV gamma rays detected by positron emission tomography (PET) scanners.

Production

Fluorine-18 is produced in a cyclotron, most commonly by proton bombardment of oxygen-18-enriched water via the 18O(p,n)18F reaction, yielding aqueous [18F]fluoride. Its roughly two-hour half-life is long enough for labelled compounds to be synthesised and shipped to hospitals within a region, yet short enough to keep patient radiation doses low — a major advantage over other positron emitters such as carbon-11 (20 minutes).

Radiopharmaceuticals

The most widely used 18F tracer is fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), in which a hydroxyl group of glucose is replaced by 18F. FDG-PET measures glucose metabolism and is a mainstay of oncology (staging and monitoring tumours), neurology and cardiology. Other tracers include sodium fluoride (Na18F) for bone imaging, florbetapir and related agents for amyloid imaging in Alzheimer's disease, fluciclovine for prostate cancer, and fluorodopa for neuroendocrine tumours and movement disorders. Radiolabelling typically proceeds by nucleophilic substitution of activated precursors using [18F]fluoride with a phase-transfer catalyst such as Kryptofix 2.2.2, often automated in shielded synthesis modules.
