Zine

A zine (short for fanzine or magazine) is a small-circulation, self-published work of original or appropriated texts and images, usually reproduced via photocopier. Zines are the product either of a single person or of a very small group, and are popularly photocopied into physical prints for circulation. They are typically motivated by a desire for self-expression rather than profit, and cover a wide variety of subjects including fan fiction, politics, art, music, and personal memoir.

History

The zine tradition grew out of science-fiction fandom in the 1930s, when fans produced mimeographed fanzines to exchange commentary and fiction. The format was adopted by successive subcultures: the underground press of the 1960s, the punk movement of the 1970s — where publications such as Sniffin' Glue documented the scene — and the riot grrrl movement of the early 1990s, whose zines addressed feminism and personal politics. Photocopiers and later cheap desktop publishing made zines accessible to anyone, and the 1990s saw a "zine explosion" tracked by review publications such as Factsheet Five.

Characteristics

Zines are usually handmade or produced with minimal resources, have print runs from a handful to a few thousand copies, and are distributed by hand, by mail, through independent shops, or at zine fairs and distros. Because they exist outside commercial publishing, zines often carry highly personal, niche, or dissenting content. Libraries and archives, including university collections, now preserve zines as records of grassroots culture.

Digital era

With the rise of the web, some zine culture migrated to blogs and "e-zines", but print zines have persisted and experienced revivals, supported by zine fairs in many cities and by renewed interest in tangible, small-scale media.
