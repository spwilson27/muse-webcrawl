Resistant starch

Resistant starch (RS) is starch, including its degradation products, that escapes digestion in the small intestine of healthy individuals and reaches the large intestine, where it is fermented by the gut microbiota. In this respect it behaves much like dietary fibre. Fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate, which is an important energy source for colonocytes.

Types

Resistant starches are usually grouped into five types: RS1, physically inaccessible starch trapped within intact cell walls, as in whole grains and seeds; RS2, native granular starch with a structure resistant to enzymes, as in raw potato and green banana; RS3, retrograded starch formed when cooked starchy foods such as rice and potatoes are cooled; RS4, chemically modified starches; and RS5, amylose–lipid complexes formed during processing.

Health effects

Because it is not digested to glucose in the small intestine, resistant starch produces a smaller rise in blood glucose than digestible starch, and studies have associated it with improved insulin sensitivity, greater satiety and beneficial changes to the gut microbiome. On average it yields about 2 kilocalories per gram, roughly half the energy of fully digestible starch.
