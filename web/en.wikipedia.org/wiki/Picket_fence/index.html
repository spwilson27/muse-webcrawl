Picket fence

A white picket fence, an enduring symbol of suburban domesticity in the United States.

A picket fence is a type of fence traditionally used to define the boundary of a front garden or yard. It consists of evenly spaced vertical boards, the pickets, attached to horizontal rails, and is distinguished by pickets with pointed or decorated tops. Picket fences are usually around knee to waist height and are primarily decorative or for demarcation rather than security or privacy.

History

Picket fences have been used in the United States since the early colonial period, when they defended gardens from free-ranging livestock. They became increasingly elaborate during the 19th century, with pickets cut into decorative shapes by scroll saw, and were associated with the Gothic Revival cottage style promoted by pattern books such as those of Andrew Jackson Downing. With suburbanisation in the 20th century, the white picket fence became a cultural icon of the American suburban ideal and middle-class domestic life; the phrase "white picket fence" is commonly used metaphorically for that ideal.

Construction

Traditional picket fences are made of wood — commonly cedar or pine — painted white, but modern versions are made of vinyl (PVC) and other low-maintenance materials. Pickets are typically 3–4 feet long, spaced a few inches apart, and topped with pointed, rounded or Gothic points. Sections are held between posts, often with matching gates.
