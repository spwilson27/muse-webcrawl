Kedleston Hall is a Neoclassical country house near Kedleston, Derbyshire, England, about four miles north-west of Derby. It was built in 1759–1765 for Nathaniel Curzon, 1st Baron Scarsdale, and is regarded as one of the finest examples of the work of Robert Adam, who designed its celebrated interiors. The house is now in the care of the National Trust.

History

The Curzon family had owned the Kedleston estate since the 13th century. The medieval village of Kedleston was demolished to make way for the new house and its park. Construction began in 1759 to designs first by Matthew Brettingham and then James Paine, working in the Palladian style. In 1760 the young Robert Adam was brought in, initially to design garden buildings, but he soon took over the decoration of the principal rooms and completed the south front.

Architecture

The house consists of a central block linked by curved corridors to two flanking pavilions. The north front is Palladian in character, while the south front, by Adam, is based on the Arch of Constantine in Rome, with a central triumphal-arch motif. Inside, the Marble Hall rises the full height of the house with columns of pink Derbyshire alabaster, and the saloon behind it is crowned by a dome with an oculus. The state rooms contain important collections of paintings, furniture and sculpture, including Indian artefacts collected by Lord Curzon, Viceroy of India (1899–1905).

Lord Curzon and the Eastern Museum

George Curzon, 1st Marquess Curzon of Kedleston, Viceroy of India, assembled a notable collection of Indian and Far Eastern objects, now displayed in the Eastern Museum within the hall. The parkland, landscaped in the 18th century, includes a lake, the Fishing Room and a bridge over the lake designed by Adam.
