Lammas, also known as Loaf Mass Day, is a Christian holiday celebrated in some English-speaking countries on 1 August. The name originates from the Old English word hlafmaesse, "loaves mass". It is a festival to mark the blessing of the First Fruits of harvest, with a loaf of bread being brought to the church for this purpose.

History

In early medieval England, Lammas was one of the major festivals of the agricultural year, falling at the beginning of the harvest season when the first corn (wheat) was ripe. Farmers would bake loaves from the new crop and present them at Mass; in Anglo-Saxon custom the loaves were also used in protective folk magic, placed in barns to guard the stored grain. Lammas lands were common lands open for grazing after the hay harvest from "Lammas rights".

Customs

The festival shares its date with the Gaelic festival of Lughnasadh, one of the four Celtic seasonal festivals, and the two likely share pre-Christian roots in harvest celebration. In modern times the Church of England and other churches have revived Lammas services, with congregations presenting bread baked from locally grown grain. In the liturgical calendar of some traditions it coincides with the Feast of St Peter in Chains. In Scotland, Lammas was one of the traditional "quarter days" on which rents were paid and contracts settled.

Neopaganism

In Wicca and other forms of modern paganism, Lammas (as Lughnasadh) is one of the eight sabbats of the Wheel of the Year, a cross-quarter day midway between the summer solstice and the autumn equinox.
