Jazz funk is a subgenre of jazz that emerged in the United States in the late 1960s and flourished through the 1970s, characterized by a strong backbeat, electrified sounds, and analog synthesizers. It combines the harmonic and improvisational language of jazz — particularly hard bop and soul jazz — with the grooves of funk and rhythm and blues. Though sometimes used interchangeably with jazz fusion, jazz funk is generally earthier and more groove-driven, and less influenced by rock.

History

The style developed as jazz musicians responded to the commercial dominance of funk and soul. Herbie Hancock's album Head Hunters (1973) — featuring the hit "Chameleon" — became one of the best-selling jazz records of all time and a landmark of the genre. Donald Byrd's work with the Mizell brothers, including Black Byrd (1973), brought jazz funk to the pop charts, while artists such as Roy Ayers, Lonnie Liston Smith, George Benson, Freddie Hubbard, Bob James, the Crusaders, and Grover Washington Jr. recorded extensively in the idiom. Organist Jimmy Smith and guitarist Grant Green had anticipated the style in their soul jazz recordings of the 1960s.

Characteristics

Jazz funk typically emphasizes rhythm-section interplay: syncopated electric bass lines, wah-wah guitar, Fender Rhodes or Clavinet keyboards, and tight horn riffs, over which soloists improvise. Arrangements are often built on one- or two-chord vamps, in contrast to the rapid harmonic movement of bebop. Many tracks feature extended grooves designed as much for dancing as for listening.

Influence

Jazz funk records became foundational source material for hip hop producers, who sampled their drum breaks and bass lines; the genre also fed directly into the acid jazz movement of the late 1980s and 1990s in Britain, and into later jam-band and neo-soul scenes. Rare-groove collectors revived interest in obscure 1970s jazz funk recordings, many of which were reissued decades after their original release.
