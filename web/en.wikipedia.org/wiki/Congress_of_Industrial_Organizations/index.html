The Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) was a federation of unions that organized workers in industrial unions in the United States and Canada from 1935 to 1955. It was founded as the Committee for Industrial Organization within the American Federation of Labor (AFL) by leaders, notably John L. Lewis of the United Mine Workers, who believed the AFL's craft union model could not organize mass-production industries such as steel, automobiles and rubber.

History

After the AFL expelled the CIO unions in 1936, the organization re-formed as an independent federation in 1938 under its new name. The CIO led dramatic organizing campaigns, including the Flint sit-down strike of 1936–37 against General Motors, which won recognition of the United Auto Workers. Its affiliated unions — the UAW, the United Steelworkers and others — organized millions of workers, and its political arm, the CIO Political Action Committee (founded 1943), became an important force in Democratic politics. During the late 1940s the CIO expelled several unions accused of communist influence. In 1955 the CIO merged with the AFL to form the AFL–CIO.
