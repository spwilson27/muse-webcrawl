Demetae

The Demetae were a Celtic tribe of Iron Age and Roman-era Britain who inhabited the area of modern Pembrokeshire and Carmarthenshire in south-west Wales. They gave their name to the historic county of Dyfed, whose name derives from the tribal designation through the Welsh Dyfed.

Territory

The territory of the Demetae bordered that of the Silures to the east and the Ordovices to the north. Ptolemy's Geography lists two towns for the tribe: Moridunum (modern Carmarthen) and Luentinum (near Pumsaint in Carmarthenshire, close to the Dolaucothi gold mines). Moridunum became the civitas capital of the Demetae under Roman administration.

History

Unlike their neighbours, the Demetae appear to have submitted to Rome without prolonged resistance, and their territory contains few Roman military installations beyond the early frontier period. The gold mines at Dolaucothi were worked under Roman direction. The Demetae are notable among the British tribes for issuing no coinage of their own.

After the Roman withdrawal, the region emerged as the Welsh kingdom of Dyfed. In the post-Roman period the area received settlers from Ireland, reflected in the ogham-inscribed stones found in south-west Wales recording names in both Brittonic and Goidelic traditions.
