Replication crisis


Large-scale replication projects have found that many published results do not replicate.
Replication crisis
Also calledReproducibility crisis
Fields affectedPsychology, medicine, social sciences, economics, cancer biology
CausesPublication bias, p-hacking, low statistical power, HARKing, questionable research practices

The replication crisis is an ongoing methodological crisis in which the results of many scientific studies are difficult or impossible to reproduce. Because the reproducibility of empirical results is a cornerstone of the scientific method, findings that the literature in several fields contains a high proportion of non-replicable results have generated widespread concern about the reliability of published research and have prompted reforms in research and publishing practices.

Emergence

Although discussed earlier, the crisis gained prominence around 2011–2015. Triggers included Daryl Bem's experiments purporting to show precognition, which passed peer review at a top psychology journal; the exposure of fraud in social psychology (Diederik Stapel); John Ioannidis's influential 2005 paper "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False"; and Bayer and Amgen reports that only about 11–25% of landmark preclinical cancer findings could be reproduced. In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration reported that of 97 originally significant psychology studies, only about 36% of replications yielded significant results, with effect sizes roughly halved.

Causes

  • Publication bias: journals preferentially publish positive, novel results, creating a "file drawer" of unpublished null findings.
  • P-hacking and researcher degrees of freedom: flexible analyses allow false positives to be presented as significant.
  • HARKing: hypothesising after the results are known.
  • Low statistical power: small samples produce unstable, inflated effects.
  • Incentives: careers reward quantity and novelty rather than rigour and verification.

Reforms

Responses include preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans, open data and materials, Registered Reports (peer review before results are known), larger samples and multi-lab collaborations, statistical reforms (confidence intervals, Bayesian methods, proposals to lower the significance threshold), and journals and funders dedicated to replication. Debate continues over how widespread the problem is in any given field and whether the "crisis" represents a failure of science or science's self-correcting mechanisms working as intended.
