Cauchy sequence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Hero image placeholder: illustration of terms of a Cauchy sequence clustering together]

In mathematics, a Cauchy sequence is a sequence whose elements become arbitrarily close to one another as the sequence progresses. The notion, named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy, captures the idea of convergence using only the terms of the sequence itself, without reference to a limit point, and is fundamental to the rigorous foundations of real analysis.

Definition

A sequence (x1, x2, x3, …) of real numbers is called Cauchy if for every positive real number ε there is a positive integer N such that for all natural numbers m, n > N:

|xm − xn| < ε

The definition extends directly to metric spaces, replacing the absolute value by the distance function d(xm, xn), and to other settings such as topological vector spaces and uniform spaces.

Completeness

Every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence, but the converse holds only in complete spaces. The real numbers are complete: every Cauchy sequence of reals converges to a real limit. The rational numbers are not — a sequence of rationals converging to √2 is Cauchy but has no rational limit. Indeed, one standard construction of the real numbers defines them as equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals, filling exactly these gaps.
