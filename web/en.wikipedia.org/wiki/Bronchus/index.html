Bronchus

A bronchus (plural bronchi) is a large airway in the respiratory system that conducts air into the lungs. The two main bronchi branch from the trachea at the carina and enter the right and left lungs, where they divide repeatedly into smaller bronchi and bronchioles.

Structure

The right main bronchus is shorter, wider, and more vertical than the left, which is one reason inhaled foreign objects more often enter the right lung. Within each lung the bronchi branch into lobar bronchi for each lobe, then segmental bronchi supplying bronchopulmonary segments. Their walls contain cartilage, smooth muscle, glands, and ciliated epithelium that moves mucus toward the pharynx.

Function

Bronchi distribute inhaled air to the alveoli and help protect the lungs by trapping particles in mucus and sweeping them upward. Bronchial smooth muscle regulates airway diameter; constriction is a central feature of asthma.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the bronchi is called bronchitis. Bronchoscopy allows direct inspection and sampling of the airways, and bronchiectasis describes permanent abnormal widening of bronchi, often after infection or in cystic fibrosis.
