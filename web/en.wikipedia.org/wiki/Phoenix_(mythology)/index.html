Phoenix (mythology)

The phoenix is a mythical bird associated with fire and the Sun, known in Greek folklore and related traditions for a cycle of death and rebirth: when its life approaches its end, the phoenix builds a nest of aromatic wood and spices, sets it alight, is consumed by the flames, and rises renewed from the ashes.

Origins

The Greek phoenix was probably inspired in part by the Egyptian bennu, a heron-like deity linked with the sun god Ra, creation, and renewal. Herodotus gave the earliest surviving Greek account (c. 5th century BCE), describing a bird that visits Egypt every 500 years bearing the body of its parent encased in myrrh — a tale Herodotus himself treated sceptically. Later authors, including Ovid, Pliny the Elder, and Tacitus, elaborated the story, standardising the motif of self-immolation and rebirth from the ashes, and the lifespan of the phoenix — often 500 years — became a symbol of cosmic cycles.

Symbolism

In early Christian writing the phoenix became an emblem of resurrection and eternal life; it appears in the Physiologus and in medieval bestiaries, and was depicted in church art. The phoenix also entered heraldry and emblem books as a symbol of renewal and immortality. Parallels are often drawn with other mythical fire or solar birds, such as the Persian simurgh, the Chinese fenghuang, the Slavic firebird, and the Hindu Garuda, though these traditions are distinct in origin.

In modern culture

The phoenix remains a common symbol of rebirth and recovery — of cities rebuilt after destruction, of organisations, and in popular fiction, notably Fawkes in the Harry Potter series. The city of Phoenix, Arizona, takes its name from the idea of a new settlement rising from the ruins of the earlier Hohokam civilisation.
