Reserve police

Reserve police refers to auxiliary or part-time police personnel who supplement regular full-time officers. Reserve officers may be volunteers or paid part-time staff; depending on the jurisdiction, they may hold full police powers while on duty or only limited authority. They are used to augment patrols, provide security at events, assist in emergencies and disasters, and perform duties such as prisoner transport.

By country

United States

Many American law-enforcement agencies maintain reserve corps whose members complete academy training comparable to that of regular officers and typically commit a minimum number of hours per month. Some reserves are unpaid volunteers; others receive hourly pay. Requirements and authority vary widely by state law and agency policy.

United Kingdom

The nearest British equivalents are special constables — volunteer police officers with full constabulary powers, organised in the Special Constabulary — rather than a body called reserve police.

Germany (historical)

In Nazi Germany, the Reserve Police Battalions of the Order Police (Ordnungspolizei) were units largely of older reservists. Several battalions participated in mass shootings of Jews and other civilians in occupied Eastern Europe during the Second World War; Reserve Police Battalion 101 was the subject of Christopher Browning's study Ordinary Men (1992).

Other uses

Some countries maintain standing paramilitary police reserves for riot control and internal security, such as India's state armed police battalions. In general usage, "reserve police" may refer to any organised body of trained officers held in reserve for mobilisation in emergencies.
