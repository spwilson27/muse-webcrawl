Self in Jungian psychology

The Self in Jungian psychology is one of the archetypes described by Carl Jung, representing the unified unconsciousness and consciousness of an individual. Jung often represented the Self as a circle, square or mandala. It is the central archetype of the collective unconscious and the organising principle of the psyche, toward which the process of individuation moves.

Definition

Jung defined the Self as both the totality of the psyche and its centre, in contrast to the ego, which is only the centre of consciousness. In Aion (1951) he wrote that the Self is "not only the centre but also the whole circumference which embraces both conscious and unconscious". The Self emerges as a compensating, ordering factor in dreams and symbols when the ego's one-sided attitude requires correction.

Individuation

The realisation of the Self is the goal of individuation: the lifelong process by which a person integrates shadow, anima or animus, and other unconscious contents into consciousness, becoming their distinct, indivisible whole. Jung regarded religious imagery — Christ, the Buddha, the mandala, the philosopher's stone of alchemy — as symbolic expressions of the Self.

Relation to other archetypes

The Self encompasses and orders all other archetypes. Its symbols typically exhibit quaternity and circular form, and experiences of it carry a numinous, powerful quality that Jung compared to encounters with the divine.
