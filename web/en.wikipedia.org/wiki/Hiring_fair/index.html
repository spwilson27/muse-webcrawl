Hiring fair

Hiring fairs, also called statute fairs or mop fairs, were annual gatherings in market towns of England, Wales, Scotland and Ireland at which agricultural labourers and domestic servants sought yearly employment from farmers and householders. They formed the principal labour market of rural Britain from the later Middle Ages until the early twentieth century.

Origins

The fairs grew out of sessions held under the Statute of Labourers (1351) and the Statute of Artificers (1563) at which wages were assessed and servants formally hired — hence "statute fairs". Most were held around Michaelmas (late September/early October), the traditional end of the farming year; in Scotland similar Candlemas and Whitsun "fee'in markets" performed the same function.

How the fairs worked

Servants seeking places stood in rows in the marketplace displaying an emblem of their trade: a shepherd with his crook, a carter with whipcord in his hat, a milkmaid with her stool, and housemaids carrying a mop — the emblem that gave "mop fairs" their name. A bargain was sealed with a "fastening penny" (earnest money) paid by the master, after which the remainder of the day belonged to the fair proper, with stalls, shows and amusements. Contracts normally ran for a full year — "from Martinmas to Martinmas" — and a short "runaway mop" a week later let dissatisfied parties on either side re-hire. Thomas Hardy's novels, notably The Mayor of Casterbridge with its hiring fair at Weydon-Priors, preserve vivid pictures of the institution.

Decline

Hiring declined with the advance of weekly wage labour, mechanisation and the disruption of the First World War, and most fairs lapsed as labour exchanges took over; several survive today purely as funfairs, retaining the mop fair name.
