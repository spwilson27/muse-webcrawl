Envy

Envy is an emotion that occurs when a person lacks another's quality, skill, achievement or possession and either desires it or wishes that the other lacked it. Social comparison theorists distinguish benign envy, which motivates self-improvement, from malicious envy, which motivates hostility toward the envied person and a desire to pull them down.

Psychology

Envy is typically directed at similar others — peers in age, occupation or social circle — whose advantage touches a domain central to the self. It involves feelings of inferiority, resentment and perceived injustice, and studies link chronic envy with lower well-being, while experimentally induced envy can increase both striving and schadenfreude when the envied person fails. Envy is distinguished from jealousy, which concerns the fear of losing a valued relationship or possession to a rival, though the words are often conflated in everyday speech.

Philosophical and religious views

Aristotle analysed envy (phthonos) as pain at the good fortune of equals; Kant called it a vice hostile to human fellowship. In Catholic moral teaching envy is one of the seven deadly sins, sorrow at another's good viewed as one's own loss. Buddhism treats envy (issā) as an unwholesome mental factor countered by sympathetic joy (muditā).

In society

Because envy can motivate both ambition and destructive levelling, it features in debates about inequality and taxation — critics of redistribution sometimes invoke the "politics of envy", while defenders reply that concern about unfair advantage is not mere envy. Social media, by making others' curated successes constantly visible, has prompted research on "Facebook envy" and its effects on mood.
