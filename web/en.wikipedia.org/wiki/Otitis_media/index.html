Otitis media

Otitis media
Specialty: Otorhinolaryngology
Symptoms: Ear pain, fever, irritability, temporary hearing loss
Types: Acute otitis media; otitis media with effusion; chronic suppurative otitis media

Otitis media is a group of inflammatory diseases of the middle ear. The two main types are acute otitis media (AOM) and otitis media with effusion (OME). AOM is an acute infection characterised by the presence of fluid in the middle ear together with signs of infection — typically ear pain, fever and a bulging tympanic membrane. OME, sometimes called "glue ear", is fluid without acute infection; it can follow AOM or arise on its own and commonly causes temporary hearing loss. Chronic suppurative otitis media involves a persistent perforation of the eardrum with discharge.

Epidemiology

Otitis media is one of the commonest diseases of childhood. Most children have at least one episode by the age of three, and it is a leading reason for paediatric visits and antibiotic prescriptions. Risk factors include young age, attendance at group childcare, exposure to tobacco smoke, dummy (pacifier) use, lack of breastfeeding, and anatomical predisposition (as in children with cleft palate or Down syndrome).

Cause and pathophysiology

Dysfunction of the Eustachian tube, often during a viral upper respiratory infection, leads to negative middle-ear pressure and accumulation of fluid, which may then be colonised by bacteria. The principal bacterial pathogens are Streptococcus pneumoniae, non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis. Viruses such as RSV and rhinovirus are found in many cases.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by otoscopy: in AOM the eardrum is typically bulging, opaque and erythematous with reduced mobility on pneumatic otoscopy. OME shows fluid, sometimes with an air–fluid level, behind an intact but retracted or neutral drum.

Treatment

Many episodes of AOM resolve spontaneously; guidelines recommend analgesia for all and immediate antibiotics for severe cases, children under six months, and those with certain risk factors, with a "watchful waiting" option in milder cases in older children. Amoxicillin is usually first-line. Recurrent AOM or persistent OME with hearing loss may be treated with tympanostomy tubes (grommets). Vaccination against pneumococcus and influenza reduces incidence modestly. Complications are uncommon but include mastoiditis, labyrinthitis, facial nerve palsy and intracranial infection.
