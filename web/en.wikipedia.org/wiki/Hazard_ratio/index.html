Hazard ratio

In survival analysis, the hazard ratio (HR) is the ratio of the hazard rates of two groups — for example, patients receiving a treatment versus a control. The hazard rate is the instantaneous rate at which events (such as death, relapse, or failure) occur at a given time among those still at risk. The hazard ratio is the principal summary measure reported in most clinical trials that use time-to-event outcomes.

Definition

If h1(t) is the hazard function in the treated group and h0(t) the hazard in the control group, the hazard ratio is HR = h1(t) / h0(t). Under the proportional hazards assumption, this ratio is constant over time and is estimated by the Cox proportional hazards model, introduced by David Cox in 1972.

Interpretation

A hazard ratio of 1 indicates no difference between groups. A hazard ratio of 0.5 means that, at any given time, the event rate in the treated group is half that of the control group — often described as a 50% reduction in the hazard. A hazard ratio of 2 indicates a doubling of the instantaneous event rate. Hazard ratios are reported with confidence intervals; if the interval excludes 1, the difference is conventionally regarded as statistically significant.

Unlike a risk ratio, which is computed at a single time point, the hazard ratio summarises the group difference over the whole follow-up period and accounts for censoring — participants who leave a study or have not yet experienced the event when follow-up ends.

Limitations

The hazard ratio can be misleading when the proportional hazards assumption does not hold, for example when survival curves cross. It is a relative measure and conveys nothing about absolute risk: a halving of hazard matters differently when events are rare versus common. It is also a "built-in selection" measure, since as follow-up proceeds the composition of the groups still at risk changes. Alternatives such as the difference in restricted mean survival time are sometimes reported alongside the HR.
