Relative risk reduction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Relative risk reduction compares event rates proportionally between groups

In epidemiology, the relative risk reduction (RRR) or efficacy is the relative decrease in the risk of an adverse event in the exposed (treated) group compared to the risk in the unexposed (control) group. It is computed as RRR = (CER − EER) / CER = 1 − RR, where CER is the control event rate, EER is the experimental event rate, and RR is the relative risk.

Interpretation

If a treatment reduces the risk of an outcome from 4 in 100 (4%) to 3 in 100 (3%), the relative risk is 0.75 and the relative risk reduction is 25%, whereas the absolute risk reduction is only 1 percentage point and the number needed to treat is 100. Because relative measures ignore the baseline risk, they can make a treatment effect appear much larger than it is in absolute terms; for this reason, clinical and public-health communication guidelines recommend reporting absolute risk measures alongside the relative risk reduction. The same relative reduction can correspond to a number needed to treat of 2 (if risk falls from 50% to 25%) or of 10,000 (if risk falls from 0.01% to 0.005%).

Uses

Relative risk reduction is the standard way vaccine efficacy is reported — for example, a vaccine efficacy of 95% against symptomatic disease is a relative reduction in incidence between vaccinated and unvaccinated groups in a trial. It is widely used in reports of clinical trials and in pharmaceutical marketing, which has drawn criticism when absolute effects are omitted. The complementary concept for harmful exposures is the relative risk increase.
