National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine
Founded: 1863 (National Academy of Sciences charter)
Headquarters: Washington, D.C., United States
Members: National Academy of Sciences, National Academy of Engineering, National Academy of Medicine
Purpose: Independent scientific advice to the nation

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) is a collective term for the three honorary academies of the United States: the National Academy of Sciences (NAS, founded 1863), the National Academy of Engineering (NAE, founded 1964), and the National Academy of Medicine (NAM, founded 1970 as the Institute of Medicine). Together they provide independent, objective analysis and advice to the nation on matters of science, technology, and health, and conduct other programs to advance knowledge.

Structure and work

The academies are private, nonprofit institutions operating under a congressional charter granted to the National Academy of Sciences by President Abraham Lincoln in 1863. Their programmatic work is carried out largely through committees of volunteer experts that produce consensus study reports—several hundred publications in a typical period—on topics requested by federal agencies, Congress, and other sponsors. The National Academies Press publishes these reports, most of which are freely available online.

Membership in each academy is an honor conferred by election by peers in recognition of distinguished achievement: the NAS has roughly 2,400 members, the NAE about 2,500, and the NAM more than 2,000, figures that include international members. Well-known activities include the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) journal and, historically, the National Research Council, which served as the academies' operating arm from 1916 until its functions were absorbed into the NASEM structure.

Role

The National Academies are frequently called upon to assess contested scientific questions—from climate change and vaccine safety to science education standards—and their reports are often treated as authoritative consensus statements for policymakers.
