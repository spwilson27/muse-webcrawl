Ettore Tolomei (16 August 1865 – 25 May 1952) was an Italian nationalist and fascist politician. He is best known for his campaign to assert Italian claims over South Tyrol — the largely German-speaking province annexed by Italy after the First World War — and for devising the programme of forced Italianisation imposed on the region under Benito Mussolini.

Life and career

Born in Rovereto in the then-Austrian Trentino, Tolomei studied in Florence and Rome and worked in the Italian foreign ministry's colonial sphere before turning to irredentist propaganda. In 1890 he founded the journal La Nazione Italiana, and in 1906 he made a symbolic ascent of the Klockerkarkopf, declaring it the "Vetta d'Italia" — the northernmost peak of Italy — to support Italy's claim to the Brenner frontier.

Italianisation of South Tyrol

After 1919 Tolomei prepared the Prontuario dei nomi locali dell'Alto Adige, an official handbook that replaced thousands of German and Ladin place names with Italian ones, many translated, adapted or newly invented. In 1923 he proclaimed a 32-point programme at Bolzano that banned German in public administration and schools, Italianised personal and place names, and promoted Italian immigration into the province. Appointed senator in 1923, he continued to agitate for assimilation until the fall of fascism; he was arrested by German forces after 1943 and interned, and died in Rome in 1952.

Tolomei remains a deeply controversial figure: commemorated by some Italian nationalists, he is regarded in South Tyrol as the architect of an attempted cultural erasure of the German-speaking population, and his tomb in Montan was blown up by South Tyrolean separatists in 1961.
