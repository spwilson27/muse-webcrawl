Digital electronics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A printed circuit board with integrated circuits and logic chips]

Digital electronics is the branch of electronics dealing with circuits whose signals take discrete values — almost always two voltage levels representing binary 0 and 1. It is the hardware foundation of computers, phones, and essentially all modern electronic systems.

Digital circuits are built from logic gates (AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR) realised with transistors, overwhelmingly in CMOS technology. Combinational circuits compute outputs from current inputs; sequential circuits (flip-flops, registers, counters) add memory of past states, enabling clocks, state machines, and processors.

Design

Engineers describe hardware with languages such as VHDL and Verilog, simulate and verify designs in software, and synthesise them to gate-level netlists for fabrication. Number representations (two's complement, IEEE 754 floating point) and Boolean algebra govern arithmetic units.

Advantages of digital over analogue include noise immunity, reproducibility, and programmability; disadvantages include quantisation error and the cost of conversion between analogue and digital domains (ADCs and DACs).

History

Relay and vacuum-tube logic gave way to discrete transistors, then integrated circuits (1958-59: Kilby and Noyce), then microprocessors (1971). The ensuing scaling (Moore's law) put billions of transistors on a chip and digital electronics into nearly every object.
