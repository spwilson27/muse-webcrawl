Outing

Outing

DefinitionPublic disclosure of a person's sexual orientation or gender identity without their consent
Related termDoxing; "outing" also used in politics and intelligence
Contrasted withComing out (self-disclosure)

Outing is the act of publicly disclosing the sexual orientation or gender identity of a person who has not consented to the disclosure, in contrast to "coming out", which is self-disclosure. The term is most often applied to the exposure of closeted public figures — politicians, celebrities, clergy, and officials — though it can describe disclosure harming private individuals as well. Forced outing is widely condemned by LGBT organisations as a violation of privacy, while a minority of activists have defended outing selected powerful figures, particularly those perceived as acting against LGBT interests.

History

Although outing occurred informally throughout the twentieth century — gossip column hints, police raids reported in newspapers, and blackmail all exposed hidden lives — it crystallised as a named, debated tactic during the AIDS crisis of the late 1980s. Faced with government inaction and media silence, some activists argued that closeted officials who opposed gay rights forfeited their privacy. The American magazine OutWeek (1989–1991), edited by Gabriel Rotello with contributions from Michelangelo Signorile, became the tactic's most famous vehicle, outing figures including the publisher Malcolm Forbes shortly after his death in 1990.

The practice expanded with the internet: the 2006 exposure of anti-gay-rights congressman Mark Foley's messages, and campaigns by blogger Mike Rogers against closeted officials, revived the debate, while the forced outing of private individuals — including teenagers — came to be treated as a form of bullying and, in some jurisdictions, harassment.

Arguments

Defenders of outing public figures argue that privacy claims cannot shield hypocrisy, and compare outing to investigative reporting on other hidden misconduct. Critics respond that sexuality is not misconduct, that outing endangers people in unsupportive families, workplaces, or countries where homosexuality is criminalised, and that it deters closeted people from seeking community. Many mainstream journalists adopted codes under which orientation is reported only when directly relevant to a public story.

Other senses

By extension, "outing" is used for exposing any concealed identity or affiliation — outing an undercover agent, an anonymous commentator, or a corporate front group — and overlaps with the modern practice of doxing.
