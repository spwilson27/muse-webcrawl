Dramatic irony

Dramatic irony is a form of irony in literature, theatre, and film in which the audience or reader knows considerably more about the situations, the causes of conflicts, and their resolutions before the leading characters or actors. The word of the character is thus understood by the audience but not by the character themselves. Dramatic irony is most often associated with tragedy, where it heightens suspense and sympathy: spectators watch characters act on mistaken beliefs whose consequences they can foresee.

Types

Wayne C. Booth and later scholars describe structures of knowledge asymmetry: the audience may know a character's true identity, a hidden plot, or an approaching danger. Related forms include tragic irony (a special case in drama), Socratic irony (feigned ignorance in dialogue), and cosmic irony (the irony of fate).

Examples

  • In Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, the audience knows that Oedipus himself is the murderer he seeks, and that he has married his mother, giving his speeches their double meaning.
  • In Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet, the audience knows Juliet is only asleep when Romeo believes her dead.
  • In Macbeth, Duncan praises the trustworthiness of the castle and its host while the audience knows of the planned murder.
  • In film, Hitchcock's suspense technique — his famous example of a bomb under a table that the audience sees but the characters do not — relies on dramatic irony rather than surprise.

History

The device is as old as Greek drama, whose audiences knew the myths in advance; the term gained currency in 19th-century criticism. Dramatic irony also structures horror (the viewer sees the monster behind the protagonist) and comedy (mistaken identity plots from Plautus to modern sitcoms).
