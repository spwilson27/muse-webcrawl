Viaticum

Viaticum is a term used in the Catholic Church for the Eucharist (Holy Communion) when it is administered to a person who is dying or in danger of death. The word comes from the Latin via, "way" or "road": the viaticum is "food for the journey" of the soul from this life to the next. Together with the sacraments of Penance and the Anointing of the Sick, it forms part of the Church's last rites.

Bringing the viaticum to the dying

Practice

The practice is ancient: the First Council of Nicaea (325) already legislated that the dying should not be deprived of the viaticum. In the Roman rite, any priest — and in necessity a deacon or other authorised minister — may bring communion to the dying, and the usual fasting rules are relaxed. The rite includes a renewal of baptismal profession of faith and a special form of words: "May the Lord Jesus Christ protect you and lead you to eternal life." Traditionally the host was carried to the sick person's home in procession with a bell and candle, and bystanders knelt as it passed.

Catholic canon law (canon 921) urges that the faithful in danger of death receive the viaticum, and that it may be repeated on separate days if the danger persists. Eastern Orthodox practice likewise gives communion to the dying, though the term viaticum is more characteristic of the Latin West.

Related usage

In Roman antiquity, viaticum more broadly meant provisions or money for a journey, and in Roman law it referred to an allowance given for travel on public business. By extension, classical authors used it for anything that equips a person for a passage — the sense the Church adapted for its sacramental usage.
