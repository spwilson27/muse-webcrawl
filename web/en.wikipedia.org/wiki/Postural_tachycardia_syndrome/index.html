Postural tachycardia syndrome

A tilt table test can demonstrate the abnormal heart-rate rise on standing that defines PoTS

Other namesPoTS, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome
SpecialtyCardiology, neurology
Key signHeart rate rise ≥30 bpm within 10 minutes of standing (≥40 bpm in adolescents)
OnsetOften adolescence or young adulthood

Postural tachycardia syndrome (PoTS, also written POTS) is a condition of abnormal autonomic function in which moving to an upright position causes an excessive increase in heart rate without a fall in blood pressure, accompanied by symptoms of orthostatic intolerance. Diagnostic criteria require a sustained heart-rate increase of at least 30 beats per minute within ten minutes of standing or head-up tilt (40 bpm for those aged 12–19), in the absence of orthostatic hypotension. It is a form of dysautonomia and predominantly affects women, often beginning in adolescence.

Symptoms

Characteristic symptoms include palpitations, lightheadedness, presyncope and sometimes fainting, chest discomfort, breathlessness, tremulousness, exercise intolerance, fatigue, headaches, nausea, "brain fog", and heat intolerance. Symptoms worsen with standing, heat, meals, dehydration, and prolonged immobility, and improve on lying down. Severity ranges from mild inconvenience to major disability.

Causes and mechanisms

PoTS is thought to arise through several overlapping mechanisms: excessive pooling of blood in the legs and abdomen on standing (neuropathic PoTS, associated with small-fibre nerve damage), an overactive sympathetic response with high circulating noradrenaline (hyperadrenergic PoTS), low blood volume, deconditioning, and autoimmune processes. Onset may follow viral illness, surgery, pregnancy, trauma, or rapid adolescent growth; cases increased notably after COVID-19 infection. It is frequently comorbid with hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, mast cell activation disorders, and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on history and demonstrating the heart-rate criterion by an active stand test or tilt table test, after excluding other causes such as dehydration, thyroid disease, and anaemia. Management begins with non-drug measures: increased fluid and salt intake, compression garments, avoiding triggers, and a graduated recumbent exercise programme. Medicines used include fludrocortisone, midodrine, beta blockers such as propranolol, ivabradine, and pyridostigmine, chosen according to subtype and symptoms. Many patients improve substantially over time, though some have persistent symptoms requiring long-term management.
