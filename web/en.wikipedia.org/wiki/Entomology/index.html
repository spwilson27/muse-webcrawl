Entomology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Entomology (from Ancient Greek ἔντομον entomon, "insect", and -λογία -logia, "study") is the scientific study of insects, a branch of zoology. In the past the term was sometimes used more loosely for the study of all arthropods, but today it generally refers to insects alone; the study of other arthropods falls under arachnology, myriapodology, and related fields.

Insects are the most diverse group of animals on Earth, with more than a million described species representing over half of all known living organisms. Entomology is thus of great importance to agriculture, ecology, medicine, and forensic science.

History

The study of insects has ancient roots, but entomology as a formal discipline developed in the 16th–18th centuries with figures such as Ulisse Aldrovandi, Jan Swammerdam, Maria Sibylla Merian, and René Antoine Ferchault de Réaumur. William Kirby is often regarded as the founder of entomology in England, and professional societies such as the Entomological Society of London (founded 1833) helped institutionalize the field.

Subdisciplines

Entomology includes specialized areas such as coleopterology (beetles), lepidopterology (butterflies and moths), myrmecology (ants), apiology (bees), medical entomology, agricultural entomology, and forensic entomology, which uses insect evidence to estimate time of death in criminal investigations.
