Scottish Book

The Scottish Book (Polish: Księga Szkocka) is a thick notebook used in the 1930s and 1940s by mathematicians of the Lwów School of Mathematics in Lwów, Poland (now Lviv, Ukraine), to record mathematical problems, conjectures and results discussed during their long meetings at the Scottish Café (Kawiarnia Szkocka).

History

The mathematicians — including Stefan Banach, Hugo Steinhaus, Stanisław Ulam, Stanisław Mazur, Karol Borsuk and Mark Kac — met in the Scottish Café near the university and debated problems for hours, writing directly on the marble tabletops. To preserve the results, Banach's wife Łucja purchased a large notebook, kept by the café's headwaiter, in which problems were entered from 1935 to 1941. Contributors often offered prizes for solutions: Mazur famously promised a live goose for the solution of problem 153, concerning the existence of Schauder bases in separable Banach spaces.

The notebook survived the Second World War and was later sent by Steinhaus to Ulam in the United States, who translated it into English and circulated it. An English edition, with commentary, was published as The Scottish Book: Mathematics from the Scottish Café, edited by R. Daniel Mauldin (1981).

Contents and legacy

The book contains 193 numbered problems (with later additions), spanning functional analysis, topology, measure theory, probability and set theory. Many were solved in the following decades; others remain open. Problem 153 was solved negatively by Per Enflo in 1972, and Mazur duly presented him with the promised goose in a ceremony broadcast in Poland. The Scottish Book is remembered both as a mathematical document and as a symbol of the Lwów school, which was destroyed by the war — many of its contributors were murdered in the Holocaust or scattered into exile.
