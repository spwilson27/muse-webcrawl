Villanella

The villanella (plural villanelle) is a form of light Italian secular vocal music that flourished in the second half of the 16th century. Originating in Naples—its name derives from villano, "peasant" or "rustic"—the villanella cultivated a deliberately simple, earthy style in contrast to the refined, courtly madrigal.

A sixteenth-century printed collection of Neapolitan villanelle.

Characteristics

Villanelle were typically written for three unaccompanied voices, in a strophic form with a refrain, using homophonic textures and simple chordal harmony. Their texts, often in Neapolitan dialect, dealt with rustic love, humor, and earthy double meanings. Composers of the early villanella cultivated intentional "rustic" solecisms—most famously parallel fifths—that art-music rules forbade, lending the genre a playful anti-courtly flavor. Giovanni Domenico da Nola was among its first prominent composers.

Development

As the villanella spread across Italy and Europe, it became smoother and more madrigalian, losing some of its rough character. Luca Marenzio's villanelle are polished and elegant, and the genre fed directly into the lighter madrigal and the canzonetta and balletto of the 1590s, influencing English composers such as Thomas Morley. By the early 17th century the villanella had effectively merged into these successor genres.
