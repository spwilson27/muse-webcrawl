Cabinet (government)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cabinet is a body of high-ranking state officials, typically consisting of the executive branch's top leaders — ministers or secretaries heading government departments — chaired by the head of government (or, in some systems, formally by the head of state). Cabinets are the principal decision-making forum of most governments, coordinating policy and, in parliamentary systems, exercising collective responsibility: ministers must publicly support cabinet decisions or resign.

Origin and operation

The institution grew from monarchs' privy councils and inner ("cabinet") councils in early-modern Britain — the word itself deriving from the small room where advisers met — and evolved with responsible government into the modern committee of senior parliamentarians under a prime minister. Cabinet size varies (about 15–25 full members is typical in Westminster systems), supported by cabinet committees, a secretariat, and in presidential systems by an Executive Office; the US Cabinet, by contrast, is constitutionally only advisory, each secretary being appointed by and answerable to the president.

Debates

Commentators periodically allege cabinet decline — decision-making shifting to prime-ministerial offices, bilateral deals and informal "sofa government" — followed by reassertions of cabinet process after crises or inquiries. Collective responsibility, cabinet secrecy and resignation conventions remain live topics in constitutional practice.
