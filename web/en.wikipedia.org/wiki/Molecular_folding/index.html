Molecular folding

Molecular folding
FieldMolecular biology, biophysics
ExamplesProtein folding, RNA folding
RelatedFolding funnel, Levinthal's paradox, chaperones

Molecular folding is the physical process by which a linear chain molecule — most notably a protein or nucleic acid — assumes its functional three-dimensional structure. Protein folding, in which a polypeptide chain folds into a characteristic and functional native state, is the best-studied example; RNA molecules also fold into defined shapes that determine their activity.

Mechanisms

Folding is driven by non-covalent interactions: hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effects that bury non-polar side chains in the interior, van der Waals forces, and ionic interactions. The folding funnel model describes the folding landscape as an energy funnel guiding the chain toward its lowest-energy native conformation.

Levinthal's paradox notes that a protein cannot find its native state by random search of all conformations in reasonable time, implying that folding follows directed, cooperative pathways through intermediate states.

Assistance and misfolding

Many proteins require molecular chaperones to fold correctly in the cell, particularly under stress. Protein misfolding and aggregation are implicated in diseases such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and prion diseases.

Predicting structure from sequence was long a grand challenge; the AlphaFold systems achieved major breakthroughs at the CASP competitions beginning in 2020, and in vitro folding is studied by techniques including NMR spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, and optical tweezers.
