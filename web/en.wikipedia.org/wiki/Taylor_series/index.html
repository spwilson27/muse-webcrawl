Taylor series

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A curve with polynomial approximations of increasing degree hugging it near one point]

A Taylor series is a representation of a function as an infinite sum of terms calculated from its derivatives at a single point: constants, then linear, quadratic and higher corrections. Truncated to finitely many terms it gives polynomial approximations — how calculators evaluate exponentials, sines and logarithms.

The series is named after Brook Taylor, who published the general result in 1715, though James Gregory and others had special cases decades earlier; the Maclaurin series is the case centred at zero.

Behaviour

A Taylor series may converge only in an interval around its centre (its radius of convergence) and may converge to something other than the function for pathological cases — facts that surprised early users and sharpened analysis.

Functions equal to their Taylor series are called analytic and dominate applications; in complex analysis analyticity is equivalent to differentiability, one of the subject's miracles.
