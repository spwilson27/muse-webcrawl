Byzantine enamel refers to the cloisonné enamelwork produced in the Byzantine Empire, chiefly in Constantinople, from about the 9th to the 12th century, which is regarded as among the finest enamel art ever made. Small gold plaques bearing images of Christ, the Virgin, saints and emperors were set into crowns, reliquaries, book covers, crosses and church furnishings, and were prized across medieval Europe.

Technique

In the Byzantine cloisonné method, thin gold strips were soldered on edge to a gold plate to form cells (cloisons) following the outline of the design; powdered coloured glass was then placed in the cells and fused by firing, after which the surface was polished. The Byzantines achieved exceptionally fine detail and subtle colour gradations on a miniature scale, often working plaques little more than a centimetre or two across.

History and dispersal

Enamels were diplomatic gifts and dynastic presents: the Crown of Constantine IX Monomachos and plaques sent to Hungary survive in Budapest, and the votive crown known as the Khakhuli triptych preserves many plaques in Georgia. After the sack of Constantinople in 1204, large quantities of Byzantine enamel reached the West as loot; the Pala d'Oro altarpiece in St Mark's Basilica, Venice, is largely assembled from such plaques. Byzantine craftsmen also influenced the enamel workshops of Kiev, Georgia and the medieval West, and the tradition declined after 1204, though work continued under the Palaiologoi.
