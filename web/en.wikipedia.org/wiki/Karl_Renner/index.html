Karl Renner

Karl Renner (14 December 1870 – 31 December 1950) was an Austrian politician of the Social Democratic Workers' Party. He is often called the "father of the Republic" for his role in founding both the First Austrian Republic after the First World War — as its first Chancellor (1918–1920) — and the Second Republic in 1945, serving as Federal President of Austria from 1945 until his death.

Early career and theory

Born to a poor peasant family in Moravia, Renner studied law in Vienna while working as a parliamentary librarian. He became a leading theorist of Austromarxism, writing influential works (some under pseudonyms) on the nationalities problem of the Habsburg monarchy. His proposal for national cultural autonomy — organising nations as autonomous public-law associations of persons regardless of territory — influenced later minority-rights thinking, though it failed to save the multinational empire.

First Republic

As Chancellor of the provisional government in 1918–19, Renner oversaw the proclamation of the Republic of German-Austria, led the Austrian delegation at the Paris Peace Conference, and signed the Treaty of Saint-Germain in 1919, which prohibited union with Germany and fixed Austria's borders. He remained a central figure in parliament, serving as President of the National Council in the early 1930s before the authoritarian turn of Engelbert Dollfuss.

Second Republic

In April 1945, with Soviet approval, Renner formed a provisional government uniting Socialists, the People's Party and Communists, proclaiming the restoration of an independent Austria on the basis of the 1943 Moscow Declaration. Elected President by the Federal Assembly in December 1945, he guided the republic's first years of reconstruction and occupation until his death in office at the end of 1950.
