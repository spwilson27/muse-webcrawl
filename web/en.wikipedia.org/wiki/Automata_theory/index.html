Automata theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Automata theory is the study of abstract machines (or more appropriately, abstract "mathematical" machines or systems) and the computational problems that can be solved using these machines. These abstract machines are called automata. The field is a branch of theoretical computer science and discrete mathematics.

A finite automaton represented as a state diagram.

Classes of automata

The best-known automata classes correspond to the Chomsky hierarchy of formal languages:

  • Finite-state machines recognise regular languages (see regular language).
  • Pushdown automata, finite automata augmented with a stack, recognise context-free languages.
  • Linear bounded automata recognise context-sensitive languages.
  • Turing machines recognise recursively enumerable languages and model general computation.

Applications

Automata theory underlies compilers and text processing (regular expressions), digital circuit design, protocol verification, model checking and the theory of computability. Key concepts include determinism versus nondeterminism, minimisation of automata (such as DFA minimisation), and the equivalence between automata and the languages they recognise (Kleene's theorem; the Myhill–Nerode theorem).
