The Chagatai Khanate was a Mongol and later Turkicized khanate in Central Asia, named after Chagatai Khan, the second son of Genghis Khan, who received the lands of the former Kara-Khitan Khanate — roughly Transoxiana and the Ili region, stretching from the Aral Sea to the Altai Mountains — as his inheritance.

History

Chagatai's ulus became a fully independent khanate as the Mongol Empire fragmented after the death of Möngke Khan in 1259. Under Alghu and Qaidu the khanate asserted itself against the Yuan dynasty and the Ilkhanate. The khanate's rulers gradually adopted Islam — Mubarak Shah was the first Muslim khan, and Tughlugh Timur's conversion in the 1340s was decisive in the east.

Division

In the 1340s the khanate split into two: the western part, Transoxiana, dominated by settled Turco-Mongol emirs (from which Timur would rise), and the eastern part, Moghulistan, where nomadic traditions endured. Remnant Chagatai khans ruled in the east, in the Yarkent Khanate and around Turpan, into the late 17th century. The Chagatai language — the literary Turkic language of Central Asia — and the name survived in the region's culture long after the polity vanished.
