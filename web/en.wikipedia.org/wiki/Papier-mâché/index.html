Papier-mâché

Papier-mâché (French for "chewed paper") is a composite material consisting of paper pieces or pulp bound with an adhesive such as glue, starch, or wallpaper paste. Objects are built up by layering strips of paper over a mould or armature, or by shaping a pulp mixture; when dry, the result is a light, strong, easily painted material used for sculpture, masks, piñatas, festival figures, and decorative crafts.

History

Paper-based moulded objects have a long history in China, where paper was invented, and in Persia and India, where painted papier-mâché boxes and trays became refined crafts (notably in Kashmir). In Europe, the technique flourished from the 17th century: France gave it its name, and in 18th- and 19th-century England manufacturers such as Henry Clay patented heat-hardened papier-mâché for trays, furniture panels, and even architectural ornament, often japanned and inlaid. Papier-mâché was also used for dolls' heads, theatrical props, anatomical models, and — in experiments — boats, furniture, and coffins.

Methods

In the strip method, strips of newspaper or other paper are soaked in adhesive and layered over a form such as a balloon, wire frame, or clay mould, then dried and finished with sanding, gesso, and paint. In the pulp method, paper is soaked or boiled into a mash, mixed with binder and fillers such as chalk or linseed oil, and pressed or modelled like clay. Famous applications include Mexican piñatas and alebrijes, carnival floats in Viareggio, Italy, and the giant figures of festivals worldwide.
