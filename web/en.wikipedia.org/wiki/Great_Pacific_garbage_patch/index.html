Great Pacific garbage patch

Microplastics and larger debris accumulate in the North Pacific Gyre.

The Great Pacific garbage patch, also described as the Pacific trash vortex, is a vast accumulation of marine debris — mostly plastics — in the North Pacific Ocean, gathered by the currents of the North Pacific Gyre. It comprises two main concentrations, an eastern patch between Hawaii and California and a western patch near Japan, connected by a debris corridor. A 2018 study estimated the eastern patch alone covers about 1.6 million square kilometres — roughly three times the size of France — and contains some 1.8 trillion pieces of plastic weighing around 80,000 tonnes.

Nature of the patch

Contrary to the popular image of a solid island of rubbish, the patch is mostly a diffuse soup of microplastics suspended in the upper water column, invisible from the surface and even from satellites. Larger items — fishing nets, buoys, bottles and crates — make up most of the mass; derelict fishing gear alone accounts for nearly half the total weight. Plastic enters the ocean from rivers, coasts and ships, photodegrades into ever smaller fragments, and is ingested by marine life, entering food webs.

Discovery and cleanup

The patch was predicted in a 1988 NOAA paper and publicised by sailor Charles Moore, who encountered it in 1997 while returning from a yacht race. Research voyages have mapped its extent and sampled its contents. Cleanup efforts, notably The Ocean Cleanup project founded by Boyan Slat, have deployed floating barrier systems to concentrate and remove larger plastics, while scientists stress that prevention — reducing plastic waste at source — is essential, since microplastics cannot practically be sieved from the open ocean.
