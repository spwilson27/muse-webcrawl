Fire investigation

Fire investigation, sometimes referred to as origin and cause investigation, is the analysis of fire-related incidents. After firefighters extinguish a fire, an investigation is launched to determine the origin and cause of the fire or explosion. Investigations of such incidents are done using a systematic approach and knowledge of basic fire science.

Methodology

In the United States, the principal reference is NFPA 921: Guide for Fire and Explosion Investigations, which prescribes use of the scientific method: recognising the need, defining the problem, collecting data, analysing the data using inductive reasoning, developing hypotheses, testing them deductively, and selecting a final hypothesis. Investigators systematically examine the scene from areas of least damage to areas of greatest damage, documenting burn patterns, ventilation effects, fuel loads and potential ignition sources, and excavating debris layer by layer.

Classification of causes

Fire causes are generally classified as:

  • Accidental — involving no deliberate human act to ignite the fire, such as electrical or cooking accidents.
  • Natural — such as lightning or other natural events.
  • Incendiary — deliberately set under circumstances in which the person knows the fire should not be ignited (arson).
  • Undetermined — where the cause cannot be proven to an acceptable level of certainty.

Investigators

Fire investigators may be public officials — fire marshals and fire department investigators — or private investigators working for insurers and attorneys. Certified Fire Investigator (CFI) credentials are offered by the International Association of Arson Investigators, and NFPA 1033 sets professional qualifications for the role. Laboratory analysis of debris for ignitable liquid residues, commonly by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, supports suspicions of accelerant use.

Historical misconceptions

Older investigative folklore treated indicators such as "crazed glass", concrete spalling, deep charring and certain burn patterns as reliable signs of accelerant use. Research from the 1970s onward — including full-scale test burns and the recognition of flashover effects — showed many of these indicators to be unreliable, a shift reflected in successive editions of NFPA 921 and in the re-examination of some arson convictions.
