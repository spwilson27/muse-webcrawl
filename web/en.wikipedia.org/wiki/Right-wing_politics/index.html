Right-wing politics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Right-wing politics is the range of political ideologies that view certain social orders and hierarchies as inevitable, natural, normal or desirable, typically supporting this position based on natural law, economics, authority, property, religion, or tradition. Hierarchy and inequality may be seen as natural results of traditional social differences or competition in market economies. The term originates in the French Revolution, where supporters of the monarchy and the old order sat on the right of the assembly's president.

Spectrum and currents

The mainstream right — the centre-right — includes conservatives, Christian democrats and conservative liberals who favour private property, market economies, gradual change, law and order and strong national institutions, usually within democratic and rule-of-law frameworks. Further right lie nationalist, radical-right and far-right currents that place greater emphasis on ethnic or cultural homogeneity and strong authority; the far right's extremist fringe rejects democracy itself. Libertarians overlap with the right on economics while dissenting on social and foreign policy.

History

From nineteenth-century defence of throne and altar, the right adapted to mass democracy, championed anti-communism through the Cold War, embraced market liberalisation in the 1980s, and since the 2000s has been reshaped in many countries by populist and national-conservative movements critical of globalisation, immigration and supranational institutions — developments that have strained older centre-right parties.
