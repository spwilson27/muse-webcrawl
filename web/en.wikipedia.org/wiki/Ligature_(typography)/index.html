Ligature (typography)

In writing and typography, a ligature occurs where two or more graphemes or letters are joined to form a single glyph. Examples are the characters æ and œ used in English and French, in which the letters 'a' and 'e' and 'o' and 'e' are joined. The common ampersand (&) developed from a ligature in which the Latin letters 'e' and 't' (et, meaning 'and') were combined.

History

Ligatures originated in handwriting, where scribes joined letters to write more quickly and save space on expensive writing materials. Monastic scribes of the medieval period developed extensive systems of ligatures and abbreviations in Latin manuscripts. When movable type printing emerged in Europe in the 15th century, early typefaces imitated handwriting and included large numbers of ligatures; Johannes Gutenberg's types reportedly included several hundred sorts, many of them ligatures.

Latin ligatures

Common ligatures in Latin-alphabet typography include ff, fi, fl, ffi, and ffl, which prevent the overhang of the letter f from colliding with the dot of i or the top of an adjacent letter. The long s (ſ) formed numerous ligatures in early modern printing. In German typesetting, certain letter combinations in Fraktur and in compound words are traditionally set without ligatures to preserve the intelligibility of word components.

Digital typography

In Unicode, some ligatures are encoded as separate characters in the Alphabetic Presentation Forms block, while modern font technologies such as OpenType apply ligature substitution automatically during text layout. Text-processing systems generally treat ligature glyphs as stylistic renderings of the underlying characters, so searching and indexing work on the unligatured letters.
