Seismic design

Shear walls and bracing are core elements of seismic design

Seismic design is the branch of structural engineering concerned with designing buildings, bridges and other structures to withstand earthquakes. Its governing philosophy in modern codes is life safety: a structure should resist minor earthquakes without damage, moderate earthquakes with repairable damage, and the strongest expected earthquakes without collapse.

Design principles

Seismic design provides a continuous load path from the roof to the foundations, lateral-force-resisting systems such as shear walls, braced frames and moment-resisting frames, and — crucially — ductility: the capacity of connections and members to deform plastically and dissipate energy without sudden failure. Capacity design deliberately designates sacrificial "fuses" (such as beam ends in a strong column–weak beam frame) so that damage concentrates where it can be tolerated. Detailing rules, such as closely spaced confining reinforcement in concrete columns, deliver the required ductility.

Codes and analysis

Design requirements are set by codes such as ASCE 7 and the International Building Code in the United States, Eurocode 8 in Europe, and national standards in Japan and New Zealand. Analysis ranges from the simplified equivalent lateral force method, which applies static forces derived from a design response spectrum based on the site's seismic hazard, to response-spectrum and nonlinear time-history analyses for important or irregular structures.

Advanced approaches

Beyond conventional strength and ductility, modern practice includes seismic base isolation, supplemental damping devices and rocking or self-centring systems aimed at keeping buildings functional and damage-free — the goals of performance-based earthquake engineering.
