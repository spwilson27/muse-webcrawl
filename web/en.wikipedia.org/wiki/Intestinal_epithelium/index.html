Intestinal epithelium


Villi are lined by a single layer of epithelial cells.

The intestinal epithelium is the single cell layer that forms the luminal surface of both the small and large intestine (colon) of the gastrointestinal tract. Composed of simple columnar epithelium, it serves simultaneously as a selective barrier that permits absorption of nutrients, electrolytes and water, and as a defence against pathogens and potentially harmful substances in the gut lumen.

Structure

In the small intestine the epithelium covers finger-like villi and lines the crypts of Lieberkühn at their bases. Crypt stem cells continuously divide, producing daughter cells that migrate upward, differentiate and are shed from the villus tip every few days, making the epithelium one of the fastest-renewing tissues in the body. The large intestine lacks villi but retains crypts.

Cell types

Enterocytes are the dominant absorptive cells, bearing dense microvilli that form the brush border. Goblet cells secrete protective mucus; enteroendocrine cells release gut hormones; Paneth cells at crypt bases secrete antimicrobial peptides; and M cells overlying Peyer's patches sample luminal antigens for the mucosal immune system.

Barrier function

Neighbouring cells are sealed by tight junctions whose permeability is dynamically regulated. Together with the mucus layer and secreted IgA, the epithelium maintains intestinal barrier function; its disruption is implicated in conditions such as coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease.
