Athetosis

Athetosis is a symptom of neurological disease characterised by slow, involuntary, convoluted, writhing movements, especially of the fingers, hands, toes, and feet, and in some cases the arms, legs, neck, and tongue. It results from lesions of the basal ganglia, particularly the corpus striatum. Movements that combine athetosis with the faster, jerky movements of chorea are called choreoathetosis.

Causes

Athetosis is classically associated with cerebral palsy, particularly the dyskinetic form arising from perinatal brain injury or kernicterus (bilirubin encephalopathy) in newborns. It can also follow strokes, encephalitis, or other damage affecting the striatum and related motor circuits. The term was introduced in 1871 by the American neurologist William Alexander Hammond in his treatise on diseases of the nervous system, from the Greek athetos, "without fixed position".

Features and treatment

Athetoid movements worsen with voluntary effort and emotional stress and disappear during sleep. Speech, swallowing, and fine motor tasks may be affected. There is no cure; management includes physiotherapy, occupational and speech therapy, medications such as anticholinergics or muscle relaxants in selected cases, and botulinum toxin injections for focal problems. In modern classification, athetosis is generally regarded as part of the spectrum of dystonia and dyskinetic movement disorders.
