Multiple Sleep Latency Test

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT) is a standard diagnostic tool used in the assessment of sleep disorders, particularly narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia. It measures how quickly a person falls asleep in a quiet environment during the day — their physiological sleep tendency.

Procedure

The MSLT is performed during the day immediately following an overnight polysomnogram, which documents that the patient obtained adequate sleep the night before. Beginning roughly 1.5–3 hours after waking, the patient is given four or five nap opportunities at two-hour intervals in a dark, quiet room. For each nap, technicians record electroencephalography (EEG), eye movements and muscle tone, measuring the time from lights-out to the first epoch of sleep (the sleep latency). Each nap ends after 20 minutes if no sleep occurs, or 15 minutes after sleep onset, during which the presence of REM sleep is noted.

Interpretation

The mean sleep latency across the naps is the key result. A mean latency of 8 minutes or less indicates excessive daytime sleepiness; healthy adults typically average 10–20 minutes. The occurrence of two or more sleep-onset REM periods (SOREMPs) — REM sleep appearing within 15 minutes of sleep onset — together with a short mean latency is the classic laboratory signature of narcolepsy. Before testing, patients are usually asked to stop medications that affect REM sleep, and sleep logs or actigraphy are used to confirm sufficient habitual sleep, since chronic sleep deprivation can produce false-positive results.

History

The test was developed in the 1970s by William Dement, Mary Carskadon and colleagues at Stanford University and has since become a cornerstone of sleep medicine, standardised in practice parameters of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine.
