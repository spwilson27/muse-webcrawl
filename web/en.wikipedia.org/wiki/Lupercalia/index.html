Lupercalia

Lupercalia was an ancient Roman festival of purification and fertility, observed annually on February 15. Its rites were centred on the Lupercal, the cave at the foot of the Palatine Hill where, according to legend, the she-wolf suckled Romulus and Remus, the founders of Rome. The festival was celebrated by a special college of priests called the Luperci.

Rites

The ceremonies began with the sacrifice of goats and a dog at the Lupercal. Two young Luperci were smeared on the forehead with the sacrificial blood and then wiped clean with milk-soaked wool, at which point they were expected to laugh. After a sacrificial feast, the Luperci cut strips (thongs, called februa) from the skins of the sacrificed goats and ran, naked or scantily clad, around the Palatine and through the city, striking people — especially women — with the thongs. Such blows were believed to promote fertility and ease childbirth, and women actively sought them. The month of February takes its name from these instruments of purification.

History

Lupercalia was among the most ancient Roman festivals, possibly predating the city itself and connected with the pastoral god Lupercus or Faunus. It features in the opening scene of Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, set during the festival of 44 BC, when Mark Antony offered Caesar a crown during the race. Despite its pagan character, the festival survived well into the Christian era; it was still celebrated in 494 AD, when Pope Gelasius I wrote against its observance by nominal Christians, a campaign often (though on uncertain evidence) linked to the replacement of Lupercalia by the feast of Saint Valentine or Candlemas.
