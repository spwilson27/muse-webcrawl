Decossackization

Cossack stanitsas on the Don, targeted by the Bolshevik policy of decossackization from 1919.

Decossackization (Russian: raskazachivanie) was the policy pursued by the Bolshevik government of Soviet Russia during and after the Russian Civil War to eliminate the Cossacks as a distinct social and military estate. Launched formally by a secret directive of the Communist Party's Central Committee on 24 January 1919, the policy combined mass executions, deportations, confiscation of land and property, and the abolition of Cossack institutions and self-government, particularly in the Don region.

Background

The Cossack hosts — self-governing military communities of the southern steppe frontier — had served the Tsarist state in return for land and privileges, and large numbers of them fought for the White armies during the Civil War. The Red Army's advance into the Don territory in early 1919 was followed by an uprising of the Don Cossacks at Vyoshenskaya in March, which the Bolsheviks suppressed with great severity; the repression in turn fed continued resistance.

The campaign

The January 1919 directive ordered "merciless mass terror" against wealthy Cossacks and the disarmament of the Cossack population. In practice, local revolutionary committees carried out executions of hostages and suspected sympathisers, deported whole settlements and redistributed Cossack land to non-Cossack peasants. Historians' estimates of the death toll during 1919–1920 vary widely, from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand when deaths from the accompanying famine and disease are included; the question of whether the campaign constitutes genocide is debated among scholars.

By late 1919 the policy was moderated as the Soviet leadership sought to win over poorer Cossacks and stabilise the region, but decossackization continued in other forms — the Cossack hosts were formally abolished in 1920, and Cossack lands were later collectivised. The post-Soviet Russian state formally condemned the repression: a 1991 law "On the Rehabilitation of Repressed Peoples" included the Cossacks among its subjects, and Cossack organisations and identity subsequently revived.
