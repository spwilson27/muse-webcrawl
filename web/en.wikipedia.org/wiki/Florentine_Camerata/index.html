Florentine Camerata

The Florentine Camerata, also known as the Camerata de' Bardi, was a group of humanists, musicians, poets, and intellectuals in late Renaissance Florence who gathered under the patronage of Count Giovanni de' Bardi from roughly 1573 into the 1580s and beyond. Their discussions and experiments, aimed at reviving the musical drama of ancient Greece, led directly to the invention of monody, the stile recitativo, and ultimately of opera itself.

Members and ideas

Core participants included the composer Vincenzo Galilei (father of Galileo), the singers and composers Giulio Caccini and Jacopo Peri, the poet Ottavio Rinuccini, and the theorist Girolamo Mei, whose letters on Greek music — drawing on his correspondence with Galilei — argued that ancient Greek drama was sung throughout by a single voice with simple accompaniment. Mei's claim that Greek music had powerful ethical effects because of its single melodic line inspired the Camerata's critique of Renaissance polyphony, in which, Galilei argued in his Dialogo della musica antica et della moderna (1581), the overlapping voices rendered the text unintelligible.

Works

The Camerata's practical experiments produced a new style: a solo voice declaiming text over a sparse basso continuo accompaniment. Caccini published his collection Le nuove musiche (1602), the manifesto of the style. The path culminated in Dafne (1598), by Peri and Rinuccini — the first work now generally called an opera, its music mostly lost — and Peri's Euridice (1600), the earliest opera whose score survives, performed for the wedding of Maria de' Medici and Henry IV of France.

Legacy

Though the Camerata's picture of Greek music was historically inaccurate, its creative misreading transformed Western music: monody and recitative underpin Baroque opera, cantata, and oratorio, and the group's ideal of music serving the text influenced composers from Monteverdi onward.
