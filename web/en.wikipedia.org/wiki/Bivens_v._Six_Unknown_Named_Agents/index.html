HoldingVictims of a Fourth Amendment violation by federal agents may sue the agents for damages despite the absence of a federal statute authorising the suit.

Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), is a case in which the US Supreme Court ruled that an implied cause of action existed for an individual whose Fourth Amendment protection against unreasonable search and seizure had been violated by the Federal Bureau of Narcotics. The type of lawsuit subsequently created is known as a Bivens action.

Facts and holding

In 1965, agents of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics entered Webster Bivens's Brooklyn apartment without a warrant or probable cause, manacled him in front of his family, searched his home, and detained and interrogated him. Bivens sued the unnamed agents for damages, alleging violations of his Fourth Amendment rights. The government argued that no federal statute authorised such a suit and that Bivens was limited to state-law remedies. Writing for the Court, Justice William J. Brennan Jr. held that a damages remedy is implied directly from the Constitution itself: the Fourth Amendment would be reduced to "a form of words" if a person in Bivens's position had no federal redress.

Legacy

The Supreme Court extended the Bivens rationale to Fifth Amendment due-process claims (Davis v. Passman, 1979) and Eighth Amendment claims (Carlson v. Green, 1980). Since the 1980s, however, the Court has consistently refused to extend Bivens to new contexts, most recently in Ziglar v. Abbasi (2017), Hernández v. Mesa (2020) and Egbert v. Boule (2022), reasoning that creating causes of action is primarily the role of Congress and that alternative remedial structures generally bar new claims. Bivens remains a cornerstone of constitutional tort law, complementing the statutory action against state officials under 42 U.S.C. § 1983.
