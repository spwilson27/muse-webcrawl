Hook (music)

A hook is a musical idea, often a short riff, passage, or phrase, that is used in popular music to make a song appealing and to "catch the ear of the listener." The term generally applies to popular genres such as rock, R&B, hip hop, dance, and pop. A hook can be melodic, lyrical, rhythmic, or purely sonic—such as a distinctive production sound—and it is typically the most memorable part of a song, frequently found in or just before the chorus.

Characteristics

Hooks work through repetition, simplicity, and surprise held in balance: a short motif, often one or two bars, repeated enough to lodge in memory but varied enough to avoid monotony. Musicologist Gary Burns has described a hook as the element that "stands out" in a recording, and commercial songwriting manuals treat the hook as the song's selling point—the part a listener hums after one hearing and the part used in advertisements and ringtones. In hip hop, the term also refers specifically to the sung or chanted refrain between verses.

Hooks and riffs

A hook overlaps with, but is not identical to, a riff: a riff is a repeated instrumental figure, whereas a hook is defined by its function—capturing attention—and may be a vocal phrase, a title line, a bassline, or a sound effect. Many famous hooks are lyrical title phrases, such as the repeated title lines in Motown and Brill Building pop, where songwriters like Holland–Dozier–Holland engineered choruses around a single irresistible phrase.

Commercial significance

In the streaming era, hooks have moved earlier in songs, as artists and producers aim to seize listeners within the first seconds to avoid skips. Research on "earworms" (involuntary musical imagery) suggests that hooks with simple contours, unusual intervals, and high repetition are especially likely to stick. Critics sometimes fault formulaic hook-driven writing for sameness, but the hook remains the central craft concept of commercial popular music.
