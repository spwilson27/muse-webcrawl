Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a neurocognitive disorder which involves cognitive impairments beyond those expected based on an individual's age and education but which are not significant enough to interfere with activities of daily living. It is regarded as a transitional stage between the cognitive changes of normal ageing and the more serious problems caused by dementia, most commonly Alzheimer's disease.

Classification

MCI is subdivided into amnestic MCI, in which memory impairment predominates, and non-amnestic MCI, in which other domains — language, visuospatial skills, attention or executive function — are chiefly affected. Either form may involve a single cognitive domain or multiple domains. Amnestic MCI is the form most strongly associated with progression to Alzheimer's dementia.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis generally requires a concern about cognition raised by the patient, an informant or a clinician; objective evidence of impairment on cognitive testing (often 1 to 1.5 standard deviations below age-adjusted norms); preserved independence in daily function; and absence of dementia. Screening instruments such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) are more sensitive to MCI than the Mini-Mental State Examination. Neuroimaging and, increasingly, biomarkers of amyloid and tau pathology can help identify the underlying cause.

Outcomes

People with MCI convert to dementia at a substantially elevated rate — often cited at roughly 10–15% per year in clinic-based samples for amnestic MCI, compared with 1–2% in the general older population. However, a significant proportion remain stable for years, and some revert to normal cognition, particularly when the impairment stems from treatable causes such as depression, medication effects or sleep apnoea.

Treatment and prevention

No medication is approved specifically for MCI. Management focuses on treating reversible contributors, controlling vascular risk factors (blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol), regular aerobic exercise, cognitive and social engagement, and treatment of hearing loss. Cholinesterase inhibitors have not shown sustained benefit in preventing progression. Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies approved for early Alzheimer's disease may be considered for patients whose MCI is biomarker-confirmed to be due to Alzheimer's pathology, under specialist supervision.
