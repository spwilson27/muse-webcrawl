Jus sanguinis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jus sanguinis (Latin for “right of blood”) is the principle that a person's nationality is determined by the nationality of their parents (or one parent), regardless of place of birth. It is the dominant principle in most of Europe, Asia and Africa, reflecting a conception of the nation as a community of descent — the legal counterpart of Ethnic nationalism.

Jus sanguinis transmits citizenship across generations abroad, subject in some states to registration requirements or generational limits, and it underlies diaspora-return laws: Ireland and Italy have allowed citizenship to descend through grandparents or further; Israel's Law of Return (1950) extends immigration and citizenship rights to Jews and their descendants; Germany, historically a pure jus sanguinis state, added territorial elements in its 2000 nationality reform while retaining descent as the core principle for emigrants' descendants. Mixed systems balance the two principles to avoid statelessness, which international law (1954 and 1961 conventions) seeks to reduce.
