Wilhelm Carl Grimm (24 February 1786 – 16 December 1859) was a German author and anthropologist, and the younger brother of Jacob Grimm, of the literary duo the Brothers Grimm. Although the two published jointly throughout their lives, Wilhelm was chiefly responsible for the literary shaping of the collection for which they are best remembered, Kinder- und Hausmärchen ("Children's and Household Tales"), known in English as Grimms' Fairy Tales.

Life

Wilhelm was born in Hanau, the second surviving son of Philipp Wilhelm Grimm, a lawyer, and Dorothea Grimm. After their father's death in 1796 left the family in reduced circumstances, Wilhelm and Jacob were educated at the Friedrichsgymnasium in Kassel and went on to study law at the University of Marburg, where the lectures of Friedrich Carl von Savigny awakened their interest in history and philology. Wilhelm's health was fragile throughout his life — he suffered from asthma and a heart condition — and he was long dependent on positions arranged alongside Jacob: librarian at Kassel, then, after their dismissal as two of the "Göttingen Seven" who protested against the King of Hanover's abolition of the constitution in 1837, eventually professors in Berlin from 1841 under the invitation of Frederick William IV of Prussia. In 1825 he married Henriette Dorothea ("Dortchen") Wild, a childhood friend and one of the principal storytellers from whom the brothers collected tales.

Work

The first volume of the Children's and Household Tales appeared in 1812. Over seven editions up to 1857, it was Wilhelm who progressively revised the tales: smoothing their style, adding dialogue and Christian moral colouring, softening some violence and sexual content, and fusing variant versions into polished literary stories — transforming a scholarly folk collection into a children's classic. His own scholarly publications included editions and studies of Old Danish ballads (Altdänische Heldenlieder, 1811), the German heroic saga (Die deutsche Heldensage, 1829), and the medieval poem Ruolandsliet. Together with Jacob he began the monumental Deutsches Wörterbuch (German Dictionary), the first volume of which appeared in 1854; Wilhelm worked mainly on the letter D before his death in Berlin in 1859. Jacob survived him by four years, and the brothers are buried side by side in the Alter St.-Matthäus-Kirchhof in Berlin.

Legacy

Through Wilhelm's editorial hand, tales such as "Cinderella", "Snow White", "Hansel and Gretel", "Rapunzel" and "Rumpelstiltskin" acquired the forms in which they became world literature, translated into more than 160 languages. The brothers' Kassel hand copies of the Tales were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2005.
