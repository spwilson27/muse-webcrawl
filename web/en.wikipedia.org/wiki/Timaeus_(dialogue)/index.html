Timaeus (Greek: Τίμαιος, Timaios) is one of Plato's dialogues, written c. 360 BC, that presents an account of the creation and structure of the universe and of humankind. The main speaker, Timaeus of Locri, describes how a divine craftsman — the Demiurge — fashioned the cosmos as a living, intelligent being, imitating an eternal model, out of a pre-existing chaotic material held in the "Receptacle." The dialogue also introduces the story of Atlantis, told by Critias as a preface to the cosmology.

Overview

The dialogue opens with a recapitulation of a discussion resembling the Republic's ideal city, followed by Critias's tale of ancient Athens and its victory over the island empire of Atlantis nine thousand years earlier. Timaeus then delivers the main speech, which he characterizes as a "likely story" (eikōs muthos) rather than exact knowledge, since the physical world is a changing copy of eternal being.

Cosmology

According to Timaeus, the cosmos is a single visible god: a sphere, because the sphere is the most perfect shape, rotating on its axis, endowed with a World Soul woven through its body. The Demiurge compounded the elements — fire, air, water, and earth — in mathematical proportion and constructed the regular ("Platonic") solids as the corpuscles of the elements: tetrahedron for fire, octahedron for air, icosahedron for water, cube for earth, with the dodecahedron used "for the whole." The heavenly bodies are divine beings whose motions constitute time, "a moving image of eternity." The speech concludes with the formation of living creatures and human anatomy and physiology.

Influence

The Timaeus was Plato's most influential work in late antiquity and the Middle Ages: it was the only Platonic dialogue widely available in Latin (in the partial translation and commentary of Calcidius) and shaped medieval cosmology, natural philosophy, and ideas of a rationally ordered creation. Its Atlantis story, treated variously as myth, allegory, or garbled history, has generated an enormous later literature.
