Islam and democracy

The relationship between Islam and democracy is contested among Muslim scholars, political movements and outside observers. Positions range from the view that Islam contains inherently democratic principles, through arguments for compatibility under conditions, to the claim by some Islamists and some critics alike that the two are irreconcilable.

Arguments for compatibility

Proponents cite concepts from the Qur'an and early Islamic practice: shura (consultation), ijma (consensus), ijtihad (independent reasoning) and the election of the first caliphs. Thinkers such as Rashid Rida and later scholars including Tariq Ramadan and Abdolkarim Soroush have argued that representative government, the rule of law and protection of minorities follow naturally from these principles. In practice, several Muslim-majority states — Indonesia (the world's largest), Senegal, and at times Turkey, Malaysia and Tunisia — have sustained competitive elections, and Islamist parties such as Tunisia's Ennahda have participated in and conceded power through democratic processes.

Points of tension

Critics of compatibility point to doctrines of divine sovereignty (hakimiyya) associated with Abul A'la Maududi and Sayyid Qutb, in which legislation belongs to God rather than popular majorities; to classical rules on apostasy and the status of non-Muslims and women; and to the poor democratic record of many Muslim-majority states. Others respond that such outcomes reflect colonial legacies, oil wealth, authoritarian secular regimes and geopolitics rather than theology, noting that survey research (e.g. the World Values Survey and Arab Barometer) finds strong majority support for democracy among Muslim publics, often combined with support for a public role for Islam.

"Islamic democracy"

Intermediate positions propose distinctly Islamic forms of democracy — constitutional systems combining elections with sharia review, as in Iran's Guardian Council model (which its critics regard as undemocratic) or in proposals for a "religious democracy" that critics and supporters continue to debate.
