Lancers

Lancers were cavalry soldiers armed principally with a lance. Light cavalry lancers — known in several armies as uhlans — became widespread in European warfare from the late 18th century until the First World War, combining the shock effect of the lance charge with the mobility of light horse; lancers also served in colonial and other armies around the world.

Origins

Although heavy cavalry had used lances since antiquity (the Polish winged hussars being the most famous early modern example), the modern lancer was modelled on Polish light horse of the 18th century. Frederick the Great raised lancer units after encountering them in Silesia, and Polish lancers in French service — notably the Polish Lancers of Napoleon's Imperial Guard and the Vistula Legion — won renown in the Napoleonic Wars, including the charge at Somosierra (1808) and at Albuera (1811), where Polish lancers devastated Colborne's infantry brigade. Their distinctive square-topped cap, the czapka, and short jacket were widely copied.

Nineteenth century

Most European armies converted some light cavalry regiments to lancers after 1815; Britain converted several hussar and light dragoon regiments (the 16th Light Dragoons became the first British lancers in 1816). Lancers fought in the Crimean War — the 17th Lancers rode in the Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaclava (1854) — and in colonial campaigns, where the lance proved effective against opponents without firearms; at Omdurman (1898) the 21st Lancers charged a hidden force of Mahdists, an action in which Winston Churchill took part. Indian Army lancer regiments, such as Skinner's Horse and the Bengal Lancers, were celebrated units of the Raj. The lance repeatedly fell out of and back into favour: Britain abolished it in 1903 and restored it in 1909 for front-rank use.

Decline

By 1914 the German, Russian, French, British, Italian and Austro-Hungarian armies all fielded lancer regiments — Germany alone had 26 uhlan regiments. The First World War's trenches, machine guns and artillery made mounted shock action nearly impossible on the Western Front, though lancers were still used on the Eastern Front and in the Middle East. After 1918 most armies mechanised or disbanded their lancers; Polish cavalry retained the lance into the 1930s (though the famous story of charging German tanks in 1939 is a myth). Some regiments preserve the name and ceremonial traditions today, and the lance is still carried ceremonially by units such as India's President's Bodyguard.
