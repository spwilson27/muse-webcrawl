Nihonga

Nihonga (Japanese: 日本画, "Japanese painting") is a term coined during the Meiji period (1868–1912) to distinguish Japanese paintings made with traditional materials and techniques from Western-style oil paintings, known as Yōga. Nihonga works use mineral pigments (iwa-enogu), ink, and sometimes gold and silver leaf, applied to silk or paper.

Nihonga

Country: Japan

Period: Meiji era to present

Media: Mineral pigments, ink, silk, washi paper

Key figures: Yokoyama Taikan, Hishida Shunsō, Kano Hōgai

History

The movement was promoted by the art critic Okakura Kakuzō and the educator Ernest Fenollosa, who sought to preserve Japanese painting traditions in the face of rapid Westernization. The Tokyo School of Fine Arts, founded in 1889, became its institutional centre, and artists such as Yokoyama Taikan and Hishida Shunsō developed the hazy, outline-free mōrōtai ("vague style") in response to Western techniques.

Materials and techniques

Nihonga pigments are made from ground minerals such as malachite and azurite, shells, coral and even semi-precious stones, mixed with animal-glue binder (nikawa). Techniques include layering, tarashikomi (puddling of wet pigment), and the application of metal leaf. Contemporary practitioners continue to exhibit through the Japan Art Institute's Inten exhibitions and other venues.
