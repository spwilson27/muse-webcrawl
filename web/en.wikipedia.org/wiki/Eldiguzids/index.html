Eldiguzids

The Eldiguzids, also known as the Atabegs of Azerbaijan (Persian: اتابکان آذربایجان), were a dynasty of Kipchak Turkic origin that ruled much of what is now northwestern Iran (Azerbaijan and Arran) and parts of the surrounding region from about 1135/36 to 1225. Nominally subordinate to the Seljuk sultans of western Iran, the Eldiguzid atabegs became the effective masters of the Seljuk state in its final decades.

Origins

The founder, Shams al-Din Ildigiz (Eldiguz), was a Kipchak slave commander in the service of the Seljuk sultan Mas'ud, who appointed him governor of Arran in 1135/36. Ildigiz gradually extended his authority over Azerbaijan, based at Nakhchivan and later Tabriz, and became atabeg (guardian) to the young Seljuk prince Arslan, whom he married to his own advantage through the prince's mother. In 1161 Ildigiz placed Arslan Shah on the throne in Hamadan and ruled the empire in his name.

Zenith and decline

Under Ildigiz's sons, Muhammad Jahan Pahlavan (r. 1175–1186) and Qizil Arslan (r. 1186–1191), the dynasty reached its height, dominating the Seljuk sultanate and repelling invasions by the Kingdom of Georgia. After Qizil Arslan's assassination, civil strife among his nephews weakened the state; the last Seljuk sultan, Toghrul III, was killed in 1194 with Eldiguzid involvement, ending the Great Seljuk line in Persia. The dynasty's remaining rulers, including Nusrat al-Din Abu Bakr and Muzaffar al-Din Uzbek, contended with Georgian, Khwarazmian and Ayyubid pressure. In 1225 the Khwarazmshah Jalal al-Din captured Tabriz and extinguished Eldiguzid rule.

Culture

The Eldiguzid court at Tabriz and Nakhchivan was a centre of Persian culture. The poet Nizami Ganjavi dedicated works to the atabegs, and the period produced outstanding architecture in the Nakhchivan school, including the Momine Khatun Mausoleum (1186) built under Jahan Pahlavan.
