Andre Gunder Frank (24 February 1929 – 23 April 2005) was a German-American economist and sociologist who was one of the founders of dependency theory and a major influence on world-systems analysis. His famous thesis — that contact with global capitalism produces not development but the "development of underdevelopment" in the periphery — reshaped the study of Latin America and the Third World from the 1960s onward.

Life

Frank was born in Berlin; his family fled Nazi Germany and settled in the United States in 1941. He studied economics at the University of Chicago, earning his doctorate in 1957 with a dissertation on Ukrainian agriculture — ironically under teachers including Milton Friedman, whose free-market economics he came to oppose. Teaching in Brazil, Chile, and Mexico in the 1960s, he wrote his most influential works while in Latin America; he fled Chile after the 1973 coup of Augusto Pinochet. He later held posts at universities in Britain, Germany, and the Netherlands, spending his final years in Amsterdam, where he was professor emeritus at the University of Amsterdam.

Dependency theory

In articles such as "The Development of Underdevelopment" (1966) and the book Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America (1967), Frank argued that underdevelopment is not an original condition of "traditional" societies but the historical product of their incorporation into the capitalist world economy. Wealth flows from satellites (peripheral regions) to metropoles (core countries) through chains of surplus extraction reaching down to the smallest village; regions most tightly bound to the metropolis in the past — mining enclaves and sugar plantations — are the most underdeveloped today. Development in the periphery, he argued, occurs mainly when its ties to the core weaken, as during world wars and depressions.

Later work: ReOrient

In ReOrient: Global Economy in the Asian Age (1998), Frank radicalised his argument, claiming that a single world system centred on Asia — especially China — had existed for millennia, and that Europe's dominance after 1800 was a brief interlude now ending with Asia's resurgence. He also, with Barry Gills, proposed a 5,000-year history of the world system, challenging the view that capitalism began in early-modern Europe. His work is a precursor of Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems theory, though the two differed on periodisation.

Criticism

Critics, including Robert Brenner and Ernesto Laclau, charged Frank with defining capitalism by exchange relations rather than relations of production, and with a functionalism that made development within the system seem impossible — a thesis strained by the later industrialisation of East Asia. Frank responded to such criticisms throughout his career and remained a combative, prolific polemicist to the end of his life.
