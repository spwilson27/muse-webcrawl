The Jungle Book is a 1967 American animated musical comedy film produced by Walt Disney Productions and released by Buena Vista Distribution. Based on Rudyard Kipling's 1894 book of the same name, it is the 19th Disney animated feature film and the last feature produced by Walt Disney, who died during its production in December 1966. Directed by Wolfgang Reitherman, the film follows Mowgli, a feral child raised by wolves in the Indian jungle, who must journey to the man-village to escape the tiger Shere Khan, guided — and distracted — by the panther Bagheera and the carefree bear Baloo.

Plot

Found as a baby by Bagheera, Mowgli is raised by a wolf pack. When the man-hating tiger Shere Khan returns to the jungle, the wolves decide Mowgli must go to the man-village. Bagheera escorts him, but Mowgli prefers jungle life: he is befriended by the fun-loving bear Baloo, hypnotised by the python Kaa, kidnapped by monkeys whose leader King Louie wants the secret of man's "red flower" (fire), and swept into a herd of marching elephants. After an argument with Baloo, Mowgli runs off and confronts Shere Khan; with help from Baloo, Bagheera and a chorus of vultures, he ties a burning branch to the tiger's tail and drives him away. Hearing a girl from the village singing at the river, Mowgli chooses to join his own kind, as Bagheera and Baloo look on.

Music

The songs were written mainly by Richard M. and Robert B. Sherman after Disney found Terry Gilkyson's darker score unsuitable; Gilkyson's "The Bare Necessities" was retained and became the film's signature song, earning an Academy Award nomination. Other songs include "I Wan'na Be Like You", "Trust in Me", "Colonel Hathi's March" and "That's What Friends Are For", sung by vultures modelled on the Beatles.

Reception and legacy

The film was a major commercial success, one of the highest-grossing films of 1967, and reissues brought its total gross to $378 million. It received positive reviews for its animation, characters and music and remains a beloved Disney classic. It inspired two animated television series, a 1994 live-action adaptation, a 2003 animated sequel, The Jungle Book 2, and Jon Favreau's photorealistic 2016 remake.
