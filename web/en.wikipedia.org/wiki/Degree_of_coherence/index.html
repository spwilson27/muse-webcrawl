Degree of coherence

The degree of coherence quantifies, in optics, the statistical correlation between points in an electromagnetic wave field. It measures the ability of light to produce interference fringes: fully coherent light gives maximum fringe visibility, incoherent light gives none.

Definitions

Temporal coherence is described by the (first-order) correlation function g(1)(τ), the normalised autocorrelation of the field with time delay τ; its magnitude falls from 1 to 0 over the coherence time, related to spectral bandwidth and coherence length. Spatial coherence, g(1) between two points, governs how large a source can be while still producing fringes—as in Young's double-slit experiment and stellar interferometry (van Cittert–Zernike theorem).

Second-order coherence

In quantum optics, the second-order correlation g(2) measures photon statistics: g(2)(0) = 1 for coherent laser light, 2 for thermal (bunched) light, and <1 for nonclassical, antibunched single-photon sources. Hanbury Brown and Twiss measured intensity correlations to determine stellar diameters.

Applications

Coherence concepts underpin lasers, holography, optical coherence tomography, interferometric imaging and quantum optics tests of light sources.
