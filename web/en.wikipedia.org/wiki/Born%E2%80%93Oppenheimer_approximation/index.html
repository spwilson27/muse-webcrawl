Born–Oppenheimer approximation

In quantum chemistry and molecular physics, the Born–Oppenheimer approximation is the assumption that the wave functions of atomic nuclei and electrons in a molecule can be treated separately, based on the fact that nuclei are much heavier than electrons. Named after Max Born and J. Robert Oppenheimer, who proposed it in 1927, the approximation underlies the concept of the potential energy surface and much of computational chemistry.

Rationale

Because protons and neutrons are roughly 1,836 times heavier than electrons, nuclei move much more slowly than electrons. The approximation therefore treats the nuclei as fixed while solving the electronic Schrödinger equation; the resulting electronic energy, as a function of nuclear positions, then serves as the potential energy governing nuclear motion in a second step.

Method

The molecular wavefunction is written as a product of an electronic wavefunction, depending parametrically on nuclear coordinates, and a nuclear wavefunction. Neglecting derivative couplings between electronic states (the adiabatic approximation) yields separate electronic and nuclear equations. The electronic energies at many nuclear geometries trace out a potential energy surface on which the nuclei vibrate and rotate.

Breakdown

The approximation fails when electronic states come close in energy, as at conical intersections, where nonadiabatic couplings become large. Such regions are essential in photochemistry, and beyond-Born–Oppenheimer methods are required to describe them.
