Aerofoil

Aerofoil (airfoil)
DefinitionCross-sectional shape of a wing, blade or sail
Key parametersChord, camber, thickness, leading/trailing edge
ProducesLift (aerodynamic force perpendicular to flow)
Standard familiesNACA 4-digit and 6-series sections

An aerofoil (British English) or airfoil (American English) is the cross-sectional shape of a wing, a propeller or turbine blade, a rotor, or a sail, designed so that air flowing over it produces a useful aerodynamic force — most commonly lift. The solid body bounded by the aerofoil's upper and lower surfaces generates a pressure difference between them as air accelerates over the curved upper surface.

Terminology

The straight line joining the leading and trailing edges is the chord line; its length is the chord. The curvature of the mean line between the surfaces is the camber; the maximum distance between upper and lower surfaces is the thickness, usually quoted as a percentage of chord. Angle of attack is measured between the chord line and the oncoming flow.

How lift is generated

Lift is explained by the circulation of air around the aerofoil and the resulting pressure field — faster flow and lower pressure above, slower flow and higher pressure below — consistent with Newton's third law as the wing deflects air downward. Beyond a critical angle of attack the flow separates and the wing stalls, with a sharp loss of lift.

Design families

Systematic families of sections were developed in wind tunnels in the 1930s and 1940s by the U.S. NACA (for example the NACA 2412 four-digit section and the laminar-flow 6-series), and remain standard references. Modern supercritical aerofoils, used on airliners, flatten the upper surface and sweep the pressure rise rearward to delay shock-wave drag in transonic cruise.
