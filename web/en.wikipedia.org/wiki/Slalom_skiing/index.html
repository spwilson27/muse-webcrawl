Slalom skiing

Slalom is an alpine skiing and snowboarding discipline involving skiing between poles or gates. It is the most technical of the alpine disciplines, with the gates spaced closest together, requiring rapid, short-radius turns. The word slalom comes from the Norwegian slalåm, meaning a track that slopes gently down over a smooth surface.

Course and rules

A slalom course is set with alternating red and blue gates — pairs of poles joined, in modern racing, by flexible spring-loaded poles that bend when struck. Men race courses with a vertical drop of roughly 180–220 metres and about 55–75 gates; women's courses are slightly shorter. Each skier makes two runs on differently set courses, and the combined time decides the result. Straddling a gate or missing one leads to disqualification. Racers wear shin guards, pole guards, and often chin bars to deflect the gates as they cross-block them.

History

Slalom was developed in the Alps in the early twentieth century, notably by Arnold Lunn, who organized the first modern slalom race at Mürren, Switzerland, in 1922. It became an Olympic event at the 1948 Winter Games in St. Moritz and is a fixture of the FIS Alpine Ski World Cup. Legendary slalom racers include Ingemar Stenmark, whose 86 World Cup wins were mostly in slalom and giant slalom, and Mikaela Shiffrin, the most successful slalom skier in World Cup history.

Related disciplines

Giant slalom uses wider gate spacing and longer turns; Super-G and downhill progressively emphasize speed over technique. Parallel slalom pits two racers head-to-head on identical short courses.
