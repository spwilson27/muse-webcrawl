Black-figure pottery

Black-figure pottery painting, also known as the black-figure style or black-figure ceramic, is one of the styles of painting on antique Greek vases. It was especially common between the 7th and 5th centuries BC, although there are specimens dating as late as the 2nd century BC. Stylistically it can be distinguished from the preceding Orientalizing period and the subsequent red-figure pottery style.

Technique

Figures and ornaments were painted on the body of the vessel using a glossy slip of refined clay that turned black during firing; details within the silhouettes were incised with a sharp tool, revealing the red clay beneath, and touches of white and purple-red slip were added for details such as women's skin and drapery. The black colour resulted from a three-stage firing: an oxidising phase, a reducing phase (in which the slip vitrified and turned black), and a re-oxidising phase that returned the porous body to red while the sintered slip remained black.

Development

The technique was developed in Corinth around 700 BC and adopted in Athens, where it reached its height in the 6th century BC. Celebrated painter-potters include the Nessos Painter, Kleitias (painter of the François Vase, c. 570 BC), Lydos, and Exekias, whose amphorae depicting Achilles and Ajax playing dice are masterpieces of the style. Around 530 BC the red-figure technique was invented, and the two coexisted for decades before black-figure declined in fine ware, surviving longer in Panathenaic prize amphorae and in regional production in Etruria and South Italy.

Subjects

Scenes draw chiefly on Greek mythology — the deeds of Herakles, the Trojan War, Dionysian revels — as well as scenes of daily life, athletics, warfare, and processions. Inscriptions naming figures, and sometimes painters' and potters' signatures, are a key source for Greek art history and literacy.
