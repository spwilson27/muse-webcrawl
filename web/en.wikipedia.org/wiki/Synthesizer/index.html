A synthesizer (also synthesiser, or simply synth) is an electronic musical instrument that generates audio signals. Synthesizers typically create sounds by generating waveforms through methods including subtractive, additive, and frequency modulation synthesis. These sounds may be altered by components such as filters, envelopes, and low-frequency oscillators. Synthesizers are typically played with a keyboard or controlled by sequencers, another musical instrument, or software.

History

Precursors included the Telharmonium (1906) and the Theremin (1920), followed by the RCA Mark II Sound Synthesizer (1957). The modern era began in 1964, when Robert Moog and Don Buchla independently introduced modular synthesizers using voltage control. The Minimoog (1970) packaged synthesis into a portable keyboard instrument and defined the standard layout of oscillators, filter, and envelope generators. The Prophet-5 (1978) added polyphony and patch memory; the Yamaha DX7 (1983) brought digital FM synthesis to mass market; and MIDI, introduced the same year, standardised instrument control. Software synthesizers became dominant in the 2000s, alongside a revival of modular and analogue hardware (Eurorack) in the 2010s.

Synthesis methods

In subtractive synthesis, harmonically rich waveforms are sculpted by filters. Additive synthesis sums sine waves; FM synthesis uses one oscillator to modulate another's frequency; wavetable, granular, physical modelling, and sampling-based methods provide further approaches. Common components include voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs), filters (VCFs), amplifiers (VCAs), envelope generators (often ADSR), and LFOs.

Impact

Synthesizers reshaped popular music from the 1960s onward, underpinning progressive rock, disco, synth-pop, hip hop, house, techno and most contemporary pop production, and becoming a fixture of film and game soundtracks.
