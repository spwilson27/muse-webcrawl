Great crested grebe

Scientific name: Podiceps cristatus

Family: Podicipedidae

Order: Podicipediformes

Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)

The great crested grebe (Podiceps cristatus) is a member of the grebe family of water birds. It is notable for its elaborate mating display, it was named for the ornament on its head, its ornate head plumes, which led to it being hunted for its feathers, almost leading to its extermination in the United Kingdom in the late 19th century.

Description

The great crested grebe is the largest member of the grebe family found in the Old World, with some larger species residing in the Americas. It measures 46–51 cm (18–20 in) long with a 59–73 cm (23–29 in) wingspan and weighs 0.9 to 1.5 kg (2.0 to 3.3 lb). It is an excellent swimmer and diver, and pursues its fish prey underwater. The adults are unmistakable in summer with head and neck decorations. In winter it is whiter than most grebes, with white above the eye, and a pink bill.

The young are distinctive because their heads are striped black and white. They lose these markings when they become adults.

Distribution and habitat

The great crested grebe breeds in vegetated areas of freshwater lakes across Europe and central Asia. It winters on more open water, such as reservoirs and coasts. Subspecies are found across Africa and Australasia, including Australia and New Zealand, where it is known to the Māori as pūteketeke.

Behaviour

Like all grebes, the great crested grebe nests on the water's edge, since its legs are set relatively far back and it cannot walk well. Usually two eggs are laid, and the fluffy, striped young grebes are often carried on the adult's back. In a clutch of two or more hatchlings, the male and female each take one offspring, and the young are soon able to swim and dive.

Courtship

The grebe has an elaborate courtship display. Like other grebes, it performs a "penguin dance" and a "cat display", and mirrors its partner's movements. H. Huxley's classic study of the grebe's courtship displays, published in 1914, was an important early work in ethology.

Relationship with humans

The bird was hunted almost to extinction in the United Kingdom in the 19th century for its head plumes, which were used to decorate hats and ladies' undergarments. The Royal Society for the Protection of Birds was set up in part to protect this species, which is again a common sight. In 2023 the Australasian subspecies won New Zealand's Bird of the Century competition after a campaign by comedian John Oliver.
