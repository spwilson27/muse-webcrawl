Islam in Turkey

Islam in Turkey is the largest religion in the country. The vast majority of Turkey's population is Muslim, with official figures commonly stating around 99%, although surveys suggest a significant share identify as cultural or non-practising Muslims. Most Turkish Muslims are Sunni, predominantly of the Hanafi school, while a substantial minority — often estimated at 10–20% — are Alevis, whose practices combine elements of Shia Islam, Sufism and Anatolian folk religion.

History

Islam spread into Anatolia following the Seljuk victory over the Byzantines at the Battle of Manzikert in 1071. Under the Ottoman Empire, which succeeded the Seljuks of Rum, Islam became the religion of the ruling state; the Ottoman sultan also claimed the title of caliph from the early 16th century, after Selim I's conquest of Egypt and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina.

The empire governed its religiously diverse population partly through the millet system, under which recognised non-Muslim communities managed their own religious and civil affairs. Sufi orders such as the Mevlevi (the "whirling dervishes"), Bektashi and Naqshbandi played a major role in Turkish religious life.

The republic and secularism

The Republic of Turkey, founded in 1923 under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, abolished the caliphate in 1924 and pursued a programme of secularisation (laiklik). Religious courts and schools were closed, the Arabic alphabet was replaced with a Latin one, and in 1928 Islam was removed as the state religion from the constitution.

At the same time, the state established the Directorate of Religious Affairs (Diyanet) in 1924 to administer Sunni Islam, appointing imams and preparing Friday sermons — a system of state control over religion rather than strict separation of religion and state. Debates over the headscarf in universities and public institutions, the place of religious (Imam Hatip) schools, and the status of Alevis have been central to Turkish politics for decades. Governments under the Justice and Development Party (AKP) since 2002 have expanded religious education and the Diyanet's budget, while critics argue secularism has been eroded.

Practices and institutions

Turkey is home to about 90,000 mosques. Landmarks include the Hagia Sophia in Istanbul — a Byzantine cathedral converted into a mosque under the Ottomans, a museum from 1935, and reconsecrated as a mosque in 2020 — the Süleymaniye Mosque, and the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, considered the masterpiece of the architect Mimar Sinan. Ramadan fasting, the two great festivals (Ramazan Bayramı and Kurban Bayramı), and circumcision celebrations are widely observed, though levels of daily prayer observance vary considerably between urban and rural areas.
