Spirulina

Spirulina (dietary supplement)
SourceArthrospira platensis and A. maxima (cyanobacteria)
ProteinAbout 55–70% of dry weight
Notable nutrientsPhycocyanin, beta-carotene, iron, B vitamins, gamma-linolenic acid

Spirulina is a biomass of cyanobacteria — photosynthetic blue-green bacteria, principally Arthrospira platensis and Arthrospira maxima — that is dried and consumed by humans as a dietary supplement and whole food. The organisms grow naturally in warm, alkaline lakes and form spiral-shaped microscopic filaments, from which the name derives.

History

Spirulina was a food source for the Aztecs of Mexico, who harvested it from Lake Texcoco and sold it in cake form (tecuitlatl) in the markets of Tenochtitlan. Around Lake Chad in Africa, the Kanembu people traditionally harvest a related biomass, dihé, which is dried into cakes and used in sauces. Commercial production began in the 1970s, and spirulina is now cultivated in open ponds and photobioreactors in many countries; it has also been studied as a food and oxygen source for long-duration space missions.

Nutrition

Dried spirulina is unusually protein-rich for a plant-like food, containing all essential amino acids, though in lower proportions than meat, eggs, or soy. It supplies iron, potassium, B vitamins (except that much of its vitamin B12 is an inactive analogue), beta-carotene, and the blue pigment phycocyanin, which is also used as a natural food colouring. It contains the omega-6 fatty acid gamma-linolenic acid.

Health claims and safety

Spirulina is marketed for antioxidant, cholesterol-lowering, and immune effects; small clinical studies suggest modest benefits for blood lipids and blood pressure, but evidence remains limited. Because open-pond cultures can be contaminated by toxin-producing cyanobacteria such as Microcystis, product quality varies and microcystin contamination is a recognised concern; people with phenylketonuria or autoimmune disease are often advised to avoid it.
