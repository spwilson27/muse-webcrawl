Five whys

Five whys

TypeRoot cause analysis technique
OriginToyota Production System
Attributed toSakichi Toyoda; popularised by Taiichi Ohno
Related methodsIshikawa diagram, fault tree analysis

The five whys is an iterative interrogative technique used to explore the cause-and-effect relationships underlying a particular problem. The primary goal of the technique is to determine the root cause of a defect or problem by repeating the question “why?” five times. The answer to the fifth why should reveal the root cause of the problem. The technique was originally developed by Sakichi Toyoda and was used within the Toyota Motor Corporation as a critical component of its problem-solving training, and it forms part of the Toyota Production System and of lean manufacturing more generally.

Example

A classic example given by Taiichi Ohno, architect of the Toyota Production System, concerns a machine that stopped working:

  1. “Why did the machine stop?” — There was an overload and the fuse blew.
  2. “Why was there an overload?” — The bearing was not sufficiently lubricated.
  3. “Why was it not lubricated?” — The lubrication pump was not pumping sufficiently.
  4. “Why was it not pumping?” — The shaft of the pump was worn and rattling.
  5. “Why was the shaft worn out?” — There was no strainer attached and metal scraps got in.

Without repeatedly asking why, managers might simply replace the fuse or the pump, whereas the countermeasure — attaching a strainer — addresses the root cause.

Method and use

In practice the number five is a rule of thumb: some problems are resolved in fewer steps, others require more. The method is often used together with the Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram, which organises possible causes, after which five-whys chains are pursued for the most likely causes. Effective use requires basing each answer on direct observation at the workplace (genchi genbutsu) rather than speculation.

Criticism

Critics note that the technique can stop at symptoms rather than deeper organisational causes, that different investigators may produce different chains of whys for the same problem, and that it assumes a single linear causal chain where real failures often have multiple interacting causes. It is therefore often recommended as one element within a broader root-cause analysis toolkit rather than a complete method in itself.
