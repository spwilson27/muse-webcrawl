Subject–object–verb

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistic typology, a subject–object–verb (SOV) language is one in which the subject, object, and verb of a sentence appear (or usually appear) in that order. If English were SOV, "Sam noodles ate" would be an ordinary sentence, as opposed to the actual English standard, subject–verb–object ("Sam ate noodles"). SOV is the most common word order among the world's languages, found in roughly 45 percent of them.

Distribution

Languages with SOV structure include Japanese, Korean, Turkish, Persian, Hindi and the other Indo-Aryan languages, the Dravidian languages, Mongolian, Tibetan, Burmese, Basque, Latin (in its classical prosaic default), and many indigenous languages of the Americas. German and Dutch display SOV order in subordinate clauses, with verb-second order in main clauses.

Properties

SOV languages tend to share certain characteristics: they commonly use postpositions rather than prepositions, place relative clauses and genitives before the noun they modify, and put auxiliary verbs after the main verb. These correlations were noted in the typological work of Joseph Greenberg and are often discussed in terms of "head-final" structure, in which modifiers precede their heads.

Examples

In Japanese, a sentence such as "Tarō ga ringo o tabeta" ("Taro [subject marker] apple [object marker] ate") follows SOV order. In Turkish, "Ahmet kitabı okudu" ("Ahmet the book read") has the same pattern. Latin often arranged sentences this way, as in "Puella puerum amat" ("The girl loves the boy", literally "girl boy loves").
