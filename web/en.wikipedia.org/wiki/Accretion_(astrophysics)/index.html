Accretion (astrophysics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In astrophysics, accretion is the accumulation of matter onto a massive body by gravitational attraction, generally gas drawn from the surrounding interstellar medium or from a companion star. Accretion is one of the most efficient energy-releasing processes known: matter falling into the deep gravitational well of a compact object such as a black hole, neutron star or white dwarf can convert a substantial fraction of its rest mass into radiation — for a non-rotating black hole about 6 per cent, and up to about 40 per cent for a maximally rotating one — far exceeding the efficiency of nuclear fusion in stars.

Accretion disks

Because infalling gas almost always carries angular momentum, it rarely falls directly onto the central object; instead it settles into a rotating accretion disk. Viscosity — in real disks thought to arise mainly from turbulence driven by the magnetorotational instability — transports angular momentum outward, allowing matter to spiral inward while heating through friction. The inner disk can reach millions of degrees and radiate strongly in X-rays. Disks are observed around young stars (protoplanetary disks, from which planets form), in X-ray binaries, in cataclysmic variables, and at the centres of active galaxies, where accretion onto a supermassive black hole powers quasars.

Modes of accretion

In binary systems, a compact object may accrete by capturing the stellar wind of its companion or by Roche-lobe overflow, in which the companion's outer layers spill through the inner Lagrange point as a stream. Isolated compact objects and stars can accrete directly from the interstellar medium (Bondi accretion, after Hermann Bondi, who described spherical accretion in 1952). At very high inflow rates the flow becomes radiatively inefficient, and the radiation pressure of an accreting source cannot exceed the Eddington limit, at which radiation pressure balances gravity. Episodes of Eddington-limited or super-Eddington growth are invoked to explain how supermassive black holes reached billions of solar masses early in the universe's history.

Planet formation

In star formation, accretion describes both the growth of a protostar from its collapsing molecular cloud core and the later build-up of planets in a protoplanetary disk, where dust grains stick, grow into planetesimals, and then into planetary embryos — the core accretion model of planet formation.
