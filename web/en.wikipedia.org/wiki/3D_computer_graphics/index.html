3D computer graphics

3D computer graphics, sometimes called CGI (computer-generated imagery), are graphics that use a three-dimensional representation of geometric data (often Cartesian) stored in the computer for the purpose of performing calculations and rendering digital images, usually 2D images but sometimes 3D images.

Overview

Creating 3D graphics generally proceeds in three stages. In modeling, the shape of an object is described, commonly as a polygon mesh of vertices, edges and faces, or with curved surfaces such as NURBS. In layout and animation, models are placed in a scene with lights and a virtual camera, and moved using techniques such as keyframing, rigging and motion capture. In rendering, the scene is converted into an image, by rasterization (fast, used in games) or ray tracing (slower, physically based light simulation), adding shading, textures and shadows.

History

Modern 3D graphics research began in the 1960s at the University of Utah, where Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad and later work by students including Ed Catmull and Jim Blinn produced foundational techniques: the Utah teapot model (1975), texture mapping, and the Phong shading model (Bui Tuong Phong). Futureworld (1976) showed the first 3D CGI in a feature film; Pixar's Toy Story (1995) was the first fully computer-animated feature. Dedicated graphics processing units (GPUs) from the late 1990s made real-time 3D routine in games and workstations.

Software and APIs

Popular modeling and animation packages include Blender, Autodesk Maya, 3ds Max, Cinema 4D and Houdini. Real-time rendering is programmed through APIs such as OpenGL, Vulkan and Microsoft Direct3D, running on GPUs, while offline film rendering uses engines such as RenderMan, Arnold and Cycles.
