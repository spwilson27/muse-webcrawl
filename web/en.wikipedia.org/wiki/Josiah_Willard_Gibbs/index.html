Josiah Willard Gibbs (11 February 1839 – 28 April 1903) was an American scientist who made significant theoretical contributions to physics, chemistry and mathematics. His work on the applications of thermodynamics was instrumental in transforming physical chemistry into a rigorous deductive science. Together with James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann, he created statistical mechanics, and his book Elementary Principles in Statistical Mechanics (1902) founded the field as a mature discipline. Albert Einstein called him "the greatest mind in American history."

Life

Gibbs was born and spent his whole life in New Haven, Connecticut, where his father was a distinguished Yale theologian. He graduated from Yale in 1858 and in 1863 received the first American doctorate in engineering. After three years of study in Paris, Berlin and Heidelberg, he returned to Yale as professor of mathematical physics in 1871 — a post he held, famously working without salary for the first nine years, until his death in 1903. He rarely left New England and published much of his work in the obscure Transactions of the Connecticut Academy, which long delayed its impact in Europe.

Thermodynamics

In a series of papers culminating in the monograph On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances (1876–78), Gibbs developed the concepts of chemical potential and free energy (the quantity now called Gibbs free energy), formulated the phase rule governing equilibria in mixtures, and presented thermodynamics in a unified geometrical form. Wilhelm Ostwald called the work "the foundation of modern chemistry" and translated it into German.

Statistical mechanics and vectors

Gibbs developed the ensemble methods of statistical mechanics, introducing the concepts of phase space and canonical ensembles, and explaining thermodynamic behaviour from probability over microstates. Separately, he recast Hamilton's quaternions into modern vector analysis — the dot and cross products used throughout physics and engineering derive substantially from his privately printed lecture notes.

Legacy

Gibbs received the Royal Society's Copley Medal in 1901. His name is attached to the Gibbs free energy, the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation, the Gibbs phase rule, the Gibbs paradox, the Gibbs phenomenon in Fourier analysis, and the Gibbs sampling algorithm. A memorial lecture and a Yale professorship honour him; the USS Gibbs and the Gibbs crater on the Moon were also named for him.
