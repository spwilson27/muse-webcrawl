Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
Abbreviation: TTP
Specialty: Hematology
Cause: Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency
Treatment: Plasma exchange, rituximab, caplacizumab
ICD-10: M31.1

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare blood disorder characterised by the formation of tiny blood clots (thrombi) in small blood vessels throughout the body, leading to a low platelet count (thrombocytopenia), destruction of red blood cells (microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia), and organ damage, especially of the brain and kidneys. It is a medical emergency that is fatal in most cases if untreated.

Schistocytes — fragmented red cells — on a blood smear in microangiopathic haemolysis.

Cause

TTP results from severe deficiency of the enzyme ADAMTS13, which normally cleaves large multimers of von Willebrand factor. Without it, ultra-large multimers persist and spontaneously bind platelets, forming microthrombi. The deficiency may be inherited (congenital TTP, or Upshaw–Schulman syndrome) or, more commonly, acquired when autoantibodies inhibit ADAMTS13. Acquired TTP may be associated with pregnancy, infections, autoimmune disease, or certain drugs.

Signs and symptoms

The classic pentad — haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, neurological symptoms, kidney impairment, and fever — is now rarely seen in full. Typical features include purpura and bleeding, fatigue, confusion, headache, seizures, stroke-like symptoms, jaundice, and dark urine. Diagnosis is supported by finding schistocytes on the blood smear, a very low platelet count, raised lactate dehydrogenase, and ADAMTS13 activity below 10%.

Treatment

Urgent plasma exchange (plasmapheresis with plasma replacement) is the mainstay of treatment and has reduced mortality from over 90% to roughly 10–20%. Corticosteroids and rituximab suppress autoantibody production; caplacizumab, a nanobody that blocks platelet–von Willebrand factor interaction, speeds platelet recovery. Congenital TTP is treated with plasma infusions.
