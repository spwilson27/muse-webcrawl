Text-to-speech (TTS) is a form of speech synthesis that converts written text into spoken audio. TTS systems — commonly called "voice engines" or "readers" — are used in screen readers for blind users, virtual assistants, public-announcement systems, language learning, and audiobook production.

How it works

A TTS pipeline has a text-analysis front end and a synthesis back end. The front end normalises text (expanding numbers, abbreviations, and symbols into words), tokenises it, assigns pronunciations using dictionaries and grapheme-to-phoneme rules, and computes prosody — phrasing, stress, and intonation. The back end then renders audio. Classic approaches include formant synthesis (rule-based, intelligible but robotic, as in DECtalk — famously the voice of Stephen Hawking), concatenative synthesis (joining recorded speech fragments, especially diphones or longer "unit selection" segments), and statistical parametric synthesis using hidden Markov models.

Neural era

From 2016, deep-learning systems transformed naturalness: Google's WaveNet generated raw audio sample-by-sample with neural networks; Tacotron and its successors predicted spectrograms end-to-end, followed by neural vocoders. Modern systems such as FastSpeech and diffusion- or transformer-based models synthesise near-human speech in real time, support voice cloning from short samples, and have raised new concerns about deepfakes and consent alongside their accessibility benefits.

Applications

TTS underpins screen readers (JAWS, NVDA, VoiceOver), GPS navigation prompts, telephone IVR systems, e-learning, and assistive communication devices, and is the counterpart of automatic speech recognition in spoken dialogue systems.
