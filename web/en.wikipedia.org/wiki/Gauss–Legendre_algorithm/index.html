Gauss–Legendre algorithm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Gauss–Legendre algorithm is an algorithm to compute the digits of π. It is notable for its extremely rapid convergence — the number of correct digits roughly doubles with each iteration — and for its use in several record-setting computations of π. The method is based on the individual work of Carl Friedrich Gauss and Adrien-Marie Legendre on the arithmetic-geometric mean and elliptic integrals.

The algorithm

Set the initial values:

  • a0 = 1
  • b0 = 1/√2
  • t0 = 1/4
  • p0 = 1

Then iterate:

  • an+1 = (an + bn) / 2
  • bn+1 = √(an bn)
  • tn+1 = tn − pn (an − an+1)2
  • pn+1 = 2 pn

π is then approximated by:

π ≈ (an+1 + bn+1)2 / (4 tn+1)

Convergence

The algorithm converges quadratically: just three iterations give π correct to about 19 digits, five iterations give about 84 digits, and 25 iterations are more than sufficient for over a billion digits. This extraordinary speed made it the method of choice for high-precision computations of π in the late 20th century, including record calculations by Yasumasa Kanada's team, before it was largely superseded in practice by the Chudnovsky algorithm, which has lower memory traffic.

History

Gauss developed the arithmetic-geometric mean in the 1790s and used identities discovered in conjunction with Legendre's work on elliptic integrals to relate the mean to π. The algorithm in its modern form was published in the 1970s; Richard Brent and, independently, Eugene Salamin rediscovered and popularised closely related formulations in 1975–76, and it is sometimes called the Brent–Salamin algorithm.
