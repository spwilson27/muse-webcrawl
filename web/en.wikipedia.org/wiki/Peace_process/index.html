Peace process

A peace process is a set of negotiations, mediation, and institutional arrangements through which parties to an armed conflict attempt to reach a durable settlement. Peace processes typically move through stages — exploratory talks, ceasefire, formal negotiation, agreement, and implementation — and may span years or decades, often punctuated by renewed violence.

Stages

  • Pre-negotiation: back-channel contacts, confidence-building measures, and agreement on who negotiates and about what
  • Ceasefire: a halt to hostilities, sometimes formalised as an armistice such as the Korean Armistice Agreement
  • Negotiation: bargaining over core issues — territory, power-sharing, security arrangements, justice, and reparations
  • Agreement and implementation: signing, ratification, demobilisation, elections, and monitoring by third parties

Examples

Notable peace processes include the Northern Ireland process, culminating in the Good Friday Agreement of 1998; the Oslo process between Israel and the PLO, which produced interim accords but no final settlement; the Colombian process with the FARC, concluded in 2016; and the South African transition from apartheid, negotiated between 1990 and 1994.

Challenges

Peace processes are vulnerable to "spoilers" who use violence to derail talks, to mistrust between parties, and to disagreements over sequencing — for example, whether armed groups should disarm before or after political reforms. Mediation by states, the United Nations, or private organisations often helps parties overcome these obstacles.
