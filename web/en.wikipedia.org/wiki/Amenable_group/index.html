Amenable group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: invariant mean diagram]

In mathematics, an amenable group is a locally compact topological group that admits a kind of invariant mean on bounded functions. Intuitively, an amenable group is one on which averaging can be performed in a way that does not depend on how the group elements are labelled.

Definition

A discrete group G is amenable if there exists a finitely additive probability measure on all subsets of G that is invariant under left multiplication. Equivalently, there is a linear functional (a "mean") on the space of bounded functions on G that is positive, normalised, and invariant under translation. The notion was introduced by John von Neumann in 1929 in his analysis of the Banach–Tarski paradox.

Examples and non-examples

All finite groups are amenable, as are all abelian groups and all solvable groups; amenability passes to subgroups, quotients, extensions, and directed unions of amenable groups. The free group on two generators is not amenable — this is precisely what allows the Banach–Tarski paradoxical decomposition of the sphere. Non-abelian free subgroups are the basic obstruction: a group containing a free subgroup on two generators cannot be amenable.

Characterisations

Amenability has many equivalent formulations, including the existence of Følner sequences (finite sets almost invariant under translation), fixed-point properties for affine actions on compact convex sets, and, for discrete groups, nuclearity of the group C*-algebra and conditions from Kesten's spectral criterion for random walks.
