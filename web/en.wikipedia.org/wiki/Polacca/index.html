Polacca

The polacca (in Italian, polacca; in Spanish, polacra) is a type of sailing vessel that was commonly used in the Mediterranean Sea from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century. It is characterised by tall masts with lateen sails forward and square sails aft, and by a hull form derived from earlier xebec and galleon designs.

Design

The polacca typically carried three masts. The foremast was raked forward and carried lateen sails, while the mainmast and mizzenmast carried square sails similar to those of a full-rigged ship. Later polaccas, sometimes called polacca-brigs, carried square sails on all masts. The hull was usually built with a long, fine bow and a relatively high sterncastle, giving the vessels a distinctive profile prized by Mediterranean merchants.

Because of their speed and weatherliness, polaccas were popular with traders, privateers and the Barbary corsairs. They could be rowed in calms on some earlier examples, though most relied entirely on sail.

History

The name is thought to derive from the Italian polacca, meaning "Polish woman", though the reason for the name is uncertain. The type reached its greatest popularity in the eighteenth century, when Venetian, Neapolitan, Catalan and Greek shipowners used polaccas extensively in the Levant trade. By the mid-nineteenth century the type had largely been superseded by the brig and the schooner.
