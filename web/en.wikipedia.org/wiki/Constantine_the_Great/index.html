Constantine the Great

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Colossal marble head of Constantine from the Capitoline Museums]

Constantine I (c. AD 272 – 22 May 337), known as Constantine the Great, was Roman emperor from 306 to 337 and the first Roman emperor to convert to Christianity. Proclaimed emperor by his troops at York on his father Constantius' death, he won the civil wars of the tetrarchy — defeating Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge (312), where Christian tradition places his vision of the cross, and Licinius at Chrysopolis (324) — to reunite the empire under one ruler. He legalised Christianity (Edict of Milan, 313), convened the Council of Nicaea (325), and founded Constantinople as a new, Christian capital, reshaping the empire and European history.

Road to sole rule

Constantine's victory in 312 was followed, in the Christian sources (Lactantius, Eusebius), by accounts of a dream or vision — 'in this sign, conquer' — and the painting of the Chi-Rho on his soldiers' shields. Historians debate what he saw and when he truly converted (he was baptised only on his deathbed, then a common practice). With Licinius he issued toleration in 313; sole rule from 324 let him shower the Church with wealth, basilicas (Old St Peter's, the Holy Sepulchre) and privilege.

Constantinople and legacy

Dedicated in 330, Constantinople ('New Rome') on the Bosporus became the empire's eastern capital and, as Byzantium's heart, endured a thousand years after Rome's fall. Constantine reformed the coinage (the solidus), the army and the administration, and his entanglement of church and empire — he summoned and presided over Nicaea's condemnation of Arianism — set the pattern of Byzantine caesaropapism. The forged 'Donation of Constantine' later underpinned papal claims. He is venerated as a saint and 'Equal to the Apostles' in Eastern Christianity.
