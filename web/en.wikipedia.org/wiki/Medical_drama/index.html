Medical drama

A medical drama is a television programme or film genre in which events centre upon a hospital, an ambulance staff, or any other medical environment. In the United States, most medical dramas are approximately an hour long and set in a hospital. The genre combines patient-of-the-week storylines with serialised plots about the professional and personal lives of doctors, nurses, and other medical staff, and frequently addresses ethical dilemmas and social issues through its medical cases.


The emergency department is the characteristic setting of the modern medical drama.

History

The genre emerged in the 1950s and 1960s with series such as Medic (1954), Dr. Kildare (1961), and Ben Casey (1961), which idealised the medical profession. The 1970s brought Marcus Welby, M.D. and the more socially conscious St. Elsewhere (1982–1988), praised for its complex serial storytelling. The genre reached a commercial peak in the 1990s with ER (1994–2009), whose fast-paced, technically dense depiction of an emergency room reshaped the form, and Chicago Hope. In the 2000s, Grey's Anatomy (2005– ) emphasised the romantic and personal lives of surgical interns, while House (2004–2012) recast the medical mystery as a detective story centred on a brilliant diagnostician. Other notable series include Scrubs, a medical comedy-drama, the British series Casualty and Holby City, and later successes such as The Good Doctor.

Conventions

Typical conventions include a case-of-the-week structure, ensemble casts, storylines about hospital politics and resource constraints, romantic subplots among staff, and episodes engaging with topical issues such as epidemics, organ transplantation, end-of-life care, and medical error. Medical dramas are regularly advised by practising clinicians for technical accuracy, and studies have examined their influence on public perceptions of medicine, resuscitation, and organ donation.
