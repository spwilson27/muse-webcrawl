Lyssavirus

Lyssavirus
Family: Rhabdoviridae
Genome: Negative-sense single-stranded RNA
Hosts: Mammals, especially bats
Type species: Rabies lyssavirus

Lyssavirus is a genus of RNA viruses in the family Rhabdoviridae. Lyssaviruses infect mammals and cause rabies-like encephalitis; the best known is rabies virus (Rabies lyssavirus), which causes virtually all human rabies cases. The genus name derives from Lyssa, the Greek goddess of madness and rage.

Taxonomy and species

The genus includes more than 17 recognized species, among them rabies virus, Lagos bat virus, Mokola virus, Duvenhage virus, European bat lyssaviruses 1 and 2, Australian bat lyssavirus, Irkut virus, Aravan virus, Khujand virus, Bokeloh bat lyssavirus, Ikoma lyssavirus, and West Caucasian bat virus. Most are associated with bats, the presumed ancestral hosts.

Structure and genome

Virions are bullet-shaped, about 180 nm long, with a negative-sense single-stranded RNA genome of roughly 12 kilobases encoding five proteins: nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), glycoprotein (G), and the RNA polymerase (L). The glycoprotein mediates entry into neurons and is the target of rabies vaccines and immunoglobulin.

Disease

After transmission, usually by a bite, the virus travels along peripheral nerves to the central nervous system, causing fatal encephalomyelitis once symptoms appear. Post-exposure prophylaxis with vaccine and rabies immunoglobulin is highly effective if given before symptoms begin.
