Neckerchief

A neckerchief is a type of neckwear associated with Scouts, and historically with sailors, cowboys, and working people. It consists of a triangular or square-folded cloth worn around the neck, tied or fastened with a slide or ring known as a woggle. In Scouting it is a distinctive part of the uniform, often coloured to identify the wearer's group or troop.

History

Neckerchiefs were worn by military forces as early as the 17th century, and Croatian mercenaries are credited with popularising the cravat in France. Sailors adopted the neckerchief as practical dress, and in the American West the bandana served cowboys as protection against dust and sun. Robert Baden-Powell adopted the neckerchief for the Scout movement in the early 20th century, valuing it as a practical garment that could serve as a bandage, sling, or signalling cloth in an emergency.

Scouting use

In the Scout uniform, the neckerchief is worn rolled or folded and secured with a woggle (slide). Colours and patterns vary by group, country, and event; commemorative neckerchiefs are issued for jamborees and are widely exchanged between Scouts as souvenirs. On Gilwell Park training courses, leaders completing Wood Badge training receive a distinctive neckerchief with a Maclaren tartan backing, together with wooden beads.

Other uses

Neckerchiefs remain part of some naval uniforms, are worn as a fashion accessory, and appear in outdoor and agricultural clothing as functional protection for the neck. The similar neckerchief of the French and other European scout movements is likewise a symbol of membership.
