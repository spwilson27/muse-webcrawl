Bayonet Constitution

The Bayonet Constitution is the nickname given to the Constitution of 1887 of the Hawaiian Kingdom, promulgated by King Kalākaua under threat of force on 6 July 1887. Drafted by reformers led by Interior Minister Lorrin A. Thurston and backed by the armed militia known as the Honolulu Rifles, the constitution severely curtailed the power of the Hawaiian monarchy and disenfranchised many Native Hawaiian and Asian subjects.

Background

During the 1880s, a group of American and European businessmen and politicians, organised as the Hawaiian League, agitated for constitutional reform and closer ties with the United States. After an armed demonstration in June–July 1887, Kalākaua was compelled to dismiss his cabinet, appoint a reform cabinet, and sign the new constitution — hence the epithet "Bayonet Constitution."

Provisions

The constitution made the cabinet responsible to the legislature rather than the monarch, reduced the king largely to a figurehead, and allowed him only a suspensive veto. It imposed income and property qualifications on voters for the upper house (the House of Nobles), whose members were thereafter elected rather than appointed. Suffrage provisions extended voting rights to resident non-citizen men of American or European descent while excluding Asian residents entirely, and the constitution facilitated the eventual extension of American influence, including the renewal of the Reciprocity Treaty with exclusive U.S. rights to Pearl Harbor.

Legacy

The Bayonet Constitution is widely regarded as a pivotal step toward the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy in 1893 and annexation by the United States in 1898. Queen Liliʻuokalani sought to replace it with a new constitution restoring royal authority and Native Hawaiian suffrage, a move that precipitated her overthrow.
