Ennis Friary

Ennis Friary
Location: Ennis, County Clare, Ireland
Founded: c. 1240s
Order: Franciscan (Observantine)
Patrons: O'Brien dynasty, kings of Thomond

Ennis Friary is a ruined Franciscan friary in the town of Ennis, County Clare, Ireland. Founded in the thirteenth century under the patronage of the O'Brien family, kings of Thomond, it became one of the most important Franciscan houses in Ireland, noted for its fine carved stonework, its large east window, and the royal tombs of the O'Briens within its chancel.

History

Tradition dates the foundation to about 1240–1250, when Donnchadh Ó Briain granted the site to the Franciscans. The friary grew wealthy on O'Brien patronage and became a noted centre of learning, at times housing several hundred friars and students. The church was substantially rebuilt in the fifteenth century. After the dissolution of the monasteries under Henry VIII it was granted away, but friars continued to occupy parts of the site intermittently until the mid-seventeenth century. The nave later served as a Church of Ireland parish church until the 1870s.

Architecture and carvings

The church consists of a long nave and chancel divided by a slender bell tower typical of Irish mendicant houses. Its most celebrated features are the fifteenth-century stone carvings: panels on the MacMahon tomb depicting the apostles, a figure of St Francis showing the stigmata, and the ornate canopy tomb of the O'Briens in the chancel. The great east window's tracery survives, as do cloister fragments.

Today

The friary is a National Monument in state care, managed by the Office of Public Works, and is open to visitors. Its position in the centre of Ennis makes it one of the town's principal historic landmarks.
