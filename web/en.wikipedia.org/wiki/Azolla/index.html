Azolla is a genus of seven species of small aquatic ferns in the family Salviniaceae, commonly known as mosquito ferns or water ferns. The plants float on the surface of still and slow-moving fresh water, forming dense mats of tiny overlapping scale-like leaves, typically 1–2 cm across per plant. Azolla is remarkable for its symbiosis with the nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium Anabaena azollae, which lives in cavities in the fern's leaves and supplies the plant with fixed nitrogen — a partnership that has made Azolla one of the most productive and agriculturally useful aquatic plants.

Description and life cycle

Each plant consists of a branching stem with bilobed leaves and thread-like roots hanging into the water. The dorsal leaf lobe contains the symbiotic cyanobacterium, which is passed to successive generations through the fern's reproductive structures — a rare case of vertical inheritance of a photosynthetic symbiont. Azolla reproduces rapidly by fragmentation, doubling its biomass in as little as a few days under favourable conditions, and also sexually via sporocarps. Under stress or in autumn, anthocyanin pigments turn the mats red.

The Azolla event

Around 49 million years ago, during the Eocene, enormous blooms of Azolla in the then-freshwater Arctic Ocean are hypothesised to have drawn down vast quantities of atmospheric carbon dioxide as the plants sank and were buried in anoxic sediments. This "Azolla event" is proposed by some researchers to have contributed to the transition from the Eocene hothouse climate to the cooler "icehouse" world of the later Cenozoic.

Agricultural use

Azolla has been used for centuries in China and Vietnam as a green manure in rice paddies: grown alongside or before the rice, it fixes nitrogen (tens of kilograms per hectare per crop), suppresses weeds by shading the water, and reduces mosquito breeding by covering the surface — hence the name "mosquito fern". It is also used as a protein-rich feed supplement for poultry, pigs, cattle and fish, as a compost ingredient, and experimentally for phytoremediation of wastewater and for biogas production. In some regions, introduced species such as Azolla filiculoides have become invasive weeds of dams and slow rivers.

Species

Recognised species include A. caroliniana, A. filiculoides, A. mexicana, A. microphylla, A. nilotica, A. pinnata and A. rubra, distributed across the Americas, Africa, Asia and Australasia.
