Heian-kyō (Japanese: 平安京, literally "capital of peace and tranquillity") was one of several former names for the city now known as Kyoto, and the capital of Japan for over a millennium. It was founded in 794 by Emperor Kanmu, who moved the court from Nagaoka-kyō, and gave its name to the Heian period (794–1185) of Japanese history.

Layout

The city was laid out on a rectangular grid modelled on Chang'an, the capital of Tang-dynasty China, divided by the broad Suzaku Avenue running south from the Imperial Palace (Daidairi) at the northern centre to the Rajōmon gate. The eastern half (Sakyō) and western half (Ukyō) were mirror images; in practice the marshy western half declined early while the eastern half prospered. Temples Tō-ji and Sai-ji guarded the southern entrance.

History

Under the Fujiwara regents, Heian-kyō became the refined court culture that produced The Tale of Genji and the Pillow Book. Although political power shifted to the Kamakura (1185) and later shogunates, the emperor continued to reside in Kyoto, and the city remained Japan's cultural heart through the Muromachi and Edo periods, surviving the devastation of the Ōnin War (1467–77) and repeated fires. It remained the imperial capital until 1868, when the court moved to Tokyo in the Meiji Restoration.

Legacy

Modern Kyoto preserves much of the ancient grid south of the old palace grounds; the present Kyoto Imperial Palace stands on a later site to the northeast. The city's temples, shrines, and gardens anchor its status as Japan's foremost historic city, protected in part because it was spared bombing in the Second World War.
