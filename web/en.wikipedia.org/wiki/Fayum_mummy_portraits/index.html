Fayum mummy portraits

Fayum mummy portraits (also spelled Faiyum mummy portraits) are a type of naturalistic painted portrait on wooden boards attached to upper-class mummies from Roman Egypt. They belong to the tradition of panel painting, one of the most highly regarded forms of art in the classical world, and are among the only large bodies of such painting to survive. About 900 are known, mostly recovered from the Faiyum Oasis necropolises, including Hawara, er-Rubayat, and Antinoopolis.

Description

The portraits date principally from the 1st century BC to the 3rd century AD. They depict the head, or head and upper chest, of a single person viewed frontally, painted in encaustic (pigment mixed with hot beeswax) or tempera on thin wooden panels, which were then bound into the linen wrappings over the face of the mummy. The sitters are typically shown in the prime of life, formally dressed and wearing jewellery, with hairstyles and fashions that closely track contemporary Roman styles and therefore aid dating. Inscriptions in Greek sometimes record the name and age of the deceased.

Significance

The portraits are a striking fusion of traditions: Egyptian funerary practice, Greek portraiture, and Roman realism, produced for a wealthy, ethnically mixed population. Their immediacy and individuality made them famous when the first major finds reached Europe in the 1880s, through excavations by Flinders Petrie at Hawara and the collection of Theodor Graf. Today they are dispersed among museums worldwide, including the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, the British Museum, the Louvre, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and they remain the single most important source for what ancient panel painting looked like.
