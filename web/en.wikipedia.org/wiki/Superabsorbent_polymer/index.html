Superabsorbent polymer

A few grams of superabsorbent polymer can absorb hundreds of times their weight in water.

A superabsorbent polymer (SAP) (also called slush powder) is a water-absorbing polymer that can absorb and retain extremely large amounts of a liquid relative to its own mass. Water-absorbing polymers, classified as hydrogels when cross-linked, absorb aqueous solutions through hydrogen bonding with water molecules. An SAP's ability to absorb water depends on the ionic concentration of the aqueous solution: in deionized water an SAP may absorb 300 times its weight or more, while in saline solutions the capacity falls substantially because dissolved cations screen the polymer's charged groups.

Chemistry

Most commercial SAPs are cross-linked sodium polyacrylates — copolymers of acrylic acid and sodium hydroxide, polymerised in the presence of a cross-linker. The sodium ions inside the network draw water in by osmosis, while the cross-links prevent the polymer from dissolving, so the material swells into a gel instead. Other SAPs are based on polyacrylamide copolymers, ethylene maleic anhydride copolymers, and grafted starch or cellulose backbones.

History

Superabsorbent materials were first developed by the United States Department of Agriculture in the 1960s from starch-graft copolymers ("Super Slurper"). Commercial production for hygiene products began in Japan and the United States in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and SAPs transformed the design of disposable diapers, which became thinner as fluff pulp was replaced by polymer cores.

Uses

  • Disposable diapers, training pants, and adult incontinence products
  • Feminine hygiene products
  • Water-retaining granules for agriculture and horticulture
  • Artificial snow for motion pictures and displays
  • Spill control, cable water-blocking tapes, and gel packs
  • Wound dressings and medical waste solidification
