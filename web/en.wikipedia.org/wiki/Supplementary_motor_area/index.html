The supplementary motor area (SMA) is a part of the cerebral cortex of primates that contributes to the control of movement. It is located on the midline surface of the hemisphere just in front of (anterior to) the primary motor cortex leg representation. In monkeys the SMA contains a rough map of the body. In humans the SMA is part of Brodmann area 6. Because most of its area lies in the banks of the paracentral sulcus, the SMA is not generally visible on the lateral surface of the brain.

The SMA was first described by Wilder Penfield and Keasley Welch in 1951. Its role has been confirmed by more recent research using techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electrophysiology.

Subregions

The SMA is commonly divided into two subregions. The SMA proper lies caudally, closer to the primary motor cortex, and is involved in the execution and coordination of movement, particularly bilateral and sequential movements. The pre-SMA lies rostrally and is more involved in higher-level aspects of motor control such as response inhibition, task switching, and the planning of movements.

Function

The supplementary motor area is active during the planning of complex movements, especially movements that are internally generated rather than triggered by external stimuli. It plays a role in the control of movements that are bilateral, in the control of postural stability, and in the internal generation of sequences of movements. Research has also implicated the SMA in timing and rhythm, in language production, and in some aspects of music processing. Damage to the SMA can produce the supplementary motor area syndrome, characterized by a reduction in spontaneous movement and speech.

Connections

The SMA is reciprocally connected with the primary motor cortex and projects directly to the brainstem and spinal cord. It also receives input from the basal ganglia via the motor circuit, and from the premotor cortex and cingulate cortex.
