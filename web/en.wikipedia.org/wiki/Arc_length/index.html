Arc length

Arc length is the distance along a curve between two of its points. Determining the length of an irregular arc segment is called rectification of the curve. The advent of infinitesimal calculus led to a general formula that works in many cases.

Definition and formula

The length of a curve can be approximated by dividing it into many small straight segments and summing their lengths; in the limit, for a smooth curve given parametrically by (x(t), y(t)), the length is the integral of the speed: L = ∫ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt. For a function y = f(x) this becomes L = ∫ √(1 + (f′(x))²) dx. For a circle of radius r, an arc subtending angle θ (radians) has length rθ, and a full circumference is 2πr.

History

Ancient geometers such as Archimedes approximated curved lengths with polygons. Exact rectifications were rare before the 17th century, when the length of the semicubical parabola (rectified by William Neile in 1657), the cycloid, and the logarithmic spiral were found. Many familiar curves, such as ellipses, have arc lengths given by elliptic integrals with no elementary closed form — a fact that motivated much of 18th- and 19th-century analysis. Curves whose length is infinite in a bounded region, such as fractals, are called non-rectifiable. See also arc (geometry) and elliptic integral.
