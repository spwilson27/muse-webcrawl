Black powder, also known as gunpowder, is the earliest known chemical explosive. It consists of a mixture of sulfur, carbon (in the form of charcoal) and potassium nitrate (saltpetre). The sulfur and charcoal act as fuels while the saltpetre is the oxidiser. Invented in China during the Tang dynasty, it was the only known chemical explosive until the mid-19th century.

History

Chinese alchemists searching for an elixir of life discovered the mixture's explosive properties by the 9th century; the first written formula appears in the Wujing Zongyao of 1044. Gunpowder weapons — fire lances, bombs, rockets and eventually guns and cannon — spread westward along the Silk Road and through the Islamic world to Europe by the 13th century, transforming warfare and state formation worldwide.

Properties and manufacture

Traditional proportions are about 75% saltpetre, 15% charcoal and 10% sulfur by weight. Black powder is classified as a low explosive: it deflagrates rather than detonates, producing a dense white smoke and substantial solid residue, which fouls barrels and corrodes metal. Manufacturing historically involved grinding and "corning" the ingredients into grains graded by size for cannon, muskets, blasting or fireworks.

Uses today

Superseded as a propellant by smokeless powder from the 1880s, black powder remains in use in fireworks, model rockets, theatrical effects, quarrying, time fuses and by enthusiasts shooting historical muzzle-loading firearms.
