The Darien scheme was an unsuccessful attempt, backed largely by investors of the Kingdom of Scotland, to gain wealth and influence by colonising the isthmus of Panama and establishing an entrepôt for trade between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans. The scheme, promoted by the banker William Paterson through the Company of Scotland, drew in a huge share of Scotland's liquid capital — perhaps a quarter to a half of all money in circulation — and its catastrophic failure in 1698–1700 was a major factor in pushing Scotland toward the Union with England in 1707.

The Company of Scotland

The Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies was chartered by the Scots Parliament in 1695 with the power to establish colonies in unclaimed territories. When English investors were forced to withdraw under pressure from the English Parliament and the East India Company, the Scots raised the capital at home in a wave of patriotic enthusiasm.

The expeditions

The first expedition of five ships and about 1,200 settlers sailed from Leith in July 1698 and founded the colony of New Caledonia at Darién, building Fort St Andrew. The site proved disastrous: the land was swampy and disease-ridden, supplies failed, the Spanish — who claimed the territory — attacked, and English colonies were forbidden to help. Of roughly 2,500 settlers sent out across two expeditions, only a few hundred survived; the colony was abandoned in April 1700.

Consequences

The loss ruined thousands of Scottish investors and caused bitter resentment against England, whose government had undermined the venture. Under the terms of the Acts of Union 1707, Scotland received a payment known as the Equivalent, part of which compensated Darien investors with interest — a provision that helped secure support for the Union.
