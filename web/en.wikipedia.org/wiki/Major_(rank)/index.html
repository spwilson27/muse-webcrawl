Major is a senior military officer rank used in many countries. It is the lowest of the field-grade (senior) officer ranks, ranking above captain and below lieutenant colonel. Under the NATO standardisation system it corresponds to grade OF-3. The word derives from the Latin maior, "greater", and entered military use through titles such as sergeant major and major general.

History

The rank emerged in the 16th and 17th centuries, when regiments had three senior officers — colonel, lieutenant colonel and major — the major originally serving as the regimental chief of staff and officer responsible for drill, training and administration. In the Germanic tradition the rank is Major; in French it is commandant or chef de bataillon/escadron; in Spanish-speaking armies mayor or comandante.

Role

A major typically commands a sub-unit of company, squadron or battery size (roughly 100–250 personnel) where company-grade command has passed beyond captain, or serves as a staff officer — often as the operations officer (S-3/G-3) or executive officer of a battalion or regiment. In the British Army, where companies are normally commanded by majors, the rank is the first at which an officer is considered for command of a battalion-sized unit later in their career. In the United States Army, majors also serve as staff officers at brigade and division level and in specialist roles such as chaplains and medical officers entering service at that grade.

Insignia

In the United States, a major wears a gold oak leaf on the shoulder. In the British Army and many Commonwealth forces, the insignia is a single crown. Many European armies use one or more stars or bars on shoulder straps, and the air force equivalent in the Royal Air Force is squadron leader.
