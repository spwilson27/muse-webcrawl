Japanese culture

[Image: Mount Fuji and cherry blossoms]

Japanese culture has evolved over thousands of years, from the country's prehistoric Jōmon period to its contemporary hybrid of traditional customs and modern popular culture. It has been shaped by the native Shinto religion, by Buddhism imported from the Asian mainland, by centuries of relative isolation, and by the rapid modernisation that began in the Meiji era.

Language and writing

Japanese is spoken by almost the entire population of Japan and by diaspora communities worldwide. It is written using three scripts: the logographic kanji, adopted from Chinese, and the two syllabaries hiragana and katakana. The language features distinct levels of politeness and honorific speech (keigo) that reflect social relationships.

Arts

Japanese art forms include painting, calligraphy, ceramics, and printmaking. Ukiyo-e woodblock prints of the Edo period influenced Western art and remain among the country's best-known exports. Traditional theatre includes Noh, with its masked performers and slow, formal movement, and the more flamboyant Kabuki, while bunraku presents plays through large puppets.

Religion and customs

Shinto and Buddhism coexist and are often practised by the same individuals, with Shinto rites marking birth and marriage and Buddhist rites associated with death. Seasonal festivals (matsuri) punctuate the year, and concepts such as wa (harmony) and the appreciation of seasonal change, celebrated in cherry blossom viewing (hanami), remain central to social life.

Popular culture

Modern Japanese popular culture — anime, manga, video games, and fashion — has become a global phenomenon. The government has promoted "Cool Japan" as a form of soft power, and Japanese cuisine, including sushi, ramen, and washoku, was inscribed by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2013.
