Generative grammar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Generative grammar is a research tradition in linguistics that regards grammar as a system of rules which generates exactly those combinations of words that form grammatical sentences in a given language. The term is especially associated with the work of Noam Chomsky, who launched the approach with his 1957 monograph Syntactic Structures and transformed linguistics by treating language as a cognitive, ultimately biological, capacity of the human mind.

Core ideas

A generative grammar is an explicit, formal model: a finite set of rules that enumerates ("generates") the infinite set of well-formed sentences of a language and assigns each a structural description. The theory distinguishes a speaker's competence — the internalised knowledge of language — from performance, its actual use. Because children acquire this system rapidly from limited and imperfect input (the "poverty of the stimulus" argument), generative linguists posit an innate Universal Grammar: a genetically endowed initial state of the language faculty that constrains the grammars children can construct.

Development

Early transformational grammar (1957–1960s) combined phrase-structure rules with transformations mapping deep structures to surface structures. The Standard Theory of Aspects of the Theory of Syntax (1965) added a semantic component. Later frameworks included:

  • Government and Binding Theory (1981) — a modular system of principles (X-bar theory, case theory, binding theory) with language-specific parameters, the "Principles and Parameters" approach;
  • The Minimalist Program (1995– ) — seeking the simplest design for the language faculty, built around the operation Merge;
  • Rival generative frameworks such as Lexical Functional Grammar, Head-Driven Phrase Structure Grammar, and Generalized Phrase Structure Grammar, which dispense with transformations.

Criticism and influence

Generative grammar dominated theoretical linguistics in the second half of the twentieth century and influenced philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and psychology. It has been criticised by proponents of usage-based and cognitive linguistics, who argue that language is learned from experience without domain-specific innate structure, and by corpus linguists who question the reliance on native-speaker intuition. The "linguistics wars" of the late 1960s and 1970s saw bitter disputes between Chomsky's followers and advocates of generative semantics. The field's central questions — what knowledge of language consists in, and how much of it is innate — remain actively debated.
