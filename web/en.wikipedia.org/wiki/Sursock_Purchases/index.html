Sursock Purchases

The Sursock Purchases were a series of land purchases in Ottoman and Mandate Palestine by the Sursock family, a Greek Orthodox Christian banking and merchant family from Beirut, and their subsequent sales to Jewish organizations, most notably the Jewish National Fund. Between the 1870s and the 1920s the Sursocks acquired large tracts of land, concentrated in the fertile Jezreel Valley (Marj Ibn Amer), and sold some 240,000 dunams (about 240 km²) to Zionist purchasers. The 1920–1925 sales of the Jezreel Valley lands were among the largest single land transfers of the Mandate period and displaced Arab tenant farmers (fellaheen), becoming a central episode in debates over land and displacement in Palestine.

Background

The Sursock family acquired much of the Jezreel Valley in 1872 from the Ottoman authorities for a modest sum, and expanded their holdings in the following decades, collecting rents from Arab tenant farmers who worked the villages of the valley. Under the British Mandate after the First World War, the family — resident in Beirut and Alexandria — decided to sell their Palestinian estates.

The sales

Negotiations with Zionist organizations culminated in sales between 1920 and 1925 of the valley lands, including the sites of numerous Arab villages. Settlers founded kibbutzim and moshavim such as Nahalal and Kfar Yehoshua on the purchased land. The eviction of tenant farmers, sometimes resisted and compensated under Mandate land ordinances, generated grievances examined by commissions including the Shaw Commission (1929) and the Hope Simpson Report (1930), and the purchases remain a frequently cited case in the historiography of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict.
