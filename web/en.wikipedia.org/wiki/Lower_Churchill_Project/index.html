The Lower Churchill Project is a hydroelectric development on the lower Churchill River in Labrador, Canada, undertaken by the provincial Crown corporation Nalcor Energy. Its first phase, the Muskrat Falls generating station (824 MW) with associated transmission links to the island of Newfoundland and to Nova Scotia, was sanctioned in 2012 and completed in the early 2020s amid major cost overruns and controversy. A second, larger phase at Gull Island (about 2,250 MW) has been studied for decades but not built.

Background

The Churchill River's hydroelectric potential is among the largest undeveloped resources in North America. The Upper Churchill was developed in the 1960s–70s as the Churchill Falls project (5,428 MW) under a 1969 contract selling power to Hydro-Québec at fixed low prices until 2041 — a lasting grievance in Newfoundland and Labrador that shaped provincial determination to develop the Lower Churchill on different terms. Sites at Gull Island and Muskrat Falls, downstream of Churchill Falls, offer a combined potential of more than 3,000 MW.

Muskrat Falls

The sanctioned project comprised the 824 MW Muskrat Falls station, the Labrador–Island Link (a subsea HVDC crossing of the Strait of Belle Isle) and the Maritime Link to Nova Scotia, built with Emera Inc. Originally estimated at $6.2 billion (excluding financing), costs escalated to roughly $13 billion including financing, prompting a provincial public inquiry (the Muskrat Falls Inquiry, reporting in 2020) that found the project had been based on underestimated costs and suppressed risk analysis. First power was achieved in 2020–2021, and the Labrador–Island Link was commissioned after software problems with the HVDC converter systems were resolved. Electricity rate mitigation agreements between Ottawa and the province were concluded to shield Newfoundland ratepayers.

The project also raised environmental and Indigenous concerns: Innu, Inuit (Nunatsiavut) and NunatuKavut groups and downstream residents protested over methylmercury contamination of fish in the Lake Melville food web from reservoir flooding, leading to agreements on clearing, monitoring and compensation. In December 2024, Newfoundland and Labrador and Quebec announced a draft agreement in principle to replace the 1969 Churchill Falls contract and jointly develop Gull Island, potentially transforming the project's second phase.
