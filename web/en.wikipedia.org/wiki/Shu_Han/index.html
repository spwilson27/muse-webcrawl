Shu Han (Chinese: 蜀漢; pinyin: Shǔ Hàn), often referred to simply as Shu, was one of the three major states that competed for supremacy over China during the Three Kingdoms period (220–280 AD), alongside Cao Wei and Eastern Wu. The state was based in the Sichuan Basin — the territory of the ancient state of Shu — with its capital at Chengdu, in present-day Sichuan.

Shu Han was founded in 221 by the warlord Liu Bei, a distant descendant of the Han imperial clan, who claimed to be continuing the Han dynasty after Cao Pi forced the abdication of Emperor Xian and founded Wei in 220. Liu Bei's claim to legitimacy gave the state its dynastic name "Han"; the prefix "Shu" (after its geographic base) was applied by later historians to distinguish it from other states named Han.

History

Liu Bei seized Yi Province from Liu Zhang in 214 and took Hanzhong from Cao Cao in 219, reaching the peak of his power. In the same year, however, his general Guan Yu lost Jing Province to Sun Quan's Wu forces and was executed, a blow from which Shu never fully recovered. Liu Bei launched a punitive campaign against Wu but was decisively defeated at the Battle of Xiaoting (Yiling) in 222 and died at Baidicheng in 223.

Regency passed to the chancellor Zhuge Liang, who re-allied with Wu, pacified the southern Nanzhong region in 225, and from 228 launched five Northern Expeditions against Wei in an attempt to restore the Han. All failed to achieve decisive gains, and Zhuge Liang died on campaign in 234. His successors Jiang Wan and Fei Yi maintained a more defensive posture; after Fei Yi's assassination in 253, the general Jiang Wei resumed large-scale northern campaigns, which drained the state's limited resources.

At its height Shu could field perhaps 100,000 soldiers and register a population of under one million households — far smaller than Wei. In 263 the Wei general Deng Ai marched through the Yinping passes into the Sichuan plain and appeared before Chengdu; the second and last emperor, Liu Shan, surrendered, ending Shu Han after 42 years.

Government and society

Shu Han's administration under Zhuge Liang was noted for strict, impartial law enforcement and relatively competent governance despite the state's small resource base. The economy relied on Sichuan's agricultural wealth, the state salt and iron monopolies, and the export of Shu brocade, which helped finance the military. The state's chronic manpower shortage and the logistical difficulty of crossing the Qinling Mountains into Wei territory were persistent strategic constraints.

Legacy

The history of Shu Han is recorded in Chen Shou's Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi). The 14th-century novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms cast Liu Bei and Zhuge Liang as paragons of benevolence and loyalty, shaping the enduring popular image of Shu Han as the righteous underdog of the era — a portrayal modern historians treat with considerably more caution.
