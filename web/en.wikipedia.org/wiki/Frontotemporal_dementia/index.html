Frontotemporal dementia (FTD), also called frontotemporal degeneration, is a group of brain disorders caused by progressive nerve cell loss in the brain's frontal lobes (the areas behind the forehead) or temporal lobes (the regions behind the ears) — collectively termed frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD). Signs and symptoms vary, depending on the portion of the brain affected, and commonly include marked changes in personality, behaviour and language, while memory is often relatively preserved early in the course of the disease.

FTD is the most common form of dementia to occur before the age of 60, with onset typically between 45 and 65 years. It is estimated to account for roughly 10–20% of dementia cases.

Types

The three main clinical subtypes are behavioural variant FTD (bvFTD), characterised by changes in personality, social behaviour and inhibition; semantic variant primary progressive aphasia, involving loss of word meaning; and nonfluent/agrammatic variant primary progressive aphasia, involving effortful, halting speech. Related conditions include FTD with motor neuron disease (FTD–ALS), corticobasal syndrome and progressive supranuclear palsy.

Causes

FTD results from abnormal accumulation of proteins in brain cells, most commonly tau or TDP-43. A substantial minority of cases are hereditary, with mutations in the MAPT, GRN (progranulin) and C9orf72 genes being the most common known causes.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical pattern, neuropsychological testing, and neuroimaging (MRI or PET) showing frontal and/or temporal atrophy or hypometabolism; genetic testing may be offered. There is currently no disease-modifying treatment; management is symptomatic and supportive, sometimes including antidepressants such as SSRIs for behavioural symptoms, speech therapy, and carer support.
