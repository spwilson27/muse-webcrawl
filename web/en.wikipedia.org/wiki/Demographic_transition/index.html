Demographic transition

The demographic transition model (DTM) describes the historical shift of populations from high birth rates and high death rates in societies with minimal technology, education (especially of women), and economic development, to low birth rates and low death rates in societies with advanced technology, education, and economic development, as well as the stages between these extremes. The theory was first developed by the American demographer Warren Thompson in 1929 and further elaborated by Frank W. Notestein in the 1940s, drawing on the experience of Europe and North America.

Stages

The classical model has four stages. In stage one, pre-industrial societies have both high birth and death rates, so population growth is slow and fluctuating. In stage two, improvements in food supply, sanitation, and medicine reduce death rates, especially among children, while birth rates remain high, producing rapid population growth. In stage three, birth rates fall — because of contraception, urbanization, rising wages, and the education and employment of women — and growth slows. In stage four, both rates are low and the population is large but stable or slowly growing. Some demographers add a stage five, in which fertility falls below replacement level and the population ages and begins to decline, as observed in parts of Europe and East Asia.

Effects

Because death rates fall before birth rates, the transition is accompanied by a population explosion and dramatic changes in age structure. A temporary bulge of working-age people relative to dependents — the demographic dividend — can support economic growth, as argued for the East Asian "tiger" economies. Later stages bring population aging, with consequences for pensions, healthcare, and labor markets.

Criticism

The model is a generalization based on the European experience and does not fit all countries: mortality declines in many developing countries were driven by imported medical technology rather than local economic development, birth rate declines have varied in timing and cause, and migration — ignored by the model — significantly affects population change. Nevertheless, it remains a standard framework in demography and geography teaching.
