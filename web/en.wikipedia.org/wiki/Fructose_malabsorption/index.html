Fructose malabsorption

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fructose is absorbed across the intestinal wall mainly via the GLUT5 transporter.

Fructose malabsorption, formerly named dietary fructose intolerance (DFI), is a digestive disorder in which absorption of fructose is impaired by deficient fructose carriers in the small intestine's enterocytes. This results in an increased concentration of fructose in the entire intestine, where it is fermented by gut bacteria, producing gases and drawing water into the lumen by osmosis.

Fructose malabsorption should not be confused with hereditary fructose intolerance, a potentially fatal condition caused by a deficiency of liver enzymes that metabolise fructose.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically resemble those of irritable bowel syndrome and lactose intolerance, and include:

  • Bloating and abdominal distension
  • Abdominal pain and cramping
  • Flatulence
  • Diarrhoea or, less commonly, constipation
  • Nausea and, in some cases, vomiting
  • Reflux and belching

Symptoms usually begin 30 minutes to several hours after eating foods containing fructose, especially when fructose is present in excess of glucose, as in apples, pears, honey, agave syrup and high-fructose corn syrup.

Pathophysiology

Fructose is absorbed in the small intestine primarily by the facilitative transporter GLUT5, a capacity-limited pathway. When the fructose load exceeds absorptive capacity — or when GLUT5 expression is low — unabsorbed fructose passes into the colon, where bacteria ferment it to hydrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and short-chain fatty acids. The unabsorbed sugar also exerts an osmotic effect, increasing the water content of the bowel. Co-ingested glucose improves fructose absorption, which is why foods with a roughly equal glucose-to-fructose ratio (such as table sugar, sucrose) are often better tolerated than foods with excess free fructose.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually made with a hydrogen breath test: after an overnight fast, the patient drinks a measured fructose solution (commonly 25 g), and breath hydrogen is sampled at intervals. A rise of more than about 20 ppm over baseline indicates malabsorption. The test's clinical utility is debated, because a substantial proportion of healthy people malabsorb a 25 g dose without symptoms.

Management

There is no cure; management is dietary. Patients reduce intake of foods with excess free fructose and of fructans, and may benefit from the guidance used in the low-FODMAP diet developed at Monash University. Glucose taken with fructose-containing foods can improve tolerance. Enzyme products based on xylose isomerase, which converts fructose to glucose in the gut, are marketed in some countries.
