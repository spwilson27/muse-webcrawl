Trivers–Willard hypothesis

In evolutionary biology, the Trivers–Willard hypothesis, proposed by Robert Trivers and Dan Willard in 1973, suggests that female mammals in good physical condition who can invest more in their offspring should bias the sex ratio of their offspring toward males, while females in poor condition should bias their offspring toward females.

Logic of the hypothesis

The reasoning rests on three assumptions: (1) offspring condition at the end of parental investment tends to correlate with the mother's condition during investment; (2) offspring condition endures into adulthood; and (3) being in good condition confers a greater reproductive advantage on males than on females, because a high-quality male can out-reproduce any female by mating multiply, whereas a low-quality male may fail to reproduce at all. A female in poor condition passes on a greater expected number of grandchildren through a daughter, whose reproduction is less variable, than through a son likely to be outcompeted.

Evidence

The hypothesis was originally supported by studies of red deer on the Isle of Rum, where dominant females produced more sons that went on to breed successfully. Further tests in ungulates, rodents, primates and birds have produced mixed results; meta-analyses have found small and heterogeneous effects, and the precise mechanisms — which may act at conception, during gestation through selective resorption or abortion, or after birth through differential care — remain debated.

Extension to humans

Researchers have investigated whether the hypothesis applies to humans, for example examining whether high-status or wealthy families produce more sons. Results have been inconsistent, and effect sizes, where found, are small. Related phenomena studied under this umbrella include sex-ratio shifts after wars, famines and natural disasters.
