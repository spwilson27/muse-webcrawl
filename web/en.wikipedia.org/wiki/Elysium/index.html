Elysium

Elysium (Ancient Greek: Ἠλύσιον πεδίον, Ēlýsion pedíon), or the Elysian Fields, is a conception of the afterlife in Greek mythology and religion. Originally separate from Hades, admission was reserved for mortals related to the gods and other heroes, chosen by the gods. Later it expanded to include those chosen by the gods, the righteous, and the heroic, where they would remain after death to live a blessed and happy life.

In literature

Elysium is first described in Homer's Odyssey, where Menelaus is told by Proteus that he will be taken to the Elysian plain at the ends of the earth, where Rhadamanthus dwells and where life is easiest for men — no snow, no storm, only the gentle west wind from Oceanus. Hesiod describes the "Isles of the Blessed" where heroes of the fourth race live free from sorrow. In Virgil's Aeneid, Aeneas visits Elysium in the underworld and meets his father Anchises.

Pindar and later tradition

Pindar's Second Olympian Ode describes a single path of judgment: souls that have kept themselves free of wrongdoing three times pass to the tower of Cronus on the Isles of the Blessed. Over time, Elysium came to be viewed as a section of the underworld itself rather than a place at the edge of the world.

Cultural influence

The name survives in the Champs-Élysées in Paris, and in countless works of art, poetry, and film depicting an earthly or heavenly paradise.
