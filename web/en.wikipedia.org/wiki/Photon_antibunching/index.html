Photon antibunching

Antibunching shows photons arriving one at a time, never in pairs at zero delay.

Photon antibunching is a phenomenon in quantum optics in which a light source emits photons one at a time, such that photons are less likely to be detected in coincidence at short time intervals than for ordinary light. Formally, the second-order correlation function g(2)(τ) at zero delay is less than the value for a coherent (laser-like) beam, with g(2)(0) < g(2)(τ); for an ideal single-photon source, g(2)(0) = 0.

Explanation

Antibunching cannot be explained by classical wave theory and is a signature of the quantised nature of light. A single atom or quantum emitter must be re-excited after each emission before it can emit again, so two photons are never emitted simultaneously. By contrast, photons from thermal light exhibit bunching (g(2)(0) = 2), while coherent light shows Poissonian statistics (g(2)(0) = 1).

Experiments

The effect was first observed in the 1970s in resonance fluorescence from sodium atoms by Kimble, Dagenais, and Mandel, and later demonstrated with single molecules, quantum dots, and colour centres. Measurements use a Hanbury Brown and Twiss interferometer, splitting the light between two detectors and recording coincidences. Antibunching is the standard test used to certify single-photon sources for quantum cryptography and quantum information processing.
