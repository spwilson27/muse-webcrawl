The Community Reinvestment Act (CRA), enacted by the United States Congress in 1977, encourages depository institutions to help meet the credit needs of the communities in which they operate, including low- and moderate-income (LMI) neighbourhoods, consistent with safe and sound banking. It was passed to combat redlining — the practice of denying credit to residents of certain areas, often racially defined.

Implementation

Federal banking regulators (the Federal Reserve, FDIC, and OCC) periodically evaluate each insured bank's record of lending, investment, and services in its assessment areas and assign one of four ratings from "Outstanding" to "Substantial Noncompliance". Ratings are public and are considered when banks apply to merge, acquire, or open branches. The CRA imposes no lending quotas and does not require any particular loan to be made.

Reforms and debate

The Act was strengthened in the 1990s, when regulations shifted evaluation toward measurable lending outcomes. After the 2007–2008 financial crisis, some critics argued the CRA encouraged risky subprime lending; most studies by the Federal Reserve and independent researchers concluded CRA-covered lending played a small role in the crisis, as most subprime loans were made by institutions not subject to the Act. Regulators finalised a major modernisation rule in 2023, much of which was then contested in court and slated for rescission by subsequent rulemaking.
