Soft skills, also known as power skills, common skills, or core skills, are a set of personal attributes, personality traits, inherent social cues, and communication abilities needed for success on the job. Soft skills characterise how a person interacts with other people and complement hard skills, which are the technical abilities specific to an occupation. Commonly cited soft skills include communication, teamwork, adaptability, problem-solving, critical thinking, time management, leadership, work ethic, and emotional intelligence.

History

The term emerged in the United States military around the late 1960s and early 1970s, when training researchers distinguished job-related skills involving machines ("hard") from those involving people and paperwork ("soft"). It diffused into management and human-resources usage in the following decades. A 1918 study by Charles Riborg Mann is often cited in the training industry as early evidence that interpersonal ability dominates career success, though the popular "85% soft skills" figure attributed to such studies is contested.

Importance in employment

Employer surveys, such as those by the World Economic Forum (Future of Jobs) and LinkedIn, consistently rank communication, collaboration, and analytical thinking among the most sought-after capabilities. As automation absorbs routine technical tasks, demand for distinctly human interpersonal and cognitive skills has been argued to rise — a point emphasised in discussions of artificial intelligence and the future of work.

Assessment and development

Soft skills are harder to measure than hard skills; employers rely on interviews, situational-judgement tests, references, and probationary performance. Educators embed soft-skill development in group projects, presentations, and dedicated frameworks such as social and emotional learning in schools, while employers use coaching, mentoring, and feedback. Critics note the term's vagueness, the risk of cultural and gender bias in how "soft" behaviours are judged, and the tendency to relabel ordinary social competence as a marketable commodity.
