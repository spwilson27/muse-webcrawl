Vertigo (film)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Scottie clinging to the rooftop gutter in the opening chase, San Francisco falling away beneath him — the acrophobia that drives the film]

Vertigo is a 1958 American psychological thriller directed by Alfred Hitchcock, starring James Stewart as John 'Scottie' Ferguson, a retired detective with acrophobia hired to follow an old friend's suicidal wife Madeleine (Kim Novak) through San Francisco — and falling, catastrophically, in love. Adapted from Boileau-Narcejac's novel D'entre les morts (1954), with a score by Bernard Herrmann and title design by Saul Bass, it is Hitchcock's most personal and most analysed film.

Initially a disappointment (critics found it slow, audiences stayed away), Vertigo was withdrawn by Hitchcock and unseen for decades; its restoration-era return culminated in topping the Sight and Sound critics' poll in 2012, displacing Citizen Kane after fifty years — and placing second in 2022.

Form and themes

The contra-zoom (dolly out, zoom in) invented for Scottie's vertigo, the spirals in titles, hair, staircase and camera movement, Herrmann's circling arpeggios: the film's form is its obsession. The second half — Scottie remaking the shopgirl Judy into the dead Madeleine, unaware (as the audience, uniquely for Hitchcock, is told early) that they are the same woman — turns a thriller into cinema's bleakest study of male fantasy and control.

San Francisco

Shot on location across the city (the Mission San Juan Bautista tower, added by matte painting; Fort Point under the Golden Gate; the Legion of Honor), Vertigo is also the great San Francisco film — its geography now a pilgrimage route. Hitchcock called it his most personal picture; scholars have obliged with libraries.
