Young's modulus

Young's modulus (symbol E) is a mechanical property of solid materials that measures the tensile or compressive stiffness of a material when the force is applied lengthwise. It quantifies the relationship between tensile/compressive stress σ (force per unit area) and axial strain ε (proportional deformation) in the linear elastic region of a material, and is determined by the formula:

E = σ / ε = F L₀ / (A ΔL)

Young's modulus is named after the 19th-century British polymath Thomas Young, who also formalised the concept in terms of the length of an imaginary pillar. The property was conceptualised by Leonhard Euler in 1727, and the first experiments that used the concept of Young's modulus in its current form were performed by the Italian scientist Giordano Riccati in 1782, pre-dating Young's work by 25 years.

Definition

A solid material undergoes elastic deformation when a small load is applied to it in compression or extension. Elastic deformation is reversible, meaning the material returns to its original shape after the load is removed. At near-zero stress and strain, the stress–strain curve is linear, and the relationship between stress and strain is described by Hooke's law, which states that stress is proportional to strain. The coefficient of proportionality is Young's modulus. The higher the modulus, the more stress is needed to create the same amount of strain; an idealised rigid body would have an infinite Young's modulus. Conversely, a very soft material, such as a fluid, would deform without force and would have a Young's modulus of zero.

Not many materials are linear and elastic beyond a small amount of deformation. Young's modulus only applies to the part of the stress–strain curve in which Hooke's law is valid.

Typical values

Young's modulus can vary somewhat due to differences in sample composition and test method. The rate of deformation has the greatest impact on the data collected, especially in polymers. Approximate values include: rubber (small strain) about 0.01–0.1 GPa; low-density polyethylene about 0.11–0.86 GPa; aluminium about 69 GPa; structural steel about 200 GPa; carbon fibre reinforced plastic (unidirectional, along grain) about 181 GPa; diamond about 1050–1210 GPa, among the highest known for any material.

Units

Young's modulus has units of pressure, since it is stress divided by dimensionless strain. In SI units this is the pascal (Pa), most commonly expressed in gigapascals (GPa). In Imperial units it is expressed in pounds-force per square inch (psi).

Uses

Young's modulus enables the calculation of the change in the dimension of a bar made of an isotropic elastic material under tensile or compressive loads. For instance, it predicts how much a material sample extends under tension or shortens under compression. It is used in finite element analysis, in the design of beams, columns and shafts, and in materials selection charts, where it is often plotted against density as in the Ashby chart.

Relation to other moduli

For homogeneous isotropic materials, Young's modulus is related to the shear modulus G and bulk modulus K through Poisson's ratio ν: E = 2G(1 + ν) = 3K(1 − 2ν). These relations allow any one elastic constant to be derived from the others for isotropic solids.
