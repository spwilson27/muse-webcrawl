Nivelle offensive

The Nivelle offensive (16 April – 9 May 1917) was a Franco-British operation on the Western Front in the First World War. Named for its architect, General Robert Nivelle, commander-in-chief of the French armies, it promised a decisive breakthrough within 24–48 hours using massive artillery preparation and creeping barrages. The main assault along the Chemin des Dames ridge (the Second Battle of the Aisne) failed with very heavy casualties, triggering widespread mutinies in the French Army.

Background

Nivelle, an artillery officer whose methods had succeeded in limited counterattacks at Verdun in late 1916, replaced General Joffre in December 1916. He promised the French and British governments a war-winning rupture of the German front on the Aisne, supported by a British diversionary attack at Arras. His plans were widely discussed in advance — security was notoriously poor — and the Germans, who had captured details of the plan, withdrew in March 1917 to the prepared Hindenburg Line (Operation Alberich), shortening their front and disrupting Allied preparations; they also fortified the Chemin des Dames ridge, including its underground quarries, in depth.

The offensive

The British attack at Arras began on 9 April with notable initial success, including the Canadian Corps' capture of Vimy Ridge. The main French assault went in on 16 April along a broad front on the Aisne. Despite over a million shells in the preliminary bombardment, German defences in depth, machine guns in deep shelters, poor weather (including sleet), and the failure of the new French Schneider tanks — most of those committed were destroyed — meant gains were limited to a few kilometres at a cost of well over 100,000 French casualties in the first days. Secondary attacks continued into May without achieving the promised breakthrough.

Aftermath: the mutinies

Nivelle was removed in mid-May and replaced by Philippe Pétain. Disillusionment with futile attacks, poor leave and conditions sparked the French Army mutinies of spring 1917, in which units in roughly half the army's divisions refused orders to attack, though most held the line defensively. Pétain restored discipline through a combination of improved leave, rest and conditions, and courts-martial — thousands were convicted and several dozen executed — while the French army largely avoided major offensives until 1918, leaving the British to bear the main burden at Passchendaele.
