Solar neutrino problem

The solar neutrino problem was a major discrepancy, from the late 1960s to 2001, between the flux of neutrinos arriving from the Sun as measured by detectors on Earth and the flux predicted by the standard solar model: only about one-third to one-half of the expected electron neutrinos were detected.

The Homestake experiment

Raymond Davis Jr. built a detector of tetrachloroethylene 1,478 m underground in the Homestake Mine, South Dakota, to shield it from cosmic rays. John Bahcall calculated the predicted capture rate. From 1970 onward Davis consistently measured about one-third of the prediction. Kamiokande in Japan confirmed the deficit in the 1980s and showed the neutrinos did come from the Sun's direction, ruling out many experimental explanations.

Resolution: neutrino oscillation

The solution was neutrino oscillation, enhanced in matter by the Mikheyev–Smirnov–Wolfenstein (MSW) effect: electron neutrinos produced in the Sun's core change flavour into muon and tau neutrinos en route, which the early detectors could not see. In 2001 the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in Canada measured the total flux of all flavours and found it matched the solar model, while the electron-neutrino flux alone was about one-third. Super-Kamiokande's atmospheric-neutrino results had already established oscillation in 1998. The discovery implies neutrinos have non-zero mass, beyond the original Standard Model; Davis and Masatoshi Koshiba shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics, and Arthur McDonald and Takaaki Kajita the 2015 prize for oscillation.
