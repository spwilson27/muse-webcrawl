Federal Communications Commission

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) is an independent agency of the United States government that regulates communications by radio, television, wire, satellite and cable across the country. It was established by the Communications Act of 1934.

FormedJune 19, 1934
HeadquartersWashington, D.C.
Commissioners5, appointed by the President
PredecessorFederal Radio Commission

Functions

The FCC licenses broadcasters and spectrum, runs spectrum auctions, enforces indecency and ownership rules, oversees universal service and emergency communications (E911, Emergency Alert System), and regulates interstate telephone and broadband services.

Structure

Five commissioners serve staggered five-year terms; no more than three may belong to one political party, and the President designates the chair. Bureaus cover media, wireless telecommunications, wireline competition, enforcement and consumer affairs.

Major debates

Recurring controversies include network neutrality — rules adopted in 2015, repealed in 2017 and restored then struck down in later litigation — media consolidation, spectrum policy for 5G, and robocall enforcement.
