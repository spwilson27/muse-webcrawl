Karl Barth (10 May 1886 – 10 December 1968) was a Swiss Reformed theologian widely regarded as one of the most important Christian thinkers of the 20th century. His massive Church Dogmatics and his early commentary on Romans reshaped Protestant theology, and he was the principal author of the Barmen Declaration opposing Nazi influence over the German church.

Life and career

Born in Basel, Barth studied at Bern, Berlin, Tübingen and Marburg, where he was influenced by liberal theologians such as Adolf von Harnack and Wilhelm Herrmann. As a young pastor in the industrial village of Safenwil (1911–1921), his horror at his teachers' support for German war policy in 1914 pushed him to break with liberal theology. His commentary The Epistle to the Romans (1919; radically revised 1922) argued that God is known through God's own self-revelation in Christ, not through human religion or experience — a programme called dialectical theology.

Barth held professorships at Göttingen, Münster and Bonn. After Hitler came to power he helped found the Confessing Church and drafted the Barmen Declaration (1934). Refusing to swear an unconditional oath to Hitler, he was dismissed from Bonn in 1935 and returned to Basel, where he taught until 1962.

Theology

Barth's Church Dogmatics (1932–1967), left unfinished at over 9,000 pages, centres on the doctrine of the Word of God, the election of humanity in Jesus Christ, and the reconciliation accomplished in him. Critics accused his theology of "Christomonism" and of universalist tendencies regarding salvation; admirers see in it a thoroughgoing return to Scripture and the Reformation. Pope Pius XII reportedly called him the greatest theologian since Thomas Aquinas.
