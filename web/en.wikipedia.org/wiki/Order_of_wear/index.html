Order of wear

Order of wear

Field: Phaleristics

Synonym: Order of precedence for decorations

An order of wear is the officially prescribed sequence in which a country's orders, decorations and medals are to be worn and displayed, relative to one another. Because a decorated person may hold many awards, the order of wear fixes which appears first — conventionally the senior award is placed first, nearest the centre of the chest or first on a medal bar — and governs the arrangement of ribbons, stars, sashes and neck badges on uniform or court dress.

Purpose

Orders of wear are issued by a sovereign, government or armed force to give uniformity in dress regulations and to express the hierarchy of the national honours system. Gallantry awards typically rank ahead of long-service awards, and national awards ahead of foreign ones; foreign decorations are usually listed in the order in which they were received or in order of the seniority of the awarding states, and in many countries require official permission to accept and wear.

Examples

  • The United Kingdom publishes an official Order of Wear, most recently promulgated in the London Gazette, beginning with the Victoria Cross and George Cross and proceeding through the orders of chivalry, campaign medals, jubilee and coronation medals, and long-service awards.
  • The armed forces of the United States prescribe precedence in service regulations, with the Medal of Honor first.
  • Canada, Australia and New Zealand maintain their own orders of wear reflecting their separate national honours systems.
  • India's order of wear is headed by the Bharat Ratna and the Param Vir Chakra, followed by the Padma awards and other national decorations.

On a ribbon bar, the sequence is generally the same as for full medals, read from the wearer's right (the viewer's left) in British and Commonwealth practice. Miniature medals follow the same order. Breaches of the order of wear are a matter of dress-regulation discipline rather than criminal law in most countries, though some states, including the United Kingdom, restrict the wearing of unearned decorations.
