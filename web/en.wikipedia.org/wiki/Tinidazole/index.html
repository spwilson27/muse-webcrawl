Tinidazole


Tinidazole, a nitroimidazole antimicrobial

Tinidazole is an anti-parasitic drug used against protozoan infections. It is a nitroimidazole antibiotic structurally related to metronidazole, developed in 1972 and widely used in Europe and the developing world for many years before being introduced in the United States in 2004. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Tinidazole is effective against giardiasis, amoebiasis (including amoebic liver abscess), trichomoniasis, and bacterial vaginosis, and it is active against anaerobic bacteria. Its longer half-life compared with metronidazole often permits shorter courses or single-dose treatment, which can improve adherence. Like other nitroimidazoles, it works by generating free radicals that damage microbial DNA, and it works only in low-oxygen environments typical of anaerobic organisms.

Side effects

Common side effects include a metallic taste, nausea, and loss of appetite. Drinking alcohol while taking tinidazole can provoke a disulfiram-like reaction with nausea and vomiting, so alcohol is avoided during treatment and for several days afterward. Serious reactions are rare.
