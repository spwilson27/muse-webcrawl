Gauls

The Gauls (Latin: Galli) were a group of Celtic peoples inhabiting Gaul — roughly modern France, Belgium, and parts of neighbouring countries — during the Iron Age and the Roman era. Speaking Gaulish, a Continental Celtic language, they were organized into numerous tribes and city-states, including the Arverni, Aedui, Sequani, and Helvetii, and were famed as warriors, metalworkers, and farmers.

History

Gaulish culture emerged from the Hallstatt and La Tène cultures of the first millennium BC. Gauls raided Italy, sacking Rome around 390 BC, and settled in northern Italy (Cisalpine Gaul) and, in the 270s BC, in central Anatolia, where they became the Galatians. Gaulish tribes fought among themselves and with Rome until Julius Caesar conquered all of Gaul in the Gallic Wars (58–50 BC), culminating in Vercingetorix's defeat at Alesia in 52 BC. Under Roman rule, Gaul was Romanized; Latin largely replaced Gaulish by the 5th century AD, though the region's identity survived in the name "Gaul" and in later French national mythology, which embraced figures like Vercingetorix and the comic hero Astérix.

Society and religion

Gaulish society comprised nobles, commoners, and a learned class of druids who served as priests, judges, and teachers and conducted sacrifices at sacred groves and sanctuaries. The Gauls built fortified hilltop towns called oppida, minted coinage, and traded widely. Roman writers depicted them as brave but impulsive, with customs including head-hunting and human sacrifice, accounts modern historians treat with caution.
