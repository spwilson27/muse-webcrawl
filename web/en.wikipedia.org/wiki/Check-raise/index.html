Check-raise

The check-raise: pass first, then raise the bet that follows

A check-raise in poker is a deceptive play in which a player first checks (declines to bet when it is their turn) and then, in the same betting round, raises after an opponent bets. Checking suggests weakness; the subsequent raise therefore often represents a strong hand and extracts more money from the pot, while leaving the original bettor facing a difficult decision.

Purposes

  • Building the pot — with a very strong hand, check-raising can win more than betting directly, especially when an aggressive opponent is likely to bet.
  • Trapping — disguising strength so opponents commit chips before the hand's power is revealed.
  • Semi-bluffing — check-raising with a drawing hand applies pressure and may win the pot immediately, while retaining outs if called.
  • Information and protection — a check-raise can narrow opponents' ranges and charge drawing hands to continue.

Etiquette and rules

The check-raise was historically considered unsporting in some home games and was even prohibited in certain card rooms, a stigma satirised in poker folklore. In modern casino and tournament poker it is a standard, legal and expected part of the game in virtually all rule sets, and players who object to it are in a small minority. It must not be confused with a string raise, in which a player pushes chips forward in more than one motion; a check-raise is announced or made with a single legal raise after a genuine check.

Strategic considerations

Check-raising is most effective out of position against opponents who bet frequently, including "continuation bettors" in Texas hold 'em who bet most flops after raising pre-flop. Overuse can be exploited: opponents may check behind for a free card, neutralising the trap. Strong players therefore balance their check-raising range between value hands and bluffs, particularly on coordinated boards where draws are plentiful.
