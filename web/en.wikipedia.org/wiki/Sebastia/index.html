Sebastia (Arabic: سبسطية, Sabastiyah) is a Palestinian village in the Nablus Governorate of the West Bank, located about 12 kilometers northwest of Nablus. Adjacent to the village lies the archaeological site of ancient Samaria, capital of the northern Kingdom of Israel, which Herod the Great rebuilt around 27 BCE and renamed Sebaste (Sebastia) in honor of the Roman emperor Augustus (Sebastos in Greek).

Ancient Samaria

According to the Hebrew Bible, King Omri of Israel purchased the hill of Samaria and built his capital there around 880 BCE; his son Ahab constructed a temple of Baal in the city. Samaria fell to the Assyrians in 722 BCE, ending the northern kingdom. After periods of Persian and Hellenistic rule — including settlement by Macedonian veterans under Alexander the Great — the city was destroyed by John Hyrcanus and later magnificently rebuilt by Herod, who erected a temple to Augustus, a forum, a stadium, and a colonnaded street some 600 meters long.

Later history

In the Roman and Byzantine periods the city remained a regional center and became a Christian bishopric; tradition associates Sebastia with the burial place of John the Baptist, and a Byzantine church and later a Crusader cathedral (converted into the Nabi Yahya Mosque) marked the site. Excavations by Harvard University (1908–1910) and British and Jordanian teams in the 1930s uncovered Israelite palace remains, the celebrated Samaria ivories, Roman temples, and Byzantine churches.

The modern village

The modern village, with a population of a few thousand, is administered by the Palestinian Authority, while the archaeological park lies in Area C under Israeli control, a division that has made the site a focus of political dispute and periodic tension. Tourism to the ruins, once significant, has declined sharply since the Second Intifada.
