Creatine

Creatine is an organic compound with the formula (H2N)(HN)CN(CH3)CH2CO2H. It occurs naturally in vertebrates, where it helps recycle adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the cell's main energy currency, particularly in muscle and brain tissue. About 95% of the body's creatine is stored in skeletal muscle, roughly two-thirds of it as phosphocreatine.

Biological role

The creatine kinase enzyme system shuttles high-energy phosphate groups between ATP and phosphocreatine. During intense muscular effort, phosphocreatine donates its phosphate group to ADP to regenerate ATP, buffering energy supply for a few seconds of maximal work. The body synthesises about 1 gram of creatine per day, mainly in the liver and kidneys from the amino acids arginine, glycine and methionine, and obtains a similar amount from food, especially red meat and fish. Creatine spontaneously degrades to creatinine, which is excreted in urine and used clinically as a marker of kidney function.

Supplementation

Creatine monohydrate is one of the most popular and extensively studied dietary supplements in sport. Research shows it can increase muscle phosphocreatine stores, improving performance in short, high-intensity activities such as sprinting and weightlifting and promoting gains in lean mass when combined with resistance training. Major sports-medicine bodies consider it safe for healthy adults at recommended doses (typically 3–5 g per day), with weight gain from water retention the most common side effect. It has also been investigated for neurological and muscular diseases, including creatine deficiency syndromes, in which supplementation can be therapeutic.

History

Creatine was first isolated from meat extract in 1832 by Michel Eugène Chevreul, who named it from the Greek kreas ("flesh"). Its role in muscle metabolism was clarified in the 1920s with the discovery of phosphocreatine.
