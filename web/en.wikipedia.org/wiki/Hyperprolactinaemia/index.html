Hyperprolactinaemia (also spelled hyperprolactinemia) is the presence of abnormally high levels of the hormone prolactin in the blood. Prolactin is secreted by the anterior pituitary gland and is normally elevated in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Outside those states, hyperprolactinaemia suppresses the reproductive axis and causes characteristic symptoms.

Signs and symptoms

In women, high prolactin suppresses gonadotropin-releasing hormone, leading to irregular or absent periods (amenorrhoea), infertility and inappropriate milk production (galactorrhoea). In men it causes reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility and, less often, galactorrhoea and gynaecomastia. Long-standing sex-hormone deficiency reduces bone density. A large pituitary tumour may additionally cause headaches and visual field defects (classically bitemporal hemianopia) by pressing on the optic chiasm.

Causes

The most common pathological cause is a prolactinoma, a benign prolactin-secreting pituitary adenoma. Frequent non-tumour causes include dopamine-blocking drugs — especially antipsychotics such as risperidone — primary hypothyroidism (raised TRH stimulates prolactin), chronic kidney failure, and stalk compression by other pituitary masses, which interrupts dopamine's tonic inhibition of prolactin release ("stalk effect").

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by a blood prolactin measurement, repeated if borderline, with thyroid function tests and MRI of the pituitary when a tumour is suspected; assay interference from macroprolactin is a recognised pitfall. Most cases are treated with dopamine agonists — cabergoline is usually preferred over bromocriptine — which normalise prolactin and shrink prolactinomas in the great majority of patients. Surgery (transsphenoidal) or radiotherapy is reserved for drug-resistant or intolerant cases.
