Muon-catalyzed fusion

Muon-catalyzed fusion (abbreviated μCF or MCF) is a process that allows nuclear fusion to take place at temperatures far below those required for thermonuclear fusion, even at room temperature. A negative muon — about 207 times heavier than an electron — can bind two hydrogen isotope nuclei into a tiny muonic molecule, drawing them so close together that fusion occurs almost immediately.

Mechanism

When a muon replaces the electron in a deuterium–tritium system, it forms a dtμ molecular ion. Because the muon is so heavy, the nuclei sit roughly 200 times closer than in an ordinary molecule, and their tunneling through the Coulomb barrier becomes rapid. The fusion reaction releases about 17.6 MeV, mostly in a neutron, and usually frees the muon to catalyze further reactions. In principle a single muon can catalyze hundreds of fusions during its 2.2-microsecond lifetime.

History

The process was predicted by F. C. Frank in 1947 and observed unexpectedly by Luis Alvarez's group in bubble-chamber experiments in 1956. Interest revived in the 1980s after experiments at Los Alamos and elsewhere measured high cycling rates.

Obstacles

Practical energy production has not been achieved. The muon sometimes sticks to the helium nucleus produced by fusion (the "alpha sticking" problem), terminating the cycle; each muon also costs considerable energy to produce in a particle accelerator, and with current technology the energy cost of making the muon exceeds the fusion energy it can release. Muon-catalyzed fusion remains an active subject of research and a practical source of intense neutrons.
