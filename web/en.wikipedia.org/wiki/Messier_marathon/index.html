Messier marathon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Messier marathon is an attempt, usually organized by amateur astronomers, to find as many Messier objects as possible during one night. The Messier catalogue was compiled by French astronomer Charles Messier during the late 18th century and consists of 110 objects, which are mostly galaxies, nebulae, and star clusters.

Timing and feasibility

A complete marathon is possible only during a window of a few weeks between mid-March and early April, when the Sun is positioned in a region of sky (Pisces) devoid of Messier objects, allowing every object to be above the horizon at some point during the night. The observer must work quickly at dusk, beginning with objects setting in the west such as the galaxy M33 and the Andromeda Galaxy, proceed through the rich spring sky galaxy fields of Virgo and Coma Berenices at midnight, and finish at dawn with the last objects rising in the east, typically the globular cluster M30 in Capricornus, which is often the hardest and may be caught only briefly in twilight.

History

The marathon concept was developed in the 1970s by amateur astronomers in North America, including Tom Hoffelder, Tom Reiland, and Donald Machholz; the first near-complete runs were achieved around 1977–1979, and Gerry Rattley and others completed all 110 objects in a single night in March 1985 in Arizona. Marathon events are now a fixture of star parties and astronomy clubs worldwide, held from mid-northern latitudes where all objects can be seen. Observers usually use a prepared search sequence ordered by right ascension and setting time, star-hopping with a telescope or binoculars; using automated "goto" mounts is sometimes allowed and sometimes disfavoured depending on the event's rules.
