Loudspeaker

A loudspeaker (commonly referred to as a speaker or, in some cases, a speaker driver when referring to the individual transducer) is an electroacoustic transducer that converts an electrical audio signal into a corresponding sound. The most common type, the dynamic loudspeaker invented by Edward W. Kellogg and Chester W. Rice in 1925, drives a diaphragm with a voice coil suspended in a magnetic field.

Operation

When alternating current flows through the voice coil, the interaction with the permanent magnet's field produces a force that moves the coil and attached cone (or dome) back and forth, creating sound pressure waves. Drivers are optimised for frequency ranges: woofers for low frequencies, mid-range drivers, and tweeters for high frequencies. Multi-way systems divide the signal with a crossover network. Enclosures — sealed, bass-reflex, horn-loaded and others — control the rear radiation and extend or shape bass response.

Other technologies

Alternatives include electrostatic speakers (e.g., Quad ESL), planar magnetic and ribbon drivers, piezoelectric speakers, horn loudspeakers for high efficiency and public address, and distributed-mode panels. Key performance parameters are frequency response, sensitivity (dB per watt at 1 metre), impedance (typically 4–8 ohms), distortion and directivity.

Applications

Loudspeakers are ubiquitous: hi-fi and home theatre systems, studio monitors, public address and concert sound reinforcement (line arrays), headphones and earbuds (miniature drivers), smartphones, and smart speakers. The industry was transformed in the 2010s by networked and voice-controlled speakers.
