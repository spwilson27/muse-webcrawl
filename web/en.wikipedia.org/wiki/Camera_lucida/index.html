Camera lucida

A camera lucida in use as a drawing aid.

A camera lucida is an optical device used as a drawing aid by artists and microscopists. Patented in 1807 by William Hyde Wollaston, it uses a prism or mirrors to superimpose a virtual image of a subject onto the drawing surface, so that the artist sees the subject and the paper at once and can trace its outlines accurately. The name means "light chamber", in contrast to the camera obscura ("dark chamber").

How it works

In Wollaston's design, a four-sided prism is mounted on an adjustable stand at the edge of the drawing board. By looking through the edge of the prism, the user sees the scene reflected twice within the prism superimposed on the paper below. Because the image is virtual rather than projected, the device works in ordinary light, but the balance of illumination between subject and paper must be adjusted, sometimes with tinted filters, for comfortable drawing.

History and use

Although Johannes Kepler had described a similar portable device in 1611, Wollaston's practical instrument became popular in the nineteenth century among travellers, naturalists and portraitists. It was widely used at the microscope for making accurate drawings of specimens before photomicrography became routine. The young William Henry Fox Talbot's frustrations with the camera lucida during his Italian honeymoon in 1833 helped spur his invention of photography. Modern revivals of the device are marketed to art students, and smartphone apps emulate its effect.
