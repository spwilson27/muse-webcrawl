Congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Adrenal steroidogenesis, showing the common enzymatic blocks.

SpecialtyEndocrinology
CauseInherited enzyme defects in cortisol synthesis (most often 21-hydroxylase deficiency)
InheritanceAutosomal recessive
TreatmentGlucocorticoid (and often mineralocorticoid) replacement

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a group of autosomal recessive disorders in which an enzyme needed for the synthesis of cortisol—and sometimes aldosterone—by the adrenal glands is deficient. Loss of cortisol feedback raises ACTH secretion, which overstimulates the adrenal cortex (producing the hyperplasia) and shunts precursor steroids into androgen pathways. More than 90 percent of cases result from 21-hydroxylase deficiency caused by mutations in the CYP21A2 gene.

Forms and symptoms

Classic CAH presents in infancy. In the severe "salt-wasting" form, aldosterone deficiency causes life-threatening dehydration and electrolyte imbalance in the first weeks of life; a "simple virilizing" form preserves enough aldosterone function to avoid salt loss. Excess adrenal androgens virilize female infants, sometimes causing atypical genitalia at birth, and accelerate growth and puberty in both sexes, compromising adult height. Non-classic (late-onset) CAH is milder and may present in adolescence or adulthood with hirsutism, acne, and fertility problems.

Diagnosis and treatment

Newborn screening in many countries measures 17-hydroxyprogesterone, which accumulates behind the 21-hydroxylase block. Treatment is lifelong replacement with glucocorticoids (such as hydrocortisone in children) to suppress excess androgen production, plus fludrocortisone and salt supplementation in salt-wasting cases. Management must balance suppressing androgens against the side effects of excess glucocorticoid exposure, with monitoring of growth, bone age, and hormone levels.
