Urban enterprise zone

An urban enterprise zone is a designated area within a city in which governments offer tax incentives, reduced regulation, and other concessions to encourage private investment and job creation in economically distressed neighbourhoods. The concept was developed in the late 1970s — notably by the British geographer Peter Hall and promoted by the Conservative politician Geoffrey Howe, whose 1978 speech in London's Docklands proposed "enterprise zones" freed from many taxes and planning controls.

United Kingdom

The United Kingdom established its first enterprise zones in 1981, eventually designating more than thirty, including the London Docklands and areas in Merseyside, Tyneside, and the West Midlands. Benefits typically included exemption from local business rates, relief from development land tax, 100% capital allowances, and simplified planning. A new generation of enterprise zones was announced in England from 2011 onward, administered through local enterprise partnerships.

United States

In the United States, the idea was championed by Congressman Jack Kemp and others; although a comprehensive federal enterprise zone law was not enacted in the early 1980s, most states created their own programmes, and the federal government later established Empowerment Zones and Renewal Communities in the 1990s, and Opportunity Zones under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, which defer and reduce capital-gains taxes on investments in designated low-income census tracts.

Evaluation

Assessments of enterprise zones are mixed. Supporters credit them with attracting firms and catalysing regeneration; critics argue that much of the subsidised activity would have occurred anyway, that jobs often go to commuters rather than local residents, and that zones can merely displace economic activity from adjacent areas.
