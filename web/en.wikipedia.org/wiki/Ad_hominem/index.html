Ad hominem

Ad hominem (Latin for "to the person"), short for argumentum ad hominem, refers to arguments directed against a person rather than against the position they are maintaining. In informal logic it is classified as a fallacy of relevance when used to dismiss a claim by attacking its source, though arguments about a speaker's credibility can be legitimate in some contexts, such as assessing testimony.

Types

  • Abusive ad hominem — attacking the person's character, motives, or attributes (e.g., dismissing an argument because its proponent is "corrupt" or "stupid")
  • Circumstantial ad hominem — alleging that the person is disposed to take their position because of their circumstances or interests
  • Tu quoque ("you also") — deflecting criticism by alleging the critic's own hypocrisy
  • Guilt by association — discrediting a position by associating its holder with disreputable people or causes
  • Poisoning the well — presenting adverse information about a person before they speak, so that anything they say is discounted

Analysis

The distinguishing mark of a fallacious ad hominem is that the personal attack is irrelevant to the truth of the claim: the origin of an argument does not determine whether its premises and conclusions hold (to reject a claim solely because of its source is also called the genetic fallacy). By contrast, in legal and everyday settings where testimony must be evaluated, evidence of a witness's bias, dishonesty, or track record may be genuinely relevant. The term gained wide currency in the 19th century, and John Locke used "argumentum ad hominem" in a somewhat different sense: pressing an opponent with consequences drawn from their own premises.
