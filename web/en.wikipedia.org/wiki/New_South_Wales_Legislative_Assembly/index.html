The Legislative Assembly is the lower of the two houses of the Parliament of New South Wales, an Australian state. The upper house is the Legislative Council. Both sit at Parliament House in the state capital, Sydney. Members are known as MPs, and the chamber's traditional colour is green. The party or coalition with a majority in the Legislative Assembly forms government, and its leader becomes Premier of New South Wales.

History

The Legislative Assembly was created in 1856, when New South Wales was granted responsible self-government under a new constitution, replacing the partly elected Legislative Council established in 1843. It originally had 54 members elected from multi- and single-member districts. Women gained the vote in 1902, and the first woman member, Millicent Preston-Stanley, was elected in 1925. Proportional representation was used between 1920 and 1926 before a return to single-member districts. The number of seats has varied; since 1999 the Assembly has had 93 members.

Elections and composition

Members are elected for fixed four-year terms from single-member electoral districts using optional preferential voting: voters may rank as many or as few candidates as they wish. Elections for the Assembly and half the Legislative Council are normally held together on the fourth Saturday in March every four years. The executive government — the Premier and ministers — is drawn predominantly from the Assembly, and money bills must originate there.

Functions

The Assembly initiates and passes legislation (which also requires the agreement of the Legislative Council and royal assent from the Governor), scrutinises the executive through question time and committees, and controls supply. Its presiding officer is the Speaker. A member of the Assembly chairs the chamber's standing committees, including the Public Accounts Committee.
