Chiltern Hills

The Chiltern Hills, or simply the Chilterns, are a chalk escarpment in southern England, northwest of London, spanning parts of Oxfordshire, Buckinghamshire, Hertfordshire, and Bedfordshire. The range runs about 45 miles (72 km) from southwest to northeast. Much of the area is designated an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty (now styled a National Landscape).

Geography and geology

The Chilterns are formed of chalk laid down in the Late Cretaceous period. A steep scarp slope faces northwest, while the dip slope to the southeast is cut by dry valleys and covered in many places by clay-with-flints. Beech woodlands — historically managed for the furniture trade centred on High Wycombe — are a characteristic feature, and red kites, reintroduced from 1989, are now common.

History

The ancient Icknield Way runs along the foot of the scarp. In the early medieval period the area gave its name to the Chiltern Hundreds, whose stewardship became the procedural device by which Members of Parliament resign their seats. The region's chalk streams and market towns such as Amersham, Berkhamsted, and Henley-on-Thames lie within or at the edge of the hills.
