Arc pair grammar is a theoretical framework in linguistics developed by David E. Johnson and Paul M. Postal, set out in their 1980 book Arc Pair Grammar. It grew out of relational grammar and aims to describe the syntax of natural languages in terms of networks of grammatical relations rather than phrase-structure trees alone.

Overview

In arc pair grammar, a sentence is represented as a set of arcs — directed links between nodes — each arc bearing a label corresponding to a grammatical relation such as subject, object, or indirect object, and a "level" or "stratum" index. Arcs occur in pairs: an arc at one level can sponsor, or be succeeded by, an arc at another level, allowing the theory to describe phenomena in which an element bears different grammatical relations at different stages of a derivation, such as in passivisation or raising constructions.

Relation networks

The fundamental structure is the relation network, a graph of arcs satisfying formal well-formedness conditions ("laws") stated by the theory. Unlike transformational frameworks, arc pair grammar rejects movement operations; instead, multi-level relations within a single network capture the patterning that transformations were designed to express. Johnson and Postal used the framework to analyse constructions including passives, causatives and quantifier scope in a variety of languages.

Influence

Although arc pair grammar never became a mainstream framework, it influenced later relational and constraint-based theories and is often discussed alongside relational grammar, lexical functional grammar and head-driven phrase structure grammar as part of the family of non-transformational approaches to syntax that emerged in the 1970s and 1980s.
