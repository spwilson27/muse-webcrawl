Staff (music)

In Western musical notation, the staff (UK also stave; plural: staffs or staves) is a set of five horizontal lines and four spaces between them, on which musical notes and other symbols are placed to indicate pitch and rhythm. The staff is the foundational framework of the modern system of written music, in use, in evolving forms, for roughly a thousand years.

Structure

Each line and each space of the staff corresponds to a successive step of the diatonic scale. The exact pitches represented are fixed by a clef placed at the beginning of the staff — most commonly the treble (G) clef, the bass (F) clef, or one of the C clefs. Notes whose pitches lie beyond the range of the five lines are written with short additional ledger lines above or below the staff.

Notes are read from left to right. Their vertical position indicates pitch; their shape (whole note, half note, quarter note, and so on) and attachments such as stems, beams and dots indicate duration. Other symbols written on or around the staff include key signatures, time signatures, rests, dynamics and articulation marks.

Grand staff

Music for piano and other instruments with a wide range is written on a grand staff: a treble staff and a bass staff joined by a brace and a connecting barline. The two staves together cover the full range of the instrument, with middle C (C4) written on a ledger line between them.

History

The staff developed from the neumatic notation of medieval plainchant. Early chant books placed neumes above the text without exact pitch reference; a single line, then two, were added as guides, and the eleventh-century theorist Guido d'Arezzo is credited with systematising a four-line staff. The five-line staff became standard during the Renaissance and has remained the basis of Western notation ever since. Alternative systems, such as guitar tablature — which uses a six-line staff representing strings rather than pitches — coexist with it for particular instruments.
