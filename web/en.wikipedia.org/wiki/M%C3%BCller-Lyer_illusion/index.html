Müller-Lyer illusion

Müller-Lyer illusion
TypeOptical illusion
Described byFranz Carl Müller-Lyer (1889)

The Müller-Lyer illusion is an optical illusion consisting of two lines of identical length, one terminated by arrowheads pointing inward and the other by fins pointing outward. The line with outward-pointing fins appears considerably longer than the other, although both are the same length.

Explanation

Several explanations have been proposed. The misapplied size-constancy account holds that the visual system interprets the fin configurations as perspective cues for depth: the inward-arrow figure is read as a nearer object and the outward-fin figure as a farther one, so the brain scales their apparent sizes accordingly. Low-level filtering and centroid-of-mass theories offer alternative mechanisms.

Cross-cultural variation

Susceptibility to the illusion varies across cultures. Studies beginning with Segall, Campbell, and Herskovits in the 1960s found that people raised in "carpentered" environments with rectangular architecture tend to experience the effect more strongly than some non-Western populations, a finding used to argue that perceptual inference is shaped by visual experience.

History

The illusion was described by the German sociologist and psychiatrist Franz Carl Müller-Lyer in 1889 and remains one of the most studied phenomena in perceptual psychology.
