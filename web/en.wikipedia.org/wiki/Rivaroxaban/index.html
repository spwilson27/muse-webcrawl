Rivaroxaban, sold under the brand name Xarelto among others, is an anticoagulant medication (blood thinner) used to treat and prevent blood clots. Specifically it is used to treat deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism and to prevent blood clots in atrial fibrillation and following hip or knee replacement surgery. It is taken by mouth.

Rivaroxaban was the first orally active direct factor Xa inhibitor to reach the market. It was developed by Bayer and is marketed in many countries by Bayer and by Janssen Pharmaceuticals (a Johnson & Johnson company) in the United States. It was approved for medical use in the European Union in 2008 and in the United States in 2011. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Rivaroxaban is used for:

  • Prevention of stroke and systemic embolism in people with non-valvular atrial fibrillation.
  • Treatment of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), and prevention of their recurrence.
  • Prevention of venous thromboembolism after elective hip or knee replacement surgery.
  • In combination with aspirin, reduction of the risk of major cardiovascular events in people with coronary artery disease or symptomatic peripheral artery disease (approved in some jurisdictions on the basis of the COMPASS trial).

Mechanism of action

Rivaroxaban directly and reversibly inhibits free and clot-bound factor Xa, a key enzyme in the coagulation cascade. Factor Xa converts prothrombin to thrombin; blocking it decreases thrombin generation and thereby the formation of fibrin clots. Unlike indirect inhibitors such as fondaparinux, rivaroxaban does not require antithrombin as a cofactor, and unlike warfarin it does not inhibit multiple vitamin K–dependent factors, giving it a more predictable dose–response relationship that does not require routine coagulation monitoring.

Adverse effects

The principal adverse effect is bleeding, including gastrointestinal bleeding and, rarely, intracranial haemorrhage. Risk rises with age, impaired kidney function, and combined use with antiplatelet drugs or other anticoagulants. Compared with warfarin, rivaroxaban has been associated with similar or lower rates of major bleeding but higher rates of gastrointestinal bleeding in some trials. Andexanet alfa is approved as a specific reversal agent for life-threatening bleeding due to rivaroxaban and apixaban.

Pharmacology

Rivaroxaban reaches peak plasma concentrations in 2–4 hours. The 10 mg dose is well absorbed regardless of food, while the 20 mg dose should be taken with food to achieve full bioavailability. About a third of the drug is excreted unchanged by the kidneys; the remainder is metabolised, mainly via CYP3A4 and CYP2J2, so strong inhibitors or inducers of these pathways alter its effect. Dosing must be adjusted in renal impairment, and the drug is generally avoided in severe liver disease and in patients with mechanical heart valves or antiphospholipid syndrome.
