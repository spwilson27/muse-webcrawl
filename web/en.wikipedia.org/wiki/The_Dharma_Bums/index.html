The Dharma Bums is a 1958 novel by Beat Generation author Jack Kerouac. The semi-fictional accounts in the novel are based on events that occurred years after the events of his previous novel, On the Road. The main characters are the narrator Ray Smith, based on Kerouac, and Japhy Ryder, based on the poet and essayist Gary Snyder, who was instrumental in Kerouac's introduction to Buddhism in the mid-1950s.

Plot

Ray Smith, a wandering writer, falls in with Japhy Ryder and a circle of West Coast poets and mountaineers who combine Zen Buddhism, poetry, hiking, and wine-fuelled parties. The novel's centrepiece is the pair's climb of the Matterhorn Peak in the Sierra Nevada, during which Japhy's joyful descent — running and leaping down the scree — teaches Ray that "you can't fall off a mountain". The book ends with Ray spending a summer alone as a fire lookout on Desolation Peak in the North Cascades, seeking enlightenment in solitude.

Themes and influence

The novel explores the tension between solitary spiritual seeking and the pull of friendship, drink, and the low life. It is credited with popularising Buddhism among young Americans and with inspiring the backpacking and outdoor culture of the 1960s; the "dharma bum" became a model for a generation of travellers. The book also fictionalises the famous 1955 Six Gallery reading at which Allen Ginsberg first performed Howl.
