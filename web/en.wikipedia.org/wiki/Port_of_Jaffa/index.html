The Port of Jaffa (Hebrew: נמל יפו, Nemal Yafo; Arabic: ميناء يافا) is an ancient port on the Mediterranean Sea located in Jaffa, now part of Tel Aviv-Yafo, Israel. Used for more than four thousand years, it is among the oldest working harbours in the world, and served for centuries as the principal gateway to the Holy Land for pilgrims, traders and armies.

History

Jaffa's natural anchorage, sheltered in part by the rocks known as Andromeda's Rock, was used from the Bronze Age. Egyptian, Phoenician and Israelite rulers all controlled it; according to tradition, cedar logs for Solomon's Temple were floated down the coast to Jaffa, and the prophet Jonah was said to have embarked here. The port passed through Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Crusader, Mamluk and Ottoman hands.

Under Ottoman rule Jaffa exported grain, cotton and later its famous oranges — the "Jaffa orange" became a global brand in the late 19th century. Because the harbour is shallow and reef-strewn, passengers and cargo were famously transferred by lighter boats even in heavy seas. The opening of Haifa's deep-water port in 1933, and the 1936–1939 Arab revolt — during which the Jewish community built Tel Aviv Port — reduced Jaffa's commercial role; cargo operations ended in 1965 with the opening of Ashdod Port.

Today

The old port is now a fishing harbour and leisure area, with restaurants, galleries, a lighthouse (Jaffa Light, built 1865), markets and boat tours, and is a popular landmark on the Tel Aviv promenade.
