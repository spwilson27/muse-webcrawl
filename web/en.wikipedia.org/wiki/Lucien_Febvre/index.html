Lucien Paul Victor Febvre (22 July 1878 – 11 September 1956) was a French historian best known for co-founding, with Marc Bloch, the Annales school of historiography and its journal, the Annales d'histoire économique et sociale, founded in 1929. Febvre championed a "total history" that combined geography, economics, sociology, psychology, and linguistics, and that studied collective mentalities (mentalités) rather than only political events and great individuals.

Life and career

Febvre was born in Nancy and studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris. Influenced by the geographer Paul Vidal de la Blache, his early work, including his doctoral thesis on Philip II and the Franche-Comté (1911), already blended geography with social and economic history. He served as an officer in the First World War. In 1919 he became professor at the University of Strasbourg, where he met Marc Bloch; together they founded the Annales in 1929. In 1933 Febvre took a chair at the Collège de France. After the Second World War he helped found the Sixth Section of the École Pratique des Hautes Études, devoted to the social sciences, and continued to lead the Annales after Bloch's death in 1944.

Major works

Febvre's most famous book is The Problem of Unbelief in the Sixteenth Century: The Religion of Rabelais (1942), which argued that atheism was effectively inconceivable in Rabelais's age because the mental equipment of the period lacked the concepts needed to sustain it — a landmark study of historical mentalities. His Martin Luther: A Destiny (1928) reinterpreted Luther within his social and religious context. With Henri-Jean Martin he wrote The Coming of the Book (1958, posthumous), a foundational work in the history of printing and the book.

Legacy

Febvre's insistence on problem-oriented history, interdisciplinarity, and the study of collective psychology profoundly shaped twentieth-century historiography in France and beyond, paving the way for later Annales historians such as Fernand Braudel.
