Nikolai Semyonovich Leskov (Russian: Никола́й Семёнович Леско́в; 16 February 1831 – 5 March 1895) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer, playwright and journalist, who also wrote under the pseudonym M. Stebnitsky. He is celebrated for his vivid portrayal of provincial Russian life and for his mastery of skaz, a narration technique that mimics the oral speech of a storyteller, often in dialect.

Life

Leskov was born in the village of Gorokhovo in Oryol province, the son of a civil servant and a mother from a clerical family. He worked as a civil servant and then as an agent for a commercial firm, travelling widely in Russia — experience that supplied much of his material. He settled in St. Petersburg in the 1860s and turned to journalism and fiction, frequently courting controversy: his novel No Way Out (1864), a satire on nihilist circles, damaged his standing with the radical intelligentsia, while the authorities also distrusted his independence.

Works

His best-known works include the novella Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District (1865), the story of a merchant's wife driven to murder, which Shostakovich later adapted into an opera; Cathedral Folk (1872), a chronicle of a provincial clergy family; The Enchanted Wanderer (1873); and "Lefty" (1881), about a Tula craftsman who shoes a mechanical flea. His tales of "righteous men" sought goodness in ordinary Russians and reflected his own unorthodox religious views, influenced in later life by Tolstoyan Christianity.

Legacy

Long overshadowed by Tolstoy, Dostoevsky and Turgenev, Leskov was championed by critics and by Walter Benjamin, whose essay "The Storyteller" (1936) honoured him as a master of oral narration. Modern criticism rates him among the finest Russian prose stylists of the nineteenth century.
