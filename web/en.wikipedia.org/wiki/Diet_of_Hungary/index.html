Diet of Hungary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Diet of Hungary (Hungarian: Országgyűlés, literally "national assembly"; originally also called the Dieta) was the supreme legislative institution of the medieval Kingdom of Hungary from the 1290s until its dissolution in the early 20th century. For centuries it was the meeting place of the Hungarian estates — the magnates (barons), the lesser nobility, and representatives of the royal free towns — and the central forum in which the laws of the kingdom were debated and enacted.

Origins

The roots of the Hungarian Diet reach back to the assemblies of the early Árpád-era kingdom. The Golden Bull of 1222, issued by King Andrew II, granted the nobility the right to assemble annually at Székesfehérvár, and the decrees of 1290 under Andrew III are often regarded as the Diet's formal emergence as a regular legislative body. By the 14th and 15th centuries, under the Angevin and later Jagiellonian kings, the Diet had become the principal venue for negotiating taxation, military obligations, and royal elections.

Composition

From the 17th century onwards the Diet was bicameral in practice. The Upper Table (Főrendiház) comprised the great officers of state, the archbishops and bishops, and the hereditary magnates. The Lower Table (Alsótábla) was formed by elected deputies of the counties, representatives of the chapter houses and royal free towns, and proxies of absent magnates. The counties' deputies, instructed by their county assemblies, formed the backbone of the Lower Table and the traditional guardians of noble liberties.

The reform era

The Diet of 1825–27, summoned by Francis I after a long interval, opened the Hungarian Reform Era. Count István Széchenyi offered a year's income from his estates to found the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, and figures such as Ferenc Deák, Lajos Kossuth, and Miklós Wesselényi pressed for the abolition of serfdom, the taxation of the nobility, and the wider use of the Hungarian language. The Diet of 1847–48, meeting in Pozsony (Pressburg), enacted the April Laws of 1848, which transformed Hungary into a constitutional state with a responsible ministry, before the revolution was crushed by the Habsburgs with Russian assistance.

Later history

After the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867, a reconstituted Hungarian parliament — formally still styled Diet — governed the Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen within the Dual Monarchy. It moved permanently to Budapest, and in 1904 the deputies took their seats in the newly completed Parliament Building on the Danube. The institution's historic continuity ended with the collapse of the monarchy in 1918, when the National Assembly assumed the role of legislature of the Hungarian republic.
