Mentalism

Mentalists create the illusion of extraordinary mental powers.

Mentalism is a performing art in which practitioners, known as mentalists, present demonstrations that appear to show highly developed mental or intuitive abilities — mind reading, telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis and feats of memory or rapid calculation. Unlike magicians, who typically use visible props and sleight of hand, mentalists frame their effects as feats of the mind, achieved — in the fiction of the performance — through psychology, suggestion, hypnosis, observation and showmanship.

History

Mentalism grew out of nineteenth-century stage magic and the spiritualist movement. Early pioneers included the American performer Joseph Dunninger, who popularised mentalism on radio and early television in the 1940s and 1950s, and the Dutch-born performer Kreskin. The 13-step primer Thirteen Steps to Mentalism by Tony Corinda (1958–1960) remains the discipline's foundational text. Modern practitioners include Marc Salem, Banachek, Derren Brown, Oz Pearlman, Lior Suchard and Gerry McCambridge; television series such as The Mentalist have drawn on the figure of the observant mentalist.

Techniques

Mentalists' real methods include cold reading (drawing inferences from appearance and responses), hot reading (prior research), muscle reading, billet reading and switching, pre-show work, stooges, suggestion, misdirection, mnemonics and specially gaffed props. Many mentalists are explicit that their powers are illusions. Some, however — so-called "psychic entertainers" — have blurred the line, presenting effects as genuine paranormal powers; the magician James Randi famously campaigned against such claims and exposed the faith healer Peter Popoff's use of a hidden earpiece.

Differences from magic

The professional boundary between mentalism and magic is largely presentational: the same secret technique may be presented as a card trick or as a demonstration of psychological influence. Mentalists' organisations sometimes discouraged mixing the two, though the distinction has loosened considerably.
