Distributive justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Distributive justice concerns the normative principles governing the allocation of benefits and burdens in society — income and wealth, but also opportunities, rights, honours and political power. Competing criteria include equality (equal shares), equity or desert (shares proportioned to contribution or merit), need (shares by requirement), entitlement (shares by legitimate acquisition and transfer), and welfare maximisation.

Theories

Utilitarians distribute to maximise total welfare (diminishing marginal utility often justifying transfers); Rawls permits inequalities only under fair equality of opportunity and where they benefit the least advantaged; Nozick rejects "patterned" distributions entirely — justice is historical entitlement, and redistribution of holdings justly acquired is on a par with forced labour. Between them lie luck egalitarianism (neutralise brute luck, respect choice), sufficientarianism (everyone should have enough), prioritarianism and capabilities approaches (Sen, Nussbaum: distribute real freedoms to function). The empirical debate — what actual tax, welfare, education and inheritance regimes do to outcomes — runs alongside the philosophy, from Piketty's r > g debates to mobility research.
