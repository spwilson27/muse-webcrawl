In graph theory, a tree is an undirected graph in which any two vertices are connected by exactly one path — equivalently, a connected acyclic graph. A disjoint union of trees is called a forest. Trees are among the most studied structures in combinatorics and computer science, where the unrelated but similar concept of a tree data structure also appears.

Equivalent definitions

For a finite graph G with V vertices, the following statements are equivalent: G is a tree; G is connected and has V − 1 edges; G is acyclic and has V − 1 edges; G is connected and every edge is a bridge; any two vertices are joined by a unique simple path; and G is minimally connected or maximally acyclic.

Properties

Every tree has at least two leaves (vertices of degree one). Removing any edge splits a tree into two components; adding any non-edge creates exactly one cycle. A rooted tree designates one vertex as root, inducing parent–child relationships, levels and subtrees; rooted trees underlie file systems, parse trees and decision trees. A spanning tree of a connected graph is a sub-tree containing all its vertices; the number of spanning trees is given by Kirchhoff's matrix tree theorem. The number of unlabelled trees and of labelled trees (Cayley's formula gives nn−2 for n labelled vertices) are classical enumeration results.
