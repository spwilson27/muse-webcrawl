1923 Great Kantō earthquake

The Great Kantō earthquake (関東大震災, Kantō daishinsai) struck the Kantō Plain on the main Japanese island of Honshū at 11:58:44 JST on Saturday, September 1, 1923. The earthquake had a magnitude estimated at 7.9–8.2 and devastated Tokyo, the port city of Yokohama, and the surrounding prefectures. Firestorms ignited by the quake caused the great majority of the deaths, which are estimated at between 105,000 and 142,000.

The earthquake

The shock ruptured the fault beneath Sagami Bay along the Sagami Trough, where the Philippine Sea Plate subducts beneath the Okhotsk Plate. Shaking lasted between four and ten minutes by contemporary accounts. The quake struck at lunchtime, when charcoal braziers and stoves were in use across the cities; fires broke out simultaneously in hundreds of places and, driven by strong winds from a typhoon passing offshore, merged into firestorms. In one notorious incident, some 38,000 people sheltering at an open space in Honjo were killed by a single fire whirl. Tsunamis up to 11 metres struck the coasts of Sagami Bay and the Bōsō Peninsula, and landslides caused further devastation.

Aftermath

In the chaos, rumours that Koreans were poisoning wells and committing arson spread widely, and vigilante groups, police and soldiers massacred thousands of Korean residents — and also Chinese migrants, socialists, and others mistaken for Koreans — in the Kantō Massacre, a dark companion to the disaster. Martial law was declared, and the government of Yamamoto Gonnohyōe organised relief and reconstruction. Tokyo was rebuilt with wider roads and new parks, though budget cuts scaled back the most ambitious plans. September 1 is now observed in Japan as Disaster Prevention Day, with nationwide earthquake drills.
