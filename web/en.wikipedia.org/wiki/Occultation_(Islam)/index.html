Occultation (Islam)

The Occultation (Arabic: الغيبة, al-Ghayba) is a doctrine in Shia Islam, especially Twelver Shi'ism, holding that the twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi — a messianic figure believed to be alive and in hiding — will return at the end of time to restore justice. The occultation is divided into the Minor Occultation (874–941 CE), during which the Imam communicated with his followers through four deputies, and the Major Occultation (941–present), which continues until his reappearance.

Background

According to Twelver belief, the eleventh Imam, Hasan al-Askari, died in 874 in Samarra, leaving a young son who went into hiding to escape Abbasid persecution. During the Minor Occultation (al-Ghayba al-Ṣughrā), the Imam was represented by four successive agents (safīrs), beginning with Uthman ibn Sa'id al-Asadi, who collected religious taxes and conveyed the Imam's signed rescripts (tawqīʿāt) to the community. Before the death of the fourth deputy, Ali ibn Muhammad al-Samarri, in 941, a final rescript announced the beginning of the Major Occultation, in which no further deputy would be appointed.

Theology and practice

During the Major Occultation, guidance of the community devolved upon qualified jurists (fuqahāʾ); in the modern era this doctrine underpins the theory of velāyat-e faqīh (guardianship of the jurist) implemented in the Islamic Republic of Iran. Believers await the Mahdi's return (ẓuhūr) alongside Jesus (Isa), praying for his reappearance in the supplication Du'a al-Ahad and gathering at sites such as Jamkaran Mosque near Qom. Other Shia branches have their own doctrines of occultation — the Ismaili concept differs, and the Druze await the return of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah — while the concept of a hidden, expected saviour also shaped early Shia movements such as the Kaysanites.
