Padua (Italian: Padova; Venetian: Pàdova) is a city and commune in Veneto, northern Italy, and the capital of the province of Padua. It is one of the oldest cities in northern Italy, traditionally founded, according to legend repeated by Virgil, by the Trojan Antenor. It is known for the University of Padua, founded in 1222, where Galileo Galilei lectured, and for the Basilica of Saint Anthony, a major pilgrimage site.

History

Roman Patavium was one of the wealthiest cities of the early empire and the birthplace of the historian Livy. After periods of Lombard, Frankish and communal rule, Padua flourished under the Carrara family in the fourteenth century, when Giotto painted the Scrovegni Chapel (c. 1305), one of the masterpieces of Western art. From 1405 until 1797 the city was part of the Republic of Venice. It later passed to Austria and, in 1866, to the Kingdom of Italy.

Main sights

Landmarks include the Scrovegni Chapel with Giotto's fresco cycle, the Basilica of Saint Anthony ("Il Santo"), the Palazzo della Ragione with its vast medieval hall, the Prato della Valle — one of Europe's largest squares — the Botanical Garden of Padua (founded 1545, the oldest academic botanical garden still in its original location, a UNESCO World Heritage Site), and the university's Palazzo Bo with Galileo's lectern and the world's first permanent anatomical theatre (1594).

University

The University of Padua, founded in 1222 by scholars seceding from Bologna, is among Europe's oldest universities. Its motto, Universa Universis Patavina Libertas, celebrates its tradition of academic freedom. Nicolaus Copernicus studied there, and William Harvey took his medical degree there in 1602. In 1678 Elena Lucrezia Cornaro Piscopia became the first woman in the world to receive a university degree, at Padua.
