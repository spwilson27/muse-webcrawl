Zorro (Spanish for "fox") is a fictional character created in 1919 by American pulp writer Johnston McCulley, appearing in works set in the Pueblo of Los Angeles in Spanish California. He is the secret identity of Don Diego de la Vega, a young Californio nobleman who by day appears an effete aristocrat, but by night rides as a masked, black-clad swordsman defending the common people and Indigenous Californians against corrupt officials. Zorro's calling card is the letter "Z," which he carves with three quick strokes of his rapier.

Character

Zorro is a master swordsman, marksman and horseman, aided by his mute servant Bernardo and his black horse, Tornado, and based in a secret cave beneath the de la Vega hacienda. McCulley drew on figures such as the Scarlet Pimpernel, Robin Hood, and Californian bandit legends including Joaquín Murrieta. The character first appeared in the serial novel The Curse of Capistrano (1919), and its 1920 film adaptation The Mark of Zorro, starring Douglas Fairbanks, fixed his classic image.

In media

Zorro has appeared in dozens of films, television series, books, comics and stage works over more than a century. Notable portrayals include Tyrone Power in The Mark of Zorro (1940), Guy Williams in Disney's Zorro television series (1957–1959), and Antonio Banderas in The Mask of Zorro (1998) and The Legend of Zorro (2005). Isabel Allende's 2005 novel Zorro offered a literary origin story for the hero.

Legacy

Zorro is one of the earliest and most influential masked heroes in popular fiction. His double identity, mask, cape and secret lair made him a direct inspiration for later superheroes — most famously Batman, whose creators acknowledged the debt; in Batman lore, the film the Wayne family sees on the night of their murder is often The Mark of Zorro.
