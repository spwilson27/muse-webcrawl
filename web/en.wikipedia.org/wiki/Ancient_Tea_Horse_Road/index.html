Ancient Tea Horse Road

The Ancient Tea Horse Road (Chinese: 茶马古道, Chámǎ Gǔdào) was a network of caravan and porter paths winding through the mountains of Sichuan, Yunnan and Tibet in Southwest China. For more than a thousand years the routes carried tea from China to Tibet, where it was traded principally for horses needed by Chinese armies. The road is sometimes called the Southern Silk Road.

History

The tea–horse trade is documented from the Tang dynasty (7th century AD), when tea became a staple of the Tibetan diet — valued for its vitamins in a high-altitude diet poor in vegetables, and consumed as butter tea. Under the Song dynasty the trade was formalised in state-run Tea and Horse Agencies, exchanging Sichuan tea for Tibetan horses for the cavalry. The system continued under the Ming and Qing dynasties, and the trails saw their last great era of use during the Second Sino-Japanese War and the 1940s, when the route became a supply line into China.

Routes

Two principal corridors existed: the Sichuan route, from the tea-producing region around Ya'an through Kangding and Batang to Lhasa, and onward to Nepal and India; and the Yunnan route, from the Pu'er tea country through Dali and Lijiang into Tibet. The full journey to Lhasa could take many months, crossing passes above 4,500 metres.

The trade

Tea was compressed into bricks or discs for transport; tea bricks also circulated as a form of currency in Tibet and Mongolia. Porters famously carried loads of compressed tea exceeding their own body weight on the steepest sections, supported by staffs, while mule and yak caravans handled gentler ground. Return cargoes included horses, wool, medicinal herbs, musk and gold.

Legacy

With the building of motor roads in the 1950s–60s the caravans ended, but surviving sections of trail, caravanserais and staging towns such as Kangding and Lijiang remain, and the road has become a focus of heritage tourism and scholarship.
