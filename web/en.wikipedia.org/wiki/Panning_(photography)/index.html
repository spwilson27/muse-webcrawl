Panning (photography)

In photography, panning is the horizontal movement of a camera as it scans a moving subject, following the subject with the camera during exposure. When done successfully with a relatively slow shutter speed, the subject appears sharp while the background is blurred by motion, conveying a strong sense of speed and movement. The technique is widely used in sports, motorsport, and wildlife photography.

Technique

The photographer selects a shutter speed slow enough to blur the background — often between 1/15 and 1/125 of a second, depending on the subject's speed and distance — then tracks the moving subject smoothly, pressing the shutter while continuing to swing the camera in an arc, following through after the exposure, much like a golfer's or baseball player's swing. A stable stance, pivoting at the waist, and pre-focusing on the point where the subject will pass all improve results.

Equipment considerations

Panning can be done hand-held or with the aid of a monopod. Image stabilization systems may help or hinder: many lenses offer a dedicated panning mode (often "Mode 2") that stabilizes only vertical movement while allowing horizontal motion to blur naturally. Neutral-density filters can help achieve slow shutter speeds in bright conditions.

Difficulties

The chief difficulty is matching the camera's swing exactly to the subject's speed and trajectory; subjects moving toward or away from the camera, or changing speed, blur easily. Panning also works poorly for subjects whose parts move independently — a cyclist's legs, for instance, will blur even in a successful pan. A low keeper rate is normal even for experienced photographers.

Related techniques

Panning is the converse of using a fast shutter to freeze motion. Related effects include motion blur in general, zoom bursts, and the use of rear-curtain flash synchronization to combine a sharp flash-lit subject with a blurred trail.
