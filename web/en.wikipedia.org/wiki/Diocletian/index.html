Diocletian

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diocletian's Palace at Split, Croatia, its walls forming the old town's core]

Diocletian (AD 244–311; Gaius Aurelius Valerius Diocletianus) was Roman emperor from 284 to 305, who ended the Crisis of the Third Century and refounded the Roman state. A soldier of humble Dalmatian birth raised by the army, he divided imperial rule (the Tetrarchy, from 293: two Augusti, two Caesars), doubled the provinces, rebuilt the army and taxation, attempted price controls by edict, launched the last and greatest persecution of Christians (303), and then did what no Roman emperor had done: retired voluntarily (305) to his palace at Split, growing cabbages — replying to envoys begging his return that he preferred his garden to the empire. His reforms bought the Roman world another century and a half in the west, and far longer in the east.

The refoundation

Diocletian's diagnosis was structural: the empire was too large for one ruler and too poor for its army. He subdivided provinces (eventually about a hundred), grouped them in dioceses under vicars, separated civil from military careers, rebuilt frontier fortifications, reformed the coinage and the tax census (capitatio-iugatio), and made the court a sacred monarchy of Persian-style ceremony — the Dominate. The Tetrarchy institutionalised succession by adoption and promotion, and for twenty years it worked: frontiers were recovered from Britain (under Constantius) to Mesopotamia (Galerius' Persian victory, 298).

Persecution, abdication, collapse

The 'Great Persecution' from 303 — churches razed, scriptures burned, clergy arrested, sacrifice demanded of all — fell hardest in the east under Galerius and failed of its purpose. In 305 Diocletian, ill and exhausted, abdicated at Nicomedia and compelled Maximian to do the same; within a year the Tetrarchy collapsed into civil war among its heirs (Constantine's rise among them). Diocletian watched from Split, refusing every summons to resume power, and died in 311 — the year his successor Galerius, on his own deathbed, ended the persecution.
