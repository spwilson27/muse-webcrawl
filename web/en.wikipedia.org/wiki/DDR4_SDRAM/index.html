DDR4 SDRAM (double data rate fourth-generation synchronous dynamic random-access memory) is a type of SDRAM with a high-bandwidth, double data rate interface. Released to the market in 2014, it is one of the latest variants of dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) and succeeded DDR3 SDRAM as the main memory standard of most computers during the late 2010s and early 2020s.

Features

Compared with DDR3, DDR4 offers higher module density, higher data rates and lower voltage — 1.2 V versus DDR3's 1.5 V (or 1.35 V for DDR3L) — reducing power consumption. JEDEC-standard DDR4 modules run at transfer rates from 1600 to 3200 million transfers per second, with enthusiast and overclocked modules exceeding this. Internally, DDR4 uses bank groups to increase throughput, and introduces features such as on-die termination improvements, CRC error detection on writes and command/address parity for reliability.

Form factors

Desktop DDR4 DIMMs have 288 pins and a notch in a different position from DDR3, so the generations are not physically or electrically interchangeable. Laptop SO-DIMMs have 260 pins. ECC and registered (buffered) variants are produced for servers and workstations.

Succession

DDR4 was succeeded by DDR5 SDRAM, whose specification was published in 2020 and which entered mainstream platforms from 2021 onward, though DDR4 remained in widespread use for years afterward.
