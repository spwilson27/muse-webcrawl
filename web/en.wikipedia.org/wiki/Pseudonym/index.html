Pseudonym

A pseudonym (from Greek pseudṓnymos, "falsely named") is a fictitious name used by a person instead of their real name. Pseudonyms include pen names of authors, stage names of performers, aliases, noms de guerre, usernames and assumed identities. Unlike an anonymous work, a pseudonymous work is attributed to a persistent (if fictitious) persona.

Reasons for use

People adopt pseudonyms for many reasons: to conceal gender (as the Brontë sisters did when publishing as the brothers Bell, and Mary Ann Evans when writing as George Eliot); to separate different kinds of work (the mathematician Charles Dodgson wrote fiction as Lewis Carroll); to avoid persecution or prosecution; to create a more memorable or marketable name (Mark Twain for Samuel Clemens); or to maintain privacy online. Criminals, spies and resistance fighters use aliases for concealment, while monarchs, popes and religious take new names on assuming office — regnal and papal names being a formalised kind of pseudonym.

Legal status

In many jurisdictions a person may use a pseudonym so long as it is not for fraudulent purposes; authors can hold copyright and sign contracts under pen names, and trademark and "doing business as" registrations can protect them. The right to publish pseudonymously has been recognised as part of freedom of expression, notably by the US Supreme Court in McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission (1995).
