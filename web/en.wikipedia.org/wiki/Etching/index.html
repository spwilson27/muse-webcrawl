Etching

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Etching is traditionally the process of using strong acid or mordant to cut into the unprotected parts of a metal surface to create a design in intaglio in the metal. In modern manufacturing, other chemicals may be used, and the process is fundamental to technologies such as printed circuit boards and semiconductor fabrication. As a method of printmaking, etching, along with engraving, is one of the most important techniques for old master prints, and remains widely practised today.

Process

The etcher covers a metal plate — typically copper or zinc — with an acid-resistant ground of wax or varnish. The design is drawn through the ground with a pointed needle, exposing the metal. The plate is then immersed in an acid bath; the acid "bites" into the exposed lines. Deeper biting yields darker printed lines, and areas can be protected mid-process by "stopping out" with varnish, allowing a range of tones. After the ground is removed, ink is worked into the bitten lines, the surface is wiped, and dampened paper is pressed against the plate in a printing press, pulling the image from the grooves.

History

Etching of armour and metalwork was practised by gunsmiths and goldsmiths in late medieval Germany; Daniel Hopfer is credited with the earliest dated etchings on iron around 1500. The technique migrated to copper plates in the 17th century, when Rembrandt raised etching to a major art form, exploiting repeated biting, drypoint and plate tone. Goya's series Los Caprichos and The Disasters of War combined etching with aquatint. The 19th-century "etching revival" in France, Britain and America renewed interest in the medium, and artists such as Whistler and Haden championed original etching over reproductive engraving.

Variants

Related techniques include aquatint (tonal areas created with powdered resin), soft-ground etching (textures pressed into a soft ground), lift-ground or sugar-lift, spit bite, and photogravure. Industrial chemical milling and photolithography are descendants of the same principle.
