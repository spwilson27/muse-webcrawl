The Libor scandal was a series of fraudulent actions connected to the London Interbank Offered Rate (Libor), revealed in 2012, in which traders and rate submitters at major banks manipulated the world's most important interest-rate benchmark for profit and, during the 2008 financial crisis, to make their banks appear more creditworthy than they were. Libor underpinned an estimated $300 trillion of financial contracts — derivatives, mortgages, student loans, and corporate debt — so even small distortions affected borrowers and investors worldwide. The scandal produced more than $9 billion in regulatory fines, a number of criminal convictions, the resignation of senior bank executives, and ultimately the abolition of Libor itself.

Background

Libor was set each London business day from submissions by a panel of banks estimating the rate at which they could borrow from one another. Because the submissions were estimates rather than records of actual transactions, and interbank lending largely froze during the crisis, the rate was vulnerable to dishonest submissions. From at least the mid-2000s, derivatives traders at panel banks routinely asked their banks' submitters to nudge submissions up or down on days when the traders' positions would profit — requests documented in chat messages and emails later released by regulators.

Revelation and consequences

Journalistic and academic suspicions surfaced as early as 2007–2008, including a Wall Street Journal study suggesting banks understated borrowing costs during the crisis. The scandal broke publicly on 27 June 2012, when Barclays settled with British and American regulators for £290 million, admitting misconduct; its chairman Marcus Agius and chief executive Bob Diamond resigned within days. Settlements followed for UBS ($1.5 billion), RBS, Deutsche Bank ($2.5 billion), Citigroup, JPMorgan, and the broker ICAP, among others. In 2015 the former UBS and Citigroup trader Tom Hayes became the first person convicted by a jury of manipulating Libor, receiving a fourteen-year sentence (reduced to eleven on appeal); other traders were convicted in London and New York, though several convictions were later overturned — and in 2025 the UK Supreme Court quashed the convictions of Hayes and Carlo Palombo, holding that the jury directions had been legally flawed. Investigations also exposed manipulation of other benchmarks, including the foreign-exchange market and Euribor.

Reform and the end of Libor

The Wheatley Review (2012) transferred Libor administration from the British Bankers' Association to ICE Benchmark Administration, made manipulation a criminal offence in the UK, and cut the number of Libor currencies and tenors. Regulators concluded, however, that a benchmark based on a shrinking unsecured interbank market could not be made robust, and Libor was phased out: most settings ceased at the end of 2021, and the last US dollar settings were published in June 2023. Replacement "risk-free rates" based on actual transactions — SOFR in the United States and SONIA in the United Kingdom — took its place.
