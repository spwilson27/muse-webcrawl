Economic surplus

In mainstream economics, economic surplus refers to two related quantities: consumer surplus and producer surplus. Together they measure the total welfare a market generates for buyers and sellers.

Consumer surplus

Consumer surplus is the difference between the total amount that consumers are willing and able to pay for a good or service (indicated by the demand curve) and the total amount they actually pay at the market price. On a supply-and-demand diagram it is the area above the price line and below the demand curve. The concept descends from Jules Dupuit and was formalised by Alfred Marshall.

Producer surplus

Producer surplus is the difference between the amount a producer actually receives for a good and the minimum amount it would be willing to accept (indicated by the supply curve, reflecting marginal cost). It is the area below the price line and above the supply curve. It is related to, but not identical with, profit, because it does not deduct fixed costs.

Total surplus and efficiency

The sum of consumer and producer surplus is maximised at the competitive equilibrium, a standard argument for the efficiency of free markets. Taxes, price controls and monopoly create a deadweight loss — surplus destroyed and transferred to no one. In Marxian economics, "surplus" has a different sense, relating to surplus value produced by labour.
