Labyrinth

In Greek mythology, the Labyrinth (Ancient Greek: λαβύρινθος, labýrinthos) was an elaborate, confusing structure designed and built by the legendary artificer Daedalus for King Minos of Crete at Knossos. Its function was to hold the Minotaur, the monster eventually killed by the hero Theseus, who navigated back out using a thread given to him by Minos's daughter Ariadne. In later usage the word came to denote any maze-like structure, although strictly a classical labyrinth is unicursal — a single non-branching path to the centre — as distinct from a multicursal maze.

Origins and etymology

The word is of pre-Greek origin and has been connected with labrys, the double-headed axe prominent in Minoan religion — hence "house of the double axe", perhaps referring to the palace of Knossos itself. Pliny the Elder and other ancient authors counted the Cretan labyrinth among several great labyrinths of the ancient world, including the Egyptian labyrinth at Hawara described by Herodotus, which seems to have been a vast mortuary temple complex.

Development of the design

The unicursal Cretan design — usually drawn with seven circuits — appears on coins of Knossos, on clay tablets, in Roman mosaics (where it often frames Theseus and the Minotaur at its centre), and in turf and stone labyrinths across northern Europe. Medieval Christianity adopted the pattern for cathedral pavement labyrinths, most famously at Chartres Cathedral (c. 1200), which pilgrims walked as a symbolic journey. Garden mazes of hedges became fashionable in Renaissance and Baroque Europe, and the terms labyrinth and maze are now often used interchangeably in popular culture, from literature to video games.

Cultural significance

Beyond its mythic setting, the labyrinth functions as a symbol of the journey, of death and rebirth, and of intricate design; modern labyrinths are installed in churches, hospitals and public parks as tools for walking meditation. In anatomy, the word also names the inner ear's bony and membranous labyrinth.
