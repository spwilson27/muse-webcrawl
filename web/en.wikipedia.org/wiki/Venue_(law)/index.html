Venue (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, venue is the particular geographical district (county, division or court) in which a case is or may be tried. Venue is distinct from jurisdiction: jurisdiction is a court's power over the parties and subject matter; venue rules allocate the case among courts that have power, for convenience and fairness (where the parties reside, where events occurred, where evidence and witnesses are).

Venue rules are usually statutory and often waivable (unlike subject-matter jurisdiction); improper venue leads to transfer or dismissal under rules such as 28 U.S.C. §§ 1391 (general federal venue) and 1404/1406 (transfer), and criminal venue has constitutional roots (Article III and the Sixth Amendment's vicinage requirement — trial by a jury of the district where the crime was committed). Change-of-venue motions address prejudicial publicity (Sheppard v. Maxwell, 1966, is the classic). Strategic venue choice (“forum shopping”) is a permanent feature of litigation economics, most visibly in patent cases' concentration in particular districts — prompting statutory and judicial responses — and in transnational litigation between legal systems.
