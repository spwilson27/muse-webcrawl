Locking differential

A locking differential is a mechanical device that overcomes the main limitation of a standard open differential: when one driven wheel loses traction, an open differential sends most torque to that spinning wheel and the vehicle stops. A locking differential temporarily locks the two output shafts together so both wheels rotate at the same speed, ensuring that the wheel with grip receives torque. Locking differentials ("lockers") are widely used in off-road vehicles, trucks, tractors and military vehicles.

How it works

In a locked state the differential carrier forces both axle shafts to turn together, regardless of the traction available at each wheel. This is ideal on mud, sand, snow or rock, but on high-grip surfaces a locked axle causes driveline "wind-up", tyre scrub and heavy steering, so lockers are disengaged for normal road use (manually selectable designs) or engage and disengage automatically.

Types

  • Selectable lockers — engaged on demand by the driver via a switch actuating compressed air (e.g. ARB Air Lockers), an electric solenoid (e.g. Eaton E-Locker), or a cable. Used in serious off-road vehicles such as the Jeep Wrangler Rubicon and Mercedes-Benz G-Class (which offers front, centre and rear lockers).
  • Automatic lockers — such as the Detroit Locker; ratcheting mechanisms that stay locked under power but allow one wheel to overrun on corners, sometimes with audible clicking and abrupt road manners.
  • Spool — not a differential at all but a solid coupling permanently joining both axles; used in drag racing and some off-road applications.
  • Welded differential — an open differential crudely converted by welding its gears; cheap but harsh, common in drift and budget off-road cars.

Comparison with related devices

A limited-slip differential (LSD) biases torque between wheels using clutches, gears (Torsen) or viscous couplings, but generally does not lock completely. Modern electronic traction control achieves a similar effect by braking a spinning wheel, and many SUVs combine brake-based systems with true mechanical lockers. Centre differentials in four-wheel-drive systems may likewise be lockable to split torque evenly between front and rear axles.
