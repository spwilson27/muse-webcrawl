Catholic theology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Catholic theology is the understanding of Catholic doctrine or teachings, and the study of the system of beliefs held by the Catholic Church. It draws on Scripture and sacred tradition as interpreted by the magisterium, and seeks an intellectually coherent account of the faith — summarised in the classical formula fides quaerens intellectum, "faith seeking understanding".

Foundations

Catholic theology affirms one God in three persons (the Trinity), the Incarnation of the Word in Jesus Christ, and salvation through grace. It holds that divine revelation is transmitted through both sacred Scripture and sacred tradition, authentically interpreted by the Church's teaching office. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) provides the modern authoritative summary of doctrine.

Major areas

Dogmatic theology treats the Trinity, Christology, Mariology, ecclesiology and eschatology. Sacramental theology covers the seven sacraments — baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance, anointing of the sick, holy orders and matrimony — understood as efficacious signs of grace instituted by Christ. Moral theology addresses natural law, conscience, virtue and sin, and underlies Catholic social teaching. Distinctively Catholic doctrines include transubstantiation, purgatory, the Immaculate Conception and Assumption of Mary, and papal infallibility as defined at the First Vatican Council (1870).

Historical development

The patristic era (Augustine, the Cappadocians) laid its foundations; medieval scholasticism, above all Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae, integrated faith with Aristotelian philosophy. The Council of Trent systematised doctrine in response to the Reformation. The Second Vatican Council (1962–65) renewed theology's engagement with Scripture, liturgy and the modern world, and 20th-century movements such as the nouvelle théologie shaped contemporary Catholic thought.
