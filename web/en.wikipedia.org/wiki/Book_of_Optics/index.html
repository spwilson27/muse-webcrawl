Book of Optics

The Book of Optics (Arabic: Kitāb al-Manāẓir; Latin: De Aspectibus or Opticae Thesaurus) is a seven-volume treatise on optics and vision written by the medieval Arab scholar Ibn al-Haytham (known in Latin as Alhazen) in the early 11th century, around 1011–1021. It transformed the understanding of light and sight, establishing that vision occurs when light reflected from objects enters the eye, and it is regarded as one of the most influential works in the history of physics and of the experimental scientific method.

Contents

The first three books treat direct vision: the nature of light, the anatomy of the eye, and how the eye perceives form, colour, and distance. Ibn al-Haytham rejected the ancient "emission theory" — that the eye sends out visual rays — arguing instead from experiments, including work with the camera obscura, that light travels into the eye from the object. Books IV and V treat reflection and images formed by mirrors, and Books VI and VII treat refraction, including the bending of light at the surfaces of glass and water and attempts to measure it.

Influence

Translated into Latin in the late 12th or early 13th century, the work shaped medieval and Renaissance "perspectivist" optics through Roger Bacon, Witelo, and John Peckham. Its approach influenced Johannes Kepler's theory of the retinal image and, more broadly, the development of experimental physics; the problem of how the eye and mind interpret the inverted optical image — "Alhazen's problem" of reflection from spherical mirrors is also named for him — remained a touchstone for centuries. UNESCO marked the work's millennium during the International Year of Light in 2015.
