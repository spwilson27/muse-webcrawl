Counterfactual conditional

Counterfactual conditionals (also subjunctive conditionals) are conditional sentences which discuss what would have been true under circumstances that did not in fact occur, e.g. "If Kennedy had not been assassinated, ..." Typically expressed in English with the past subjunctive and "would have," they are a central topic in the philosophy of language and logic.

Semantics

Unlike the material conditional of classical logic — which is automatically true when its antecedent is false — counterfactuals are not trivially true. Philosophers such as Robert Stalnaker and David Lewis analysed them with possible-worlds semantics: "If A had been the case, B would have been the case" is true when, in the closest possible world(s) where A holds, B also holds. Lewis's account underlies his defence of counterfactual theories of causation.

Problems and uses

Difficulties include judging closeness among worlds, "backtracking" counterfactuals, and conditionals with impossible antecedents (counterpossibles). Counterfactual reasoning is nonetheless indispensable in causal inference, historiography, law (but-for causation) and everyday regret, and statisticians formalise it in frameworks such as Judea Pearl's structural causal models.
