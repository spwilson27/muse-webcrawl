Omission bias

Omission bias
FieldCognitive psychology; behavioural economics
DefinitionTendency to judge harmful actions as worse than equally harmful omissions (inactions)
Related conceptsStatus quo bias; inaction inertia; trolley problem
Key researchersIlana Ritov; Jonathan Baron; Mark Spranca

Omission bias is the tendency to regard harmful actions as worse, or less moral, than equally harmful omissions. It is a type of cognitive bias in which people judge a harmful act of commission more harshly than a harmful failure to act, even when the consequences are identical or worse.

Examples

A classic illustration concerns vaccination: some parents refuse to vaccinate their children because they focus on the small risk of harm caused by the act of vaccinating, while discounting the larger risk of disease arising from the omission. Another example is found in medicine, where physicians may avoid an intervention with a chance of a bad outcome even when withholding it carries a greater expected risk.

In experimental settings, the bias appears in versions of the trolley problem: participants are more willing to allow harm to occur by inaction than to cause the same harm by intervening, suggesting that the act–omission distinction carries moral weight independent of outcomes.

Explanations

Proposed explanations include the greater causal salience of actions, anticipated regret and personal responsibility for outcomes one directly causes, and moral heuristics that treat doing harm as categorically different from allowing harm. The bias is related to, but distinct from, the status quo bias and the identifiable-victim effect.
