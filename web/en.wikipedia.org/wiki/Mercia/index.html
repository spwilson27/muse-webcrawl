Mercia (Old English: Mierce, "the border people") was one of the kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England, occupying the region now known as the English Midlands, centred on the valley of the River Trent. Founded by Angles in the 6th century, Mercia rose to become the dominant power in England south of the Humber during the 7th and 8th centuries — the period known as the Mercian Supremacy — before declining under Viking attack in the 9th century and absorption into the kingdom of Wessex.

Origins and expansion

The Mercians' royal house, the Iclingas, traced its descent from Icel, a figure of Germanic legend. The first historically firm kings are Creoda and his son Pybba (r. c. 593–606). Penda (r. c. 626–655), the last pagan king of Mercia, made the kingdom a great military power, defeating and killing two Northumbrian kings, Edwin (633, at Hatfield Chase, in alliance with Cadwallon of Gwynedd) and Oswald (642, at Maserfield). Penda himself fell at the Battle of the Winwaed in 655. His son Wulfhere (r. 658–675) accepted Christianity and extended Mercian overlordship over much of southern England.

Mercian Supremacy

Under Æthelbald (r. 716–757) and above all Offa (r. 757–796), Mercia dominated all England south of the Humber. Offa controlled Kent, Sussex, East Anglia and Essex, treated with Charlemagne as an equal, introduced the silver penny that became the standard English coinage, and is traditionally credited with Offa's Dyke, the great earthwork along the Welsh border. Mercian influence is also reflected in the Tribal Hidage, a list of tribute-paying peoples.

Decline

After Offa's death Mercian power waned as Wessex rose under Egbert and his successors. Danish Viking armies overran eastern Mercia in the 870s; the kingdom was partitioned, the west remaining under Æthelred, Lord of the Mercians, and his wife Æthelflæd — daughter of Alfred the Great — who governed as "Lady of the Mercians" (911–918) and recovered much territory before her death. Her brother Edward the Elder then absorbed Mercia fully into the emerging Kingdom of England. The name survives in the modern West Mercia Police and in regional usage.
