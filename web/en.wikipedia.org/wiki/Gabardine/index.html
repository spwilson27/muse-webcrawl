Gabardine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

MaterialWool (traditionally), also cotton and synthetics
WeaveWarp-faced twill
Invented1879, by Thomas Burberry
Common usesRaincoats, suits, uniforms, trousers

Gabardine is a tough, tightly woven fabric with a fine diagonal rib on its face and a smooth back. Traditionally made of worsted wool, it is now also produced in cotton, synthetic fibres and blends. The material is durable, resistant to wrinkling and, in its original wool form, notably water-repellent while remaining breathable.

History

The fabric was invented in 1879 by Thomas Burberry, founder of the Burberry fashion house, and patented in 1888. Burberry's innovation was to waterproof the wool yarn before weaving rather than coating the finished cloth, so the resulting gabardine shed rain without becoming stiff or airless. It was adopted for military uniforms — most famously the First World War trench coat — and for expedition clothing: garments of Burberry gabardine were worn by Roald Amundsen's party at the South Pole and by Ernest Shackleton's expeditions, and it clothed early aviators and mountaineers, including the 1953 Everest expedition's outer layers.

Characteristics and uses

Gabardine is woven as a warp-faced twill with many more warp than weft threads, producing the characteristic fine diagonal lines. Because the surface is smooth and hard-wearing, it resists snagging and holds a crease well. Today it is used for suits, trousers, skirts, uniforms, raincoats and overcoats.

Etymology

The word is commonly linked to "gaberdine", an archaic term for a long coat worn by the poor, itself possibly related to the Spanish gabardina. The similarity to "gabardina" and to the word for a waterproof cloak in several Romance languages reinforced its adoption for the rain-resistant cloth.
