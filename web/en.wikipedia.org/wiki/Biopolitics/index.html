Biopolitics

Hero image: public-health administration — vaccination, census-taking, and vital statistics are classic sites of biopolitics.

Biopolitics is a concept in political theory denoting the governance of populations through the management of life itself — birth rates, health, disease, reproduction, and mortality. The term was used by the Swedish political scientist Rudolf Kjellén in the early twentieth century and given its influential modern meaning by the French philosopher Michel Foucault in the 1970s.

Foucault's concept

In The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1 (1976) and his Collège de France lectures Society Must Be Defended (1975–76) and Security, Territory, Population (1977–78), Foucault described a transformation of power: where sovereign power was the right "to make die and let live," modern biopower works "to make live and let die." It operates on two poles: an "anatomo-politics" disciplining individual bodies (in schools, barracks, hospitals), and a "biopolitics of the population" regulating collective biological processes through statistics, public hygiene, demography, and welfare institutions. The emergence of biopolitics in the eighteenth century, Foucault argued, marks the threshold of political modernity.

Later developments

Giorgio Agamben's Homo Sacer (1995) pushed the concept darker, arguing that the production of "bare life" — life that can be killed without sacrifice or punishment, exemplified by the concentration camp — is the hidden foundation of Western politics from antiquity onward. Roberto Esposito countered with an "affirmative biopolitics," while Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri linked biopolitics to the production of social life itself under contemporary capitalism. Achille Mbembe's "necropolitics" examined power over death in colonial and postcolonial contexts.

Applications and criticism

Biopolitics is now applied to public health emergencies, pandemic governance, reproductive politics, genetic screening, immigration control, and eugenics history. Critics fault the concept's elasticity and its tendency to totalize diverse institutions, while defenders value it for revealing how ostensibly neutral policies about health and population are exercises of power.
