Tapir

Tapir
Family: Tapiridae
Order: Perissodactyla (odd-toed ungulates)
Habitat: Forests of Central and South America and Southeast Asia

Tapirs (family Tapiridae, genus Tapirus) are large, herbivorous mammals belonging to the order of odd-toed ungulates, Perissodactyla, which also includes horses and rhinoceroses. They are distinguished by their short, prehensile trunk, formed from the fusion of the nose and upper lip, which they use to grasp leaves and fruit.

Species

Four species are widely recognised. The Brazilian or South American tapir (Tapirus terrestris), the Baird's tapir (T. bairdii) of Central America, and the mountain tapir (T. pinchaque) of the Andes inhabit the Americas, while the Malayan tapir (T. indicus) — the largest species, recognisable by its grey-white "saddle" marking — lives in Southeast Asia. A proposed fifth species, the kabomani tapir, remains disputed.

Behaviour and ecology

Tapirs are generally solitary and nocturnal or crepuscular. They are strong swimmers and often take to water to feed, escape predators or cool off. Their diet consists of leaves, twigs, shoots, fruit and aquatic plants; as consumers of fruit they are important seed dispersers in tropical forests. Calves are born with striped and spotted coats that provide camouflage and fade within months.

Conservation

All tapir species are threatened to varying degrees by habitat loss and hunting, and are listed as Endangered or Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. The mountain tapir is considered the most threatened species in the family.

Evolution

The tapir body plan has changed little over tens of millions of years; fossil tapirs are known from the Eocene of North America and Eurasia, making them a classic example of a conservative mammalian lineage.
