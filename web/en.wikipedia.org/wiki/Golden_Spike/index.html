Golden Spike

The Golden Spike (also known as the Last Spike) is the ceremonial 17.6-karat gold final spike driven by Leland Stanford to join the rails of the first transcontinental railroad across the United States, connecting the Central Pacific Railroad from Sacramento and the Union Pacific Railroad from Omaha, at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869.

The spike was made of solid gold, weighed about 14 ounces, and was approximately 5⅝ inches long. It was inscribed with the names of the railroad officers and directors and the words "May God continue the unity of our Country, as this Railroad unites the two great Oceans of the world."

The ceremony

The completion ceremony, immortalised in A. J. Russell's photograph East and West Shaking Hands, brought together the locomotives Jupiter of the Central Pacific and No. 119 of the Union Pacific. Stanford took the first swing at the spike with a silver hammer and missed; Union Pacific vice-president Thomas Durant also swung and missed. The spike was finally tapped in by telegrapher W. N. Shilling, and the single word "DONE" was telegraphed across the country, setting off celebrations.

Because the gold spike was too valuable to remain in place, it was removed after the ceremony and replaced with an ordinary iron spike. The gold spike was donated to Stanford University by the Stanford family and is now displayed at the Cantor Arts Center there. A second, identical presentation spike and related souvenirs also survive.

Legacy

The site is preserved as the Golden Spike National Historical Park. Each year the ceremony is re-enacted on May 10. The completion of the railroad cut transcontinental travel time from months to about a week and transformed the settlement and economy of the American West.
