Leaving group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In chemistry, a leaving group (also called a nucleofuge) is a molecular fragment that departs with a pair of electrons in a heterolytic bond cleavage. Common leaving groups are anions, neutral molecules such as water and ammonia, and substances with the ability to stabilize the extra electron pair, such as a protonated alcohol.

Leaving group ability

The nature of the leaving group strongly affects reactivity in substitution and elimination reactions. A good rule of thumb is that the weaker the base, the better the leaving group: the conjugate base of a strong acid is a weak base and therefore a good leaving group. Thus the halides I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ >> F⁻ (conjugate bases of strong hydrohalic acids) are good leaving groups, whereas hydroxide, alkoxides, and amide ions are poor ones. Converting a hydroxyl group into a tosylate, mesylate, or triflate turns a poor leaving group into an excellent one.

Leaving group ability also depends on the reaction: in SN1 reactions the leaving group's departure is rate-determining, while in SN2 reactions an incoming nucleophile displaces it in a concerted step. In electrophilic aromatic substitution and addition–elimination chemistry, leaving groups such as halides depart after attack by a nucleophile.

Examples

  • Halides: chloride, bromide, iodide in alkyl halides
  • Water, from protonated alcohols
  • Tosylates, mesylates, triflates (sulfonate esters)
  • Nitrogen gas, from diazonium salts
  • Carboxylates and phenoxides in acyl substitution
