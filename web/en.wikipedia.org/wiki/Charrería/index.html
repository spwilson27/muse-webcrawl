Charrería is the traditional Mexican equestrian culture and competitive sport derived from the cattle-working practices of the colonial haciendas. Its practitioners, charros, perform a codified series of events (suertes) in an arena called a lienzo charro, wearing elaborate embroidered costumes. It is regarded as Mexico's national sport and was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016.

History

Charrería descends from the horsemanship of Spanish colonial New Spain, where Indigenous and mestizo vaqueros on the great haciendas developed techniques for herding cattle. After the Mexican Revolution, as haciendas were broken up, urban charro associations were founded — the Asociación Nacional de Charros dates to 1921 — to preserve the tradition, codifying the events and the distinctive dress derived from the working costume of the hacienda horseman and the styles of Salamanca, Spain.

Events

A charreada (men's competition) comprises nine scored events, including the cala de caballo (demonstration of the horse's training and sliding stop), piales (roping a mare's hind legs), colas (bull tailing), bull riding, terna (team roping of a bull), mare riding, manganas (roping a horse's front legs, on foot and on horseback), and the paso de la muerte ("pass of death"), leaping from one galloping horse onto a bareback mare. Women's teams, escaramuzas, perform synchronized precision riding routines in traditional dresses, riding sidesaddle.

Dress and culture

The charro suit — fitted trousers, short embroidered jacket, and wide-brimmed sombrero — became an emblem of Mexican identity, popularized worldwide by the singing charros of Mexico's Golden Age of cinema and by mariachi musicians, whose dress derives from it. Charrería remains widely practiced in Mexico and the Mexican diaspora in the United States, though some events have drawn criticism from animal-welfare advocates.
