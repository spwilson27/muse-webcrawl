Cosmological argument

The cosmological argument is an argument for the existence of God which claims that everything that exists must have a cause for its existence, and that this chain of causes cannot regress infinitely, so there must be a first cause (or an uncaused, necessary being), identified with God. It is a type of a posteriori argument, reasoning from the existence of the cosmos to its ultimate explanation.

Forms

Argument from contingency

Associated with Thomas Aquinas's "Third Way" and with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, this version argues that the world consists of contingent things — things that exist but might not have — and that the existence of contingent things requires a necessary being whose existence is explained by its own nature.

Kalam cosmological argument

The kalām cosmological argument, developed in medieval Islamic theology and revived by William Lane Craig, runs: whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause. Supporters appeal to the philosophical difficulty of an actual infinite past and to the Big Bang model in cosmology.

Argument from motion

Aquinas's "First Way" argues from change or motion: things are moved by other things, and the series of movers must terminate in a first unmoved mover.

Criticism

David Hume questioned why the universe itself could not be the necessary being, and why a cause must be sought for the whole rather than its parts. Immanuel Kant argued that the argument covertly depends on the ontological argument. Bertrand Russell, debating Frederick Copleston in 1948, held that the universe "is just there, and that's all". Modern critics also ask why the first cause must be a single, personal God rather than something else, and dispute the premise that the universe "began to exist".
