Bilirubin is a yellow-orange pigment formed by the breakdown of heme, the iron-containing component of haemoglobin in red blood cells. It is the principal pigment of bile and is responsible for the yellow colour of bruises as they heal and for the yellow discoloration of the skin and eyes in jaundice.

Metabolism

Senescent red blood cells are destroyed in the spleen and elsewhere by macrophages. Their haemoglobin is split into globin and heme; the enzyme heme oxygenase converts heme to green biliverdin, which is then reduced to bilirubin. This "unconjugated" (indirect) bilirubin is insoluble in water and travels in the blood bound to albumin to the liver. There, the enzyme UDP-glucuronosyltransferase conjugates it with glucuronic acid, forming water-soluble "direct" bilirubin, which is excreted in bile into the intestine. Gut bacteria convert bilirubin to urobilinogen and stercobilin, the latter giving faeces their brown colour; some urobilinogen is reabsorbed and excreted in urine as urobilin.

Clinical significance

Elevated blood bilirubin (hyperbilirubinemia) causes jaundice and may result from excessive red-cell breakdown (haemolysis), impaired liver conjugation — as in the benign inherited Gilbert's syndrome — or obstruction of bile flow. Newborns commonly develop physiological jaundice because their conjugation enzymes are immature; severe cases are treated with phototherapy, which photo-isomerises bilirubin into excretable forms, and untreated extreme elevation can cause brain damage (kernicterus). Bilirubin is measured in liver function tests, reported as total and direct fractions. Bilirubin also acts as an antioxidant at physiological concentrations, and is a major component of some gallstones (pigment stones).
