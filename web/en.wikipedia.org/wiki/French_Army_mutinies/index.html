French Army mutinies

French poilus in 1917, the year of the mutinies.

The French Army mutinies of 1917 took place among French troops on the Western Front during the First World War. They began in the aftermath of the disastrous Nivelle Offensive of April 1917, in which General Robert Nivelle's promised decisive breakthrough along the Chemin des Dames collapsed with heavy casualties.

Course of the mutinies

Beginning in late April and spreading through May and June, soldiers in dozens of divisions — eventually affecting nearly half the French infantry divisions on the front — refused orders to attack, though in most cases they continued to hold defensive positions. Their grievances included futile offensives, poor food and rest, infrequent leave, and low pay. News of the Russian Revolution and pacifist agitation played a smaller role than material conditions.

Suppression and reform

General Philippe Pétain replaced Nivelle as commander-in-chief and combined repression with reform: thousands of soldiers were tried by court-martial, several hundred death sentences were pronounced, and around fifty mutineers were executed, though estimates vary. Pétain simultaneously improved leave, rest, and food, and limited major offensives until American forces and tanks arrived in strength in 1918. The mutinies were kept secret from the Germans and largely from the French public; official acknowledgment and historical study developed only decades later.
