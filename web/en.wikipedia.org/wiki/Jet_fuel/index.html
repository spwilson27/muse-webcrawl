Jet fuel

Type: Aviation turbine fuel
Main grades: Jet A, Jet A-1, Jet B
Base: Kerosene-range hydrocarbons
Freezing point: −40 °C (Jet A); −47 °C (Jet A-1)
Flash point: minimum 38 °C

Jet fuel or aviation turbine fuel (ATF) is a type of aviation fuel designed for use in aircraft powered by gas-turbine engines. It is a kerosene-range mixture of hydrocarbons, colorless to straw-colored in appearance, and is the most common fuel for commercial and military jet aircraft worldwide.

Types

The principal civil grades are Jet A, used mainly in the United States, and Jet A-1, used in most of the rest of the world; they differ chiefly in freezing point (Jet A-1's lower maximum of −47 °C suits long polar routes) and in the mandatory inclusion of an antistatic additive in Jet A-1. Jet B, a wider-cut blend of kerosene and naphtha, offers better cold-weather performance and is used in very cold climates such as northern Canada and Alaska, though it is more hazardous to handle. Military grades include JP-5 for aircraft carriers and JP-8, widely used by NATO forces. The Soviet/Russian standard TS-1 remains in use in parts of Eastern Europe and Central Asia.

Properties and additives

Jet fuel must remain liquid at the low temperatures of high-altitude flight, resist thermal degradation, and burn cleanly and stably. Additives may include antioxidants, metal deactivators, corrosion inhibitors, fuel-system icing inhibitors, and static dissipaters. Quality is tightly specified (for example, by ASTM D1655 and Defence Standard 91-091) and checked throughout the supply chain.

Production, use, and alternatives

Jet fuel is refined from crude oil, accounting for roughly 6–10 percent of a typical barrel's output. Global consumption before the COVID-19 pandemic was on the order of 8 million barrels per day. Because aviation is difficult to decarbonize, sustainable aviation fuels (SAFs) made from biomass, waste oils, or synthetic processes are being blended with conventional jet fuel and promoted by industry and regulators.
