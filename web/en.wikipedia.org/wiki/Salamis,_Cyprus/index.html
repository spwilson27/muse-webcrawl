Salamis (Greek: Σαλαμίς) was an ancient Greek city-state on the east coast of Cyprus, at the mouth of the Pedieos River, some 6 km north of modern Famagusta. For centuries it was the most important city of the island. According to tradition it was founded after the Trojan War by Teucer, son of Telamon, king of the Greek island of Salamis, from which it took its name.

History

Archaeological evidence shows settlement from the Late Bronze Age; the nearby royal necropolis contains rich tombs of the 8th and 7th centuries BC, with burials accompanied by horses and chariots recalling Homeric funerary customs. Salamis resisted Persian rule, joining the Ionian Revolt, and its king Evagoras I (r. 411–374 BC) made it a flourishing centre of Greek culture. The city passed through Hellenistic and Roman hands, prospering as a great commercial port; it was the traditional birthplace of Saint Barnabas, companion of Paul, who preached in its synagogues. Renamed Constantia by the emperor Constantius II after an earthquake in AD 342, the city was finally abandoned after Arab raids in the 7th century, its population moving to Famagusta.

Archaeology

The excavated ruins include a large gymnasium with marble colonnades, Roman baths, a theatre seating some 15,000 spectators, an amphitheatre, a forum, the temple of Zeus Salaminios, and extensive Roman villas with mosaics. The site lies in the area administered since 1974 by the Turkish Cypriot authorities.
