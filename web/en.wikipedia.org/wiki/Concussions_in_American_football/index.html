Concussions in American football

Concussions in American football have been a major public-health and legal concern since the early 2000s, when research linked repeated head impacts in the National Football League (NFL) and other levels of the sport to long-term brain damage, including the neurodegenerative disease chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE).

Background

A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury caused by a blow to the head or body that makes the brain move rapidly inside the skull. Linemen and other players may sustain hundreds of subconcussive impacts per season. In 2002, pathologist Bennet Omalu reported CTE in the brain of former Pittsburgh Steelers center Mike Webster; subsequent studies by Boston University researchers found CTE in a large proportion of examined former professional players, though researchers caution about selection bias in donated brains.

Response

The NFL, after years of dispute over the science, introduced a formal concussion protocol, independent neurological consultants on sidelines, and rule changes penalising helmet-to-helmet hits and hits on defenceless players. In 2015 the league reached a settlement, ultimately valued at over one billion dollars, with retired players over claims it concealed the risks of head injury. Youth and high-school football participation declined in many areas as awareness grew, and equipment, tackling technique ("heads up" tackling), and practice-contact limits were revised across levels of the game.
