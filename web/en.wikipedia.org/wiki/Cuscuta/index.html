Cuscuta, commonly known as dodder, is a genus of about 200 species of parasitic plants in the morning glory family, Convolvulaceae. Dodder plants are leafless or nearly so, forming tangled masses of thin yellow, orange, or red thread-like stems that twine around host plants and draw nutrients from them through specialized organs called haustoria.

Description

A dodder seed germinates in the soil, and the seedling must quickly find a host stem, guided partly by volatile chemicals released by suitable hosts. Once attached, it inserts haustoria into the host's vascular tissue, tapping both xylem and phloem. The original root in the soil then withers, and the parasite becomes entirely dependent on its host. Because dodder contains little or no chlorophyll, it performs little photosynthesis of its own.

The flowers are small, bell-shaped, and white, pink, or cream, borne in clusters; the fruit is a small capsule with tiny seeds that can persist in soil for many years.

Ecology and impact

Dodder can seriously weaken host plants and reduce crop yields, and several species are serious agricultural weeds of crops such as alfalfa, clover, and tomatoes. Dodder also transmits plant viruses between hosts and can exchange messenger RNA, proteins, and even genetic material with its hosts. Research has shown that dodders connected to multiple hosts can pass herbivore-induced warning signals from one host plant to another.
