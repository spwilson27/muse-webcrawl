Copts (Coptic: ⲛⲓⲣⲉⲙⲛ̀ⲭⲏⲙⲓ, niremənkhēmi; Arabic: أَقْبَاط, aqbāṭ) are a Christian ethnoreligious group native to Northeast Africa who primarily inhabit the area of modern Egypt. They are the largest Christian community in Egypt and the Middle East, and trace their church to the Apostle Mark in the 1st century AD.

The word "Copt" derives, via Arabic qubṭ, from the Greek Aigýptios (Egyptian); in earlier usage it referred to Egyptians generally, but after the Arab conquest it came to denote the Christian Egyptians who retained their faith.

History

Egypt became predominantly Christian under Roman and Byzantine rule, and the Church of Alexandria became a leading centre of theology and monasticism. The Council of Chalcedon (451) split the Egyptian church from the imperial church over Christology; the Coptic Orthodox Church adheres to Oriental Orthodoxy. After the Arab conquest (639–642), Copts gradually became a minority as Egypt's population converted to Islam and adopted Arabic, though Copts remained prominent in administration and finance for centuries. In modern Egypt, Copts have experienced periods of tolerance and of discrimination and sectarian violence.

Diaspora

Significant Coptic emigration began in the second half of the 20th century, especially after the 1950s–60s. Large communities exist in the United States, Canada, Australia and Europe, where hundreds of Coptic parishes have been established.

Culture

Coptic culture preserves ancient Egyptian continuities — including elements of its calendar (the Coptic calendar of Martyrs) and liturgical language — within a Christian framework. Coptic art and architecture, monastic traditions centred on desert monasteries such as those of Wadi El Natrun, and festivals such as Sham Ennessim are notable features.
