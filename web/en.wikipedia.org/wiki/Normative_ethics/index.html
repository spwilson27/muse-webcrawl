Normative ethics


Labelled placeholder: classical ethics

Normative ethics is the study of ethical action and sets out the rightness or wrongness of actions. It is the branch of philosophical ethics that investigates the questions that arise regarding how one ought to act, morally speaking. Normative ethics is distinct from meta-ethics in that normative ethics examines standards for the rightness and wrongness of actions, while meta-ethics studies the meaning and nature of moral language and metaphysics of moral facts. It is also distinct from descriptive ethics, which presents empirical findings on people's moral beliefs.

Approaches

Virtue ethics

Virtue ethics, founded by Aristotle and revived in the 20th century by philosophers such as G. E. M. Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre, emphasises the character of the moral agent and the virtues (courage, temperance, justice, practical wisdom) rather than rules or consequences.

Deontology

Deontological ethics holds that the morality of an action depends on whether it conforms to rules or duties. Kant's categorical imperative is the best-known formulation: act only according to maxims that can be universalised, and treat humanity always also as an end, never merely as a means.

Consequentialism

Consequentialism holds that the consequences of one's conduct are the ultimate basis for judgement; utilitarianism, associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, directs agents to maximise overall well-being. Rule consequentialism and act consequentialism are major variants.

Other approaches

Additional normative frameworks include moral contractarianism and contractualism, care ethics, pragmatist ethics, and discourse ethics.

Criteria of moral rightness

Commonly proposed criteria include intrinsic value, the good of others, universalisability, impartiality, and respect for autonomy. Debates over moral dilemmas — such as trolley problems — test how these criteria apply in conflict situations.
