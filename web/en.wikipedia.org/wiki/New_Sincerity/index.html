New Sincerity

New Sincerity is a term used in music, aesthetics, and cultural criticism to describe art that values earnestness, honesty, and direct emotional expression over irony, detachment, and postmodern self-consciousness. The phrase has been applied at various times to movements in literature, film, television, and popular music, generally as a reaction against the dominance of irony in late twentieth-century culture.

History

The term gained currency in the 1980s and 1990s, when critics applied it to musicians working outside the irony-laden mainstream of rock and alternative music. It was revived in the 1990s in connection with the writer David Foster Wallace, whose essay "E Unibus Pluram: Television and U.S. Fiction" (1993) called for a new generation of literary "anti-rebels" who would dare to endorse single-entendre principles and treat old human problems with reverence and conviction. Wallace's fiction, particularly Infinite Jest (1996), became closely associated with the sensibility.

Characteristics

Works described as New Sincerity typically favor plain emotional directness, moral seriousness, and a willingness to risk sentimentality or embarrassment. In film, the label has been linked to directors such as Wes Anderson and to the mumblecore movement; in television, it has been applied to series that balance comedy with genuine emotional stakes. Critics debate whether the term names a coherent movement or simply a recurring cultural mood that surfaces whenever irony is felt to be exhausted.

Related concepts

New Sincerity is related to the broader idea of post-irony and to metamodernism, which describes a cultural oscillation between modern earnestness and postmodern irony. See also New Thought for an unrelated nineteenth-century movement with a similar name.
