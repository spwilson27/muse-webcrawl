Horizontal integration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Horizontal integration is the process of a company increasing production of goods or services at the same part of the supply chain, typically by acquiring or merging with competitors operating at the same level. It contrasts with vertical integration, in which a company expands into different stages of production, such as a manufacturer acquiring its suppliers or distributors.

Purposes and effects

Companies pursue horizontal integration to increase market share, achieve economies of scale, reduce competition, gain access to new customers and markets, and eliminate duplicate costs. A horizontal merger between direct competitors can create a monopoly or oligopoly if the combined firm controls a dominant share of the market, which is why such combinations are closely scrutinised by competition and antitrust authorities.

Examples

Classic examples include John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, which grew by acquiring rival refiners in the late 19th century, and the formation of large media, banking, and airline groups through competitor mergers in the 20th and 21st centuries. The Walt Disney Company's acquisition of 21st Century Fox in 2019 is a prominent modern example of horizontal integration in the entertainment industry.

Criticism

Critics argue that horizontal integration can reduce consumer choice, raise prices, and stifle innovation. Regulators such as the United States Federal Trade Commission and the European Commission may block or impose conditions on horizontal mergers that would substantially lessen competition.
