The 1693 Sicily earthquake struck parts of southern Italy near Sicily and Calabria on 11 January at around 21:00 local time. This earthquake — preceded by a damaging foreshock on 9 January — destroyed at least 70 towns and cities, seriously damaged an area of 5,600 square kilometres (2,200 sq mi), and caused the deaths of about 60,000 people. It is among the most powerful earthquakes recorded in Italian history, with an estimated moment magnitude of 7.4, and was followed by a tsunami that devastated coastal settlements along the Ionian Sea.

Earthquake

A strong foreshock on 9 January 1693 damaged towns in the Val di Noto in southeastern Sicily, including Augusta, Avola, and Syracuse. The main shock two days later was far more destructive: Catania, Sicily's second city, was almost completely destroyed with a large fraction of its population killed. Towns such as Noto, Ragusa, Modica, Ispica, Sortino, and Lentini were levelled. The shaking was felt across southern Italy, Malta, and as far away as North Africa.

Tsunami

The earthquake generated a tsunami along the east coast of Sicily. Contemporary accounts describe the sea receding and then returning in waves that struck Augusta and Catania, destroying harbour installations and inundating low-lying districts, causing additional deaths.

Aftermath and reconstruction

The disaster prompted one of the great urban reconstruction programmes of the Baroque era. Under the direction of the viceroy of Sicily and local planners, destroyed towns were rebuilt — some on new sites, such as Noto and Ragusa — in the distinctive style now known as Sicilian Baroque. The Late Baroque Towns of the Val di Noto were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2002.
