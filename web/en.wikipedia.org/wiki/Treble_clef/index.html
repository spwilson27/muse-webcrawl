Treble clef

The treble clef, or G clef, at the start of a staff.

The treble clef, also known as the G clef, is a musical clef used to notate pitches in a higher range. It is the most commonly used clef in modern music notation, employed for most female voices, the violin, flute, oboe, clarinet (in most registers), trumpet, horn parts, and the right hand of the piano, among many others.

Position and meaning

The symbol is a stylized letter G whose spiral curls around the second line of the staff, indicating that the line is the G above middle C (G4). The notes on the lines of the treble staff, from bottom to top, are E–G–B–D–F, commonly remembered by mnemonics such as "Every Good Boy Does Fine," while the spaces spell F–A–C–E.

History

The treble clef evolved from the Gothic letter G used in early staff notation. During the 17th and 18th centuries it acquired its elaborate curled shape in French violin music, and is sometimes still called the "French clef" when placed on the first line, a variant once used for violin music. An octave-shifted variant, with a small "8" below, is standard for tenor voices and the guitar.

Use

Instruments in the treble range — and many transposing instruments such as the B-flat clarinet and trumpet — read the treble clef. Singers of soprano and alto parts typically read it as well, while tenor parts are written in the treble clef sounding an octave lower. Together with the bass clef it forms the grand staff used for piano and harp music.
