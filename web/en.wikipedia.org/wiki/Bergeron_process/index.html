Bergeron process
Also known asWegener–Bergeron–Findeisen process; cold rain process
Proposed byAlfred Wegener (1911); Tor Bergeron (1928–1935); Walter Findeisen (1938)
Key conditionMixed-phase cloud with ice crystals and supercooled water droplets below 0 °C
MechanismGrowth of ice by vapour deposition at the expense of evaporating droplets, driven by the difference in saturation vapour pressure over ice and water

The Bergeron process, also called the Wegener–Bergeron–Findeisen process, is a mechanism of precipitation growth in cold clouds. It explains how ice crystals in a mixed-phase cloud grow rapidly to precipitable size at the expense of surrounding supercooled water droplets. It is the dominant way rain forms in much of the mid-latitudes: most raindrops begin as snowflakes that melt as they fall.

Mechanism

Below 0 °C, cloud droplets often remain liquid in a supercooled state, while any ice crystals present coexist with them. The saturation vapour pressure over ice is lower than that over supercooled water at the same temperature — with the difference greatest at about −12 °C. Air that is exactly saturated with respect to water is therefore supersaturated (by roughly 10–12 per cent) with respect to ice.

Consequently water vapour deposits onto the ice crystals, the ambient vapour pressure drops below water saturation, and the droplets evaporate to replenish it. The net effect transfers mass from droplets to crystals: the crystals grow quickly by deposition, develop into snowflakes, aggregate and begin to fall, while the droplets shrink. In about 20–30 minutes crystals can reach sizes large enough to precipitate.

History

Alfred Wegener noted the vapour-pressure difference in 1911. Tor Bergeron, of the Bergen School, developed the theory in his 1928 dissertation and 1935 paper on precipitation physics, and Walter Findeisen extended and promoted it in 1938, hence the triple name. Observations of aircraft icing and radar studies later confirmed that much mid-latitude rain originates in the ice phase aloft.

Significance

The process is fundamental to cloud seeding: introducing artificial ice nuclei (such as silver iodide or dry ice) into supercooled clouds aims to trigger Bergeron growth. It also explains the rapid glaciation of clouds and the bright band seen on weather radar where snowflakes melt. In warmer clouds lacking ice, rain instead forms by the collision–coalescence ("warm rain") process.
