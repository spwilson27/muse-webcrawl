Rotational symmetry

Rotational symmetry, also known as radial symmetry in geometry, is the property a shape has when it looks the same after some rotation by a partial turn. The number of distinct orientations in which the object looks identical is its order of rotational symmetry; the angles at which this occurs are multiples of 360° divided by the order.

Examples

A square has rotational symmetry of order 4 (every 90°), an equilateral triangle of order 3 (every 120°), and a regular hexagon of order 6. A circle has rotational symmetry of infinite order. Letters such as S, N and Z have order 2 (point symmetry) under 180° rotation. Many flowers, snowflakes, starfish and manufactured objects such as wheels and propellers exhibit rotational symmetry.

Formal treatment

In group-theoretic terms, the rotations preserving a planar object form a cyclic group Cn; combined with reflections they form the dihedral group Dn. Rotational symmetry about a point in the plane or an axis in space is fundamental in crystallography, where only orders 2, 3, 4 and 6 are compatible with a periodic lattice (the crystallographic restriction theorem).
