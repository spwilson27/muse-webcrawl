Titration

Titration is a quantitative analytical technique in which a solution of known concentration (the titrant) is added from a burette to a measured volume of analyte until the reaction between them is just complete — the endpoint, usually shown by an indicator's colour change or a pH meter.

Because the stoichiometry is known, the volume used reveals the analyte's amount. Acid–base titrations are the classroom staple, but redox (permanganate), complexometric (EDTA) and precipitation (silver nitrate) titrations are equally standard.

[Image: A burette dripping into a conical flask as the indicator turns pink]

Technique

The analyte is pipetted into a conical flask with a few drops of indicator; titrant is added slowly near the endpoint until one drop changes the colour permanently (the end point should match the theoretical equivalence point). Concordant titres are averaged, and a back-titration handles insoluble or slow-reacting samples such as antacid tablets.

History

Volumetric analysis grew up in the late 18th and early 19th centuries with the French alkali and bleach industries; Gay-Lussac's methods and Mohr's burette (1855) gave it roughly its modern form.
