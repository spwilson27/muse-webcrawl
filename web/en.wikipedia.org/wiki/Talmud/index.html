The Talmud is the central text of Rabbinic Judaism and the primary source of Jewish religious law (halakha) and theology. It consists of the Mishnah (c. 200 CE), a written compilation of the Oral Torah edited by Judah the Prince, and the Gemara, a commentary and elaboration on the Mishnah composed over the following centuries. Two versions exist: the Jerusalem Talmud (c. 400 CE) and the longer, more authoritative Babylonian Talmud (c. 500 CE).

Content and style

Organised in six orders and 63 tractates, the Talmud ranges over civil, criminal and ritual law, ethics, medicine, astronomy, folklore and history. Its characteristic style is dialectical: rabbis of successive generations argue cases, test definitions against hypotheticals, and often leave questions unresolved ("teiku"). Narrative passages (aggadah) sit alongside legal analysis (halakha). Study of the Talmud, traditionally in pairs (chavruta) in a yeshiva, remains the core of advanced Jewish learning; the page-a-day (Daf Yomi) cycle, begun in 1923, completes the Babylonian Talmud in about seven and a half years.

History

Printed first in full in Venice (1520s, the Bomberg edition), the Talmud fixed a standard page layout — central text flanked by Rashi's commentary and the Tosafot — still used today. It was repeatedly censored, confiscated and burned in medieval Christian Europe, including the Paris disputation of 1240 and the burning of Talmuds in 1242, yet survived as the foundation of Jewish communal life and law into the modern period.
