Jeans

Image placeholder: Jeans

Jeans are a type of trousers made from denim or dungaree cloth. Often the term "jeans" refers to a particular style of trousers, called "blue jeans", which were popularized by Levi Strauss & Co. in 1873. Because of their durability, jeans were originally intended as workwear for labourers in the American West. Starting in the 1950s, jeans became popular clothing among young people, and today they are worn worldwide in many styles and colours.

History

The fabrics denim and dungaree originated in Nîmes, France, and Dongari Killa, India, respectively, and durable twill trousers were made in Genoa, Italy — the English word "jean" derives from Gênes, French for Genoa. In 1873, Levi Strauss and tailor Jacob Davis patented the use of copper rivets to strengthen stress points on denim work pants, creating the modern blue jean. Competing makers such as Lee and Wrangler followed.

In the 20th century jeans were popularized by cowboys and Hollywood westerns, adopted by rebellious youth in the 1950s, and became designer fashion from the 1970s onward. Styles include straight, skinny, bootcut, flare, and wide-leg fits.

Manufacture and impact

Traditional jeans are dyed blue with indigo and use orange stitching, rivets, and a button fly or zipper. Denim production and the fashion industry's "stone-washing" and distressing processes have raised environmental and labour concerns, including water use and sandblasting hazards, prompting growth in sustainable production methods.
