Petra is an archaeological city in southern Jordan, famous for its rock-cut architecture and water conduit system. Established possibly as early as 312 BC as Raqmu, the capital of the Nabataean Kingdom, it prospered from the incense trade routes carrying frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia toward the Mediterranean and Silk Road markets. The Nabataeans, Arab nomads turned master hydraulic engineers, made a desert city of perhaps 20,000–30,000 people bloom through dams, cisterns and channels that harvested flash-flood water.

The site

Visitors enter through the Siq, a narrow gorge over a kilometre long flanked by 80-metre cliffs, which opens suddenly onto Al-Khazneh, "the Treasury" — a 40-metre-high Hellenistic facade carved into rose-red sandstone, probably a royal tomb of the 1st century BC. Beyond lie the Street of Facades, a Roman-style theatre, the colonnaded street, and the royal tombs; a steep climb leads to Ad Deir, the Monastery, even larger than the Treasury. Rome annexed the kingdom in 106 AD; an earthquake in 363 and shifting trade routes led to abandonment. Petra remained known locally but was revealed to the Western world by the explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt in 1812.
