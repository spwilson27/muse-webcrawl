Hindu reform movements

Hindu reform movements are religious and social movements within Hinduism that seek to reform Hindu society and practice. They arose particularly during the 19th century, in the context of British colonial rule in India, when reformers responded both to social problems such as sati, child marriage and caste discrimination, and to Western and missionary critiques of Hindu religion.

Major movements

The Brahmo Samaj, founded by Ram Mohan Roy in 1828, rejected image worship and promoted monotheism based on the Upanishads; Roy campaigned successfully for the abolition of sati in 1829. The Arya Samaj, founded by Dayananda Saraswati in 1875, called for a return to the authority of the Vedas, opposed caste by birth, and promoted education and the shuddhi (reconversion) movement.

The Ramakrishna Movement, inspired by the saint Ramakrishna and organised by his disciple Swami Vivekananda, who represented Hinduism at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893, emphasised the harmony of religions, Vedanta philosophy and social service. Other movements include the Prarthana Samaj in Maharashtra, the Theosophical Society's work in India, and 20th-century movements such as the Self-Respect Movement's critique of caste (in its Tamil context), the Swaminarayan tradition's reforms, and modern organisations such as the Chinmaya Mission and ISKCON.

Legacy

The reform movements contributed to the codification of Hindu personal law after Indian independence, the legal abolition of untouchability, and the broader currents of Indian nationalism. Scholars sometimes group these currents under the term "Neo-Hinduism," though the appropriateness of the label is debated.
