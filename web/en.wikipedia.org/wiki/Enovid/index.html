Enovid

Enovid
Type: Combined oral contraceptive pill
Components: Norethynodrel + mestranol
Manufacturer: G. D. Searle & Company
Approved as contraceptive (US): 1960
Discontinued (US): 1988

Enovid was a medication consisting of the progestin norethynodrel and the estrogen mestranol, manufactured by G. D. Searle & Company. It was the first combined oral contraceptive pill, approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for contraceptive use in 1960 (having been marketed since 1957 for gynaecological disorders), and it became simply known as "the Pill".

Development

Enovid grew out of research by Gregory Pincus and Min Chueh Chang at the Worcester Foundation, financed largely by Katharine McCormick and championed by Margaret Sanger, with clinical development by John Rock and Celso-Ramón García. Large-scale field trials in Puerto Rico in 1956 demonstrated near-perfect contraceptive efficacy. Because marketing a contraceptive was legally and socially sensitive, Searle first obtained approval for menstrual disorders, with a label that warned of the "side effect" of preventing ovulation.

Formulation and history

The original Enovid 10 mg contained far higher hormone doses than modern pills: 9.85 mg norethynodrel and 150 µg mestranol. Lower-dose versions (5 mg, 2.5 mg and 2 mg) followed as concerns grew about thromboembolism and other side effects, which led to Senate hearings in 1970 and the first patient package inserts for a prescription drug in the United States. Searle discontinued Enovid in the US in 1988.

Impact

Enovid's arrival had profound social consequences: for the first time women could reliably control fertility independently of intercourse, contributing to changes in sexual behaviour, marriage timing, and women's participation in higher education and professional careers. It is routinely listed among the most influential pharmaceutical products of the twentieth century.
