Neo-Darwinism

Neo-Darwinism is generally used to describe any integration of Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection with Gregor Mendel's theory of genetics. Originally used in the late 19th century for the views of August Weismann and Alfred Russel Wallace — who rejected Lamarckian inheritance of acquired characteristics — the term later came to be applied to the modern synthesis of the 1930s and 1940s.

History

After Darwin's death, some of his followers, notably Weismann with his germ plasm theory, argued that natural selection was the sole major driver of evolution and that acquired traits were not inherited. Weismann's barrier between germ line and somatic cells became a central tenet. The rediscovery of Mendel's laws in 1900 initially seemed at odds with gradual selection, but work by R. A. Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane and Sewall Wright unified genetics and selection mathematically, forming population genetics and the modern synthesis.

Usage

The term is used inconsistently: sometimes as a synonym for the modern synthesis, sometimes by critics of mainstream evolutionary biology, and sometimes for the gene-centred view associated with Richard Dawkins.
