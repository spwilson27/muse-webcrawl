Silver Arrows

A Mercedes-Benz W25, one of the original Silberpfeile.

Silver Arrows (German: Silberpfeile) is the legendary nickname given to the silver racing cars of Germany — above all the Grand Prix machines of Mercedes-Benz and Auto Union in the 1930s, and later the cars of Mercedes-Benz in Formula One and sports-car racing. The name is inseparable from the tradition that German racing cars are painted (or left) silver, Germany's international racing colour in the early 20th century.

Origin of the legend

According to the famous story, at the 1934 Eifelrennen at the Nürburgring the new Mercedes-Benz W25 weighed in slightly over the 750 kg maximum; overnight, the team stripped off the car's white paint, leaving the bare aluminium body in gleaming silver and bringing the car within the limit. Manfred von Brauchitsch then won the race, and the "Silver Arrow" was born. Historians debate details of the tale — photographs show silver Mercedes cars earlier — but racing manager Alfred Neubauer's memoir fixed it in legend. Through the later 1930s the Mercedes and Auto Union Silver Arrows, backed by the Nazi regime as propaganda, dominated Grand Prix racing with drivers such as Rudolf Caracciola, Bernd Rosemeyer, Hermann Lang and Tazio Nuvolari (for Auto Union).

Post-war revival

Mercedes-Benz returned in 1954 with the W196 Formula One car and the 300 SLR sports car; Juan Manuel Fangio won the 1954 and 1955 world championships in silver. Mercedes withdrew from motorsport after the 1955 Le Mans disaster and returned as an engine supplier in the 1990s, then as a full works team in 2010. In striking silver liveries, the team won eight consecutive Constructors' Championships (2014–2021) and Lewis Hamilton won six of his seven drivers' titles with the team.

Legacy

The term is now used for Mercedes-AMG Formula One cars generally, and occasionally for other silver German racers. The image of the silver arrow also influenced national racing colours long after painted liveries gave way to sponsorship branding.
