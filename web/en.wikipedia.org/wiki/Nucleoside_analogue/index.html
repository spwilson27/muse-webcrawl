Nucleoside analogue

Nucleoside analogues are a class of drugs that mimic the structure of natural nucleosides — the building blocks of DNA and RNA — and interfere with nucleic acid synthesis. They are used chiefly as antiviral and anticancer agents. Related compounds in which the sugar moiety carries further modifications or which include a phosphate group are often grouped with them as nucleotide analogues.

Mechanism

After being taken up by cells and phosphorylated to their triphosphate forms, nucleoside analogues are incorporated by viral or cellular polymerases into a growing nucleic acid chain. Many lack a 3′-hydroxyl group, so the next nucleotide cannot be added and chain elongation terminates. Others induce mutations or inhibit enzymes of nucleotide metabolism. Selectivity arises because viral polymerases often accept the analogues more readily than human polymerases do, though mitochondrial toxicity is a known adverse effect of some agents.

Examples

Important nucleoside analogues include aciclovir and ganciclovir (against herpesviruses), zidovudine (AZT), lamivudine and tenofovir (the latter a nucleotide analogue) against HIV, ribavirin and sofosbuvir against hepatitis C, remdesivir against coronaviruses, and cytarabine, gemcitabine and azacitidine in cancer chemotherapy. The fluoropyrimidine capecitabine, a prodrug of 5-fluorouracil, is another antimetabolite of this family.
