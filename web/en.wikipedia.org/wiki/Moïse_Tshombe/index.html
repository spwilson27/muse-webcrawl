Moïse Kapenda Tshombe (10 November 1919 – 29 June 1969) was a Congolese businessman and statesman. He led the CONAKAT party in the Belgian Congo and, days after Congolese independence in 1960, proclaimed the secession of the mineral-rich southern province of Katanga, precipitating the Congo Crisis. Backed by Belgian mining interests and white mercenaries, his State of Katanga resisted the central government and the United Nations until 1963. After a period in exile, Tshombe returned as prime minister of a reunified Congo in 1964, suppressed the Simba rebellion with mercenary and Western support, and was dismissed by President Joseph Kasa-Vubu in 1965. He died in detention in Algeria after his plane was hijacked in 1967.

Early life and career

Tshombe was born into a wealthy Lunda trading family in Musumba and was educated at American Methodist mission schools. He took over and expanded his father's chain of stores and mills in Katanga, becoming one of the Congo's most successful African businessmen, though some ventures failed and he was briefly imprisoned for embezzlement charges he denied. In 1958 he co-founded CONAKAT, a party advocating a federal Congo dominated by Katanga's "authentic" peoples and close ties to Belgium.

Katanga secession

When the Congo became independent on 30 June 1960 under Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba, Tshombe — having become provincial president of Katanga — declared Katanga's independence on 11 July, with Belgian troops intervening against the mutinous Congolese army. The secession protected the copper and cobalt revenues of the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga, which paid taxes to Tshombe's regime rather than Léopoldville. The UN operation in the Congo (ONUC) gradually escalated against Katanga, particularly after the murder of Lumumba in January 1961, for which a Belgian parliamentary inquiry later found moral responsibility among Belgian and Katangese authorities. Fighting in late 1962 (Operation Grandslam) ended the secession in January 1963, and Tshombe fled to Spain.

Prime minister and final years

Recalled in July 1964 to head a government of national reconciliation, Tshombe used South African and Rhodesian-recruited mercenaries, led by figures such as Mike Hoare, and American air support to crush the eastern Simba rebellion. His reliance on mercenaries and his popularity in the West made him a Cold War symbol; at home, his dismissal by Kasa-Vubu in October 1965 set the stage for Mobutu Sese Seko's coup. Sentenced to death in absentia for treason in 1967, Tshombe was living in Spain when the aircraft carrying him was hijacked to Algeria in June 1967; he was held under house arrest and died there of heart failure two years later.
