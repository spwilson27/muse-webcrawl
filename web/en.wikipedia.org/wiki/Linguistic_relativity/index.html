Linguistic relativity

FieldLinguistic anthropology; cognitive science

The principle of linguistic relativity, commonly known as the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, holds that the structure of a language influences its speakers' worldview and cognition, so that speakers of different languages may perceive and think about the world differently.

Versions and history

The idea has roots in the work of Wilhelm von Humboldt and was developed by Franz Boas, Edward Sapir and Sapir's student Benjamin Lee Whorf, who drew on examples such as Hopi concepts of time and the vocabulary of industrial safety ("empty" gasoline drums being treated as harmless). In its strong form — linguistic determinism, the claim that language determines thought and that untranslatable categories imprison their speakers — the hypothesis is now generally rejected. Weaker forms, in which language merely biases attention, memory and categorisation, remain an active research programme.

Evidence and debate

Classic test domains include colour terminology, where languages divide the spectrum differently and experiments have found small effects on colour discrimination; spatial reference, where languages such as Guugu Yimithirr that use absolute (compass-like) directions correlate with remarkable dead-reckoning abilities; grammatical gender, number marking and time metaphors. Critics from Steven Pinker onward have argued that thought is conducted in a "mentalese" independent of natural language, while neo-Whorfian researchers such as John Lucy, Stephen Levinson and Lera Boroditsky have reported experimental effects of grammar on non-linguistic cognition, keeping a moderate version of relativity alive in cognitive science.
