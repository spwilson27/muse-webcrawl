Conga

The conga, also known as tumbadora, is a tall, narrow, single-headed drum from Cuba of African origin. It is a staple of Afro-Cuban music — including son cubano, salsa, Afro-Cuban jazz and Latin jazz — and of the comparsa ensembles of Cuban carnival, from which both the drum's name and the associated conga line dance derive.

A set of congas: quinto, conga and tumba, from highest to lowest pitch.

Description

Congas are traditionally made from wooden staves, like barrels, with a rawhide head tensioned by metal hardware; modern instruments are often fibreglass. Drums are typically played in sets of two or three, tuned to different pitches: the quinto (highest), the conga (middle) and the tumba (lowest). They are played with the hands and fingers using a vocabulary of tones — open tone, bass tone, slap and muffled or muted strokes — combined into rhythmic patterns such as the marcha or tumbao of salsa.

History

The instrument descends from African drums brought to Cuba through the slave trade and developed in Cuban religious and secular music. In the 1940s, players such as Arsenio Rodríguez expanded the use of multiple congas in dance bands, and virtuosos including Mongo Santamaría, Ray Barretto and Giovanni Hidalgo carried the instrument into jazz and popular music worldwide. "Conga" was also the name of a carnival dance and rhythm popularised internationally in the late 1930s by Desi Arnaz and Xavier Cugat.
