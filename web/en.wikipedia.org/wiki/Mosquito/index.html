Mosquitoes are small flies of the family Culicidae, about 3,600 known species, found on every continent except Antarctica. The word is Spanish for "little fly". Only females of many species bite animals — including humans — sucking blood to obtain protein for egg production; both sexes feed on nectar. Through their bites, mosquitoes transmit some of humanity's deadliest diseases, and by this measure they are often called the deadliest animal to humans in history.

Life cycle and biting

Mosquitoes undergo complete metamorphosis: eggs are laid on or near water, larvae ("wrigglers") live in water feeding on microorganisms, pupae ("tumblers") float at the surface, and adults emerge to fly. Females locate hosts by carbon dioxide, body odour, heat and movement, and pierce the skin with a needle-like proboscis, injecting saliva containing anticoagulants — the cause of itchy allergic welts.

Disease and control

Anopheles mosquitoes transmit malaria, which still kills roughly 600,000 people a year, mostly African children; Aedes aegypti spreads dengue, Zika, yellow fever and chikungunya. Ronald Ross demonstrated the mosquito–malaria link in 1897 (Nobel Prize 1902), enabling targeted control: draining breeding sites, insecticides, bed nets treated with insecticide, and larvicides. Newer approaches include releasing sterile males, gene drives designed to suppress wild populations, and mosquitoes infected with Wolbachia bacteria that block virus transmission. Control efforts coordinated with the World Health Organization's malaria programmes have cut deaths greatly since 2000, though insecticide resistance and drug resistance threaten progress.
