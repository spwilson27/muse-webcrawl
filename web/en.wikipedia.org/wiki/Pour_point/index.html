Pour point

The pour point of a liquid is the temperature below which the liquid loses its flow characteristics. In crude oil a high pour point is generally associated with a high paraffin content, typically found in crude deriving from a larger proportion of plant material. It is defined as the minimum temperature at which the oil will still pour or flow under prescribed laboratory conditions.

Measurement

The standard test (ASTM D97) cools a specimen in a test jar at a specified rate. At each 3 °C interval the jar is briefly tilted to check whether the surface still moves. The pour point is recorded as 3 °C above the temperature at which the oil shows no movement when the jar is held horizontal for five seconds.

Causes

As a waxy oil cools, long-chain paraffin (wax) crystals precipitate — beginning at the cloud point — and eventually interlock into a three-dimensional gel network that traps the remaining liquid and stops flow. The pour point is therefore not a sharp physical constant but a practical measure of when this gel structure forms.

Pour point depressants

Chemical additives called pour point depressants (PPDs), commonly polymethacrylates and related polymers, co-crystallize with the wax and disrupt the growth of large interlocking crystals, lowering the pour point of lubricants and fuels so that engines and pipelines can operate in cold climates. Mechanical and thermal treatments, and dilution with lighter fractions, are also used to manage waxy crudes in production and transport.
