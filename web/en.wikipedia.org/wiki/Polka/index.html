The polka is a lively Central European dance and genre of dance music familiar throughout Europe and the Americas. It originated in the middle of the nineteenth century in Bohemia, now part of the Czech Republic. The polka remains a popular folk music genre in many European countries, and is performed by folk artists in the Czech Republic, Germany, Austria, Poland, Slovenia, Switzerland, Croatia and Ukraine, and also to a lesser extent by North Americans of Czech, Slovak, Polish, German, Croatian, Slovenian, and Texan descent.

History

The term polka possibly comes from the Czech word půlka ("half"), referring to the short half-steps characteristic of the dance, or from the Polish word Polka meaning a Polish woman. The dance is generally dated to around 1830–1834 in Bohemia. According to legend, it was invented by a peasant girl, Anna Chadimová (later Slezáková), in the village of Labská, danced to a local folk song. The steps were observed and popularised by the dance teacher Josef Neruda, who brought the dance to Prague ballrooms.

From Prague the polka spread rapidly: to Vienna in 1839, Paris in 1840, and London shortly afterwards. It became an international craze in the 1840s, displacing older couple dances in fashionable ballrooms and lending its name to fashions such as the polka-dot pattern. Composers such as Johann Strauss I and his sons Johann Strauss II and Josef Strauss in Vienna wrote numerous polkas — fast pieces such as the "Tritsch-Tratsch-Polka" and slower polka-mazurkas.

The dance

The polka is a fast dance in 2/4 time performed by couples in closed position. Its basic figure is a series of short quick steps — a half-step, close, and step pattern — often with hops and turns. The dancers progress around the floor, frequently rotating rapidly. Regional variants include the Czech, Polish, German, and American "polka hop" styles, as well as the slower French polka-mazurka.

Music and instrumentation

Polka music is written in 2/4 time with a brisk tempo and strong accents on the beat. In Europe, village bands typically feature brass and woodwind instruments with accordion. In the United States, distinct regional styles developed among immigrant communities: the German "oom-pah" style, the Polish-American style of Chicago and the Northeast, the Czech-Texan style, and the Slovenian (Cleveland) style popularised by Frankie Yankovic, known as "America's Polka King". Lawrence Welk brought polka to a national television audience. Instruments commonly include accordion or concertina, clarinet, trumpet, tuba, banjo, and drums.

Polka also strongly influenced Mexican norteño and conjunto music, brought by Central European immigrants to northern Mexico and Texas in the nineteenth century, where it blended with local traditions.

In popular culture

The United States Polka Association and state polka halls of fame honour leading performers. Grammy Awards included a polka category from 1986 until 2009. "Weird Al" Yankovic, whose father was of Slovenian descent, recorded polka medleys of popular songs on many of his albums.
