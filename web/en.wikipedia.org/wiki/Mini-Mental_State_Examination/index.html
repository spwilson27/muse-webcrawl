Mini–Mental State Examination

MMSE
Authors: Folstein, Folstein & McHugh (1975)
Maximum score: 30
Time: ~10 minutes
Purpose: Screening for cognitive impairment

The Mini–Mental State Examination (MMSE) is a widely used 30-point questionnaire for screening cognitive impairment, introduced by Marshal Folstein, Susan Folstein and Paul McHugh in 1975. It is routinely used in medicine and allied health to screen for dementia and to track changes in cognition over time.

Structure

The test samples several cognitive functions: orientation to time and place (10 points), registration and short-term recall of three words (6), attention and calculation — such as counting backwards by sevens (5), language (naming objects, repeating a phrase, following a three-stage command, reading and writing a sentence, 8), and copying a drawing of intersecting pentagons (1). Administration takes roughly ten minutes.

Interpretation

Scores of 24 or above (out of 30) are conventionally considered normal; lower ranges suggest mild, moderate or severe impairment, though cut-offs must be adjusted for age, education and language background. The MMSE cannot by itself diagnose dementia and is relatively insensitive to mild cognitive impairment and frontal-lobe dysfunction, so it is often combined with other instruments such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment.
