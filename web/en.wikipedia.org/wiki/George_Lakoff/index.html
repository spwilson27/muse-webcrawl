George Philip Lakoff (born 24 May 1941) is an American cognitive linguist and philosopher, professor at the University of California, Berkeley. He is best known for his thesis that human thought is largely metaphorical — structured by systematic mappings from bodily experience — set out in Metaphors We Live By (1980), written with Mark Johnson.

Career

Lakoff earned his PhD at Indiana University and taught at Harvard before joining Berkeley in 1972. In the 1960s–70s he was a central figure, with colleagues such as John Robert Ross, in the "linguistics wars" over generative semantics, a movement that broke with Noam Chomsky's transformational grammar and fed into the emergence of cognitive linguistics.

Conceptual metaphor

In Lakoff's conceptual metaphor theory, abstract reasoning reuses the sensorimotor systems of the brain: we understand argument as war ("he attacked my position"), time as motion, and ideas as objects. With Rafael Núñez he extended the argument to mathematics in Where Mathematics Comes From (2000). His work on categorisation, notably Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things (1987), drew on Eleanor Rosch's prototype theory to challenge classical definitions.

Political framing

Lakoff has applied his ideas to politics, arguing in Moral Politics (1996) and Don't Think of an Elephant! (2004) that American conservative and progressive worldviews rest on opposing family-based moral metaphors — the "strict father" and "nurturant parent" models — and that political persuasion works through framing rather than facts alone.
