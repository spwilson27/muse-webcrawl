Kale is a leafy green vegetable belonging to the cabbage family (Brassica oleracea), cultivated for its edible leaves, which do not form a head. Like cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, and Brussels sprouts, kale descends from wild cabbage of the Mediterranean coast and has been grown since at least Greek and Roman times. It became one of the most popular vegetables in northern Europe because it tolerates frost — its flavour sweetens after exposure to cold — and in the 2010s it enjoyed a surge of popularity in North America as a "superfood". History

Leafy forms of B. oleracea were among the earliest brassicas domesticated in the eastern Mediterranean, and Greek and Roman authors described curly and smooth-leafed types resembling modern kale. Kale was a staple peasant food across medieval Europe and a fixture of the Scottish kailyard (kitchen garden), where the word "kail" long served as a generic term for dinner itself. It spread to North America with colonists, and during the Second World War Dig for Victory campaigns promoted it in Britain. In the 2010s, marketing campaigns — kale was famously promoted by a publicist for the American Kale Association — made it a symbol of fashionable healthy eating in the United States.

Varieties

Common culinary types include curly kale (var. sabellica), with tightly ruffled blue-green leaves; Tuscan or dinosaur kale, also called cavolo nero or lacinato kale, with long, dark, blistered leaves; flat-leaf Red Russian kale with purple stems and oak-like leaves; and Portuguese kale (tronchuda). Ornamental kale and cabbage are bred for rosettes of white, pink, and purple and are used in bedding displays, though they are also edible.

Cultivation

Kale is a hardy biennial usually grown as an annual. It germinates readily, tolerates poor soils, and survives temperatures well below freezing; leaves are harvested progressively from the bottom of the stem. Pests include cabbage white butterflies, aphids, and clubroot disease. Because it overwinters in mild climates, kale provides fresh greens in early spring when few other vegetables are available.

Nutrition and culinary use

Raw kale is rich in vitamins K, A, and C, a good source of folate, calcium, and the carotenoids lutein and zeaxanthin, and supplies glucosinolates typical of brassicas. Per 100 g it provides roughly 43–49 kcal.

Kale is eaten raw in salads (often massaged with dressing to soften it), sautéed, simmered in soups such as Portuguese caldo verde and Italian ribollita, blended into smoothies, and baked into crisps. In the Netherlands it is central to boerenkool, mashed with potatoes and served with smoked sausage; in northern Germany, kale feasts (Grünkohlessen) are a winter social tradition.
