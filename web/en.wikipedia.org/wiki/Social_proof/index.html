Social proof

A queue is a classic visible signal of social proof
FieldSocial psychology
Popularised byRobert Cialdini, Influence (1984)
Related phenomenaInformational social influence, conformity, bandwagon effect, herd behaviour
Typical usesTestimonials, ratings and reviews, follower counts, bestseller lists

Social proof is a psychological and social phenomenon wherein people copy the actions of others in an attempt to undertake behaviour in a given situation. When a person is unsure how to act, they assume that the surrounding people possess more knowledge about the situation and that their behaviour is the correct one to follow. The term was popularised by Robert Cialdini in his 1984 book Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, where it is presented as one of the principal weapons of influence.

Mechanisms

Social proof is a form of informational social influence. It is strongest when the situation is ambiguous or a crisis, and when the others being observed are similar to the observer or perceived as experts. Muzafer Sherif's 1936 light-judgement experiments demonstrated the convergence of judgements under uncertainty. The darker side of the principle appears in the bystander effect: in an emergency, each witness looks to the others' inaction as proof that nothing is wrong (pluralistic ignorance), delaying help.

Examples

Everyday instances include canned laughter prompting audiences to laugh, nightclubs letting a queue form outside even when not full, and bartenders "salting" their tip jars with their own money at the start of a shift. In the digital sphere, social proof drives the display of customer reviews and star ratings, download and subscriber counts, "most popular" rankings, testimonials and influencer endorsements; experiments on music-download sites have shown that displaying others' choices substantially increases a song's popularity, increasing inequality of outcomes between items of similar quality.

Criticism and manipulation

Because social proof can be manufactured — through fake reviews, purchased followers, astroturfed campaigns or bot activity — regulators increasingly police deceptive endorsements and review practices. Psychologists also note that the heuristic, while usually efficient, can propagate errors, panics and harmful fads when the crowd itself is misinformed.
