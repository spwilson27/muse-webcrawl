The Lazic War, also known as the Colchian War, was fought between the Byzantine Empire and the Sasanian Empire of Persia from 541 to 562 over the kingdom of Lazica, on the eastern shore of the Black Sea in present-day Georgia. Together with the Iberian War and other conflicts, it formed part of the long series of Roman–Persian wars between the two great powers of late antiquity.

Background

Lazica was strategically valuable because it guarded the Caucasus passes against steppe peoples and the coastal road along the Black Sea. Under the "Eternal Peace" of 532, Persia had recognised Lazica as a Roman client state. However, Byzantine officials — notably the tax official John Tzibus — imposed monopolies and heavy-handed rule, so that King Gubazes II of Lazica secretly invited the Persians to intervene. In 541 the Sasanian king Khosrow I marched into Lazica, captured the fortress of Petra, and established a Persian garrison.

Course of the war

Fighting centred on Petra and on a succession of sieges, betrayals and shifting Laz allegiances. The Lazi, alienated by Persian attempts to impose Zoroastrianism and to assassinate Gubazes, swung back to the Byzantines; Gubazes himself was murdered by Byzantine commanders in 555, an act that nearly wrecked the alliance before an imperial investigation restored confidence. Byzantine forces under generals including Bessas and Martin retook Petra in 551, demolishing its walls, and the war degenerated into raids and stalemate across Lazica and neighbouring Suania.

Settlement

Exhausted, both empires concluded the Fifty Years' Peace in 562. Persia evacuated Lazica and recognised it as a Byzantine client, in return for an annual Byzantine payment in gold. The war is described in detail by the contemporary historians Procopius and Agathias, whose accounts are the principal sources.
