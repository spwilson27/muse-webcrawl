Roger Wolcott Sperry

Roger Wolcott Sperry (August 20, 1913 – April 17, 1994) was an American neuropsychologist and neurobiologist. He shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel; his citation read "for his discoveries concerning the functional specialization of the cerebral hemispheres".

Early life and education

Sperry was born in Hartford, Connecticut, and raised in West Hartford. He attended Oberlin College on a scholarship, earning a degree in English in 1935 and a master's in psychology, then a PhD in zoology from the University of Chicago in 1941 under Paul Weiss.

Split-brain research

At the California Institute of Technology, Sperry and his student Michael Gazzaniga studied patients whose corpus callosum had been severed to treat epilepsy. These "split-brain" experiments demonstrated that the two cerebral hemispheres have distinct specialisations — language typically in the left, spatial and holistic processing in the right — and that each hemisphere can possess its own perceptions, concepts, and impulses to act.

Chemoaffinity hypothesis

Sperry's earlier work on nerve regeneration led to the chemoaffinity hypothesis: axons grow to their targets guided by chemical labels, refuting the idea that neural connections are formed purely by random activity and experience.
