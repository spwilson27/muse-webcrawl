The Russian-American Company (Russian: Российско-американская компания) was a state-sponsored chartered company formed in 1799 by the merger of the Shelikhov-Golikov Company and other fur-trading ventures, under a charter from Emperor Paul I. It held a monopoly on trade in Russian America — present-day Alaska and Russian outposts extending to Fort Ross in California and, briefly, Hawaii — and effectively governed the colony for over six decades.

Operations

Under its first chief manager, Alexander Baranov (1790–1818), the company built its capital at New Archangel (Sitka), organised the hunting of sea otters and fur seals using Aleut and Alutiiq labour, and traded with China, the United States and Spanish and Mexican California. Otter depletion, supply difficulties from distant Siberia, and competition from British and American traders eroded profits. The company also sponsored voyages of exploration and circumnavigation, including those of Adam Johann von Krusenstern.

Dissolution

After Russia's defeat in the Crimean War exposed the colony's vulnerability to Britain, and with the fur trade in decline, the company did not seek to renew its charter on its expiry in 1862. In 1867 Russia sold Alaska to the United States for $7.2 million, and the company's American assets were sold to the American firm that became the Alaska Commercial Company. The company's own liquidation in Russia was completed in 1881.
