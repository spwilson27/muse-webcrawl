Richmond Park

Richmond Park, in south-west London, is the largest of London's Royal Parks and the largest urban park in London, covering 2,360 acres (955 hectares). Created by Charles I in the 17th century as a deer park, it is a National Nature Reserve and a Site of Special Scientific Interest, famous for its herds of red and fallow deer and its ancient oak trees.

History

Charles I moved his court to Richmond Palace to escape plague in London and in 1637 enclosed the area with a brick wall, stocking it as a hunting park — a decision unpopular with local landowners. Public access on foot was retained and later confirmed in law. Notable buildings include White Lodge (once the home of the Royal Ballet School) and Pembroke Lodge.

Wildlife and landscape

The park supports around 600 red and fallow deer, which roam freely. Its acid grassland and ancient woodland, including over a thousand veteran oaks, support rare fungi, invertebrates such as stag beetles, and birds including ring-necked parakeets. The Isabella Plantation is a celebrated ornamental woodland garden, and Pen Ponds are two artificial lakes dating from the 18th century.

Recreation

Richmond Park is popular for walking, cycling (its perimeter road is a famous training circuit), horse riding and golf, and hosted the road cycling races of the 2012 Olympic Games. From King Henry's Mound there is a protected view of St Paul's Cathedral, ten miles away.
