Rowboat

A rowboat (or rowing boat) is a small boat propelled by oars. The rower sits facing the stern and pulls the boat backward through the water, the oars acting as levers against oarlocks (rowlocks) mounted on the gunwales. Rowboats are among the oldest forms of watercraft and remain in use worldwide for transport, fishing, recreation and sport.

Design

Rowboats range from simple flat-bottomed skiffs and prams to fine-lined pulling boats. Most have a transom stern, one or more thwarts (cross seats) for the rowers, and may be built of wood, aluminium, fibreglass or inflatable fabric. Sculling uses two oars per rower; sweep rowing, as in racing eights, uses one oar per rower. Some rowboats are fitted with a sliding seat and outriggers for efficient racing, while traditional working boats use fixed seats.

History

Oared vessels dominated naval and commercial transport from ancient Egyptian, Phoenician and Greek galleys through the Viking longship era and into the age of sail, when ships' boats were rowed between ship and shore. Small rowboats served as tenders, lifeboats and fishing craft in every maritime culture. The 19th century saw rowing develop into an organised sport, culminating in rowing's place in the Olympic Games from 1900.

Technique

The rowing stroke consists of the catch (blades placed in the water), the drive (legs, back and arms propel the boat), the release and the recovery, during which the rower slides forward with blades feathered above the water. Because the rower faces backward, a coxswain or a passenger may steer, or the rower glances over the shoulder to navigate.
