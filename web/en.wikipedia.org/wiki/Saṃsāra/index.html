Saṃsāra

Saṃsāra (Sanskrit: संसार, saṃsāra; Pali: saṃsāra) is a Sanskrit term meaning the "wandering" or cyclical, circuitous change of existence. In Indian religions — Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism — it denotes the beginningless cycle of repeated birth, death, and rebirth (reincarnation) to which all beings in the phenomenal world are subject, driven by karma and ignorance. Liberation from saṃsāra — called moksha in Hinduism and Jainism, and nirvana in Buddhism — is the ultimate spiritual goal in these traditions.

Etymology

The word derives from the Sanskrit root saṃ-sṛ, "to flow together, to go or pass through states, to wander," and appears in the early Upanishads. It is closely related to concepts of rebirth, transmigration of the soul (ātman), and the doctrine that actions in one life determine the conditions of the next.

In Hinduism

In Hindu thought, saṃsāra is the cycle of death and rebirth governed by karma: virtuous actions generate merit that yields more favourable future births, while harmful actions yield the opposite. The cycle is sustained by avidyā (ignorance) and attachment to the fruits of action and to the phenomenal world (māyā). The self (ātman) is eternal and transmigrates between bodies at death. Release (moksha) occurs through knowledge, devotion, or disciplined action, ending rebirth and uniting or realising the self's identity with ultimate reality (Brahman).

In Buddhism

Buddhism teaches that saṃsāra is a beginningless cycle of rebirth through six realms of existence — gods, demigods (asuras), humans, animals, hungry ghosts (pretas), and hell beings — depicted in the bhavacakra (wheel of life). Unlike Hinduism, Buddhism denies a permanent soul (anattā); rebirth is instead a causal continuum of consciousness conditioned by karma and the twelve links of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda). Because all conditioned existence is marked by suffering (dukkha), impermanence, and non-self, the goal is awakening (bodhi) and entry into nirvana, which ends the cycle.

In Jainism

In Jainism, the soul (jīva) is bound to saṃsāra by karmic matter accumulated through action, passion, and violence. Rebirth may occur in heavenly, human, animal or plant, or hellish states. Liberation (kevala) is achieved through right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct — especially non-violence (ahiṃsā) — after which the purified soul rises to the top of the universe and abides in blissful omniscience, free of rebirth.

In Sikhism

Sikh teaching likewise accepts reincarnation and karma, holding that beings wander through many births (the traditional figure is 8.4 million life forms) until, by God's grace (nadar) and devotion to the divine Name, the soul escapes the cycle and unites with God.
