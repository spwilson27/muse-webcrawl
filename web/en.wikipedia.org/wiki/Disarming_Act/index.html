Disarming Act

The Disarming Act (6 Geo. II, c. ?? — formally "An Act for the more effectual disarming the Highlands in Scotland") is the name given to British legislation passed after the Jacobite rising of 1745 to suppress the military power of the Scottish Highland clans. The most famous of these measures is the Act of Proscription 1746 (19 Geo. 2, c. 39), which came into force on 1 August 1747.

Background

Earlier disarming acts had followed the Jacobite rising of 1715; the Disarming Act of 1716 and the Disarming Act of 1725 (associated with General George Wade's road-building and enforcement programme) had attempted to collect weapons in the Highlands but were widely evaded — obedient clans surrendered old and worthless arms while Jacobite clans kept modern weapons. After the defeat of the 1745 rising at the Battle of Culloden in April 1746, the government of the Duke of Newcastle resolved on much harsher measures.

Provisions

The 1746 act forbade Highlanders from carrying or owning broadswords, targets (shields), dirks and other weapons, imposing fines and imprisonment — and, for repeat offences, transportation. Distinctively, it also banned the wearing of Highland dress: the plaid, kilt (philabeg), trews and tartan garments, for men and boys, punishable by up to six months' imprisonment for a first offence and transportation for a second. The act formed part of a wider legislative package including the Heritable Jurisdictions (Scotland) Act 1746, which abolished the traditional judicial powers of clan chiefs.

Repeal and legacy

The dress provisions were repealed in 1782, an event celebrated by Highland societies and commemorated by the wearing of tartan at George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822. In the intervening decades the disarming and dress bans, together with military road-building and forfeited estates administration, contributed to the dismantling of the traditional clan system.
