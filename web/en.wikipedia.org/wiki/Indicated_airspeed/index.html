Indicated airspeed

Indicated airspeed (IAS) is the airspeed of an aircraft as shown on its airspeed indicator, which is driven by the pitot-static system. The indicator compares the total pressure from the pitot tube with the static pressure and displays the result as a speed, calibrated as though the aircraft were at sea level in standard atmospheric conditions.

Relation to other airspeeds

IAS differs from the aircraft's true speed through the air because air density decreases with altitude and temperature. Correcting IAS for instrument and position errors gives calibrated airspeed (CAS); correcting CAS for compressibility gives equivalent airspeed (EAS); and correcting EAS for density gives true airspeed (TAS). At cruising altitude a jet's TAS may be roughly twice its IAS, while ground speed further depends on wind.

Operational significance

Pilots fly by indicated airspeed because the aerodynamic forces on the aircraft — lift, stall, and structural loads — depend on dynamic pressure, which is exactly what the indicator senses. A wing therefore stalls at the same indicated airspeed regardless of altitude, and aircraft operating limitations such as VNE, flap extension speeds, and approach speeds are all published in indicated (or calibrated) terms. At low speeds and sea level, IAS, CAS, and TAS are nearly equal.

Units

Indicated airspeed is usually expressed in knots (KIAS) or kilometres per hour, and in some light aircraft, miles per hour. Mach number, used at high altitude, is derived from the same pitot-static pressures by the air data computer.
