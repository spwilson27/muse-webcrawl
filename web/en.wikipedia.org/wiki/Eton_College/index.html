Eton College is a public school (an independent boarding school for boys aged 13–18) in Eton, Berkshire, England. It was founded in 1440 by Henry VI as the King's College of Our Lady of Eton beside Windsor, modelled on Winchester College. Eton is one of Britain's most famous and expensive schools and has educated generations of the British elite, including twenty prime ministers.

History

Henry VI intended Eton as a feeder school to King's College, Cambridge, granting it vast endowments that were partly stripped away after Edward IV deposed him. The school survived and grew in prestige through the 17th and 18th centuries. Its historic buildings include the College Chapel, a fine example of Perpendicular Gothic, and Lower School, among the oldest schoolrooms in England. Seventy "King's Scholars" still receive places by competitive examination, while most pupils ("Oppidans") pay full fees.

School life and traditions

Eton maintains distinctive traditions: a formal dress code of tailcoat, waistcoat and striped trousers; the Eton wall game and Eton fives; the Fourth of June celebrations; and its own slang and timetable division into "schools" (lessons) and "chambers" (prep). Boys live in houses of about 50 under a housemaster, and daily life is organised around house loyalties.

Alumni and criticism

Old Etonians include the Duke of Wellington, Robert Walpole, Boris Johnson, David Cameron, Princes William and Harry, and writers such as George Orwell and Aldous Huxley. The school's dominance in producing political leaders has long drawn criticism as a symbol of privilege in British life, while its charitable status and bursary programmes are subjects of continuing debate.
