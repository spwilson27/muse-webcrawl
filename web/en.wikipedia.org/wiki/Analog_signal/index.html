Analog signal

An analog signal is any continuous-time signal representing some other quantity, i.e., analogous to another quantity. For example, in an analog audio signal, the instantaneous signal voltage varies continuously with the pressure of the sound waves.

In contrast, a digital signal represents the original time-varying quantity as a sampled sequence of quantized values, which imposes bandwidth and dynamic range constraints on the representation and is based on two amplitude levels.

Representation

The term analog signal usually refers to electrical signals; however, mechanical, pneumatic, hydraulic and other systems may also convey analog signals. An analog signal uses some property of the medium, such as its electric current or its voltage, to convey the signal's information. Any information may be conveyed by an analog signal; it may be a response to changes in a physical variable, such as sound, light, temperature, position, or pressure.

Advantages and disadvantages

Analog signals are subject to noise and distortion, which accumulate irreversibly at every stage of copying or transmission; a key advantage is their potentially infinite resolution in principle. Analog circuits and devices can also be simpler than equivalent digital systems for some tasks. The primary disadvantage is that any noise introduced into the signal becomes part of the signal itself, degrading fidelity, whereas digital signals can be regenerated exactly. For these reasons, most modern communications and audio systems convert signals between analog and digital domains using analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog converters.

Modulation

Analog signals are commonly carried on a high-frequency carrier wave using modulation techniques such as amplitude modulation (AM) and frequency modulation (FM), long used in radio and television broadcasting. Analogue television and telephony networks have largely been replaced by digital systems since the late twentieth century.
