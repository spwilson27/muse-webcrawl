Appropriations bill (United States)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the United States, an appropriations bill is legislation to appropriate federal funds to specific federal government departments, agencies and programs. The money typically provides funding for operations, personnel, equipment and activities. Regular appropriations bills are passed annually, with the funding they provide covering one fiscal year; the federal fiscal year runs from October 1 to September 30 of the following calendar year.

Article One of the United States Constitution provides that "No money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." Appropriations bills are one part of the federal budget process, distinct from authorisation bills, which establish or continue programs and set spending limits but do not themselves provide money.

The twelve bills

By tradition, Congress passes twelve regular appropriations bills each year, drawn up by the twelve subcommittees of the House and Senate Appropriations Committees, covering areas including Defense; Energy and Water Development; Financial Services and General Government; Homeland Security; Interior and Environment; Labor, Health and Human Services, and Education; Military Construction and Veterans Affairs; State and Foreign Operations; Transportation and Housing and Urban Development; Agriculture; Commerce, Justice and Science; and the Legislative Branch.

Continuing resolutions and shutdowns

When Congress fails to enact regular appropriations before the fiscal year begins, it may pass a continuing resolution funding the government temporarily at prior-year levels. If neither appropriations nor a continuing resolution is enacted, a funding gap triggers a government shutdown, during which agencies furlough staff and suspend non-essential operations under the Antideficiency Act. Omnibus bills combine several appropriations into one large measure; "minibus" bills combine a few.

Supplemental appropriations

Congress may also pass supplemental appropriations for needs beyond the regular bills, such as disaster relief or military operations. Emergency designations can exempt such spending from budget-enforcement limits. Together with the money supply and tax policy, appropriations constitute the expenditure side of federal fiscal policy — the "G" in aggregate demand.
