Whitlow
SpecialtyDermatology, infectious disease
TypesHerpetic whitlow; bacterial (melanotic) whitlow
CausesHerpes simplex virus (HSV-1/HSV-2); bacterial infection
SymptomsPainful swelling, redness and blistering of a fingertip

A whitlow is a painful infection of the fingertip, most precisely an abscess of the pulp of the distal phalanx, and the term is also used for the common viral infection herpetic whitlow. Historically, "whitlow" (from words meaning "white" and "flaw/crack") covered several infections around the nail and fingertip, including forms of paronychia and the fingertip pulp-space infection known surgically as a felon.

Herpetic whitlow

Herpetic whitlow is caused by the herpes simplex virus (HSV-1 or HSV-2) entering through a break in the skin of a finger. It produces intense pain, swelling, redness and clusters of small clear vesicles, sometimes with fluid that later turns cloudy. It was classically an occupational hazard of healthcare workers — especially dental personnel before universal glove use — exposed to patients' oral secretions, and it is also seen in children who suck their thumbs while they have herpetic gingivostomatitis, and in adults via genital herpes contact. The condition is self-limiting over about two to three weeks, though it can recur. Importantly, herpetic whitlow should not be incised, as surgical drainage can worsen it and spread infection; diagnosis is clinical, supported if needed by viral testing, and severe cases may be treated with antivirals such as aciclovir.

Bacterial whitlow / felon

A bacterial whitlow of the fingertip pulp (a felon) is a closed-space abscess, usually caused by Staphylococcus aureus following a puncture or splinter. The fingertip becomes tense, throbbing and exquisitely tender; because the pulp is divided by fibrous septa, pressure can compromise blood supply, and treatment generally requires prompt incision and drainage plus antibiotics to prevent osteomyelitis or tissue loss.

Differential diagnosis

Whitlow must be distinguished from acute paronychia (infection of the nail fold), cellulitis, and contact dermatitis. The presence of grouped vesicles and disproportionate "burning" pain suggests the herpetic form.
