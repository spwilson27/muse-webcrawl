Durham University (legally the University of Durham) is a collegiate public research university in Durham, England, founded by an Act of Parliament in 1832 and incorporated by royal charter in 1837. It was the first recognised university to open in England for more than 600 years, after Oxford and Cambridge, and is thus, following standard historical practice, the third-oldest university in England.

As a collegiate university, its main functions are divided between the academic departments of the university and its 17 constituent colleges. In general, the departments perform research and provide teaching to students, while the colleges are responsible for housing, welfare, and much of the social life of their undergraduate members. The university consistently ranks among the top universities in the United Kingdom and is a member of the Russell Group of research-intensive universities.

History

Strong traditions of theological teaching in Durham gave rise to various attempts to found a university there, notably under Henry VIII and Oliver Cromwell. However, it was not until 1832 that Parliament, at the instigation of Bishop William van Mildert and the Chapter of Durham Cathedral, passed an Act enabling the cathedral chapter to use part of its funds to establish a university. University College, the first college, was founded in 1833, and Durham Castle, formerly the palace of the Prince-Bishops, was given over to house it.

The university expanded in the 19th century with affiliated colleges and institutions in Newcastle (which later became Newcastle University), Barbados, Sierra Leone, and elsewhere. In the 20th and 21st centuries the university grew substantially, developing the science site at Mountjoy, expanding its collegiate system, and opening new facilities including a business school and the Palatine Centre.

Campus and colleges

The university occupies a large area of the city of Durham, including part of the Durham Castle and Cathedral UNESCO World Heritage Site. Its colleges include University College (1833, housed in the castle), Hatfield, St Chad's, St John's, Grey, Van Mildert, Trevelyan, Collingwood, St Mary's, St Aidan's, and the Hill colleges, along with newer foundations such as Josephine Butler and South College. Students are members of a college for the duration of their degree and participate in college sports, societies, and the distinctive formal hall dinners.

Academics

Teaching and research are organised into four faculties: Arts and Humanities, Business, Science, and Social Sciences and Health. Durham is known for strengths in subjects including archaeology, English, geography, physics, theology, and law. Alumni and faculty have included fellows of the Royal Society, notable scientists, authors, and public figures.
