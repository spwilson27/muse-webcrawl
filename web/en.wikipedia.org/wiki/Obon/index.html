Obon

Obon (お盆)
TypeBuddhist festival
ObservedMid-August (most of Japan); mid-July (parts of eastern Japan); 15th day of 7th lunar month elsewhere
SignificanceHonouring the spirits of one's ancestors
ActivitiesBon Odori dancing, grave visits, floating lanterns (tōrō nagashi)
Floating lanterns (tōrō nagashi) guide ancestral spirits back to the world of the dead at the close of Obon.

Obon (お盆) or just Bon (盆) is a Japanese Buddhist custom to honour the spirits of one's ancestors. This Buddhist–Confucian custom has evolved into a family reunion holiday during which people return to ancestral family places and visit and clean their ancestors' graves; the spirits of ancestors are believed to revisit the household altars during this time. It has been celebrated in Japan for more than 500 years and traditionally includes a dance, known as Bon Odori.

Timing

The festival lasts three days. Because Japan moved from a lunar to the Gregorian calendar in the Meiji period, the timing varies by region: most of Japan observes "Awa Odori"-season Obon in mid-August, while parts of the Kantō region observe it in mid-July, and Okinawa and China follow the lunar date — the 15th day of the seventh lunar month.

Origin

Obon derives from the Buddhist Ullambana (Ghost Festival) tradition, based on the Ullambana Sutra, in which the disciple Maudgalyayana (Mokuren) used his supernatural powers to see his deceased mother suffering in the Realm of Hungry Ghosts; through offerings to monks he secured her release and danced with joy — the origin, in legend, of Bon Odori.

Observances

Families place food offerings at household altars (butsudan) and lanterns at graves. On the first day, welcoming fires (mukaebi) are lit to guide the spirits home; on the last day, send-off fires (okuribi) and floating lanterns guide them back. In Kyoto, the famous Gozan no Okuribi lights giant characters on five mountains. Many Japanese companies close for the Obon week in August, making it one of Japan's busiest travel periods.
