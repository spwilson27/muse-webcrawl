Redox (reduction–oxidation) reactions are chemical reactions in which atoms change oxidation state by transferring electrons. Oxidation is the loss of electrons and reduction the gain — remembered by 'OIL RIG' (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain). The two always occur together.

Redox chemistry runs much of the world: combustion, rusting, batteries, metal extraction, respiration and photosynthesis are all redox processes. Assigning oxidation numbers lets chemists book-keep electrons even in covalent compounds where transfer is only partial.

[Image: A rusty iron nail beside shiny metal, showing oxidation]

Examples

When iron rusts, iron is oxidised to iron(III) oxide while oxygen is reduced. In a Daniell cell, zinc is oxidised (losing electrons to an external circuit) while copper ions are reduced to copper metal — the basis of electrochemistry.

Agents

An oxidising agent (oxidant) accepts electrons and is itself reduced — oxygen, chlorine and concentrated nitric acid are examples. A reducing agent donates electrons, like carbon in a blast furnace reducing iron ore.
