Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code (Title 11 of the United States Code) permits reorganization under the bankruptcy laws of the United States. Such reorganization, known as Chapter 11 bankruptcy, is normally proposed by, and carried out for, a corporation or partnership, although individuals may also file. A debtor will usually propose a plan of reorganization to keep its business alive and pay creditors over time.

Overview

When a business files under Chapter 11, an automatic stay halts most collection actions. The debtor generally remains in possession of its assets and operates the business as a "debtor in possession", subject to the oversight of the bankruptcy court and, in larger cases, a committee of unsecured creditors and a United States Trustee. The debtor has the exclusive right, for an initial period, to file a plan of reorganization.

The plan

A plan of reorganization classifies creditors' claims and specifies how each class will be treated. Classes of creditors vote on the plan; if the statutory requirements are met, the court may confirm the plan even over the objection of a dissenting class ("cramdown"), provided the plan is fair and equitable and does not discriminate unfairly. Upon confirmation, the debtor receives a discharge of pre-petition debts as provided in the plan.

Notable cases

  • General Motors (2009) — one of the largest industrial bankruptcies.
  • Enron (2001) — then the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history.
  • Lehman Brothers (2008) — the largest bankruptcy filing in U.S. history, though primarily liquidated.
  • Texaco (1987) — prompted by the Pennzoil litigation judgment.

Subchapter V and other variants

The Small Business Reorganization Act of 2019 created Subchapter V, a streamlined form of Chapter 11 for small business debtors. Chapter 11 also has special provisions for railroad reorganizations and for single-asset real estate cases.
