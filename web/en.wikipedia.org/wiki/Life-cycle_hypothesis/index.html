Life-cycle hypothesis

Income, consumption, and wealth over the life cycle
Proposed byFranco Modigliani and Richard Brumberg
Year1954
FieldMacroeconomics, consumption theory

The life-cycle hypothesis (LCH) is an economic model developed by Franco Modigliani and his student Richard Brumberg in the early 1950s. It proposes that individuals plan their consumption and saving over their whole lifetimes, aiming to smooth consumption: they borrow when young (when income is low), save during their peak earning years, and draw down their savings ("dissave") in retirement.

The model

In the simplest version, a person expects to live T more years, work for R of them at annual income Y, and hold current wealth W. To keep consumption constant, they consume each year C = (W + RY)/T. It follows that the aggregate saving rate of an economy depends not on the level of income itself but on its growth rate and on demographics — in a stationary economy, lifetime saving nets to zero, while a growing economy shows positive aggregate saving even though each individual dissaves in old age.

Implications and evidence

The hypothesis explains why saving rates rise with economic growth and why the age structure of a population affects national saving. It underlies policies such as retirement pensions and the idea that temporary tax changes have smaller effects on consumption than permanent ones. It is closely related to Milton Friedman's permanent income hypothesis. Empirical problems include the observed failure of the elderly to dissave as fast as predicted, the role of bequests, and liquidity constraints — topics explored by later research, and recognised when Modigliani received the 1985 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences partly for this work.
