Hagen–Poiseuille equation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hagen–Poiseuille equation, also known as the Hagen–Poiseuille law or simply Poiseuille's law, is a physical law in fluid mechanics that gives the pressure drop in an incompressible, Newtonian fluid in laminar flow through a long cylindrical pipe of constant cross-section. It was derived independently by Gotthilf Heinrich Ludwig Hagen (1839) and Jean Léonard Marie Poiseuille (1838–1840), the latter from careful experiments on the flow of water through narrow glass tubes motivated partly by the flow of blood in vessels.

The equation

Δp = (8 μ L Q) / (π R4)  ,  Q = (π R4 Δp) / (8 μ L)

where Δp is the pressure difference between the two ends of the pipe, L is the pipe length, μ is the dynamic viscosity, Q is the volumetric flow rate, and R is the pipe radius. The striking fourth-power dependence on radius means that halving a vessel's radius increases the pressure needed to sustain a given flow sixteen-fold — a fact of great consequence in physiology and in piping design. The equation can be derived from the Navier–Stokes equations under the assumptions of steady, fully developed, axisymmetric laminar flow with no-slip walls; the resulting velocity profile is parabolic, fastest at the centreline.

Assumptions and limits

The law holds only for laminar flow (low Reynolds number), incompressible Newtonian fluids, long straight pipes (so entrance effects are negligible), and steady conditions. For turbulent flow, non-Newtonian fluids such as blood in fine vessels (whose apparent viscosity varies with vessel diameter, the Fåhræus–Lindqvist effect), or compressible gases, corrected or alternative relations are required. In electrical analogy, the equation corresponds to Ohm's law, with pressure drop as voltage and flow rate as current, defining a hydrodynamic resistance 8μL/πR4.

Applications

Beyond hydraulics and microfluidics, Poiseuille's law underlies the clinical understanding of blood flow: small changes in the radius of arterioles, controlled by vasodilation and vasoconstriction, regulate peripheral resistance and blood pressure, and stenoses sharply raise the pressure gradient across a narrowed vessel.
