Iapetus (/aɪˈæpɪtəs/) is the third-largest natural satellite of Saturn and the eleventh-largest in the Solar System. It was discovered by Giovanni Domenico Cassini in 1671 and is named after the Titan Iapetus from Greek mythology.

Iapetus is best known for its dramatic two-toned coloration: its leading hemisphere is dark with a slight reddish-brown tint, while its trailing hemisphere is bright and icy. It is also known for its massive equatorial ridge, which runs more than three-quarters of the way around the moon and gives it a walnut-like shape.

Discovery

Cassini discovered Iapetus in October 1671, on the western side of Saturn. He tried unsuccessfully to observe it on the eastern side early the following year; the moon was much fainter there. Cassini correctly surmised that Iapetus had a bright side and a dark side and that it was tidally locked, always keeping the same face toward Saturn.

Physical characteristics

Iapetus has a low density of about 1.088 g/cm³, indicating that it is composed mostly of water ice with only a small amount of rocky material. Unlike most large moons, its overall shape is not in hydrostatic equilibrium, though its shape is close to an oblate spheroid.

Two-toned coloration

The difference in colouring between the two hemispheres is striking: the leading hemisphere and poles are dark with an albedo of 0.03–0.05, while most of the trailing hemisphere and poles have an albedo of 0.5–0.6, bright like Europa. The dark material is thought to originate from Phoebe, an outer moon, and to be concentrated on the leading hemisphere by a runaway thermal segregation process.

Equatorial ridge

Iapetus has a prominent ridge around its equator, up to 20 km high and 200 km wide in places, running more than 1,600 km. It was discovered in images taken by the Cassini spacecraft on 31 December 2004. Its origin is debated; proposals include an ancient faster rotation period, compression as the moon cooled, or accretion from a ring or sub-satellite.

Exploration

The Cassini orbiter imaged Iapetus multiple times from moderate distances, including a close flyby in September 2007 that produced the best views of the equatorial ridge and the dichotomy boundary.
