Simile

A simile is a figure of speech that directly compares two things. Similes differ from metaphors by highlighting the similarities between two things using words such as "like", "as", "so", or "than", while metaphors create an implicit comparison. The word comes from the Latin similis, meaning "similar, like". Similes are used in everyday language as well as in literature, where extended or "epic" similes are a hallmark of classical poetry.

Uses

In everyday language

Similes occur constantly in ordinary English, often in conventionalised forms: "as busy as a bee", "as blind as a bat", "like a bull in a china shop", "slept like a log". Many have become clichés, but they remain a quick means of conveying an impression by reference to a familiar image.

In literature

Writers use similes to create vivid imagery and to illuminate character or atmosphere. Robert Burns's "O my Luve is like a red, red rose" (1794) is among the most famous lyric similes. The Homeric simile (or epic simile), developed in the Iliad and Odyssey, extends a comparison over many lines, digressing into a self-contained scene — for example, comparing a warrior's onslaught to a lion attacking a flock — before returning to the narrative. Epic similes were imitated by Virgil, Dante and Milton.

In comedy and rhetoric

Similes are also a staple of comic writing, where an unexpected or incongruous comparison produces humour, as in P. G. Wodehouse's "She looked as if she had been poured into her clothes and had forgotten to say 'when'". In rhetoric and advertising, similes help make abstract products or claims concrete and memorable.

Simile and metaphor

Simile and metaphor are closely related figures of comparison. A simile states the resemblance explicitly ("My love is like a rose"), whereas a metaphor asserts an identity ("My love is a rose"). Some theorists treat the simile as a weaker or more literal form, while others argue the explicit marker makes the comparison's terms clearer without diminishing its figurative force. Both belong to the broader class of figures of speech involving comparison, alongside analogy.

Examples

  • "Life is like a box of chocolates." — popularised by the film Forrest Gump (1994)
  • "Float like a butterfly, sting like a bee." — Muhammad Ali
  • "As hungry as a wolf", "as white as snow", "as brave as a lion"
  • "It seems to me you lived your life like a candle in the wind." — Bernie Taupin, "Candle in the Wind" (1973)
