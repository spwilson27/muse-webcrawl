Separation process

A separation process is a method used in chemistry and chemical engineering to transform a mixture of substances into two or more distinct product mixtures. Separations exploit differences in the chemical or physical properties of the components — such as size, solubility, boiling point, charge, or affinity for a surface — and are essential to industry, since materials are usually produced and used as mixtures.

Classes of separation

Separations may be mechanical (based on physical differences without chemical change) or diffusional (based on molecular transport between phases). Common operations include distillation (differences in volatility), filtration and sieving (particle size), centrifugation (density), crystallization (solubility and freezing), extraction and absorption (partitioning between phases), adsorption, membrane processes such as reverse osmosis, and chromatography in the laboratory and for high-value products.

Completeness and energy

Separations are rarely perfectly complete; engineers describe them with measures such as recovery, purity, and separation factor. Because mixing is spontaneous and unmixing is not, separation processes typically require substantial energy — separation operations account for a large share of industrial energy consumption, with distillation alone representing a significant fraction of energy use in chemical and petroleum processing.

Applications

Examples range from the fractionation of crude oil and the enrichment of uranium isotopes to water purification, air separation, ore beneficiation, and the purification of pharmaceuticals.
