Peat is an accumulation of partially decayed vegetation or organic matter. It is unique to natural areas called peatlands, bogs, mires, moors, or muskegs. Peat forms in wetland conditions where flooding or stagnant water obstructs the flow of oxygen from the atmosphere, slowing decomposition. Over millennia, peat deposits can develop into coal.

Formation and ecology

Peatlands are dominated by moisture-loving plants such as sphagnum mosses, sedges and shrubs. Because decomposition is so slow, peat accumulates at roughly a millimetre per year, preserving pollen, archaeological remains and even human bodies ("bog bodies"). Although peatlands cover only about 3% of the Earth's land surface, they store more carbon than all the world's vegetation combined, making them crucial to the climate system.

Uses

Dried peat has traditionally been cut for fuel in Ireland, Scotland, Finland and Russia, and burned in some power stations. In horticulture, peat moss is used as a soil conditioner and growing medium, though its extraction is increasingly restricted for environmental reasons. Peat also lends characteristic smoky flavour to some Scotch whisky, via malt dried over peat fires.

Environmental concerns

Draining peatlands for agriculture or forestry releases large quantities of carbon dioxide, and dry peat is vulnerable to wildfires that can smoulder underground for months. Restoration by rewetting is a growing conservation priority.
