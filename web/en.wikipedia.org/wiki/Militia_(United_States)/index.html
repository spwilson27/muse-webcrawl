The militia of the United States, as defined by Congress, has historically comprised the able-bodied male citizenry liable for military service, organised under state authority. Under current federal law (10 U.S.C. § 246), the militia consists of an organised militia — the National Guard and the Naval Militia — and an unorganised militia of able-bodied males aged 17 to 45 who are citizens or have declared their intention to become citizens, and who are not members of the organised militia. State defence forces, authorised separately, are also militia-type organisations under state control.

History

English colonies in America relied on militia companies of local men for defence, a tradition carried into the Revolution, whose opening shots were fired by Massachusetts militiamen at Lexington and Concord. The Constitution gave Congress power to call forth, organise, arm and discipline the militia while reserving to the states the appointment of officers and conduct of training; the Second Amendment (1791) declared a "well regulated Militia" necessary to the security of a free state. The Militia Acts of 1792 required enrolment of free able-bodied white male citizens aged 18–45.

From militia to National Guard

Nineteenth-century experience exposed the system's weaknesses, and reforms culminated in the Militia Act of 1903 (the Dick Act), which divided the militia into the organised militia — to be standardised and federally funded as the National Guard — and the reserve (unorganised) militia. The National Defense Act of 1916 further integrated the Guard into the federal military structure. Some states additionally maintain state defence forces that cannot be federalised as units.

Modern usage

Beyond its statutory meaning, "militia" is also used for private paramilitary groups, which are not part of the official militia and whose unauthorised military activities are restricted in every state. The constitutional militia clauses remain central to debates over the Second Amendment; in District of Columbia v. Heller (2008), the Supreme Court discussed the militia's history in holding that the amendment protects an individual right to keep and bear arms.
