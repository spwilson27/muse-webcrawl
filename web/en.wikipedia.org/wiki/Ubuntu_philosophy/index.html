Ubuntu (philosophy)

Ubuntu (Zulu pronunciation: [ùɓúntʰù]) is a Nguni Bantu term meaning "humanity". It is sometimes translated as "I am because we are" (also "I am because you are"), or "humanity towards others" (Zulu umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu). It is often used in a more philosophical sense to mean "the belief in a universal bond of sharing that connects all humanity".

Meaning and interpretation

The concept is expressed in maxims such as umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu — "a person is a person through other people". Archbishop Desmond Tutu, a leading exponent, described a person with ubuntu as open and available to others, affirming of others, and not threatened by the abilities of others, because they have a proper self-assurance that comes from knowing they belong in a greater whole. Philosophers such as John Mbiti summarized the communitarian outlook as "I am because we are, and since we are, therefore I am."

Historical and political uses

Ubuntu became widely known outside Africa after South Africa's transition from apartheid in the 1990s, where it informed ideas of restorative justice in the Truth and Reconciliation Commission and appeared in the postamble of the interim Constitution of 1993: "there is a need for understanding but not for vengeance, a need for reparation but not for retaliation, a need for ubuntu but not for victimisation." It also influenced jurisprudence such as the Constitutional Court's abolition of the death penalty in S v Makwanyane (1995). The concept has analogues across sub-Saharan Africa, such as botho in Botswana and unhu in Zimbabwe.

In philosophy and ethics

Scholars such as Ifeanyi Menkiti and Kwame Gyekye debated the strength of the communal claim, with Gyekye defending a moderate communitarianism that preserves individual rights. Ubuntu has been applied to business ethics, education, environmental ethics, bioethics and computing, and has been both celebrated as an authentically African philosophy and critiqued when used as an imprecise slogan.
