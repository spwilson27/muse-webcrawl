Burin (engraving)

TypeHand tool
UsesEngraving, intaglio printmaking, wood engraving
RelatedGraver, scorper, tint tool

A burin, the principal tool of the engraver.

A burin (from the French burin, meaning "cold chisel") is a steel cutting tool used in engraving. It consists of a rounded wooden handle, shaped like a mushroom or knob, fitted with a tempered steel shaft that ends in a very sharp cutting point. Burins are the essential instruments of intaglio engraving on metal plates and of wood engraving on end-grain blocks.

Description

The burin is held with the handle cupped in the palm of the hand, the shaft resting between thumb and forefinger. Rather than being struck like a chisel, the tool is pushed forward through the material by pressure from the palm, while the other hand rotates the plate or block, allowing the engraver to cut curved lines. The cutting tip is ground to a lozenge or square cross-section, and the angle of the facet determines the character of the line: a shallow angle produces a fine, shallow groove, while a steeper facet cuts a broader, deeper line that holds more ink and prints darker.

Types

Burins are made in many sizes and profiles. The ordinary lozenge-section graver is used for general line work. Round-pointed and flat tools (scorpers) clear larger areas, "tint tools" cut evenly spaced parallel lines, and multiple tools carry several points at once to produce textures. In wood engraving, smaller burins with shorter handles are used on boxwood blocks.

History

The burin became the defining tool of European printmaking in the fifteenth century, when goldsmiths such as Martin Schongauer adapted the gravers of their trade to engraving copper plates for printing. Albrecht Dürer raised burin engraving to a high art in the early sixteenth century. The technique dominated reproductive printmaking until the nineteenth century, when it survived most vigorously in wood engraving and in the engraving of banknotes and stamps, where fine burin work remains difficult to counterfeit.
