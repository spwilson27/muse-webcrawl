Ehlers–Danlos syndrome

Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (EDS) are a group of thirteen genetic connective-tissue disorders. Symptoms include loose joints, joint pain, stretchy velvety skin, and abnormal scar formation. These can be noticed at birth or in early childhood. Complications may include aortic dissection, joint dislocations, scoliosis, chronic pain, or early osteoarthritis.

Skin hyperextensibility and joint hypermobility are hallmarks of the classical type.

Classification

The 2017 international classification recognizes thirteen subtypes, most caused by variants in genes encoding fibrillar collagens or collagen-modifying enzymes. The most common is the hypermobile type (hEDS), whose genetic basis remains unknown and which is diagnosed clinically. The classical type (COL5A1, COL5A2) features marked skin hyperextensibility and atrophic scarring. The vascular type (COL3A1) is the most life-threatening, predisposing to spontaneous arterial and organ rupture. Other types include kyphoscoliotic, arthrochalasia, dermatosparaxis, cardiac-valvular, and periodontal forms.

Signs and symptoms

Joint hypermobility is commonly assessed with the Beighton score. Features across types include soft, doughy or velvety skin that bruises easily; widened atrophic ("cigarette-paper") scars; recurrent joint subluxations and dislocations; chronic musculoskeletal pain; and fatigue. Associated conditions frequently reported with hEDS include postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), mast cell activation disorders, gastrointestinal dysmotility, and dysautonomia.

History

The syndrome is named after two physicians who described it in the early twentieth century: Edvard Ehlers of Denmark (1901) and Henri-Alexandre Danlos of France (1908). Earlier descriptions of unusually elastic skin date to the seventeenth century. Some historians have speculated that the violinist Niccolò Paganini's extraordinary finger span reflected a connective-tissue disorder, though this is unproven.

Management

There is no cure; management is supportive and type-specific: physiotherapy to strengthen muscles around unstable joints, occupational therapy and joint protection, pain management, screening for cardiovascular complications (especially aortic root dilation and arterial fragility in vascular EDS), and genetic counseling. People with vascular EDS may be treated with celiprolol and require emergency protocols for arterial rupture.
