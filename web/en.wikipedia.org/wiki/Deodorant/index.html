Deodorant

A deodorant is a substance applied to the body to prevent or mask body odour caused by the bacterial breakdown of perspiration, most commonly in the armpits, feet, and other areas. A subclass of deodorants, called antiperspirants, additionally reduces the flow of sweat itself.

How deodorants work

Fresh sweat is largely odourless; body odour develops when skin bacteria metabolise components of apocrine sweat into volatile, odorous compounds. Deodorants work by making the skin surface more acidic, creating an environment hostile to bacteria, and often include antimicrobial agents and fragrances. Antiperspirants typically contain aluminium salts, such as aluminium chlorohydrate, which form temporary plugs in sweat ducts and reduce wetness.

History

Aromatic preparations to disguise body odour date to ancient Egypt and Rome. The first commercial deodorant, Mum, was trademarked in 1888 in Philadelphia; the first commercial antiperspirant, Everdry, followed in 1903. Roll-on applicators appeared in the 1950s, modelled on the ballpoint pen, and aerosol sprays became popular in the 1960s before declining amid environmental and health concerns.

Forms and health topics

Deodorants are sold as sticks, roll-ons, sprays, creams, gels, and mineral "crystal" stones of potassium alum. Public discussion has periodically raised questions about aluminium compounds and parabens in antiperspirants; major health authorities have found no convincing evidence linking deodorant use to breast cancer or Alzheimer's disease. Aerosol propellants and volatile compounds have also attracted environmental scrutiny.
