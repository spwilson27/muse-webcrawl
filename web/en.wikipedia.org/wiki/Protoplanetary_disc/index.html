Protoplanetary disc

Artist's concept of a protoplanetary disc.

A protoplanetary disc is a rotating circumstellar disc of dense gas and dust surrounding a young, newly formed star — a T Tauri star or Herbig Ae/Be star. The protoplanetary disc may be considered an accretion disc, because gases or other material may be falling from the inner edge of the disc onto the surface of the star. It is within these discs that planets form.

Formation

When a molecular cloud core collapses under gravity, conservation of angular momentum flattens the infalling material into a disc around the newborn protostar. Discs typically span tens to hundreds of astronomical units, contain a few percent of a solar mass of gas and dust, and live for roughly 3–10 million years before the gas is accreted, blown away, or locked into planets.

Planet formation

Dust grains collide and stick, growing through planetesimals into planetary embryos. Beyond the snow line, where water ice is stable, solid mass is much greater, allowing cores of giant planets to grow and capture gas. Observations by the ALMA telescope have imaged rings and gaps in discs such as HL Tauri and TW Hydrae, interpreted as the signatures of forming planets clearing their orbits.

Observations

Protoplanetary discs glow in infrared from warm dust and in millimetre waves from cold grains. Hubble Space Telescope images of "proplyds" in the Orion Nebula provided the first direct pictures of discs being photoevaporated by neighbouring massive stars.
