W. H. Auden

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: W. H. Auden in 1939, photographed on his arrival in New York: the poet in his early thirties at the start of his American life]

Wystan Hugh Auden (21 February 1907 – 29 September 1973) was a British-American poet, the dominant English-language poet of the mid-twentieth century. His work ranges from the cryptic, politically charged lyrics of the 1930s — 'Spain', 'September 1, 1939', 'Musée des Beaux Arts' — through the long philosophical poems New Year Letter (1941), For the Time Being (1944) and The Age of Anxiety (1947, Pulitzer Prize), to the humane, formidably crafted verse of his later, Austrian years.

Auden's technical range was unmatched among his contemporaries: ballads, villanelles, syllabics, Anglo-Saxon alliterative measure and light verse all served a mind that treated poetry as a game of knowledge — and after his return to Anglican faith around 1940, as a mode of moral attention.

The thirties and emigration

At Oxford Auden gathered the 'Auden group' (Spender, Day Lewis, MacNeice); his Poems (1930) and The Orators (1932) made him the voice of a generation between the wars. He reported from Iceland, China (with Isherwood, Journey to a War, 1939) and Spain, in 1939 emigrated to America with Isherwood — an act denounced in Parliament — and in 1946 became a US citizen.

Later life

With his lifelong companion Chester Kallman he wrote opera libretti (Stravinsky's The Rake's Progress, 1951, chief among them). 'September 1, 1939' — whose famous closing line about loving one another or dying he later disowned as dishonest — recirculated worldwide after September 11, 2001. His 'Funeral Blues' reached a mass audience through the film Four Weddings and a Funeral (1994).
