Manifesto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A manifesto is a written declaration of the intentions, motives or views of the issuer. In politics, an election manifesto (in the United States, a party platform) is a published statement of a political party's programme — the set of policies it promises to implement if elected to government — launched as the centrepiece of its campaign and used by voters, media and opponents to hold it to account.

Function and study

Manifestos serve internal purposes (disciplining candidates, coordinating factions) and external ones (signalling priorities and competence); in the UK, the manifesto has constitutional significance through the Salisbury convention, under which the House of Lords does not block legislation promised in the governing party's manifesto. Political scientists code manifestos comparatively (the Manifesto Project, analysing thousands of programmes in dozens of countries since 1945) to measure party positions, issue salience and mandate fulfilment — research showing parties fulfil a majority of pledges more often than cynicism suggests, with coalition compromise the main solvent. Famous manifestos range from The Communist Manifesto (1848, a movement text rather than an election programme) to Labour's 1945 "Let Us Face the Future" and the policy-heavy documents of German and Scandinavian parties.
