Coniston massacre

Date: August–October 1928
Location: Coniston Station, Northern Territory, Australia
Deaths: 31 confirmed (estimates up to 170)

The Coniston massacre, which took place in the region around Coniston cattle station in the Northern Territory of Australia from August to October 1928, was the last known officially sanctioned massacre of Indigenous Australians. The Warlpiri, Anmatyerre, and Kaytetye people were killed by a police party led by Mounted Constable George Murray in retaliation for the death of dingo hunter Frederick Brooks.

Events

Brooks was killed at Yurrkuru (Brooks Soak) on 7 August 1928 by a group of Warlpiri men, after a dispute involving the dingo hunter's use of an Aboriginal woman. In response, Murray led a series of punitive raids over several weeks in which Aboriginal camps were attacked. A Board of Inquiry in 1929 concluded that the police had acted in self-defence; 31 deaths were recorded, but Aboriginal accounts and later historians place the death toll between 60 and 170.

Legacy

The massacre is remembered by Warlpiri descendants, and commemorations have been held at Coniston and Yuendumu. It became a symbol of frontier violence in Australia, and accounts of the events were retold by survivors such as Billy Stockman Tjapaltjarri, whose artwork and storytelling helped bring the massacre to wider attention.
