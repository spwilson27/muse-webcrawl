In graph theory, a perfect graph is a graph in which the chromatic number of every induced subgraph equals the order of the largest clique of that subgraph. Equivalently, a graph G is perfect if χ(H) = ω(H) for every induced subgraph H of G. The concept was introduced by Claude Berge in 1961, and perfect graphs turned out to include many important families — bipartite graphs, chordal graphs, comparability graphs and interval graphs among them — for which otherwise hard problems such as colouring, clique finding and independent set become solvable in polynomial time.

The perfect graph theorems

The (weak) perfect graph theorem, proved by László Lovász in 1972, states that a graph is perfect if and only if its complement graph is also perfect. The strong perfect graph theorem, conjectured by Berge in 1961 and proved by Maria Chudnovsky, Neil Robertson, Paul Seymour and Robin Thomas (announced 2002, published 2006), gives a structural characterisation: a graph is perfect if and only if neither the graph nor its complement contains an induced odd cycle of length at least five (an "odd hole" or "odd antihole"). The 178-page proof was a landmark of modern combinatorics.

Algorithms

Colouring and the maximum clique and maximum independent set problems are NP-hard in general, but for perfect graphs they can be solved in polynomial time using the ellipsoid method with the theta function of Lovász. Recognising perfect graphs was itself shown to be polynomial by Chudnovsky and co-authors following the strong perfect graph theorem. Because many practical problems — scheduling, register allocation, channel assignment — are modelled by perfect or near-perfect graphs, the theory has wide algorithmic application.

Examples of perfect graph classes

  • Bipartite graphs and their line graphs
  • Chordal graphs and interval graphs
  • Comparability graphs and permutation graphs
  • Split graphs and trivially perfect graphs
