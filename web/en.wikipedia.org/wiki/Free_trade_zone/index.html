Free trade zone

A free trade zone (FTZ) is a geographic area where goods may be imported, stored, handled, manufactured, or re-exported under specific customs regulation and generally not subject to customs duty. Free trade zones are generally organised around major seaports, international airports, and national frontiers — areas with many geographic advantages for trade. Related concepts include export processing zones, free economic zones, and foreign-trade zones (the term used in the United States).

Purpose

Governments establish FTZs to attract foreign investment, create employment, encourage exports, and transfer technology, by offering duty deferral or exemption, simplified customs procedures, and sometimes tax holidays and relaxed regulation. Firms can import components duty-free, process them, and re-export the finished product without the duties that would apply to goods entering the domestic market.

History

The idea descends from the free ports of antiquity and early modern Europe, such as Hamburg and Singapore. The first modern foreign-trade zone in the United States was authorised by the Foreign-Trade Zones Act of 1934. China transformed the model with its special economic zones, beginning with Shenzhen in 1980, which became engines of export-led growth. Thousands of zones now operate worldwide; notable examples include Jebel Ali in Dubai, the Colón Free Trade Zone in Panama, and the Manaus Free Trade Zone in Brazil.

Criticism

Critics argue that some zones compete through suppressed labour rights and environmental standards, that incentives can amount to a "race to the bottom", and that benefits may leak away through transfer pricing and smuggling.
