Kiwi are flightless birds endemic to New Zealand, belonging to the genus Apteryx (from Greek: ἀ- "without", πτέρυξ "wing") in the family Apterygidae. There are five recognised species, ranging in size roughly from that of a domestic chicken to that of a small turkey. Kiwi are the smallest living ratites and lay, in proportion to their body size, among the largest eggs of any bird in the world.

Description and behaviour

Kiwi are nocturnal, with a highly developed sense of smell — unusual among birds — aided by nostrils at the very tip of their long bills, which they probe into soil and leaf litter for invertebrates. Their feathers are hair-like, they have no tail, and their vestigial wings are hidden beneath the plumage. Females lay a single enormous egg — up to 20% of the female's body weight — which in most species is incubated by the male for 63 to 92 days. Chicks hatch fully feathered and independent.

Species and conservation

The five species are the great spotted kiwi (roroa), little spotted kiwi, rowi, tokoeka, and the North Island brown kiwi. All have declined severely since human settlement of New Zealand, principally through predation by introduced stoats, dogs, cats, and possums. Conservation programmes, including the Department of Conservation's Operation Nest Egg — in which eggs are collected, hatched in captivity, and chicks released when large enough to resist stoats — have stabilised or increased some populations. The kiwi is a national symbol of New Zealand, and New Zealanders are colloquially known worldwide as "Kiwis".
