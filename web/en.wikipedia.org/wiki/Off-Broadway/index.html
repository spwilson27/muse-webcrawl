Off-Broadway is a designation for professional theatre productions in New York City presented in venues with between 100 and 499 seats — smaller than Broadway theatres (500 seats or more) but larger than Off-Off-Broadway spaces. The term refers to seating capacity and union contracts rather than geography: many Off-Broadway houses are in fact located on or near Broadway itself. Off-Broadway is known for adventurous, artistically ambitious work, lower production costs, and as an incubator for plays and musicals that later transfer to Broadway or tour internationally.

Definition

The classification arises from the contract tiers of Actors' Equity Association and the League of Resident Theatres. An Off-Broadway production may be commercial or non-profit; major non-profit institutions such as the Public Theater, Lincoln Center Theater's smaller stages, Playwrights Horizons, Manhattan Theatre Club and Roundabout's Off-Broadway venues anchor the sector alongside commercial houses such as the Orpheum and New World Stages.

History

The movement emerged in the late 1940s and 1950s as artists sought alternatives to Broadway's rising costs and commercial caution, staging classics and new European work in converted spaces in Greenwich Village and elsewhere. Landmarks include José Quintero's 1952 Circle in the Square revival of Summer and Smoke, the founding of the Public Theater by Joseph Papp, and the 1955 creation of the Village Voice Obie Awards. The 1960s rock musical Hair premiered at the Public before Broadway, and The Fantasticks (1960) ran Off-Broadway for 42 years, the longest run in musical history.

Notable productions and companies

Many major works began Off-Broadway, among them A Chorus Line (Public Theater workshops), Rent (New York Theatre Workshop), Hamilton (Public Theater), Fun Home, Hadestown and Avenue Q. Resident companies such as the Atlantic Theater Company, Signature Theatre and Vineyard Theatre maintain dedicated Off-Broadway homes.

Awards

Off-Broadway productions are honoured by the Obie Awards (for Off- and Off-Off-Broadway achievement, with no fixed categories), the Lucille Lortel Awards, the Drama Desk Awards and the Outer Critics Circle; unlike the Tony Awards, which are restricted to Broadway, these recognise the broader New York theatre scene.
