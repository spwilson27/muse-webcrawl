Enthalpy of vaporization

The enthalpy of vaporization (symbol ΔHvap), also known as the (latent) heat of vaporization, is the amount of energy that must be added to a given quantity of a liquid substance to transform it into a gas at constant pressure. It is usually quoted at the substance's normal boiling point, in kilojoules per mole. Condensation, the reverse process, releases the same amount of energy.

Values and trends

For water at 100 °C, ΔHvap is about 40.7 kJ/mol (2,257 J/g at 100 °C; slightly higher, about 44 kJ/mol, at 25 °C) — much larger than its enthalpy of fusion, because boiling must break essentially all intermolecular attractions. Values fall as temperature rises, reaching zero at the critical temperature, where liquid and gas become indistinguishable. The enthalpy of vaporization underlies the cooling produced by sweating and evaporation, the energy cost of distillation, and the operation of heat pipes and refrigeration cycles.

Trouton's rule and the Clausius–Clapeyron equation

Trouton's rule observes that the entropy of vaporization at the normal boiling point is roughly constant — about 85–88 J/(mol·K) — for many liquids, so ΔHvap ≈ Tb × 88 J/(mol·K). Strongly hydrogen-bonded liquids such as water deviate upward because their liquid state is unusually ordered. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation links the enthalpy of vaporization to the way vapour pressure rises with temperature, allowing ΔHvap to be measured from vapour-pressure data.

Measurement

Values are measured calorimetrically, for example by electrically heating a liquid at its boiling point and recording the mass evaporated, or derived from vapour-pressure curves. Engineering practice also uses empirical correlations (such as the Watson correlation) to adjust a known value to other temperatures.
