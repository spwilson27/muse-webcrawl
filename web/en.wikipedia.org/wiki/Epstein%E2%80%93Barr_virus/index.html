Epstein–Barr virus

Epstein–Barr virus
Family: Herpesviridae
Also known as: Human herpesvirus 4 (HHV-4)
Discovered: 1964
Associated diseases: Infectious mononucleosis, Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma

The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), formally called human gammaherpesvirus 4, is one of the most common viruses in humans and a member of the herpes virus family. It was identified in 1964 by Michael Epstein, Yvonne Barr and colleagues from cells cultured from Burkitt lymphoma samples, making it the first virus shown to cause cancer in humans.

Infection

EBV is transmitted mainly through saliva. Primary infection in adolescence or young adulthood often causes infectious mononucleosis ("glandular fever"), while infection in early childhood is usually asymptomatic. The virus infects B lymphocytes and establishes lifelong latency; more than 90% of adults worldwide carry it.

Associated conditions

EBV is associated with several cancers, including Burkitt lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and with autoimmune conditions; research published in 2022 strengthened evidence linking EBV infection to multiple sclerosis.
