The Treaty of Paris, signed on 3 September 1783, formally ended the American Revolutionary War between Great Britain and the United States. In the treaty, Britain recognised the independence of the thirteen colonies and agreed to the western boundary of the new nation.

Terms

The treaty set the boundaries of the United States as the Atlantic Ocean in the east, Canada in the north, Florida in the south, and the Mississippi River in the west. Britain agreed to withdraw its troops "with all convenient speed". The treaty also granted Americans fishing rights off Newfoundland, called for the return of confiscated loyalist property, and pledged that creditors on both sides would meet no lawful impediment to recovering debts.

Context

The agreement was one of a series of treaties signed in Paris and Versailles in 1783: Britain also made peace with France, Spain and the Netherlands, with Spain regaining Florida and France securing fishing rights and trading concessions. Benjamin West's unfinished painting American Commissioners of the Preliminary Peace Agreement with Great Britain famously leaves the British commissioners unpainted, as they never sat for the artist.

Legacy

Ratified by Congress in January 1784, the treaty established the United States as an independent republic and doubled its territory; its vague treatment of loyalist restitution and the western frontier seeded disputes that persisted for decades.
