Hibakusha

Hibakusha (Japanese: 被爆者, "bomb-affected person") is the term for the survivors of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. The word originally referred to those directly exposed to the bombings, and its legal definition in Japan has expanded to cover several categories of exposed persons, including those exposed in utero and people who entered the affected areas shortly afterward.

Recognition and support

Under Japan's Atomic Bomb Survivors' Support Law, officially recognized hibakusha receive a health handbook, medical care, and allowances. As of the 2020s the average age of recognized survivors exceeds 80, and their number declines each year from a peak of over 370,000. Korean and other foreign nationals exposed in the two cities fought long court battles for equal recognition and benefits.

Health effects

Long-term studies, principally by the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, have documented elevated risks of leukemia and solid cancers, cataracts, and other conditions among survivors, with risk varying by dose and age at exposure. Studies of the children of survivors have not shown statistically significant increases in hereditary disease, though research continues.

Testimony and activism

Hibakusha have been central witnesses in the global movement for nuclear disarmament, giving testimony at the United Nations and in schools, and their confederation, Nihon Hidankyo, has campaigned for a nuclear weapons ban and for state compensation. Preserving their accounts as the generation ages has become a major memorial effort in Hiroshima and Nagasaki.
