North Germanic languages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The North Germanic languages make up one of the three branches of the Germanic languages — a sub-family of the Indo-European languages — along with the West Germanic and the extinct East Germanic branches. The language group is sometimes referred to as the Nordic languages, a direct translation of the most common term used among Danish, Faroese, Icelandic, Norwegian, and Swedish scholars and among ordinary people in the Scandinavian countries.

Languages

The modern North Germanic languages are Danish, Swedish, Norwegian (Bokmål and Nynorsk), Icelandic, and Faroese, together with the endangered Elfdalian. About 20 million people speak them natively in Northern Europe. Danish, Swedish, and Norwegian form a dialect continuum with a high degree of mutual intelligibility, while Icelandic and Faroese, the Insular Nordic languages, are more conservative and not readily intelligible to mainland speakers.

History

All North Germanic languages descend from Proto-Norse, itself descended from Proto-Germanic. Runic inscriptions from the early centuries AD record the earliest stage (Elder Futhark). By the Viking Age, Old Norse had differentiated into Old West Norse (Norwegian, Icelandic, Faroese) and Old East Norse (Danish, Swedish). The Middle Ages produced major literature, notably the Icelandic sagas and Eddas.

Distinctive features of the branch include the suffixed definite article (e.g., Danish huset, "the house") and, in Danish, the stød.
