Ice rise

An ice rise is a locally grounded area within a floating ice shelf where the ice rests on a bedrock high point or shoal rising above sea level, causing the shelf ice to be pushed upward into a dome or ridge. Ice rises are typically tens of kilometres across and rise a few hundred metres above the surrounding shelf surface.

Formation and flow

Where a floating ice shelf encounters a seabed obstruction, the ice grounds and forms its own locally independent flow regime, distinct from the flow of the surrounding shelf. Ice divides form on the summit of the rise, and the flow pattern around it resembles that of a small ice cap, although the feature remains embedded within, and hydraulically connected to, the surrounding shelf. Similar but smaller features, formed where the ice is only slightly raised without a fully independent flow regime, are called ice rumples.

Importance

Ice rises act as pinning points that buttress ice shelves, resisting their flow and slowing the discharge of grounded ice from the ice sheet interior; their loss or ungrounding can therefore contribute to shelf thinning and acceleration of tributary glaciers. Well-known examples include Roosevelt Island in the Ross Ice Shelf, and the Berkner Island, Henry Ice Rise, and Korff Ice Rise within the Filchner–Ronne Ice Shelf system. Because ice rises accumulate their own layered snow, they are also valuable sites for ice-core drilling and for studying ice dynamics.
