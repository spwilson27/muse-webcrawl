Time preference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, time preference (or time discounting, delay discounting, temporal discounting, discounting) is the current relative valuation placed on receiving a good or some amount of money at an earlier date compared with receiving it at a later date. Time preferences are captured mathematically in the discount function. The higher the time preference, the higher the discount placed on returns receivable or costs payable in the future.

Time preference is a central explanation, along with risk and expected changes in wealth, for the existence of interest: lenders must be compensated for deferring consumption, and borrowers are willing to pay for earlier access to resources. A person with a high time preference is more oriented toward the present (they need a large premium to wait), whereas a low time preference corresponds to patience.

Theory

The concept was developed in the 19th and early 20th centuries by economists including John Rae, Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, Irving Fisher, and Frank Ramsey. In the standard discounted-utility model introduced by Paul Samuelson in 1937, future utility is discounted at a constant exponential rate. Later behavioural research found systematic deviations: people often discount hyperbolically, valuing near-term delays much more steeply than distant ones, which produces preference reversals and self-control problems of the kind studied in intertemporal-choice experiments such as the marshmallow test.

Measurement and determinants

Time preference is measured using monetary-choice questionnaires, experimental tasks that offer smaller-sooner versus larger-later rewards, and inferred from market behaviour. It varies with age, income and wealth, culture, education, expectations of inflation and survival, and personality traits such as impulsivity. Steep delay discounting is associated with addiction, poor savings, and adverse health behaviours, and is studied in behavioural economics, psychology, and neuroscience, where choices between immediate and delayed rewards involve interacting valuation and cognitive-control systems in the brain.

Applications

Beyond interest theory, time preference informs the choice of the social discount rate in cost–benefit analysis of long-lived public projects — prominently in climate-change economics, where the debate between the Stern Review (low discount rate) and William Nordhaus (higher, market-based rate) turns substantially on the pure rate of time preference applied across generations.
