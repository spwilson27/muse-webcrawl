Allegory

An allegory is a narrative, poem, or picture that can be interpreted to reveal a hidden meaning, typically a moral, spiritual, or political one, in which characters, settings, and events function as symbols for abstract ideas. The term derives from the Greek allēgoria, "speaking otherwise."

Forms and examples

Famous literary allegories include Plato's Allegory of the Cave, Dante's Divine Comedy, John Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress (1678), in which the hero Christian journeys toward the Celestial City, and George Orwell's Animal Farm (1945), a political allegory of the Russian Revolution and Stalinism. Medieval literature is rich in allegory, from the personified virtues and vices of Prudentius's Psychomachia to the dream-vision Roman de la Rose.

Allegory versus symbol and metaphor

Allegory differs from a single metaphor in extending the figurative meaning systematically through a whole work, and from the symbol in tending toward a structured, one-to-one correspondence between the surface story and its second meaning. The allegorical interpretation of scripture — reading biblical narratives for spiritual senses beyond the literal — was a major method of medieval exegesis, articulated in the fourfold scheme of literal, allegorical, moral, and anagogical senses.

In the visual arts

Painters from Botticelli to the Dutch still-life masters used vanitas imagery and personifications (Justice with her scales, Fortune with her wheel) to convey moral messages, following handbooks such as Cesare Ripa's Iconologia.
