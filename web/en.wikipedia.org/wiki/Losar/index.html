Losar

Losar (Tibetan: ལོ་གསར་, Wylie: lo gsar, "new year") is the most important holiday in the Tibetan calendar and the festival of the Tibetan New Year. It is celebrated by Tibetans and by related Himalayan peoples, including the Sherpa (as Gyalpo Lhosar), Tamang (Sonam Lhosar), Gurung (Tamu Lhosar) and the people of Bhutan, as well as in Tibetan Buddhist communities in Nepal, India and worldwide. The festival typically falls in February or March in the Gregorian calendar and is celebrated over fifteen days, with the main festivities on the first three days.

History

Losar predates the arrival of Buddhism in Tibet and originates in the winter incense-burning customs of the indigenous Bön religion, when offerings were made to local deities and spirits. Under the influence of Buddhism, and of the calendrical systems of China and India, the festival became fixed as the beginning of the lunisolar Tibetan year, which runs in sixty-year cycles and twelve-year animal cycles. According to tradition, the systematisation of the Tibetan calendar is associated with the era of the early Tibetan empire and was later refined by scholars working with Mongolian and Chinese calendrical knowledge.

Celebrations

Preparations begin in the final days of the old year with housecleaning, the settling of debts, and the making of special foods, including khapse (fried pastries) and a dumpling soup called guthuk, eaten on the twenty-ninth day, which contains symbolic ingredients foretelling the eater's character in the coming year. Monasteries hold cham dances and ceremonies to expel the evils of the old year, and a ceremonial scapegoat or effigy may be carried out amid noise to drive away misfortune.

On the first day of the new year, families rise before dawn, make offerings at household shrines, drink changkol (barley beer soup), and exchange greetings of "Tashi Delek". The second day is traditionally for visits to monasteries and to dignitaries; the third for outdoor celebrations, raising new prayer flags on rooftops, and communal gatherings. Celebrations continue with butter-lamp offerings and prayers through the fifteenth day, which culminates in the Butter Lamp Festival (Chötrul Düchen), commemorating the Buddha's display of miracles.
