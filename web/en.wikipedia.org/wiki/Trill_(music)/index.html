Trill (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trill (or shake, as it was known from the 16th until the early 20th century) is a musical ornament consisting of a rapid alternation between two adjacent notes, usually a semitone or tone apart, which can be identified with the context of the trill (compare mordent and tremolo). It is notated with the letters tr or a wavy line above the staff.

Performance practice

The exact realisation of a trill depends on period and style. In Baroque and early Classical music, a trill conventionally begins on the upper auxiliary note and ends with a turn (termination) of two or three notes; treatises by composers such as C. P. E. Bach and Quantz give detailed rules. From the nineteenth century onward, trills more often begin on the principal note itself. The speed of alternation typically accelerates, and the number of alternations is not fixed but chosen by the performer.

In singing, a trill requires rapid oscillation between two pitches, traditionally prized in coloratura singing; the term is also applied loosely — and controversially — to vibrato-like effects in popular music.

Notation

In modern notation a trill is indicated by tr or tr followed by a wavy line to the end of the affected note. In earlier notation, signs such as a cross over a note (in French Baroque music) were used. Accidental alterations of the auxiliary note are shown with a small accidental above the trill sign.

Related ornaments

The trill belongs to the family of ornaments that includes the mordent (a single rapid alternation), the turn, the appoggiatura and the tremolo, which is a rapid repetition of a single note or alternation of widely separated notes.
