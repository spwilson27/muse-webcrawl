Imagination

Imagination
FieldPhilosophy, psychology, aesthetics
Related facultiesMemory, perception, creativity, mental imagery

Imagination is the faculty of forming new ideas, images or concepts that are not present to the senses. It allows the mind to explore possibilities beyond the immediate environment: to anticipate the future, entertain counterfactual scenarios, understand other minds, and create works of art and invention. Activity in this faculty in the absence of stimuli is also referred to as phantasy.

In philosophy

Aristotle treated phantasia as the faculty that presents images to the soul between perception and thought. Kant made imagination central to his epistemology, distinguishing the reproductive imagination from the productive imagination that synthesises the manifold of experience into objects. Romantic writers such as Coleridge celebrated imagination as a creative, unifying power, distinguishing it from mere "fancy". In contemporary philosophy, imagination is studied in connection with modal knowledge, thought experiments, pretense and mind-reading (simulation theory).

In psychology

Psychologists study imagination through mental imagery, daydreaming, pretend play in childhood, and its role in planning and episodic future thinking. Imagination overlaps heavily with memory: remembering the past and imagining the future engage many of the same brain networks, notably the default mode network. Vygotsky emphasised imagination as a specifically human activity built from experience and central to creativity and learning.

Forms and roles

  • Sensory (mental) imagery: quasi-perceptual experiences in any modality.
  • Propositional imagination: imagining that something is the case, as in supposing for argument's sake.
  • Creative imagination: generating novel combinations and solutions in art, science and everyday problem-solving.
  • Social imagination: empathising with and modelling the perspectives of others.
