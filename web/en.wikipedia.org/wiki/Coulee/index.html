Coulee

Coulee

[Image: a dry coulee gully in the badlands of the northern Great Plains]
TypeLandform / gully
RegionNorth America (Canada and the United States)
EtymologyCanadian French coulée, from couler, "to flow"

A coulee (from the Canadian French coulée, from the French verb couler meaning "to flow") is a term used in North America for several different kinds of landforms. Most often it refers to a deep, steep-sided gully or ravine, typically dry or carrying only an intermittent stream, formed by water erosion. In the northern Great Plains and the Canadian Prairies, coulees are a characteristic feature of the landscape, cutting into otherwise flat or gently rolling terrain.

Characteristics

Coulees are usually formed by the erosive action of meltwater, flash floods, or glacial runoff. Many coulees in the northern plains were carved at the end of the last ice age when enormous volumes of glacial meltwater drained across the landscape. They may range from a few metres to hundreds of metres in depth and can extend for many kilometres. Their floors often support distinctive vegetation — shrubs, grasses and sometimes trees such as cottonwood — sheltered from wind and fire, creating important wildlife habitat in otherwise open prairie.

Regional usage

The word's meaning varies by region. In Louisiana and the Gulf Coast, a coulee is a small stream or bayou. In the Pacific Northwest, particularly in Washington State, coulees are large, steep-walled, trench-like channels carved by catastrophic glacial outburst floods — the most famous being the Grand Coulee, now partly occupied by the Columbia Basin irrigation project and Banks Lake behind Grand Coulee Dam. In Wisconsin's Driftless Area, coulees are the deep valleys between ridges of the Coulee Region around La Crosse.

Role in history and ecology

On the plains, coulees provided water, wood, shelter and travel corridors for Indigenous peoples, fur traders and settlers. Their sheltered microclimates support plants and animals not found on the exposed uplands, and they are important refuges for species such as mule deer, coyotes and numerous songbirds. Well-known examples include the coulees of the Missouri Breaks in Montana, Writing-on-Stone Provincial Park in Alberta, and the Big Muddy Badlands of Saskatchewan.
