Hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic

Weimar hyperinflation
Period: 1921–1923
Currency: Papiermark
Ended by: Rentenmark, Nov 1923

Hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic was the episode in 1921–1923 when the German Papiermark fell catastrophically in value. It was among the first modern hyperinflations and became a defining trauma of the Weimar Republic.

Background

Germany financed the First World War largely by borrowing and monetary expansion rather than taxation. After defeat, the Treaty of Versailles imposed reparations — fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks — payable partly in hard currency, which Germany could obtain only by selling marks, driving depreciation. Deficits, the occupation of the Ruhr in January 1923 after a default on deliveries, and the government-funded passive resistance strike were financed by the printing press.

Course

In 1914 the dollar bought about 4.2 marks; by November 1923 it bought about 4.2 trillion. Prices doubled within days, then hours. Workers were paid twice daily and rushed to spend wages; banknotes were burned as fuel, pasted as wallpaper, and played with by children. Savings, bonds and pensions were wiped out, while debtors — including the state and mortgaged farmers — gained.

Stabilisation

Under Finance Minister Hans Luther and Reichsbank president Hjalmar Schacht, the Rentenmark was introduced in November 1923, nominally backed by land and industrial assets, exchanged at one trillion Papiermark to one. Fiscal cuts, the end of Ruhr resistance, and the Dawes Plan (1924) restructuring reparations restored confidence.

Legacy

Historians debate how far hyperinflation contributed to the later rise of Nazism — the middle-class trauma was real, but the Republic stabilised for several years afterwards; the Great Depression is usually seen as more decisive. The memory still shapes German monetary conservatism and European Central Bank culture. Compare Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe.
