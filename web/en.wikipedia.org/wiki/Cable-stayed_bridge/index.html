Cable-stayed bridge

[Hero image placeholder: cable-stayed bridge]

A cable-stayed bridge is a bridge in which one or more towers (pylons) support the deck by means of cables (stays) that run directly from the tower to the deck. Unlike a suspension bridge, whose main cables are draped between anchorages and carry vertical suspenders, the stays of a cable-stayed bridge connect the tower and deck directly and work in tension, while the deck works largely in compression.

History

The concept dates to the late 18th and early 19th centuries, but early examples suffered collapses and the idea fell out of favour. The modern cable-stayed bridge emerged after the Second World War, aided by computer analysis and high-strength steel. The Strömsund Bridge in Sweden (1956) is often cited as the first modern example. Designs proliferated from the 1960s onward, notably through the work of German engineers and the French engineer Michel Virlogeux.

Design

Stay arrangements are usually described as fan patterns (all stays converge at the tower top), harp patterns (stays parallel, attached at intervals up the tower), or modifications of these. Bridges may have a single central pylon, two pylons, or multiple pylons for very long crossings. Cable-stayed bridges are generally most economical for spans of roughly 150 to 900 metres, filling the gap between girder bridges and suspension bridges.

Notable examples

  • The Russky Bridge in Russia (2012) has the longest cable-stayed span in the world at 1,104 m.
  • The Millau Viaduct in France (2004), designed by Norman Foster and Michel Virlogeux, is the tallest bridge in the world.
  • The Sutong and Stonecutters bridges in China, and the Øresund Bridge between Denmark and Sweden, are other prominent examples.

Comparison with suspension bridges

Cable-stayed bridges need no massive anchorages, are stiffer, and can be built by balanced cantilevering from the pylons. Suspension bridges remain better suited to the very longest spans.
