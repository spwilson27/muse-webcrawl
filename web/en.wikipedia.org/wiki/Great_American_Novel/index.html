Great American Novel

The "Great American Novel" (sometimes abbreviated as GAN) is a canonical novel thought to embody and examine the essence of America, of the national character or experience — capturing, as it is often put, the nation's character and the spirit of its times in a single definitive work. The concept functions less as a formal designation than as an enduring aspiration and critical conversation piece in American letters.

Origin of the concept

The phrase is traced to an 1868 essay, "The Great American Novel", by the novelist John William De Forest, published in The Nation. Writing in the aftermath of the Civil War, De Forest called for a novel that would paint the American soul, and offered Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) as the closest approach to that point. In the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, as American literature matured and sought independence from European models, the hunt for the Great American Novel became a recurring theme of criticism.

Common candidates

Works most frequently advanced as claimants include:

  • The Scarlet Letter (1850) by Nathaniel Hawthorne
  • Moby-Dick (1851) by Herman Melville — often cited as the foremost candidate, for its fusion of whaling narrative, metaphysics and democratic scope
  • Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) by Mark Twain — praised by Ernest Hemingway as the source of all modern American literature
  • The Great Gatsby (1925) by F. Scott Fitzgerald — a compact portrait of the American Dream in the Jazz Age
  • The Grapes of Wrath (1939) by John Steinbeck
  • Invisible Man (1952) by Ralph Ellison
  • Beloved (1987) by Toni Morrison

Other frequently named works include The Catcher in the Rye, To Kill a Mockingbird, Lolita, Gravity's Rainbow, Blood Meridian and Infinite Jest.

Criticism of the concept

Modern critics often question whether a single novel could represent a country as large and diverse as the United States, and note that the traditional canon of candidates reflected narrow assumptions about whose experience counts as "American". The phrase survives as much as a marker of ambition — applied semi-ironically to sprawling, generation-spanning novels — as a serious verdict. Philip Roth titled a 1973 baseball novel The Great American Novel in playful homage to the quest.
