Spanish Apocalypse

The Spanish Apocalypses are a group of illuminated manuscripts produced in medieval Spain containing the Commentary on the Apocalypse by Beatus of Liébana, an eighth-century Asturian monk. Compiled around 776 and revised in the following decade, the commentary weaves together excerpts from earlier Church Fathers to interpret the Book of Revelation.

The Beatus manuscripts

Some twenty-five manuscripts of the commentary survive wholly or in part, most of them created between the tenth and thirteenth centuries in monasteries of northern Christian Spain, such as San Salvador de Tábara, Valcavado, and Santo Domingo de Silos. They are celebrated for their vivid, flat, intensely coloured miniatures in the Mozarabic style, depicting the angels, beasts, and heavenly Jerusalem of Revelation against bands of saturated colour. Famous examples include the Morgan Beatus (c. 940–945), the Girona Beatus (975), the Saint-Sever Beatus, and the Silos Apocalypse (1091–1109).

Context

The manuscripts were produced in a frontier society living alongside Muslim al-Andalus, and the commentary's fierce anti-heretical and eschatological tone has been linked to the Adoptionist controversy and to monastic expectations of the end of the world around the year 800 and again near 1000. The Beatus tradition is one of the great achievements of early medieval European art, and the surviving codices are held by libraries and museums in Spain, France, Britain, and the United States.
