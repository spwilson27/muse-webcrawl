Federico García Lorca

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: García Lorca in the 1930s, dark-suited and intense, the poet of duende in the last summers before the war]

Federico del Sagrado Corazón de Jesús García Lorca (5 June 1898 – 19 August 1936) was a Spanish poet, playwright and theatre director, the most internationally acclaimed Spanish writer of the twentieth century and its great martyr: murdered by Nationalist militia at the start of the Spanish Civil War, his body never recovered.

A leading figure of the Generation of '27, Lorca fused Andalusian folk tradition — flamenco, gypsy balladry, the cante jondo he and Manuel de Falla championed — with avant-garde surrealism. His books include Gypsy Ballads (1928), Poet in New York (written 1929–30, published posthumously), and the plays Blood Wedding (1932), Yerma (1934) and The House of Bernarda Alba (1936, first staged 1945).

Duende

Lorca's 1933 lecture 'Play and Theory of the Duende' named his aesthetic: the dark spirit of earth, death and improvisation that possesses flamenco and bullfighting, against the muse and the angel of mere beauty. His theatre's Andalusia — women caged by honour, desire against stone houses, the moon and the knife — made myth of his region's repressions; with his touring student company La Barraca he took classics to village squares across the Republic's Spain.

Death and afterlife

Arrested in Granada in August 1936 (he had refused chances to flee), Lorca was shot with a schoolteacher and two anarchist bullfighters near Víznar; the grave remains unlocated despite searches. Franco's Spain banned his works; the world made him its emblem of the artist against fascism, and the search for his remains became democratic Spain's memory politics in miniature.
