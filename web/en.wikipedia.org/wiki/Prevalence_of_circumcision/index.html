Prevalence of circumcision

Estimated prevalence of male circumcision by country

The prevalence of circumcision is the percentage of males in a given population who have been circumcised. Globally, roughly one-third to 39 percent of males are circumcised, according to estimates published by the World Health Organization and later research. Rates vary enormously between countries, driven by religious practice, culture, and medical policy.

Global distribution

Circumcision is nearly universal in most Muslim-majority countries and in Israel, where it is performed for religious reasons, and is also widespread in much of sub-Saharan Africa, where it is practiced in many societies as a rite of passage and has been promoted in some countries as part of HIV prevention programs. It is common in the United States, South Korea, and the Philippines (where the traditional rite of tuli is widely practiced), but rare in most of Europe, Latin America, and East Asia outside South Korea.

Examples by region

  • Africa — Very high rates (above 90 percent) in North and West Africa; rates vary in southern and eastern Africa, where voluntary medical male circumcision programs for HIV prevention have raised prevalence in several countries since the 2000s.
  • Americas — The United States historically had high rates of newborn circumcision (over 80 percent in the mid-20th century), declining to roughly 55–60 percent of newborn boys in recent decades, with regional variation. Rates are low in most of Latin America.
  • Asia — Nearly universal in the Muslim-majority countries of West and Central Asia and in Indonesia; very high in the Philippines and South Korea; low in China, India (outside Muslim communities), and Japan.
  • Europe — Generally low (often under 10–20 percent), concentrated in Jewish and Muslim communities, with somewhat higher rates in some Balkan countries.
  • Oceania — Australia historically had high rates but newborn circumcision has declined substantially; rates are low in New Zealand but high among many Pacific Islander peoples.

Trends

Worldwide prevalence has been rising slowly in absolute terms due to population growth in high-prevalence regions and the expansion of voluntary medical male circumcision for HIV prevention in eastern and southern Africa, even as routine infant circumcision has declined in several English-speaking countries. Medical organizations differ in their assessments of the health benefits and risks, and the practice remains the subject of ethical and policy debate.
