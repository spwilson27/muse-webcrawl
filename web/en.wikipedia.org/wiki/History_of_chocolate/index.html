History of chocolate

The history of chocolate traces the use of cacao from its origins in the upper Amazon and its domestication in Mesoamerica, where it was consumed as a bitter, spiced drink, through its adoption in Europe as a sweetened luxury, to its industrialisation in the 19th century, which produced the solid chocolate bar and made chocolate a global mass-market food.

Mesoamerican origins

Evidence of cacao use extends back more than 3,000 years; genetic studies place the domestication of Theobroma cacao in the upper Amazon basin, with use spreading to Mesoamerica, where the Olmec, Maya and later peoples cultivated it. The Maya drank a frothy, bitter beverage of ground cacao with water, chilli and other flavourings, used beans in ritual, and depicted cacao in art and writing. The Aztecs (Mexica), who called the drink xocolatl, obtained cacao as tribute from conquered regions; the beans also served as currency. Emperor Moctezuma II was said to drink it in quantity.

Arrival in Europe

Columbus encountered cacao in 1502, and Hernán Cortés's conquest of Mexico (1519–1521) brought the drink to Spanish attention. In Spain, chocolate was sweetened with sugar and flavoured with cinnamon and vanilla, becoming a fashionable drink at court while remaining a jealously guarded Spanish secret for nearly a century. It spread to Italy, France and England in the 17th century; London's chocolate houses, like its coffee houses, became social and political centres. Until the 19th century, chocolate remained an expensive drink for elites, produced from cacao grown largely by indigenous and enslaved labour in the Americas.

Industrialisation

In 1828 Coenraad van Houten's hydraulic press and alkalisation process ("Dutching") made cocoa powder cheap to produce. In 1847 the Bristol firm J. S. Fry & Sons created the first modern solid eating chocolate by mixing cocoa butter back into cocoa liquor with sugar. Daniel Peter's milk chocolate (1875, using Henri Nestlé's powdered milk), Rodolphe Lindt's conching machine (1879) and the founding of firms such as Cadbury, Mars and Hershey turned chocolate into an affordable mass product. In the 20th century production shifted to West Africa, which now supplies most of the world's cocoa, amid continuing concerns about child labour and deforestation in the industry.
