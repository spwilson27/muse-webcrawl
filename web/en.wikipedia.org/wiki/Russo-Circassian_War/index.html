Russo-Circassian War
Date: 1763 – 21 May 1864
Location: Circassia, Northwest Caucasus
Result: Russian victory; annexation of Circassia; mass expulsion of Circassians to the Ottoman Empire

The Russo-Circassian War (1763–1864) was the invasion of Circassia by the Russian Empire and the long resistance of the Circassian peoples of the Northwest Caucasus. It is often considered the longest war the Russian Empire ever fought, lasting roughly a century. The war ended with the annexation of Circassia and the expulsion of a large proportion of the Circassian population to the Ottoman Empire, an event remembered by Circassians as the Circassian genocide and commemorated annually on 21 May.

Background

Circassia occupied the northwestern Caucasus between the Black Sea and the Kuban River. Russia's interest in the region grew with its expansion toward the Black Sea and its acquisition of Georgia. In 1763 Russian forces built the fortress of Mozdok on Circassian (Kabardian) land, an act generally taken as the start of the conflict.

Course of the war

Russian strategy relied on building lines of fortresses and Cossack settlements (the Caucasian Defensive Line), clearing forests, and destroying villages and crops to force submission. Circassian resistance was decentralised, conducted by the various Circassian tribes and, in the 1830s–1850s, influenced by the Naqshbandi Sufi movement and envoys of Imam Shamil from the northeast Caucasus. British and other foreign agents, such as David Urquhart and James Stanislaus Bell, publicised the Circassian cause abroad, but no foreign power intervened militarily.

After Shamil's surrender in 1859 freed Russian forces, General Nikolai Yevdokimov implemented a plan for the mass expulsion of the Circassians. The war was declared over on 21 May 1864 (Old Style) after the Battle of Qbaada, the last stand of Circassian forces in the mountains near modern Krasnaya Polyana.

Expulsion and consequences

Hundreds of thousands of Circassians were deported to the Ottoman Empire in 1864 and the following years; many died of disease, hunger, and shipwreck during the crossing of the Black Sea. Estimates of the number expelled and of total deaths vary widely. The great majority of Circassians today live in the diaspora, principally in Turkey, Jordan, and Syria, while those who remained in Russia form minorities in Adygea, Kabardino-Balkaria, and Karachay-Cherkessia. In 2011, Georgia became the first UN member state to recognise the events as genocide.
