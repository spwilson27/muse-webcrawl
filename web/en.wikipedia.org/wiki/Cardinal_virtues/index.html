The cardinal virtues are four virtues of mind and character recognised in classical and Christian moral philosophy: prudence, justice, temperance and fortitude (courage). The name derives from the Latin cardo, "hinge", because all other virtues were held to hinge upon these.

Origins

Plato set out the four virtues in the Republic, mapping wisdom (prudence), courage and temperance onto the classes of his ideal city, with justice as the virtue of the whole. Aristotle, the Stoics, and Cicero elaborated the scheme. A scriptural parallel appears in the Book of Wisdom (8:7), which teaches "temperance and prudence, justice and fortitude".

In Christian thought

Church Fathers such as Ambrose and Augustine adopted the four virtues, coining the term "cardinal". Thomas Aquinas integrated them into his moral theology, distinguishing these acquired, natural virtues from the three theological virtues — faith, hope and charity — which together make the traditional seven virtues. In medieval and Renaissance art the cardinal virtues are personified as women bearing symbolic attributes: Prudence with a mirror and serpent, Justice with scales and sword, Temperance mixing water and wine, and Fortitude with a column or club.
