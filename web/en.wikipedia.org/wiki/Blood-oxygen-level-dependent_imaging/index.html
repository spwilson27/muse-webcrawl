Blood-oxygen-level-dependent imaging

BOLD imaging
MethodFunctional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)
Signal sourceMagnetic differences between oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin
Pioneered bySeiji Ogawa et al., 1990

Blood-oxygen-level-dependent imaging, or BOLD-contrast imaging, is a method used in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to observe which areas of the brain are active at a given time. It relies on the different magnetic properties of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.

Principle

Deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic and distorts the local magnetic field, reducing the MRI signal, whereas oxyhaemoglobin is diamagnetic and does not. When a brain region becomes active, local blood flow increases more than oxygen consumption, so the concentration of deoxyhaemoglobin falls and the BOLD signal rises. The response, called the haemodynamic response function, peaks several seconds after neural activity.

History

Seiji Ogawa at Bell Laboratories demonstrated the BOLD effect in rodents in 1990; human functional imaging using BOLD followed in 1991–92. The technique transformed cognitive neuroscience and psychology by allowing non-invasive mapping of task-related activity and resting-state networks.

Limitations

BOLD is an indirect, slow proxy for neural activity, with spatial resolution of millimetres and temporal resolution of seconds. Results are sensitive to statistical methods, motion, and physiological noise, and the interpretation of activations — particularly reverse inference about mental states — is a subject of ongoing methodological debate.
