Microtubule organizing center

The microtubule organizing center (MTOC) is a structure found in eukaryotic cells from which microtubules emerge. MTOCs have two main functions: organizing the microtubule arrays that form flagella and cilia, and organizing the mitotic and meiotic spindle apparatus that separates chromosomes during cell division. Microtubules are nucleated at the MTOC by the γ-tubulin ring complex, which templates the growth of new microtubules with their minus ends anchored at the center.

Forms across organisms

In animal cells the principal MTOC is the centrosome, composed of a pair of centrioles surrounded by pericentriolar material. Basal bodies — essentially centrioles docked at the cell membrane — organize the microtubules of cilia and flagella and act as MTOCs in that context. Fungi lack centrosomes and instead embed spindle pole bodies in the nuclear envelope. Flowering plants have neither centrioles nor a single discrete MTOC; nucleation is distributed at the nuclear surface and along existing microtubules, yet they still assemble functional spindles.

Cell cycle and division

The centrosome duplicates once per cell cycle, during S phase, so that two MTOCs are available to form the poles of the mitotic spindle. Errors in this duplication produce extra centrosomes, a hallmark of many cancer cells, which can lead to abnormal chromosome segregation. During interphase the centrosome typically sits near the nucleus and organizes a radial array of microtubules used in intracellular transport and cell polarity.

Non-centrosomal arrays

Many differentiated cells relocate their MTOCs: epithelial cells anchor microtubules at apical sites, neurons nucleate them from the Golgi and dendritic sites, and muscle cells use the nuclear envelope. These non-centrosomal arrangements show that microtubule nucleation by γ-tubulin complexes can be targeted to many locations besides the centrosome.
