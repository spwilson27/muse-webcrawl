Fish anatomy is the study of the form or morphology of fish. It can be contrasted with fish physiology, which is the study of how the component parts of fish function together in the living fish. In practice, fish anatomy and fish physiology complement each other: anatomy deals with structure, physiology with the function of those structures.


Anatomy of a typical bony fish

Body plan

The body of a fish is divided into a head, trunk and tail, although the divisions are not always externally visible. Most fish are fusiform — streamlined for movement through water — though many are flattened (flatfish, rays), elongated (eels) or laterally compressed. The skeleton may be bony (Osteichthyes) or cartilaginous (Chondrichthyes, such as sharks and rays).

Fins and locomotion

Fins are the most distinctive features of a fish. Paired pectoral and pelvic fins correspond broadly to the limbs of tetrapods, while unpaired dorsal, anal and caudal (tail) fins stabilise and propel. Swimming is powered mainly by lateral undulation of the body and tail fin, driven by bands of muscle called myomeres that give fish flesh its characteristic flaky texture.

Respiration and circulation

Fish extract oxygen from water using gills: feathery filaments rich in blood vessels arranged for countercurrent exchange. In bony fish the gills are protected by a bony flap, the operculum. The heart has two chambers in series and pumps blood in a single circuit, first to the gills and then to the body.

Senses and other systems

The lateral line is a system of sense organs that detects movement and vibration in the surrounding water. Many fish also possess a swim bladder, a gas-filled organ that controls buoyancy, and some groups have specialised organs such as the electric organs of electric eels and rays. The skin is typically covered with overlapping scales and secretes mucus that reduces drag and protects against parasites.
