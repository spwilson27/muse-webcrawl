Fuse (electrical)

In electronics and electrical engineering, a fuse is an electrical safety device that operates to provide overcurrent protection of an electrical circuit. Its essential component is a metal wire or strip that melts when too much current flows through it, thereby stopping or interrupting the current. It is a sacrificial device; once a fuse has operated it is an open circuit, and must be replaced or rewired, depending on its type.

Fuses have been used as essential safety devices from the early days of electrical engineering. Today there are thousands of different fuse designs which have specific current and voltage ratings, breaking capacity, and response times, depending on the application. The time and current operating characteristics of fuses are chosen to provide adequate protection without needless interruption. Wiring regulations usually define a maximum fuse current rating for particular circuits. A fuse can be used to mitigate short circuits, overloading, mismatched loads, or device failure.

History

Louis Clément François Breguet recommended the use of reduced-section conductors to protect telegraph circuits against lightning strikes in 1847. Thomas Edison patented a fuse as part of his electric distribution system in 1890.

Construction and operation

A fuse consists of a metal strip or wire fuse element, of small cross-section compared to the circuit conductors, mounted between a pair of electrical terminals, and (usually) surrounded by a non-combustible housing. The fuse is arranged in series to carry all the charge passing through the protected circuit. The resistance of the element generates heat due to the current flow. The size and construction of the element is (empirically) determined so that the heat produced for a normal current does not cause the element to attain a high temperature. If too high a current flows, the element rises to a higher temperature and either directly melts, or else melts a soldered joint within the fuse, opening the circuit.

Characteristics

Key ratings include rated current, rated voltage, breaking capacity (the maximum fault current the fuse can safely interrupt), and speed — described by time-current curves and categories such as fast-acting (F), medium, and time-delay (slow-blow, T).

Types

Common types include cartridge fuses, plug fuses, automotive blade fuses, resettable fuses (polyfuses), and high rupturing capacity (HRC) fuses used in industrial power systems. Unlike a circuit breaker, a fuse must be replaced after it operates.
