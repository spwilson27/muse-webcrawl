AMOLED (active-matrix organic light-emitting diode) is a type of OLED display device technology. It adds a layer of thin-film transistors (TFTs) arranged in a matrix on a substrate behind the organic light-emitting material, so that each pixel (or subpixel) can be activated directly and individually. AMOLED displays are used in most flagship smartphones and many wearables and televisions.

How it works

Each pixel's TFT backplane stores charge in a capacitor and drives a controlled current through the OLED during each frame, giving precise brightness control. Because pixels emit their own light and can switch completely off, AMOLED screens achieve true blacks, effectively infinite contrast ratios, wide viewing angles and fast response times. Power consumption depends on image content: dark images use much less power than on an LCD, while full-white images can use more.

Variants and marketing terms

Samsung markets its panels as "Super AMOLED" (integrating the touch layer into the display) and "Dynamic AMOLED". PenTile subpixel layouts, which share subpixels between pixels, were used to extend panel life and reduce cost, at some expense to text sharpness. More recent panels use LTPO backplanes enabling variable refresh rates from 1 to 120 Hz or higher.

Advantages and drawbacks

Advantages include thinness, flexibility (on plastic substrates, enabling curved and foldable screens), contrast and colour volume. Drawbacks include susceptibility to burn-in and differential ageing of organic materials (blue emitters degrade fastest), historically higher cost, and lower peak outdoor brightness than the best LCDs — though modern flagship panels exceed 2,000 nits.
