The United States Navy Sea, Air, and Land (SEAL) Teams, commonly known as Navy SEALs, are the U.S. Navy's primary special operations force and a component of the Naval Special Warfare Command. Among the SEALs' main functions are conducting small-unit special operation missions in maritime, jungle, urban, arctic, mountainous, and desert environments. Established by President John F. Kennedy on 1 January 1962, the SEALs trace their heritage to the Naval Combat Demolition Units and Underwater Demolition Teams ("frogmen") of the Second World War.

Selection and training

SEAL candidates undergo one of the most demanding selection pipelines in the world. Basic Underwater Demolition/SEAL (BUD/S) training at Coronado lasts about six months and includes the infamous "Hell Week," five and a half days of near-continuous training on minimal sleep; roughly 75 percent of candidates drop out or are rolled back. Graduates proceed to parachute training and SEAL Qualification Training before earning the Special Warfare insignia, known as the "Budweiser" or SEAL Trident.

Operations

SEALs operated extensively in Vietnam, where they ran counter-guerrilla and reconnaissance missions, and in Grenada, Panama, the Persian Gulf, Somalia, the Balkans, Afghanistan and Iraq. SEAL Team Six — formally the Naval Special Warfare Development Group (DEVGRU) — carried out Operation Neptune Spear, the 2011 raid in Abbottabad, Pakistan, that killed Osama bin Laden. SEALs also rescued Captain Richard Phillips from Somali pirates in 2009.

Organization

SEAL teams are organized under Naval Special Warfare Groups on the East and West Coasts, supported by SEAL Delivery Vehicle teams and Special Boat Teams operating combatant craft. SEALs have been open to qualified candidates of all genders since 2015 in principle.
