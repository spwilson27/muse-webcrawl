Margaret of Anjou (23 March 1430 – 25 August 1482) was Queen of England by marriage to King Henry VI from 1445 to 1461 and again in 1470–1471. A French noblewoman, daughter of René of Anjou, she became one of the principal figures of the Wars of the Roses, leading the Lancastrian cause during her husband's bouts of mental illness.

Margaret of Anjou, Queen consort of Henry VI

Early life and marriage

Margaret was born in the Duchy of Lorraine, daughter of René, Duke of Anjou and titular King of Naples. Her marriage to Henry VI in April 1445 was arranged as part of the Truce of Tours with France; the secret terms, including the surrender of Maine to France, later proved deeply unpopular in England.

The Wars of the Roses

As Henry VI descended into mental incapacity in 1453, Margaret — whose son Edward was born that October — fiercely resisted the protectorate of Richard, Duke of York. Open war followed. Lancastrian victories at Wakefield (1460), where York was killed, and the Second Battle of St Albans (1461) could not prevent Edward IV's accession. Margaret campaigned in Scotland and France for support, briefly restored Henry VI in 1470–71, but her army was destroyed at the Battle of Tewkesbury on 4 May 1471, where her son was killed.

Later life

Imprisoned in the Tower of London, Margaret was ransomed by Louis XI of France in 1475 under the Treaty of Picquigny. She lived out her remaining years in relative poverty in France, dying at Dampierre-sur-Loire in 1482.

Legacy

Shakespeare portrayed her across four plays as a formidable, vengeful queen. Modern historians view her as an energetic defender of her son's inheritance against long odds. She is credited with founding Queens' College, Cambridge, in 1448.
