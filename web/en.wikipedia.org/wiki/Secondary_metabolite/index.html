Secondary metabolite

The common mould Penicillium produces penicillin, a famous microbial secondary metabolite.

Secondary metabolites, also called specialised metabolites, toxins, secondary products, or natural products, are organic compounds produced by any lifeform — bacteria, fungi, plants and animals — that are not directly involved in the normal growth, development, or reproduction of the organism. Unlike primary metabolites, which are essential to basic cellular function, the absence of secondary metabolites does not usually result in immediate death, but rather in long-term impairment of the organism's survivability, fecundity, or appearance, or perhaps in no change at all.

Functions

Specific secondary metabolites are often restricted to a narrow set of species within a phylogenetic group. They frequently play an important role in plant defence against herbivory and other interspecies interactions. Many serve as antibiotics, pigments, toxins, pheromones, enzyme inhibitors, or signalling agents. Because many show potent biological activities, secondary metabolites have been a principal source of drugs: examples include penicillin, morphine, quinine, paclitaxel (taxol), artemisinin, and the statins derived from fungal metabolites.

Major classes

  • Alkaloids — nitrogen-containing compounds such as nicotine, caffeine, morphine and atropine, typically derived from amino acids.
  • Terpenoids — built from isoprene units; include essential oils, carotenoids, rubber and many hormones.
  • Phenolics — including flavonoids, tannins and lignin precursors, important in defence and pigmentation.
  • Polyketides — assembled by polyketide synthases; include many antibiotics such as tetracyclines and erythromycin.
  • Nonribosomal peptides — peptide antibiotics such as penicillins and vancomycin, assembled by large enzyme complexes rather than ribosomes.

Production and applications

In microbes, secondary metabolite production often begins as growth slows (the idiophase), and the responsible genes are typically clustered together in the genome, facilitating discovery by genome mining. Humans use secondary metabolites as medicines, flavourings, pigments, pesticides and recreational drugs, and their biosynthetic pathways are targets of metabolic engineering for industrial production.
