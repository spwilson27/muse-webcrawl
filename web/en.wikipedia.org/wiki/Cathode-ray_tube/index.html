Cathode-ray tube

Components of a cathode-ray tube

A cathode-ray tube (CRT) is a vacuum tube containing one or more electron guns, which emit electron beams that are manipulated to display images on a fluorescent screen. The images may represent electrical waveforms (in an oscilloscope), pictures (in a television set or computer monitor), radar targets, or other data.

How it works

Inside the tube, a heated cathode emits electrons by thermionic emission. The electron gun focuses them into a narrow beam, which is accelerated toward a phosphor-coated screen by a high positive voltage at the anode, typically thousands of volts. The beam is steered — deflected — by electric fields between metal plates or by magnetic fields from coils around the tube's neck, so that it sweeps across the screen in a raster pattern. Where the beam strikes, the phosphor fluoresces and glows. Colour CRTs use three electron guns and a shadow mask or aperture grille so that beams land only on red, green, or blue phosphor dots.

History

The CRT descends from the Crookes tube and Karl Ferdinand Braun's 1897 "Braun tube". Vladimir Zworykin's kinescope and Philo Farnsworth's work made electronic television practical in the 1930s. CRTs dominated displays for most of the 20th century: television sets, computer monitors, oscilloscopes, and radar screens. From the 2000s they were rapidly displaced by LCD and other flat-panel displays, which are thinner, lighter, and more energy-efficient, although CRTs retain niches among enthusiasts for their motion clarity and handling of low-resolution video game signals.

Health and disposal

CRT glass contains lead — up to several kilograms in large colour tubes — to shield users from the soft X-rays generated inside, and the funnel glass and phosphors pose hazards in disposal, making CRTs a significant stream of electronic waste.
