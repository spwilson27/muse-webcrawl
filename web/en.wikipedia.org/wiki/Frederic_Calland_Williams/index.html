DiedAugust 11, 1977 (aged 66), Manchester, England

Sir Frederic Calland Williams (26 June 1911 – 11 August 1977), known as F. C. Williams, was an English electrical engineer who invented the Williams tube — the first practical random-access memory for electronic computers — and led the team that built the Manchester Baby, the first stored-program computer to run, in 1948.

Early life and radar work

Williams was born in Romiley, Cheshire, and studied at the University of Manchester and Magdalen College, Oxford, where he gained his doctorate. During the Second World War he worked at the Telecommunications Research Establishment (TRE) on radar, becoming renowned for ingenious electronics and for work on IFF (identification friend or foe) systems.

The Williams tube and the Manchester computers

In 1946 Williams took the chair of electrotechnics at Manchester. There, with Tom Kilburn, he developed a method of storing binary digits as charged dots on the screen of a cathode-ray tube — the Williams (or Williams–Kilburn) tube. In June 1948 this memory enabled the Manchester Baby (Small-Scale Experimental Machine) to execute the first electronically stored program. The work led directly to the Manchester Mark 1 and the Ferranti Mark 1, among the first commercial computers.

Later career and honors

Williams later researched electrical machines and control systems, founding a distinguished electrical engineering department at Manchester. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1950 and was knighted in 1976.
