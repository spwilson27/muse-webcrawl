Lexicographic order

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the lexicographic or lexicographical order (also known as lexical order or dictionary order) is a generalization of the alphabetical order of dictionaries to sequences of ordered elements. Given two sequences of the same or different lengths, the first is smaller in lexicographic order if, at the first position where they differ, its element there is smaller; if one sequence is an exact prefix of the other, the shorter sequence comes first.

Definition

Formally, for sequences a = (a1, a2, …, an) and b = (b1, b2, …, bm) of elements drawn from a totally ordered set, a precedes b lexicographically if either a is a prefix of b and n < m, or there is a first index k at which the sequences differ and ak < bk. Under this rule, for example, "cat" precedes "cattle" (a prefix case) and precedes "dog" (difference at the first letter).

Uses

Lexicographic order is ubiquitous. It defines the ordering of words in dictionaries and indexes, the default sort order of strings in computing (though conventions about case, accents, and numerals vary by locale and collation), and the comparison of tuples and vectors in mathematics. In combinatorics it provides a canonical way to enumerate permutations and combinations; in order theory, the lexicographic product of ordered sets is a standard construction; in decision theory it underlies lexicographic preferences, in which criteria are ranked by priority and only the most important differing criterion matters. Multi-criteria problems in everyday life — for instance, comparing dates as (year, month, day) — are typically resolved lexicographically.
