Black Belt (region)

The Black Belt is a region in the Southern United States, principally Alabama and Mississippi, named originally for its dark, fertile prairie soil and later also associated with its large African American population. The crescent of rich black topsoil, derived from chalk of the Selma Group, made the area a centre of cotton plantation agriculture worked by enslaved people before the Civil War.

Geography and history

The Alabama Black Belt runs in an arc across the centre of the state, roughly 20–25 miles wide and some 300 miles long in the broader physiographic sense. Its rich soil attracted plantation owners in the 1820s and 1830s, and enslaved labourers soon outnumbered whites in many counties. After emancipation, sharecropping and tenant farming kept most Black residents in poverty and tied to the land.

Civil rights

Black Belt counties were central to the civil rights movement: the Montgomery bus boycott, the Selma to Montgomery marches of 1965, and voter-registration campaigns in counties such as Lowndes — "Bloody Lowndes" — where the Lowndes County Freedom Organization adopted a black panther emblem later taken up by the Black Panther Party.

Present day

The region remains largely rural, with persistent poverty, population decline, and infrastructure problems alongside rich cultural heritage, catfish farming, and timber. Booker T. Washington used "Black Belt" in a dual sense in Up from Slavery (1901), noting the term also described the concentration of Black population.
