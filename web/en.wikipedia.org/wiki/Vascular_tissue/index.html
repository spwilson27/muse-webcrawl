Vascular tissue


Vascular bundles in a stem cross-section

Vascular tissue is a complex conducting tissue, formed of more than one cell type, found in vascular plants. The primary components of vascular tissue are the xylem and the phloem. These two tissues internally transport fluid and nutrients through the plant; their evolution allowed plants to grow tall, colonize dry land and develop the size and diversity of trees. Vascular tissue is arranged into long, discrete strands called vascular bundles.

Xylem

The xylem transports water and dissolved minerals from the roots upward to the stems and leaves, driven mainly by transpiration pull and cohesion–tension. It is composed principally of dead, hollow conducting elements—tracheids in most vascular plants and vessel elements in flowering plants—together with fibres and living parenchyma. Because its cells are heavily lignified, xylem also provides most of the structural support of woody plants; in trees, accumulated secondary xylem forms wood.

Phloem

The phloem translocates the products of photosynthesis—chiefly sucrose, along with amino acids and hormones—from sources (such as mature leaves) to sinks (growing tissues, roots and fruits). Its conducting elements, the sieve-tube members in flowering plants, remain alive at maturity but lack nuclei and depend on neighbouring companion cells. Movement through the phloem is explained by the pressure-flow (Münch) hypothesis.

Arrangement

In roots the xylem and phloem form a central stele; in stems they are grouped in vascular bundles scattered through ground tissue in monocots or arranged in a ring in dicots, where a lateral meristem, the vascular cambium, produces secondary xylem and phloem and drives growth in girth.
