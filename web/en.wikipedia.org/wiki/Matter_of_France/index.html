The Matter of France, also known as the Carolingian cycle, is a body of literature and legendary material associated with the history of France, in particular involving Charlemagne and his associates. Along with the Matter of Britain (concerning King Arthur) and the Matter of Rome (retelling stories from classical antiquity), it was one of the three great literary cycles — called "the three matters" — repeatedly identified in medieval literature.

The classification comes from the 12th-century French poet Jean Bodel, who in his Chanson des Saisnes wrote that there are three matters no man should be without: those of France, of Britain, and of Rome.

Content

The Matter of France is centred on Charlemagne ("Charles the Great") and his twelve peers, or paladins, especially Roland (Orlando in Italian versions), Oliver, and Archbishop Turpin. Its best-known work is the Song of Roland (Chanson de Roland), the oldest surviving major work of French literature, which dramatises Roland's death at the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778.

The poems of the cycle, largely composed between the 11th and 14th centuries as chansons de geste (songs of deeds), are conventionally grouped into three sub-cycles: the Cycle of the King (Geste du Roi), centred on Charlemagne himself; the Cycle of William of Orange (Geste de Guillaume d'Orange); and the Cycle of Doon de Mayence (Geste de Doon de Mayence), concerning rebellious vassals.

Themes

Typical themes include the conflict between Christendom and the Saracens, feudal loyalty and betrayal (epitomised by the traitor Ganelon), chivalric heroism, and the defence of France and the faith. The legends later fed into Italian Renaissance epics such as Boiardo's Orlando Innamorato and Ariosto's Orlando Furioso.
