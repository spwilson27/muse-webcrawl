Sharia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sharia is the body of religious law derived from the Quran and the teachings and practices of the Islamic prophet Muhammad (the Sunna, recorded in Hadith), which forms part of the Islamic tradition. Classical Islamic jurisprudence (Fiqh) developed methods — analogy (qiyas), consensus (ijma) and interpretation (ijtihad) — and schools of law (madhhab: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali among Sunnis; Ja'fari among Shia) to apply Sharia to worship, family relations, commerce, crime and governance.

Sharia functions today in many registers: the personal and devotional law observed by Muslims everywhere; the family and personal-status law of many Muslim-majority states; a source or the source of legislation under several constitutions (with formulations varying widely — a “principal source” in Egypt's, “repugnant to Islam” clauses elsewhere); and, in a few states (Saudi Arabia, Iran, Afghanistan under the Taliban, and northern Nigerian states since 1999–2000, among others), criminal law including hudud penalties — whose application is contested among Muslims themselves on jurisprudential and human-rights grounds. Public debate in non-Muslim countries about “Sharia law” often conflates these registers; academic treatments distinguish Sharia (the divine ideal) from fiqh (human understanding) and from the positive law of particular states.
