An omnibus spending bill is a type of bill in the United States that packages many of the smaller regular appropriations bills into one larger single bill that can be passed with only one vote in each house of Congress.

Background

Congress is supposed to pass twelve regular appropriations bills each year to fund the federal government. When it cannot complete them individually by the start of the fiscal year on 1 October — a common occurrence since the 1970s — the unfinished bills are combined into an omnibus act, often negotiated by party leadership and the White House and passed under time pressure to avert a government shutdown.

Criticism and defence

Critics note that omnibus bills can run to thousands of pages, are often unveiled shortly before votes, and can carry unrelated policy riders and earmarks with little scrutiny. Defenders respond that they are a practical means of completing the appropriations process amid partisan gridlock and provide funding certainty to agencies.

Related measures

Smaller combinations are sometimes called "minibuses". A continuing resolution, by contrast, maintains prior-year funding levels temporarily without new appropriations detail.
