George Circle

The George Circle (German: George-Kreis) was an influential German literary group centred on the poet Stefan George.

The George Circle (German: George-Kreis) was a group of German writers, scholars, and artists who gathered around the charismatic poet Stefan George (1868–1933) from the 1890s until George's death. The circle was one of the most important literary movements in the German-speaking world before the First World War and continued to exert influence between the wars.

History

The circle formed around George's journal Blätter für die Kunst ("Leaves for Art"), founded in 1892, which published poetry in an austere, anti-naturalist style and rejected commercial publishing. George demanded absolute devotion from his followers, who addressed him as "der Meister" (the Master) and treated his circle as an almost religious order devoted to beauty, form, and the renewal of German culture.

Members

Prominent members included the poets and scholars Friedrich Gundolf, Ernst Bertram, Karl Wolfskehl, Ludwig Klages, and the brothers Claus and Berthold von Stauffenberg. Claus von Stauffenberg, later famous for the 20 July 1944 plot against Adolf Hitler, had been introduced to George as a young man and remained deeply influenced by the circle's ideal of a "Secret Germany" (geheimes Deutschland).

Ideas and influence

The circle propounded an elitist, aesthetic vision of a spiritually renewed Germany, drawing on Nietzsche, classical antiquity, and medieval models such as the emperor Frederick II and the boy-hero Maximin, whom George venerated. Its ideas influenced German conservative thought and the Conservative Revolution of the Weimar era, though George himself rejected the Nazi regime and died in exile in Switzerland in 1933.

Dissolution

After George's death in Locarno in December 1933, the circle fragmented. Some members emigrated, others accommodated themselves to the Third Reich, and several — notably the Stauffenberg brothers — became central figures in the German resistance.
