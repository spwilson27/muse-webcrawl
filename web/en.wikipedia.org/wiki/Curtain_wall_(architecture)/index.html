Curtain wall (architecture)

Curtain wall
FunctionNon-structural outer building envelope
Typical materialsAluminium framing, glass, stone or metal panels

A curtain wall is an outer covering of a building in which the exterior walls are non-structural, used only to keep out the weather and keep occupants in. Because the curtain wall carries no structural load from the building (beyond its own weight and wind loads, which it transfers to the frame), it can be made of lightweight materials, most commonly aluminium framing with glass infill panels.

History

The development of steel frame construction in the late nineteenth century freed façades from load-bearing, and early curtain walls appeared in buildings such as Oriel Chambers, Liverpool (1864). Glass curtain walls became a hallmark of mid-twentieth-century modernism — exemplified by the United Nations Secretariat Building (1950) and Lever House (1952) in New York — and are now standard for high-rise construction.

Systems

Curtain walls are built as stick systems (assembled on site), unitised systems (factory-made panels craned into place), or structural glazing, in which glass is bonded to the frame with silicone. Design must manage water infiltration, air leakage, thermal expansion, and wind deflection.
