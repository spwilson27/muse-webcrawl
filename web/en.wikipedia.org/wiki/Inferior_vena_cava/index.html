Inferior vena cava

Inferior vena cava
Source: Common iliac veins, lumbar veins, renal veins, hepatic veins
Drains to: Right atrium
Artery: Abdominal aorta

The inferior vena cava (IVC) is a large vein that carries deoxygenated blood from the lower and middle body into the right atrium of the heart. It is formed by the joining of the right and left common iliac veins, usually at the level of the fifth lumbar vertebra.

Structure

The IVC ascends on the right side of the vertebral column, passing through the caval opening of the diaphragm at the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra. Its tributaries include the lumbar veins, right gonadal vein, renal veins, right suprarenal vein, inferior phrenic veins, and hepatic veins. The left gonadal and left suprarenal veins usually drain into the left renal vein instead.

Development

The IVC develops from a complex series of embryonic veins—the posterior cardinal, subcardinal, and supracardinal veins—between the sixth and eighth weeks of gestation. Anomalies such as a left-sided or duplicated IVC, or an absent hepatic segment with azygos continuation, arise from variations in this process.

Clinical significance

Compression of the IVC can occur in pregnancy and in inferior vena cava syndrome. Blood clots in the IVC or its tributaries may be treated with anticoagulation or, in selected patients, an IVC filter to prevent pulmonary embolism. Ultrasound measurement of IVC diameter and collapsibility is used to estimate fluid status.
