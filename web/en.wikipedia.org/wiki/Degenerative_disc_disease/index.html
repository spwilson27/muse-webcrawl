Degenerative disc disease

Degenerative disc disease
SpecialtyOrthopedics, neurosurgery, rheumatology
AffectedIntervertebral discs of the spine
SymptomsChronic back or neck pain, sometimes radiating pain, numbness
Risk factorsAgeing, genetics, smoking, obesity, heavy loading, injury

Degenerative disc disease (DDD) is a medical condition typically brought on by the normal ageing process, in which intervertebral discs of the spine lose hydration, height, and elasticity, producing damage and often chronic pain. Despite its name it is not strictly a disease, nor does it invariably worsen; it is a common finding on imaging, present in most older adults, many of whom have no symptoms.

Mechanism

A healthy disc has a soft, water-rich nucleus pulposus contained by the fibrous annulus fibrosus. With age and genetic predisposition, the nucleus dehydrates and the annulus develops tears; disc height falls, load shifts to the facet joints, and bone spurs (osteophytes) may form. Degeneration can lead to disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and instability, and pain may arise from the disc itself, compressed nerve roots, or neighbouring structures.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines clinical history and examination with imaging, most sensitively MRI, which shows loss of disc signal and height, Modic endplate changes, and herniations. Because imaging findings correlate imperfectly with pain, asymptomatic degeneration is frequently an incidental finding.

Treatment

  • Conservative — exercise and physical therapy, weight management, smoking cessation, analgesics and anti-inflammatories; most episodes improve without surgery.
  • Interventional — epidural steroid injections and radiofrequency procedures for selected patients.
  • Surgical — discectomy for herniation with radiculopathy, and fusion or artificial disc replacement for carefully selected cases of disabling discogenic pain.
