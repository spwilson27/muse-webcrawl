Targeted temperature management

Targeted temperature management (TTM), previously known as therapeutic hypothermia, is an active treatment that tries to achieve and maintain a specific body temperature in a person for a specific duration of time in an effort to improve health outcomes. It is used most notably after cardiac arrest, where cooling the body — or at minimum rigorously preventing fever — is intended to reduce brain injury caused by reperfusion after circulation returns.

Mechanism

Lowering body temperature reduces the brain's metabolic rate and the release of excitotoxic neurotransmitters, free radicals and inflammatory mediators that drive secondary injury after an ischaemic event. Each degree of cooling lowers cerebral oxygen consumption by roughly 6–7%.

Evidence

Landmark trials in 2002 showed improved neurological outcomes when comatose survivors of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest were cooled to 32–34 °C. A large 2013 trial found 33 °C no better than actively maintaining 36 °C, shifting emphasis toward strict fever avoidance; the 2021 TTM2 trial found hypothermia at 33 °C no better than normothermia with early fever treatment. In newborn infants with hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, cooling to about 33.5 °C for 72 hours is established to reduce death and disability.

Technique and risks

Cooling is achieved with servo-controlled surface pads or an intravascular catheter, with sedation and shivering control; rewarming is done slowly. Complications include arrhythmias, electrolyte shifts, infection and coagulopathy.
