Perspiration, also known as sweating, is the production of fluids secreted by the sweat glands in the skin of mammals. The fluid, sweat, consists primarily of water, with small amounts of minerals, lactic acid and urea. In humans, perspiration is primarily a means of thermoregulation: evaporation of sweat from the skin surface cools the body, and adults can secrete several litres per hour during exertion in the heat.

Glands and control

Humans have two kinds of sweat glands. Eccrine glands, distributed over most of the body — two to four million in total — produce clear, hypotonic sweat directly onto the skin and are the main agents of cooling. Apocrine glands, found in the armpits and groin, open into hair follicles, become active at puberty, and produce a thicker secretion whose bacterial decomposition causes body odour. Sweating is controlled by the hypothalamus through the sympathetic nervous system, using acetylcholine as an unusual sympathetic neurotransmitter.

Functions and triggers

Besides heat, sweating is triggered by exercise, fever resolution, spicy food (gustatory sweating), anxiety and emotional stress — notably on the palms, soles and forehead. Excessive sweating is termed hyperhidrosis; abnormally reduced sweating, hypohidrosis or anhidrosis, which impairs heat tolerance.

Composition and health

Sweat is mostly water with sodium chloride as the principal solute, giving it a salty taste. Prolonged heavy sweating can cause dehydration and electrolyte loss, requiring fluid and salt replacement. Night sweats can be a symptom of infections or malignancy, and profuse sweating (diaphoresis) accompanies conditions from menopause to myocardial infarction.
