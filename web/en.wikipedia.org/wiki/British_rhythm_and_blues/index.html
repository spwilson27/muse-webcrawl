British rhythm and blues

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

British rhythm and blues (or British R&B) was a musical movement in the United Kingdom in the late 1950s and 1960s in which British musicians — largely inspired by imported American blues, jump blues and rhythm and blues records — formed bands that reinterpreted the music with the energy of rock and roll. The movement produced the Rolling Stones, the Yardbirds, the Animals, Manfred Mann, the Spencer Davis Group and many others, provided the springboard for the "British Invasion" of the United States, and fed directly into the development of blues rock, hard rock and heavy metal.

Origins

Post-war Britain had few outlets for American R&B: BBC radio played little of it, and records were expensive imports sought out by sailors, GIs and enthusiasts in ports such as London and Liverpool. Skiffle star Lonnie Donegan's repertoire introduced many future musicians to Lead Belly and other blues artists. The key pioneers were Alexis Korner and harmonica player Cyril Davies, whose London club nights and band Blues Incorporated (formed 1961) became a finishing school for young players including Charlie Watts, Jack Bruce and Ginger Baker. The Ealing Club and later the Marquee and the Flamingo in Soho formed the circuit's centre, while Chris Barber's trad jazz band had earlier brought American bluesmen such as Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf to British audiences on tour.

The R&B boom

From 1962, R&B became the fashionable club music of London's art-school and mod scenes. The Rolling Stones, formed under the initial guidance of Brian Jones and named after a Muddy Waters song, built a residency at the Crawdaddy Club in Richmond and reached the charts with covers of Chuck Berry and Willie Dixon material. The Yardbirds succeeded them at the Crawdaddy and launched the careers of Eric Clapton, Jeff Beck and Jimmy Page. Newcastle's Animals had a transatlantic number one with their electrified arrangement of "The House of the Rising Sun" (1964). Manfred Mann, the Spencer Davis Group (with the teenage Steve Winwood), the Pretty Things, Them (from Belfast, with Van Morrison) and the Graham Bond Organisation all carried the music in harder, organ-driven directions.

Influence and aftermath

British R&B groups reintroduced American blues and R&B to white American audiences during the British Invasion, helping to revive the careers of the original artists. By 1966–67 most of the leading acts had moved towards pop, psychedelia or original songwriting, while a second wave — John Mayall's Bluesbreakers, Fleetwood Mac, Savoy Brown and later Led Zeppelin — developed the louder blues rock style. The movement's do-it-yourself ethic, repertoire and club circuit profoundly shaped British popular music.
