Contractible space

In mathematics, a contractible space is a topological space that is homotopy equivalent to a point. Intuitively, a contractible space can be continuously shrunk to a point within itself: the identity map on the space is null-homotopic, i.e. homotopic to a constant map. All homotopy groups of a contractible space are trivial.

FieldAlgebraic topology
DefinitionIdentity map is null-homotopic
ExamplesEuclidean space, convex sets, cones
Non-examplesSpheres, tori

Properties and examples

Any convex subset of Euclidean space is contractible via a straight-line homotopy, as is any star-shaped set; the cone on any space is contractible. A space is contractible if and only if it is a retract of its cone in a strong sense — more precisely, contractibility is equivalent to the space being a deformation retract of some larger space built from it, and to the existence of a strong deformation retraction onto a point within itself.

Contractible does not imply that the space is homeomorphic to Euclidean space: there exist contractible 3-manifolds, such as the Whitehead manifold, that are not homeomorphic to ℝ³. In dimension two the Riemann mapping context is simpler, and finite CW complexes that are contractible need not collapse to a point without expanding first — a distinction captured by the notion of collapsibility and relevant to the Andrews–Curtis conjecture.
