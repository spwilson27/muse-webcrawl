Hasdai ibn Shaprut (c. 915 – c. 970; Hebrew: חסדאי אבן שפרוט) was a Jewish physician, diplomat, and statesman who served at the court of the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Rahman III and his son al-Hakam II in Córdoba, in Islamic Spain (Al-Andalus). He is considered the founding figure of the golden age of Jewish culture in Spain.

Career at court

Born in Jaén to a wealthy family, Hasdai studied medicine, Hebrew, Arabic, and Latin, and gained fame for allegedly rediscovering the recipe for theriac, a famed universal antidote. As the caliph's court physician and unofficial foreign minister, he conducted diplomacy with the Byzantine Empire, the Holy Roman Empire (receiving the embassy of John of Gorze), the Christian kingdoms of León and Navarre — where he famously travelled to treat the deposed king Sancho I — and other powers. Although a Jew could not formally hold the title of vizier, he exercised equivalent functions, including oversight of customs and foreign affairs.

Patronage of Jewish culture

Hasdai used his wealth and influence to patronise Hebrew poets and grammarians, most notably Menachem ben Saruq and Dunash ben Labrat, whose rivalry helped shape medieval Hebrew philology. He supported Jewish scholars and communities throughout the Mediterranean and worked to make Spanish Jewry intellectually independent of the Babylonian academies.

Correspondence with the Khazars

Hasdai is famous for his letter to Joseph, king of the Khazars, the Turkic people whose ruling class had converted to Judaism. Having heard reports of an independent Jewish kingdom, Hasdai sent a letter (c. 954) asking about the Khazar state; Joseph's detailed reply — part of the Khazar Correspondence — is a key source for the history of Khazaria, though its transmission history has been debated.
