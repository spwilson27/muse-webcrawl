Fox spirit

The fox spirit (Chinese: 狐仙 / 狐狸精, húxiān / húlijīng) is a mythical fox entity originating from Chinese mythology that is a common motif in East Asian folklore. In Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese traditions, foxes are depicted as familiar spirits possessing great magical powers, able to shape-shift — often into beautiful women — and growing in power and tails with age, culminating in the nine-tailed fox.

Traditions

In Chinese mythology, fox spirits (huli jing) range from benevolent omens — the nine-tailed fox was an auspicious symbol in early texts and accompanied Yu the Great — to dangerous seductresses who drain the vitality of their lovers, as in many tales of the Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio (Liaozhai Zhiyi) by Pu Songling. In Japanese folklore the kitsune are intelligent foxes serving the god Inari as messengers, divided into benevolent zenko and mischievous yako. In Korean folklore the kumiho (nine-tailed fox) is predominantly malevolent, said to eat the livers or hearts of men; Vietnamese tradition has the hồ ly tinh, associated with the legendary Hồ dynasty tales.

Characteristics

Fox spirits gain power over centuries of life and cultivation; at a thousand years a fox may become a celestial fox. They are typically vulnerable to dogs, mirrors that reveal their true form, and exorcists, and a transformed fox may betray itself by a visible tail. Stories of fox wives and fox scholars were a staple of Chinese literature from the Tang dynasty onward.
