The National Pact (Arabic: الميثاق الوطني, al-Mīthāq al-Waṭanī) is an unwritten agreement that laid the foundations of Lebanon as a multiconfessional state. Concluded in 1943 between the Maronite Christian president Bechara El Khoury and the Sunni Muslim prime minister Riad Al Solh, it shaped Lebanon's political system for decades.

The National Pact underpinned Lebanon's confessional power-sharing system.

Terms

Under the pact, Christians renounced French protection and accepted Lebanon as an Arab country, while Muslims renounced union with Syria or any merger into a larger Arab state. Public offices were distributed confessionally: the president would always be a Maronite Catholic, the prime minister a Sunni Muslim, and the speaker of parliament a Shia Muslim. Seats in parliament were allocated on a 6:5 ratio of Christians to Muslims, based on the 1932 census.

Legacy

The pact's confessional formula contributed to both Lebanon's pluralist character and its chronic sectarian tensions, figuring in the background of the 1958 crisis and the Lebanese Civil War (1975–1990). The 1989 Taif Agreement modified the arrangement, equalising Christian and Muslim representation in parliament, but confessional power-sharing remains central to Lebanese politics.
