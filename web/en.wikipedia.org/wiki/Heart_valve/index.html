Heart valve

A heart valve is a one-way valve that allows blood to flow in one direction through the chambers of the heart. The mammalian heart has four valves: the tricuspid and pulmonary valves on the right side, and the mitral and aortic valves on the left. The atrioventricular valves (tricuspid and mitral) are anchored by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles, while the semilunar valves (pulmonary and aortic) each have three pocket-like cusps.

Function

Valves open and close passively in response to pressure differences across them. Closure of the atrioventricular valves at the start of systole produces the first heart sound ("lub"), and closure of the semilunar valves at the end of systole the second ("dub"). The mitral valve is the only one with two cusps; the others have three.

Disease

Valvular heart disease takes two main forms: stenosis, in which a narrowed valve obstructs forward flow, and regurgitation (insufficiency), in which a leaking valve allows backflow. Causes include age-related calcific degeneration (especially of the aortic valve), rheumatic fever (historically the dominant cause of mitral stenosis), congenital malformations such as a bicuspid aortic valve, mitral valve prolapse, and infective endocarditis. Diagnosis relies on auscultation of murmurs and echocardiography. Severe disease is treated by surgical repair or replacement with mechanical or bioprosthetic valves, or increasingly by transcatheter procedures such as TAVR (transcatheter aortic valve replacement).

Artificial valves

Mechanical prostheses (e.g. tilting-disc and bileaflet designs) are durable but require lifelong anticoagulation; tissue valves (porcine or bovine pericardial) avoid anticoagulants but degenerate over 10–20 years. The choice balances patient age, lifestyle, and surgical risk.
