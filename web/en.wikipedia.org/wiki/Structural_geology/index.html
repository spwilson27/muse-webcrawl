Structural geology is the study of the three-dimensional distribution of rock units with respect to their deformational histories. Its primary goal is to use measurements of present-day rock geometries — such as the strike and dip of strata — to uncover information about the history of deformation (strain) in the rocks, and ultimately to understand the stress field that produced the observed deformation.

Structures studied

When rocks are subjected to stress beyond their strength, they deform by brittle fracture, producing faults and joints, or by ductile flow, producing folds (anticlines and synclines) and foliations such as cleavage and schistosity. Structural geologists classify faults as normal, reverse, or strike-slip according to the relative motion of the blocks and the stress regime that formed them.

Methods

The discipline combines field mapping, cross-section construction, stereographic projection of orientation data, seismic imaging, and laboratory rock-deformation experiments. Modern work adds GPS measurements of present-day crustal motion and numerical modelling of deformation.

Applications

Understanding folds and faults guides the search for oil and gas traps, groundwater, and mineral deposits; assessing active faults is essential to earthquake hazard analysis, dam and tunnel engineering, and safe siting of infrastructure.
