Interbank rate

Interbank rates fluctuate with monetary policy and market liquidity.

The interbank rate is the rate of interest charged on short-term loans between banks. Banks borrow and lend money in the interbank lending market in order to manage liquidity and satisfy regulatory requirements such as reserve requirements. The interest rate charged depends on availability of funds, prevailing market rates, and the specific terms of the contract.

Benchmark rates

Famous interbank offered rates include the former London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR), the Euro Interbank Offered Rate (EURIBOR), and overnight benchmarks such as the federal funds rate in the United States, €STR in the euro area, and SONIA in the United Kingdom. Following the LIBOR manipulation scandal and the decline of unsecured interbank term lending, regulators transitioned most markets to transaction-based "risk-free rates" such as SOFR, and LIBOR publication ceased in 2023.

Role in the economy

Interbank rates anchor the pricing of loans, mortgages, and derivatives worth hundreds of trillions of dollars. Central banks influence them through policy rates and open-market operations. Spikes in interbank rates — as in the 2007–2008 financial crisis, when trust between banks collapsed — signal stress in the financial system and can precede credit crunches.

Term structure

Rates are quoted for maturities from overnight to one year; longer maturities generally carry higher rates reflecting greater credit and liquidity risk.
