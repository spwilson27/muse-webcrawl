Emotivism

On the emotivist view, "Stealing is wrong" expresses disapproval rather than stating a fact.

Emotivism is a meta-ethical theory claiming that ethical sentences do not express propositions at all, but instead express emotional attitudes. According to emotivism, saying "Murder is wrong" is not to state a fact about murder but to express one's disapproval of it — roughly equivalent to saying "Murder — boo!" This crude version is therefore sometimes called the "boo–hurrah" theory. Emotivism is a form of moral non-cognitivism and of expressivism.

History

Emotivism developed in the early twentieth century under the influence of logical positivism and its verification principle, which held that statements that are neither analytic nor empirically verifiable are cognitively meaningless. A. J. Ayer presented a systematic emotivism in Language, Truth and Logic (1936), arguing that ethical terms add nothing to the factual content of a sentence but express and arouse feeling. Charles Stevenson developed a more sophisticated version in Ethics and Language (1944), distinguishing disagreement in belief from disagreement in attitude and analysing moral language's "emotive meaning" and persuasive function. Earlier anticipations appear in David Hume's treatment of moral distinctions as founded on sentiment and in I. A. Richards and C. K. Ogden's distinction between referential and emotive uses of language.

Influence and criticism

Emotivism shaped mid-century ethics but faced serious objections. The Frege–Geach problem asks how moral sentences can function in logical arguments — for instance in conditionals ("If stealing is wrong, then…") — if they merely express attitudes and have no truth value. Critics also argue that emotivism cannot adequately explain moral reasoning, the appearance of moral truth, or why some emotional responses seem more fitting than others. Later expressivists, such as Allan Gibbard and Simon Blackburn (with "quasi-realism"), attempted to answer these problems while preserving the non-cognitivist core.
