Thymine

Thymine
IUPAC name: 5-methylpyrimidine-2,4(1H,3H)-dione
Formula: C5H6N2O2
Molar mass: 126.115 g/mol
Symbol: T, Thy
Class: Pyrimidine nucleobase
Base pair: Adenine (2 hydrogen bonds)

Thymine is one of the four nucleobases in the nucleic acid of DNA, represented by the letter T, alongside adenine, cytosine, and guanine. It is a pyrimidine derivative — a heterocyclic aromatic ring with two nitrogen atoms — bearing oxygen atoms at positions 2 and 4 and a methyl group at position 5. Thymine was first isolated in 1893 by Albrecht Kossel and Albert Neumann from the thymus glands of calves, from which it takes its name.

The chemical structure of thymine (5-methyluracil).

Base pairing

In DNA, thymine pairs with adenine through two hydrogen bonds, helping to stabilise the double helix (adenine–thymine pairs are slightly weaker than guanine–cytosine pairs, which have three hydrogen bonds). This pairing is a direct consequence of the base's geometry, as described by the Watson–Crick model.

Thymine in RNA and metabolism

In RNA, thymine is generally replaced by uracil, which lacks the 5-methyl group; methylation of uracil to give thymine therefore distinguishes DNA chemically and helps the cell's repair machinery recognise and correct deamination damage. In cells, thymine is synthesised by methylation of deoxyuridine monophosphate (dUMP) by the enzyme thymidylate synthase, a step targeted by chemotherapy drugs such as 5-fluorouracil and methotrexate.

Damage

Ultraviolet light can cause adjacent thymine bases in DNA to fuse into thymine dimers, which distort the helix and block replication; unrepaired dimers are a principal cause of the mutations leading to skin cancer. Cells correct them by nucleotide excision repair, whose failure causes the disorder xeroderma pigmentosum.
