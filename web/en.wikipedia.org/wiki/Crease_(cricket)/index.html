Crease (cricket)

Image: Diagram of the creases marked at one end of a cricket pitch (placeholder)

In cricket, a crease is one of the white lines painted on the pitch near each of the wickets. The Laws of Cricket define several creases at each end: the bowling crease, the popping crease, and two return creases. Each crease is an imaginary extension of the painted line to the boundary.

The creases

The bowling crease is the line through the centres of the three stumps, 2.64 m (8 ft 8 in) long, with the stumps at its centre. The popping crease is marked 1.22 m (4 ft) in front of the bowling crease and is considered to extend to the edge of the field; it defines the limit of the batter's safe "ground" and the line the bowler's front foot must not overstep when delivering the ball (a no-ball is called if the foot lands wholly beyond it). The return creases run perpendicular to the other creases, from the popping crease to a minimum of 1.22 m behind the bowling crease, marking the corridor within which the bowler must deliver the ball.

Use in play

A batter is in their ground — and cannot be stumped or run out — if some part of their body or bat is grounded behind the popping crease. Since a 2017 change to the Laws, a batter who has grounded the bat or body beyond the crease and then loses contact while continuing momentum is not out ("bouncing bat" rule). Umpires and television replays use the crease lines to adjudicate stumpings, run-outs and front-foot no-balls.

History of the term

The word originally referred to a fold or scratch in the turf; early cricket used a single actual crease cut into the ground, from which the "popping" action of the bat — popping it into the crease to be safe — gave the popping crease its name.
