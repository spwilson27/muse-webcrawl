Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire (1772–1844)

Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire (15 April 1772 – 19 June 1844) was a French naturalist who established the principle of the "unity of composition" in animal anatomy — the idea that all animals are built upon a common structural plan. His debates with Georges Cuvier are considered a landmark in the history of biology and an important precursor to evolutionary thought.

Career

Geoffroy was appointed professor of zoology at the newly organised Muséum national d'histoire naturelle in Paris in 1793, at the age of twenty-one. In 1798 he joined Napoleon's scientific expedition to Egypt, collecting extensively and describing many new species, including the Nile crocodile's anatomy and Egyptian fishes.

Unity of composition

Geoffroy argued that the same anatomical elements recur, modified in form and function, across all vertebrates — and he extended the idea to invertebrates, famously suggesting in 1822 that an arthropod could be understood as a vertebrate inverted, with its nerve cord and body plan corresponding to the vertebrate arrangement. His search for such correspondences helped establish the modern concept of homology.

The Cuvier–Geoffroy debate

In 1830 Geoffroy defended a memoir claiming that molluscs were built on the same plan as vertebrates, provoking a celebrated series of public debates with Cuvier before the French Academy of Sciences. Cuvier's detailed anatomical objections prevailed on the particular case, but Geoffroy's broader vision — that form is governed by deep structural laws and that species are not fixed by function — influenced later evolutionary thinkers, including Darwin, who cited the "unity of type" as evidence for common descent.

Legacy

Geoffroy also founded the study of congenital malformations (teratology) and helped establish the Paris zoo at the Jardin des Plantes. His son, Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, continued his work and coined the term éthologie (ethology).
