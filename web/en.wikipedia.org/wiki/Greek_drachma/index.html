Greek drachma

The drachma (Greek: drachmí, plural drachmés) was the currency of Greece for much of its modern history and one of the world's oldest currencies, its name and coinage descending from the ancient Greek drachma used across the Hellenic world from the 6th century BC. The modern drachma was in use from 1833 until 2002, when Greece adopted the euro at a conversion rate of 340.75 drachmae to one euro.

Ancient drachma

The ancient drachma was a silver coin whose name derived from a verb meaning "to grasp"—a handful (drachma) of six spit-like obols. The Athenian tetradrachm, bearing Athena and her owl, became a standard trade coin of the Mediterranean. The drachma was widely imitated across the Greek world and successor kingdoms.

Modern drachma

The modern Greek state introduced the drachma in 1833, replacing the short-lived phoenix of Ioannis Kapodistrias. It joined the Latin Monetary Union in 1868. In 1954 the currency was redenominated at 1 new drachma = 1,000 old. Greece joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism in 1998 and the eurozone on 1 January 2001; euro coins and notes replaced drachma cash during a dual-circulation period ending 28 February 2002. Drachma coins and banknotes remained exchangeable at the Bank of Greece for a period (banknotes until 2012). Denominations at withdrawal included coins of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 500 drachmae and notes of 100, 200, 500, 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000 drachmae, depicting figures such as Democritus, Adamantios Korais, and Andreas Papandreou.
