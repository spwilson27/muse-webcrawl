Years of potential life lost (YPLL) or potential years of life lost (PYLL) is an estimate of the average years a person would have lived if they had not died prematurely. It is a measure of premature mortality used in epidemiology and public health that gives greater weight to deaths occurring at younger ages.

Calculation

For an individual, YPLL is calculated as a reference age — often the life expectancy at birth, or a fixed threshold such as 70 or 75 years — minus the age at death. The statistic for a population is the sum of YPLL over all deaths in the period, sometimes expressed as a rate per 100,000 population and age-standardised to permit comparisons between populations with different age structures.

Because a death in infancy contributes roughly a whole lifespan's worth of lost years while a death near the reference age contributes few, YPLL rankings of causes of death differ sharply from crude mortality rankings. Accidents, suicide, homicide, and perinatal conditions rank much higher by YPLL than by number of deaths, while diseases of old age, such as most heart disease and stroke deaths, rank lower.

Use and variants

The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has published YPLL statistics since the 1980s, and the OECD reports potential years of life lost as a health-system indicator. Variants include years of life lost (YLL) as used in the Global Burden of Disease study, where the reference is a standard life expectancy at each age, and working-age YPLL, which counts only years lost between ages such as 15 and 65 to estimate economic impact. YPLL is one component of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), alongside years lived with disability.

Limitations

Critics note that YPLL ignores morbidity and quality of life, depends on an arbitrary reference age, and can be read as valuing younger lives over older ones. It is therefore normally reported alongside other measures rather than alone.
