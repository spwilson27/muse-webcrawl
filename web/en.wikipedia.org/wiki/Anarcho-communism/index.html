Anarcho-communism, also known as anarchist communism, is a political philosophy and anarchist school of thought that advocates the abolition of the state, capitalism, wage labour and private property, in favour of common ownership of the means of production, direct democracy, and a horizontal network of voluntary associations and free communes in which goods are distributed according to need.

History

Anarchist communism emerged as a distinct current within the anti-authoritarian wing of the First International in the 1870s, articulated by Italian revolutionaries such as Carlo Cafiero and Errico Malatesta and adopted by the Jura Federation. Its most famous exponent, the Russian geographer Peter Kropotkin, set out the programme in The Conquest of Bread (1892) and defended its scientific basis in Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902). Anarcho-communists distinguished themselves from collectivist anarchists, who retained remuneration according to labour, by insisting on free access to goods from the outset of a revolution.

Practice

Anarcho-communist ideas influenced the Makhnovshchina in Ukraine (1918–1921) and the anarchist collectives of revolutionary Catalonia during the Spanish Civil War. Contemporary anarcho-communism persists in federations and platformist organisations worldwide.
