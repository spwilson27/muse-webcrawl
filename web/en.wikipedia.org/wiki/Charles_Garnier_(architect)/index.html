Charles Garnier (architect)

Jean-Louis Charles Garnier (pronounced [ʃaʁl ɡaʁnje]; 6 November 1825 – 3 August 1898) was a French architect, best known as the designer of the Palais Garnier in Paris and the Monte Carlo Casino and opera house in Monaco. His exuberant, eclectic manner became one of the defining expressions of the architecture of the French Second Empire.

Early life and training

Garnier was born in Paris to a family of modest means and trained at the École des Beaux-Arts, where he won the Grand Prix de Rome in 1848. His years at the French Academy in Rome and travel in Greece and Turkey shaped his interest in polychromy and in the classical tradition understood flexibly rather than rigidly.

The Palais Garnier

In 1861 Garnier, then little known, won the competition for a new Paris opera house commissioned by Napoleon III as part of Baron Haussmann's transformation of the capital. Construction lasted from 1861 to 1875, interrupted by the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune. The Palais Garnier — with its grand marble staircase, horseshoe auditorium in red and gold, and chandelier weighing several tons — became the archetype of "Napoleon III style". When the Empress Eugénie asked what style it was, Garnier is said to have replied, "It is Napoleon III style, Madame." The building later gained further fame as the setting of Gaston Leroux's novel The Phantom of the Opera.

Other works

Between 1878 and 1879 Garnier built the casino and Salle Garnier opera house at Monte Carlo for the Société des Bains de Mer. He also designed the Paris observatory's annexes, the Cercle de la Librairie, private villas on the Riviera — including his own Villa Garnier at Bordighera — and, with Gustave Eiffel, the dome of the Nice Observatory. He died in Paris in 1898.
