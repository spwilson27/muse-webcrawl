Overshoot (population)

In population ecology, overshoot occurs when a population temporarily exceeds the long-term carrying capacity of its environment. Overshoot is made possible by a time lag between reproduction and the resource depletion that follows; the population then typically suffers a sharp decline, or "die-off", until numbers fall back within the environment's capacity to support them. The pattern is distinct from overpopulation as a static condition: overshoot describes the dynamic of exceeding capacity and crashing.

Mechanism

When a population colonizes a rich new environment, exponential growth can carry it beyond the sustainable level before scarcity is felt, because resource stocks accumulated over long periods are consumed as if they were permanent flows. Overshoot is followed by degradation of the resource base, which may lower carrying capacity itself, deepening the crash.

Examples

A classic case is the reindeer of St. Matthew Island, Alaska: 29 animals introduced in 1944 grew to about 6,000 by 1963, stripping the island's lichens; the population then crashed to 42 by 1966. The human case is debated: the Global Footprint Network's "Earth Overshoot Day" estimates the date each year when humanity's resource demand exceeds the planet's annual regeneration, while William Catton's 1980 book Overshoot: The Ecological Basis of Revolutionary Change applied the concept to industrial civilization's dependence on fossil fuels.
