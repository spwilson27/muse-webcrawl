Krampus

Krampus is a horned, anthropomorphic figure in the Central and Eastern European Alpine folklore who, during the Christmas season, scares children who have misbehaved. Assisting Saint Nicholas, the pair visit children on the night of 5 December, known as Krampusnacht ("Krampus Night"), with Saint Nicholas rewarding well-behaved children with gifts such as dried fruit and oranges, while the badly behaved ones receive punishment from Krampus, traditionally beatings with birch branches or, in sterner tellings, being carried off in his sack.

Origins

The origin of the figure is uncertain. Krampus has been linked to pre-Christian Alpine traditions, to Norse mythology (as a son of the goddess Hel), and to figures such as the horned god of the witches. The Catholic Church discouraged Krampus celebrations for centuries, and during the Austrofascist period of the 1930s the figure was briefly banned in Austria as a symbol of paganism and socialist agitation. Despite this, the tradition survived in rural areas and has experienced a revival.

Appearance

Krampus is commonly depicted as a hairy, dark-furred creature with the cloven hooves and horns of a goat, a long pointed tongue lolling from its mouth, and fangs. He carries chains, thought to symbolise the binding of the Devil by the Christian Church, which he thrashes about for dramatic effect, along with bells of various sizes. He carries a bundle of birch branches (ruten) for swatting naughty children and, in some depictions, a sack or basket strapped to his back for carrying off especially wicked children to be drowned, eaten, or transported to Hell.

Krampusnacht and Krampuslauf

The Feast of Saint Nicholas is celebrated on 6 December; on the preceding evening, 5 December, Krampus accompanies the saint. In many Alpine towns, young men dress as Krampus and take part in a Krampuslauf ("Krampus run"), parading through the streets with torches, chains, and cowbells; alcohol is often a feature of these events, which are held in towns across Austria, southern Germany, and northern Italy. In recent decades the custom has spread to cities in North America and elsewhere.

In popular culture

Krampus appears on vintage greeting cards (Krampuskarten) exchanged since the 1800s, and in modern film and television, including the 2015 horror-comedy film Krampus. The figure has become a widely recognised counterpoint to the gentler traditions of Saint Nicholas and Santa Claus.
