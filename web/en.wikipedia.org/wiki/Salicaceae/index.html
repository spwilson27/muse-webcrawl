The Salicaceae are a family of flowering plants in the order Malpighiales. In the traditional sense the family comprised only the willows (Salix), poplars and aspens (Populus) and their close relatives, but under the APG classification the family has been greatly expanded to include many genera formerly placed in the family Flacourtiaceae, bringing it to roughly 56 genera and more than 1,200 species.

Description

Members of the Salicaceae in the narrow sense are mostly trees and shrubs of temperate regions, often found in moist habitats along rivers and streams. They typically have simple, alternate leaves with stipules, and their flowers are arranged in catkins. Plants are usually dioecious, with male and female flowers on separate individuals. The seeds are small and bear tufts of silky hairs that aid wind dispersal — the familiar "cotton" of cottonwoods and willows. The expanded family includes many tropical trees and shrubs with quite different floral structures.

Uses

Poplars and willows are of considerable economic importance. Poplars are grown for timber, pulpwood, matches and as fast-growing shelterbelts and biofuel crops; aspens are keystone species of boreal and montane forests. Willows are used for basketry (osiers), cricket bats (from Salix alba var. caerulea), and erosion control. Willow bark contains salicin, the natural precursor of aspirin, and has been used medicinally since ancient times. Many species of both genera are planted as ornamentals.

Ecology

Salicaceae species are pioneer plants adapted to colonising open, disturbed, often wet ground. They reproduce readily from broken twigs and cuttings as well as by seed. They support a rich insect fauna, especially moth larvae, and hybridise freely, which can make species identification difficult.
