Spelling alphabet

A spelling alphabet (also called a radiotelephony spelling alphabet, voice procedure alphabet, or phonetic alphabet in popular usage) is a set of code words used to represent the letters of an alphabet when spelling out words over voice communications, especially radio and telephone. Because many letters sound alike (such as B, D, E, P, and T), assigning each letter a distinctive, easily recognised word greatly reduces errors, particularly in noisy conditions or between speakers of different languages.

NATO phonetic alphabet

The most widely used spelling alphabet is the one developed by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) and adopted by NATO in 1956, formally the International Radiotelephony Spelling Alphabet:

The words were chosen after extensive testing for intelligibility across English, French, and Spanish speakers. Odd spellings such as "Alfa" (instead of "Alpha") and "Juliett" (with a double T) are deliberate, ensuring correct pronunciation by non-native speakers. The alphabet is mandatory in international aviation and maritime communication and is used by militaries, emergency services, and amateur radio operators worldwide.

Earlier alphabets

Predecessor systems include the British Army and Royal Navy alphabets of the First World War ("Apples, Butter, Charlie, Duff, Edward…"), the Allied "Able, Baker, Charlie, Dog…" alphabet of the Second World War, and numerous national police and telephone alphabets, such as the APCO alphabet used by some US police departments ("Adam, Boy, Charles…").
