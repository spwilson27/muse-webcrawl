Brake-specific fuel consumption

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC) is a measure of the fuel efficiency of any prime mover that burns fuel and produces rotational, or shaft, work. It is typically used for comparing the efficiency of internal combustion engines with a rotating shaft as output. It is the rate of fuel consumption divided by the power produced:

BSFC = (fuel consumption rate) / (brake power)

The term "brake" refers to the dynamometer brake used to measure the engine's output at its output shaft. BSFC allows the fuel efficiency of engines of different sizes to be directly compared. Common units are grams per kilowatt-hour (g/(kW·h)); in the United States, pounds per horsepower-hour (lb/(hp·h)) is also used. Lower values indicate higher efficiency.

Typical values

The best production spark-ignition (petrol) engines achieve minimum BSFC values of about 225–240 g/(kW·h), corresponding to thermal efficiencies around 35–37%. Modern passenger-car diesel engines reach roughly 200–225 g/(kW·h), and large two-stroke marine diesels — the most efficient piston engines built — can reach values near 155–170 g/(kW·h), equivalent to efficiencies over 50%. Gas turbines generally have higher (worse) BSFC at part load, improving at full power.

BSFC maps

Because BSFC varies with engine speed and load, engineers present it as a contour map ("BSFC map" or "efficiency island map") over the engine's operating range. The map shows the region of speed and torque where the engine is most efficient, and is used in powertrain design — for example, to choose gear ratios so that the engine operates near its most efficient point during typical driving, and in hybrid vehicle control strategies.

Related measures

Indicated-specific fuel consumption (ISFC) uses indicated power (before friction losses) rather than brake power. For jet engines, the analogous measure is thrust-specific fuel consumption (TSFC), which relates fuel flow to thrust produced.
