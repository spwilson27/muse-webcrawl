Deir Alla Inscription

The Deir Alla Inscription (or Balaam inscription) is an ancient ink inscription discovered in 1967 at Deir 'Alla, a Bronze and Iron Age site in the Jordan Valley about 8 km east of the Jordan River and 14 km south of the Sea of Galilee. Written in black and red ink on plastered wall fragments, it is one of the earliest known examples of an alphabetic West Semitic text and is famous for naming the prophet Balaam son of Beor, a figure also known from the Hebrew Bible.

Discovery

The fragments were found during excavations led by H. J. Franken of Leiden University following an earthquake that exposed the collapsed sanctuary walls on which the text had been written, probably in the eighth century BCE. The plaster pieces were painstakingly reassembled, and the edited text was published by Hoftijzer and van der Kooij in 1976.

Content

The inscription combines two narrative sections. The first describes a night vision in which the gods warn Balaam of coming disaster; Balaam weeps, fasts, and addresses an assembly that asks the meaning of his distress. The second, more fragmentary section appears to describe creation, order, and cosmic judgments involving deities such as El and the Shaddai gods.

Language

The language of the inscription has been much debated. It shows features of both Aramaic and a local Canaanite dialect, and is often described as a distinctive South Gileadite or Transjordanian dialect. Its script is an early Aramaic cursive.

Significance

Because Balaam appears in Numbers 22–24 as a non-Israelite seer, the inscription is important evidence that traditions about Balaam circulated in the wider Levant independently of the biblical text. It is frequently cited in discussions of the Balaam tradition, ancient prophecy, and the religion of the Iron Age Levant.
