Marsala wine

Marsala is a fortified wine, dry or sweet, produced in the region surrounding the Italian city of Marsala in Sicily. Marsala first received Denominazione di Origine Controllata (DOC) status in 1969. The wine is made using the Grillo, Inzolia, Catarratto, and Damaschino white grape varieties, among others, and is fortified with grape spirit, traditionally in a process similar to the in perpetuum system used for sherry.

History

The modern Marsala trade was established in 1773 by the English merchant John Woodhouse, who shipped the local wine to England, fortified so it would survive the voyage. Woodhouse and later firms such as Ingham and Florio built great bagli (wine lodges) at Marsala, and the wine became a staple in Britain, rivalling Madeira and sherry.

Styles

Marsala is classified by colour — Oro (golden), Ambra (amber), and Rubino (ruby, from red grapes) — and by sweetness: Secco (dry), Semisecco, and Dolce (sweet). Ageing grades run from Fine (one year) through Superiore and Vergine to Vergine Stravecchio, aged at least ten years. Dry Marsala is served as an aperitif; sweet Marsala is used in cooking, famously in chicken and veal Marsala dishes and in zabaglione and tiramisu.
