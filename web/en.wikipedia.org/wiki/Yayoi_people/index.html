The Yayoi people (弥生人, Yayoi jin) were an ethnic group and the ancestors of the modern Japanese people who migrated to the Japanese archipelago from continental Asia during the Yayoi period, conventionally dated from about the early first millennium BC to the middle of the third century AD. Originating ultimately from Northeast Asia by way of the Korean Peninsula, they brought with them wet-rice farming, metallurgy, and new forms of social organization that transformed the islands previously inhabited by the hunter-gatherer Jōmon people.

Origins

Genetic and archaeological research indicates that the Yayoi migrants derived from populations of Northeast Asia, related to peoples living around the Yellow River and Liao River basins and the Korean Peninsula. They appear to have entered Japan first through northern Kyushu, close to the Korean Strait, and then spread eastward across Honshu over several centuries. Studies of ancient DNA suggest substantial admixture between the newcomers and the indigenous Jōmon, so that present-day Japanese derive ancestry from both groups, with additional input during the later Kofun period.

Culture and society

The defining innovation of the Yayoi was irrigated paddy-field rice agriculture, accompanied by bronze and iron tools and weapons, weaving, and glass production. Settlements such as Yoshinogari in Kyushu were large, moated villages with watchtowers and raised-floor storehouses. Yayoi society was stratified: burials show marked differences in wealth, and by the late Yayoi period small polities were competing and coalescing, a process described in early Chinese histories such as the account of the shaman-queen Himiko of Yamatai.

Physical characteristics

Yayoi skeletal remains are generally taller and longer-faced than Jōmon remains, a contrast long used by anthropologists to identify the migrant population. These physical differences, together with linguistic and genetic evidence, support the dual-structure model of Japanese population history proposed by Hanihara Kazurō.

Legacy

The Yayoi migration laid the agricultural and social foundations of early Japan. Their descendants, admixed with Jōmon and later continental migrants, formed the population from which the historic Japanese people emerged.
