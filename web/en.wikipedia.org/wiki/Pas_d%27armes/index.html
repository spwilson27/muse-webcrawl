Pas d'armes

The pas d'armes (French for "passage of arms") was a type of chivalric combat that evolved in the late medieval period, particularly in the 15th century, and was especially popular at the courts of Burgundy and France. Unlike the mêlée of earlier tournaments, the pas d'armes was a staged, theatrical form of martial display in which one or more knights (the tenans, or holders) defended a passage — a bridge, a road, or a symbolic location — against all challengers (the venans, or comers).

Characteristics

Pas d'armes were frequently framed by elaborate fictional narratives drawn from chivalric romance. The defenders might guard a fountain, a lady's pavilion, or a "tree of arms" on which shields were hung; a challenger would touch a shield to indicate the type of combat he desired, such as jousting with lances or fighting on foot with swords, axes, or spears. Combat was governed by detailed chapters of arms agreed in advance, and bouts were judged by heralds and noble ladies.

Famous examples

Among the most famous pas d'armes were the Pas de la Bergère (1449), organized by René of Anjou; the Pas de l'Arbre d'Or (1468), held at Bruges for the wedding of Charles the Bold and Margaret of York; and the Pas du Perron Fée. These events were recorded in richly detailed chronicles and manuscripts, and they represent the high point of late medieval chivalric culture before gunpowder warfare and changing court tastes caused the format to decline.
