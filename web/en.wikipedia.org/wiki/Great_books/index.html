The great books are the books considered to be the essential foundation of Western culture, written by what one critic called "the best writers". They are traditionally the works of Homer, Plato, Aristotle, the Greek tragedians, Virgil, Augustine, Aquinas, Dante, Chaucer, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Montaigne, Galileo, Newton, Locke, Hume, Rousseau, Kant, Mill, Darwin, and others — a canon debated and revised ever since the idea took shape in American higher education in the early 20th century.

The movement

John Erskine's General Honors seminar at Columbia University (begun 1920) pioneered reading the classics whole and discussing them in seminars. At the University of Chicago, Robert Maynard Hutchins and Mortimer Adler built a great-books curriculum and the Great Books Foundation (1947) for adult discussion groups. Their work culminated in Encyclopædia Britannica's Great Books of the Western World (1952; 54 volumes, revised 1990 to 60), accompanied by Adler's Syntopicon, an index of 102 "great ideas". St. John's College in Annapolis (and later Santa Fe) adopted an all-required great-books curriculum under Stringfellow Barr and Scott Buchanan in 1937 and retains it today.

Criticism

The canon has been criticized for excluding women, non-European traditions, and modern and non-elite voices — the "dead white men" critique sharpened during the culture wars of the 1980s–90s (notably the 1988 Stanford curriculum controversy). Defenders reply that the list is an invitation to a continuing "great conversation" rather than a closed canon, and programmes have expanded to include works such as the Tao Te Ching, Augustine's female contemporaries' successors like Mary Wollstonecraft and Virginia Woolf, and non-Western classics in some curricula.
