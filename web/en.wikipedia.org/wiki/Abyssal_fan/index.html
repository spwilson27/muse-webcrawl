Abyssal fan

An abyssal fan, also known as a deep-sea fan or submarine fan, is a fan-shaped deposit of sediment on the deep ocean floor at the base of the continental slope. Abyssal fans are built where sediment-laden turbidity currents, channelled down submarine canyons, emerge onto the gentler continental rise and deposit their load, in much the same way that an alluvial fan forms at the foot of a mountain range on land.

A submarine fan spreads outward from the mouth of a canyon across the abyssal plain.

Formation

Sediment eroded from a continent is carried by rivers to the shelf edge and funnelled into submarine canyons. Earthquakes, storms or simple overloading can trigger turbidity currents — dense, sediment-charged flows that rush down the canyon at speed and spread out where the gradient lessens. As each current decelerates it deposits a layer of sediment known as a turbidite, typically showing graded bedding from coarse at the base to fine at the top (the classic Bouma sequence). Over thousands to millions of years, repeated flows build the fan outward, with a network of branching channels and levees on its upper surface that shifts course over time (avulsion), distributing sediment across the fan.

Major fans

The Bengal Fan, fed by the Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers draining the Himalayas, is the largest deep-sea fan on Earth, extending some 3,000 km southward across the floor of the Bay of Bengal, with sediment thicknesses exceeding 16 km near its apex. Other major fans include the Indus Fan in the Arabian Sea, the Mississippi Fan in the Gulf of Mexico, the Amazon Fan in the Atlantic, and the Laurentian Fan off eastern Canada.

Significance

Deep-sea fans are important archives of Earth history: their sediments record continental erosion, climate change and the growth of mountain belts such as the Himalayas. Economically, the porous sandstone layers within ancient fans — buried and lithified — can form reservoirs for oil and natural gas, and several modern petroleum provinces (in the Gulf of Mexico, offshore West Africa and Brazil) target submarine-fan deposits. Fan channels also carry organic carbon to the deep sea, where it may be buried for geological timescales.
