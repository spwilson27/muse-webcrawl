Video game modding

Video game modding (from "modifying") is the process by which players or fans alter one or more aspects of a video game, such as its appearance, mechanics, levels, or content. The resulting modifications are called mods. Modding ranges from small tweaks and cosmetic changes to "total conversions" that transform a game into an essentially new work. Modding is a longstanding pillar of video game culture, particularly on personal computers.

History

Modding emerged in the early 1980s with hobbyist alterations of games, and took off in the 1990s when id Software designed Wolfenstein 3D (1992) and Doom (1993) with data files separable from the engine, encouraging user-created levels. Valve's Half-Life (1998) spawned the mod Counter-Strike (1999), which became one of the most successful games in the world and led Valve to hire its creators. Similarly, the Warcraft III custom map Defense of the Ancients (2003) founded the MOBA genre.

Types of mods

  • Total conversions replace most assets and gameplay, effectively creating a new game.
  • Overhauls deeply change systems such as balance, AI, or graphics.
  • Add-ons and plugins add items, quests, maps, or quality-of-life features.
  • Art and cosmetic mods alter textures, models, and sound; graphical enhancement mods are especially popular for aging titles.
  • Unofficial patches fix bugs left unaddressed by developers.

Distribution and tools

Mods are distributed through sites such as Nexus Mods and Mod DB, and through integrated systems like the Steam Workshop (launched 2011). Many developers support modding with official toolkits and editors, as with Bethesda's Creation Kit for The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, whose modding scene kept the game commercially relevant for well over a decade.

Legal and commercial issues

Mods usually require ownership of the base game and are distributed free, since modders rarely hold rights to sell derivative works. Attempts to introduce paid mods, notably Valve and Bethesda's 2015 Steam initiative, met strong community backlash and were withdrawn. Some developers restrict modding through end-user license agreements or technical measures, while others credit modding communities as a talent pipeline into the professional industry.
