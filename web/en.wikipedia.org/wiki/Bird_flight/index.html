Bird flight is the primary mode of locomotion used by most bird species, in which they take off and fly. Flight assists birds in feeding, breeding, avoiding predators, and migrating. Bird flight includes multiple types of motion, including hovering, taking off, and landing, and many anatomical features are specialised for it: a light, strong skeleton, two powerful flight muscles constituting a quarter or more of body weight, and feathers modified into wings.

Basic mechanics

The wing is an aerofoil: air passing over its curved upper surface moves faster and creates lower pressure than the air beneath, generating lift. Flapping produces thrust as well as lift, chiefly on the downstroke, powered by the pectoralis muscles; the upstroke is powered by the supracoracoideus, which works through a pulley-like tendon over the shoulder. The alula, a small "thumb" structure, helps maintain smooth airflow at high angles of attack and low speeds.

Flight styles

Birds employ several gaits. Many species alternate flapping with glides; seabirds such as albatrosses use dynamic soaring to extract energy from wind gradients over the ocean, while vultures and eagles soar on thermal updrafts. Hummingbirds hover by generating lift on both strokes of a figure-eight wingbeat, reaching wingbeat frequencies of dozens of beats per second. Intermittent "bounding" flight, alternating flapping bursts with folded-wing bounds, is typical of small passerines.

Take-off, landing and manoeuvring

Take-off often involves a jump and powerful downstrokes, or a running start for heavy waterbirds. Landing birds raise the wing's angle of attack, spread the tail and use the alula to avoid stalling. The tail fans and twists to steer, brake and balance.

Evolution

Powered flight evolved in the theropod dinosaurs ancestral to birds, with feathered forms such as Archaeopteryx transitional between dinosaurs and modern birds. Debate continues between "ground-up" (cursorial) and "trees-down" (arboreal) theories of flight origins; some researchers propose wing-assisted incline running as an intermediate behaviour.
