Infant cognition

Infant cognition is the study of how infants perceive, learn, remember and think during roughly the first two years of life. Because infants cannot talk, researchers infer their mental processes from behaviour, using methods such as preferential looking, habituation, and measurements of sucking rate, as well as neuroimaging techniques. The field is a branch of developmental psychology and cognitive science.

History

Jean Piaget, who based his account largely on observations of his own children, portrayed infants as constructing knowledge through sensorimotor action, and argued that object permanence — knowing that objects continue to exist when hidden — emerges only gradually, around 8 to 12 months. From the 1980s, researchers such as Renée Baillargeon used violation-of-expectation experiments, in which infants look longer at events that seem physically impossible, to argue that much younger infants already understand more about objects than Piaget supposed.

Core knowledge

Elizabeth Spelke and colleagues proposed that infants are born with several core knowledge systems: systems for representing inanimate objects and their mechanical interactions, agents and their goal-directed actions, approximate number, and geometric properties of space. Experiments suggest that young infants expect objects to be solid, continuous and to move on connected paths, that they can discriminate small numbers of objects and track approximate quantities, and that even newborns show preferences for faces and biological motion. These findings feed the debate between nativist accounts, which posit innate structure, and empiricist accounts, which emphasise learning from experience.

Learning and memory

Infants learn from birth: newborns recognise their mother's voice, young infants detect statistical regularities in speech streams that help segment words, and habituation studies show rapid encoding of new stimuli. Carolyn Rovee-Collier's mobile experiments demonstrated that infants as young as two to three months can remember actions and their consequences over days or weeks. Imitation, joint attention (emerging toward the end of the first year), and early language acquisition are also central topics.

Social cognition

Research on infant social cognition includes early sensitivity to faces and eyes, the beginnings of theory of mind — some looking-time studies suggest implicit false-belief understanding in the second year, though replication of these results has been debated — and early moral evaluations, such as preferences for helpful over hindering characters in puppet-show experiments.
