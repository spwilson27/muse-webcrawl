Ohm's law

Ohm's law
StatementV = IR
Named afterGeorg Simon Ohm
First published1827, Die galvanische Kette, mathematisch bearbeitet
QuantitiesVoltage (V), current (I), resistance (R)

Ohm's law states that the electric current I flowing through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage V across the two points and inversely proportional to the resistance R between them: V = IR. It was formulated by the German physicist Georg Simon Ohm, whose 1827 treatise on the galvanic circuit was initially received coolly but later recognized as a foundation of electrical science; the SI unit of resistance, the ohm (Ω), bears his name.

Scope and limits

The law is an empirical relationship obeyed to good approximation by many materials — "ohmic" materials — at constant temperature and over wide ranges of current. It fails or must be generalized for devices such as diodes and transistors, for superconductors (which have zero resistance), and at very high fields or frequencies, where impedance and reactance replace simple resistance. In vector form for continuous media, Ohm's law relates current density to electric field through conductivity.

Applications

Ohm's law underpins circuit analysis: combined with Kirchhoff's laws it allows voltages, currents and power dissipation in networks to be calculated, guiding the design of everything from power grids to microelectronics. Analogous "Ohm's laws" exist for magnetic circuits, thermal conduction and fluid flow.
