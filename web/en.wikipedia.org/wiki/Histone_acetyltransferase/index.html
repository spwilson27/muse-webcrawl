Histone acetyltransferase

Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) are enzymes that acetylate conserved lysine amino acids on histone proteins, transferring an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to form ε-N-acetyllysine. Acetylation is one of the principal epigenetic modifications of chromatin and is generally associated with active gene transcription.

Mechanism and effect on chromatin

Histone tails are rich in lysine and therefore positively charged, binding them tightly to negatively charged DNA. Neutralising that charge by acetylation loosens the interaction between histones and DNA, relaxing chromatin into the more open euchromatin state accessible to transcription factors and RNA polymerase. Acetyl-lysine also serves as a binding site for bromodomain-containing proteins that "read" the mark. The reaction is reversed by histone deacetylases (HDACs), and the balance of HAT and HDAC activity regulates gene expression genome-wide.

Families

HATs are grouped into families by sequence and structure. Type A HATs are nuclear and act on chromatin in the context of transcription; they include the GNAT family (such as Gcn5 and PCAF), the transcriptional coactivators p300 and CBP, and the MYST family (including Tip60 and MOF). Type B HATs, exemplified by HAT1, are mainly cytoplasmic and acetylate newly synthesised free histones before their deposition onto DNA. The first HAT was identified in 1996 when the Tetrahymena enzyme proved to be a homologue of yeast Gcn5, a long-known transcriptional regulator — a landmark linking chromatin modification directly to gene control.

Clinical significance

Mutations and misregulation of HATs occur in developmental disorders — loss of CBP or p300 causes Rubinstein–Taybi syndrome — and in many cancers, making HATs (like their HDAC counterparts) targets of drug development.
