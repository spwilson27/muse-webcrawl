The Curtiss P-40 Warhawk is an American single-engined, single-seat, all-metal fighter and ground-attack aircraft that first flew in 1938. The P-40 design was a modification of the previous Curtiss P-36 Hawk which reduced development time and enabled a rapid start into production and operational service.

The Warhawk was used by most Allied powers during World War II, and remained in frontline service until the end of the war. It was the third most-produced American fighter of World War II, after the North American P-51 Mustang and Republic P-47 Thunderbolt; by November 1944, when production of the P-40 ceased, 13,738 had been built.

Names

P-40s first saw combat with the British Commonwealth squadrons of the Desert Air Force in the Middle East and North African campaigns. The aircraft was known as the Tomahawk (models equivalent to the P-40B/C) and Kittyhawk (models equivalent to the P-40D and later) in Commonwealth service. It is perhaps best known as the aircraft flown by the American Volunteer Group — the "Flying Tigers" — in China, whose planes bore distinctive shark-mouth nose art.

Performance and service

The P-40 lacked a two-stage supercharger, which limited its performance at high altitude, but it was rugged, well-armed, and effective at low and medium altitudes in fighter-bomber and escort roles. It served in the Pacific, China-Burma-India, Mediterranean, and Eastern Front (via Lend-Lease to the Soviet Union) theaters. Notable units included the 99th Fighter Squadron (the Tuskegee Airmen's first fighter unit) and Claire Chennault's Flying Tigers.

Variants

Major variants included the P-40B and C (Tomahawk), P-40D and E (Kittyhawk), and the later P-40F and L powered by Packard-built Rolls-Royce Merlin engines, and the P-40N, the most produced variant, with a lightened structure.
