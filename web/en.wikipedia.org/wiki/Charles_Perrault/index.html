Charles Perrault (12 January 1628 – 16 May 1703) was a French author and member of the Académie Française who laid the foundations for the literary fairy tale. His collection Histoires ou contes du temps passé (1697), subtitled Tales of Mother Goose, gave definitive literary form to stories including Cinderella, Sleeping Beauty, Little Red Riding Hood, Puss in Boots, and Bluebeard.

Life

Born into a wealthy Parisian family, Perrault trained as a lawyer before entering the service of Jean-Baptiste Colbert, minister to Louis XIV. He played a major administrative role in the founding of the Academy of Sciences and in the rebuilding of the Louvre. In the celebrated Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns, he championed the Moderns, arguing in Parallèle des Anciens et des Modernes (1688–1697) that the age of Louis XIV equalled or surpassed classical antiquity.

The Tales

Published under the name of his son Pierre, the Tales collected eight prose stories drawn from oral folk tradition, each closing with a versified moral. Perrault's versions became the standard texts on which the Brothers Grimm and countless later retellings depend.
