Christianity and Judaism

Christianity began as a movement within Second Temple Judaism in the 1st century CE. Early followers of Jesus were Jews who regarded him as the promised Messiah. Over the following decades, the movement spread among Gentiles and gradually separated from Judaism, a process sometimes called the "parting of the ways".

The two religions share the Hebrew Bible — called the Old Testament by Christians — as scripture, and both worship the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. They differ fundamentally on the identity of Jesus: Christians believe he is the incarnate Son of God and the Messiah, while Judaism does not accept him as Messiah or divine.

Shared heritage

Both traditions affirm monotheism, the creation of the world by God, the covenant with Abraham, and the giving of the Law to Moses on Mount Sinai. Ethical teachings such as the Ten Commandments are common to both, and much of Christian liturgy and prayer derives from Jewish practice.

Points of difference

Major differences include the Christian doctrines of the Trinity and the Incarnation, which Judaism rejects; the Christian emphasis on salvation through faith in Jesus versus the Jewish emphasis on the covenant and observance of the commandments (mitzvot); and differing views of the afterlife, original sin, and the role of the Messiah.

History of relations

Relations between the two faiths were often marked by Christian anti-Judaism over many centuries, including persecution and forced conversion. Since the Second World War and the Holocaust, many Christian churches have repudiated anti-Jewish teaching — notably the Catholic Church in Nostra aetate (1965) — and interfaith dialogue between Christians and Jews has expanded significantly.
