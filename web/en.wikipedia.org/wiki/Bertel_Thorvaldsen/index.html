Bertel Thorvaldsen

Bertel Thorvaldsen (19 November 1770 – 24 March 1844) was a Danish-Icelandic sculptor and medallist of international renown, the leading figure of Neoclassical sculpture alongside Antonio Canova. He spent most of his working life in Rome, producing statues of mythological and historical subjects for patrons across Europe, and bequeathed his works and collections to Copenhagen, where the Thorvaldsens Museum was built to house them.

Life and career

Thorvaldsen was born in Copenhagen to an Icelandic woodcarver father. Admitted to the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts at eleven, he won its major prizes and a travelling scholarship that took him to Rome in 1797. There his statue of Jason with the Golden Fleece (1803) won acclaim and a commission from the English banker Thomas Hope, establishing his career. His Roman workshop, employing many assistants, executed commissions including the tomb of Pope Pius VII in St Peter's, the Lion Monument at Lucerne (carved by Lukas Ahorn after his model), and colossal figures of Copernicus and Poniatowski for Warsaw.

Thorvaldsen's Christus (1821), depicting the resurrected Christ with outstretched arms, became one of the most reproduced religious images of the 19th century; the original stands in Copenhagen Cathedral, and copies are found worldwide.

Legacy

Returning to Denmark in 1838 to a hero's welcome, Thorvaldsen donated his extensive collection of his own works and of classical and contemporary art to the city of Copenhagen. The Thorvaldsens Museum, opened in 1848 — the first public museum building in Denmark — contains his tomb in its courtyard. His influence on Danish art and the "Golden Age" generation of painters and sculptors was profound.
