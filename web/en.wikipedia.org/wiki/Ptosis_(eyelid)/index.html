Ptosis (eyelid)

Ptosis (from Greek πτῶσις, "falling"), also called blepharoptosis, is a drooping or falling of the upper eyelid. The drooping may be worse after being awake longer, when the individual's muscles are tired. It may be present at birth (congenital ptosis) or develop later in life (acquired ptosis), and can affect one or both eyes.

Causes

Congenital ptosis usually results from underdevelopment of the levator palpebrae superioris muscle, which lifts the eyelid. Acquired ptosis may be neurogenic (for example in Horner's syndrome or a third cranial nerve palsy), myogenic (as in myasthenia gravis or chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia), aponeurotic (age-related stretching or dehiscence of the levator aponeurosis — the most common form in older adults), mechanical (caused by a tumour or swelling weighing the lid down), or traumatic.

Signs and symptoms

The upper lid normally covers the top 1–2 mm of the cornea. In ptosis the lid margin sits lower, narrowing the palpebral fissure and, in severe cases, obstructing the visual axis. Children with significant congenital ptosis may develop amblyopia. Sufferers sometimes compensate by raising the eyebrows or tilting the head back.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause and severity. Surgical options include levator resection, frontalis suspension, and Müller's muscle–conjunctival resection. Underlying conditions such as myasthenia gravis are managed medically.
