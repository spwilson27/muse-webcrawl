Richard Lovelace (9 December 1617 – 1657) was an English poet of the Caroline era, one of the so-called Cavalier poets, and a soldier and courtier who supported Charles I during the English Civil War. He is remembered chiefly for two short lyrics, "To Althea, from Prison" and "To Lucasta, Going to the Warres."

Life

Lovelace was born at Woolwich into a Kentish family with court and military connections; his father was killed in the Dutch wars when Richard was young. Educated at Charterhouse and Gloucester Hall, Oxford, he became known at court as a handsome and accomplished gentleman, and Charles I's queen reportedly called him the most amiable and beautiful person she ever saw.

A committed Royalist, Lovelace was imprisoned in 1642 after presenting a pro-episcopal petition from Kent to Parliament, and again in 1648. He served with the French army at the siege of Dunkirk in 1646, where he was wounded, and spent his fortune in the royalist cause. He died in poverty in London in 1657.

Poetry

"To Althea, from Prison", written during his 1642 imprisonment in the Gatehouse at Westminster, contains the famous lines: "Stone walls do not a prison make, / Nor iron bars a cage." "To Lucasta, Going to the Warres" defends leaving his beloved for the battlefield: "I could not love thee (Dear) so much, / Lov'd I not Honour more." His poems were collected as Lucasta (1649), with a posthumous volume, Lucasta: Posthume Poems, in 1659.
