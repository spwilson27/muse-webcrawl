Stahlhelm

The Stahlhelm (German for "steel helmet") is a type of German military combat helmet first introduced in 1916, during the First World War. Its distinctive flared, "coal scuttle" shape became one of the most recognizable symbols of the German soldier in both world wars, and the design influenced helmet development in many countries. The term Stahlhelm also refers, in a political context, to Der Stahlhelm, Bund der Frontsoldaten, a German veterans' organization of the interwar period.

Design and development

By 1915, trench warfare on the Western Front had produced large numbers of head wounds from artillery shell fragments. The German Army's leather Pickelhaube offered negligible protection. Dr. Friedrich Schwerd of the Technical Institute of Hanover, working with the surgeon August Bier, designed a deep-skirted steel helmet whose flaring rim protected the ears and neck while its dome shed fragments. The first model, the M1916, was issued to troops at Verdun in early 1916 and was an immediate success; Allied soldiers quickly dubbed it the "coal scuttle".

Variants

Successive models — M1917, M1918 (with a modified chinstrap attachment), the interwar M1933, and the Second World War M1935, M1940 and M1942 — progressively simplified production and reduced the size of the flare. Paratrooper (Fallschirmjäger) helmets dispensed with the skirt entirely. After 1945, West Germany adopted American-style helmets while East Germany developed its own M1956 design loosely inspired by late-war prototypes; several other nations, including Spain, Chile, and China (in the 1930s), used Stahlhelm-style helmets.

The veterans' organization

Der Stahlhelm, Bund der Frontsoldaten ("The Steel Helmet, League of Front-Line Soldiers"), founded in December 1918 by Franz Seldte, was one of the largest paramilitary veterans' organizations of the Weimar Republic, closely aligned with the conservative German National People's Party. It was absorbed into the Nazi Sturmabteilung structures in 1933–1934 and dissolved in 1935.
