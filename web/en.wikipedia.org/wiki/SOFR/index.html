SOFR

The Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR) is a benchmark interest rate for dollar-denominated derivatives and loans that replaced the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR) in the United States. SOFR is a broad measure of the cost of borrowing cash overnight collateralised by United States Treasury securities in the repurchase agreement (repo) market. It is published each business day by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, based on transaction-level data collected from tri-party repo, bilateral repo and the Federal Reserve's repo operations, covering roughly a trillion dollars of daily transactions.

History

Following the LIBOR manipulation scandal and the decline of unsecured interbank lending, regulators sought benchmark rates based on actual transactions rather than bank submissions. In 2014 the Federal Reserve convened the Alternative Reference Rates Committee (ARRC), a group of private-market participants, which in June 2017 selected SOFR as its preferred alternative to dollar LIBOR. Publication began in April 2018. SOFR futures launched on the CME in May 2018 grew rapidly, and regulators directed a progressive wind-down of LIBOR: new LIBOR-based contracts were discouraged after the end of 2021, and publication of the main US dollar LIBOR panels ceased on 30 June 2023.

Calculation

SOFR is computed as a volume-weighted median of transaction-level rates in three segments of the Treasury repo market: tri-party repo, data on bilateral Treasury repo cleared through the Fixed Income Clearing Corporation's delivery-versus-payment service, and repo transactions with the Federal Reserve itself. Because it is secured, transaction-based and derived from a very deep market, SOFR is considered far harder to manipulate than LIBOR. Its overnight, risk-free character differs from LIBOR, which embedded bank credit risk and term premia — a difference that required credit spread adjustments in legacy contract fallbacks.

Derived rates

Because many cash products need a forward-looking or averaged rate, several derivatives of SOFR are published: CME Term SOFR, a forward-looking term rate derived from SOFR futures used in business loans; backward-looking compounded SOFR averages for 30, 90 and 180 days published by the New York Fed, together with a SOFR Index that allows users to compute compounded averages over custom periods. Adjustable-rate mortgages, student loans, syndicated loans and floating-rate notes in US markets now overwhelmingly reference SOFR.

Criticism and volatility

SOFR has been criticised for occasional volatility arising from stress in repo markets: in September 2019 a spike in repo rates pushed SOFR to 5.25%, well above the federal funds target range, before Federal Reserve repo operations stabilised the market. Critics also note that a risk-free rate does not rise with bank funding stress in the way LIBOR did, weakening its value as a hedge for lenders, and that it lacks a credit-sensitive element valued by regional banks.
