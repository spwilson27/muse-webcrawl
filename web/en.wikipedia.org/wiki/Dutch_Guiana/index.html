Dutch Guiana

Dutch Guiana was the name for the Dutch colonies on the northern coast of South America—collectively the Dutch colonies in the Guianas—which at various times comprised Suriname, Berbice, Essequibo and Demerara. Of these, only Suriname remained Dutch into the 20th century; the others were ceded to Britain in 1814 and eventually formed British Guiana (now Guyana). "Dutch Guiana" is therefore often used as a former name of Suriname itself.

History

Dutch traders and privateers operated on the "Wild Coast" from the late 16th century. The Dutch West India Company and private societies founded plantation colonies producing sugar, coffee, cocoa and cotton, worked first by Indigenous and then overwhelmingly by enslaved African labour. Suriname was captured from the English in 1667 by Abraham Crijnssen and confirmed to the Dutch by the Treaty of Breda in exchange for New Netherland (including New Amsterdam, later New York). The Society of Suriname administered the colony from 1683.

By the Anglo-Dutch treaties of 1814–1815, Demerara, Essequibo and Berbice were ceded to Britain, leaving Suriname as the remaining Dutch colony. Suriname became an autonomous country within the Kingdom of the Netherlands in 1954 and fully independent on 25 November 1975.
