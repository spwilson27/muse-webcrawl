Evolutionary grade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In alpha taxonomy, a grade is a taxon united by similarity in morphology or physiology, generally reflecting a similar level of adaptive organisation, but not necessarily by common ancestry alone. The term was introduced into evolutionary biology by Julian Huxley in the 1950s to distinguish such assemblages from clades, which are defined strictly by descent — a clade includes an ancestor and all of its descendants.

Grades and paraphyly

Most grades correspond to paraphyletic groups: they include a common ancestor and most, but not all, of its descendants, because one or more specialised subgroups have been removed into a taxon of their own. Classic examples include:

  • Reptiles (in the traditional sense) — a grade of amniotes excluding birds, which are descendants of dinosaurs.
  • Fish — a grade of aquatic vertebrates excluding the tetrapods descended from lobe-finned fish.
  • Gymnosperms and "dicotyledons" — plant assemblages of similar organisation that are not clades.
  • Even-toed ungulates (Artiodactyla) in their traditional sense — a grade excluding the cetaceans that evolved from within them.

Use in taxonomy

Systematists of the evolutionary taxonomy school, such as Ernst Mayr and George Gaylord Simpson, argued that grades convey useful information about adaptive zones and the degree of evolutionary divergence, and defended their formal recognition. Cladists, following Willi Hennig, object that only monophyletic groups (clades) should be recognised, because grades mix ancestry with resemblance and lack objective definition. Modern phylogenetic classification largely follows the cladistic position, although grade names persist in informal usage because they describe recognisable ways of life.
