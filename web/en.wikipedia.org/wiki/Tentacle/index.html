Tentacle

In zoology, a tentacle is a flexible, mobile, elongated organ present in some species of animals, most of them invertebrates. In animal anatomy, tentacles usually occur in one or more pairs. Anatomically, the tentacles of animals work mainly like muscular hydrostats. Most forms of tentacles are used for grasping and feeding. Many are sensory organs, variously receptive to touch, vision, or to the smell or taste of particular foods or threats. Examples of such tentacles are the eyestalks of various kinds of snails.

Some kinds of tentacles have both sensory and manipulatory functions. The tentillum (plural: tentilla) is a small tentacle growing from a larger one. In cnidarians such as jellyfish, the tentacles bear cnidocytes (stinging cells) that are used both for prey capture and defence.

Tentacles in molluscs

Many molluscs have tentacles of one form or another. The most familiar are those of the land pulmonates — snails and slugs — in which there are usually two pairs of tentacles on the head; the upper pair bear the eyes, and the lower pair are organs of smell. Both pairs are fully retractable muscular hydrostats, but they are not used for manipulation or prey capture.

In many cephalopods, tentacles exist as a pair, typically modified for prey capture. The squid has two long tentacles with flattened clubs at the tips covered in suckers, in addition to its eight arms, while the octopus has only arms, which differ anatomically from true tentacles in lacking a long stalked portion.

Tentacles in cnidarians

Cnidarians, such as jellyfish, sea anemones, Hydra and corals have numerous tentacles. Cnidarians have huge numbers of cnidocytes on their tentacles. In medusoid form, the body floats on water and the tentacles hang down in a ring around the mouth. In polypoid form, such as the sea anemone and coral, the body is below with the tentacles pointing up. Hydra has a few tentacles around the mouth, used in capturing prey such as water fleas and extending to reach nutrients.

Tentacles in other groups

The ctenophores ("comb jellies") mostly have two tentacles, which in some species can be retracted into sheaths. Bryozoans possess a crown of tentacles, the lophophore, used for filter feeding. Some other groups of aquatic animals, including some polychaete worms, also bear structures called tentacles. The term is also sometimes loosely applied to the flexible appendages of other animals and even to plant structures, though in botany "tendril" is the more usual term.
