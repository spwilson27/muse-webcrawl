Whey is the liquid remaining after milk has been curdled and strained. It is a by-product of the manufacture of cheese or casein and has several commercial uses. Sweet whey results from making rennet-coagulated hard cheeses such as cheddar or Swiss; acid whey (sour whey) results from acid-coagulated products such as strained yogurt and fresh cheeses.

Composition

Liquid whey is roughly 93–95 percent water, with lactose as its main solid (about 4.5 percent), plus minerals and about 0.6–0.8 percent protein. The whey proteins — chiefly beta-lactoglobulin, alpha-lactalbumin, serum albumin, and immunoglobulins — are notably distinct from the caseins that form the curd, and they denature with heat, which is why whey can be cooked again to make cheeses such as ricotta ("recooked" in Italian) and Norwegian brunost.

Uses

Historically fed to pigs or discarded (its high biochemical oxygen demand makes raw whey a serious pollutant), whey is now extensively processed: dried into whey powder for bakery goods, confectionery, and animal feed; fractionated into whey protein concentrate and isolate, widely used in sports-nutrition supplements; and used as a source of lactose for food and pharmaceutical manufacture. Whey also appears in beverages such as the Swiss soft drink Rivella and in fermented whey drinks. Percy, the first cloned sheep experiments aside, "Little Miss Muffet" eating her curds and whey — curds being the soft fresh cheese and whey its liquid — remains the substance's best-known appearance in English folklore.
