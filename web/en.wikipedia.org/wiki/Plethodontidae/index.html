Plethodontidae

The eastern red-backed salamander (Plethodon cinereus)

Plethodontidae, the lungless salamanders, are a family of salamanders comprising the majority of all salamander species — around 500 species in some 28 genera. As their common name indicates, they entirely lack lungs, respiring instead through their skin and the lining of the mouth, which must remain moist. A nasolabial groove running from the nostril to the lip, unique to the family, aids chemoreception, helping the salamanders detect prey and recognise mates and rivals.

Distribution and habits

Plethodontids are overwhelmingly a New World family, centred on North America — especially the Appalachian Mountains, a global hotspot of salamander diversity — with species extending through Central America into South America; a handful of species of the genus Speleomantes occur in southern Europe. Most are fully terrestrial, many laying eggs on land that hatch directly into small salamanders without an aquatic larval stage, while others are aquatic, arboreal or burrowing. Tiny species of Thorius in Mexico rank among the smallest known tetrapods.

Conservation

Many plethodontids have extremely small ranges in montane forests and are threatened by habitat loss, climate change and the fungal disease chytridiomycosis, which has devastated amphibian populations in Central and South America.
