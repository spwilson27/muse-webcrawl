Concert film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A concert film or concert movie is a film that documents a live music performance — typically a rock or pop concert — before an audience, or presents a staged performance in a studio setting. Concert films range from straightforward records of a single show to highly edited works mixing performance, backstage footage, and interviews, and they form a recognised subgenre of the music documentary.

Notable examples

  • Jazz on a Summer's Day (1960) — the 1958 Newport Jazz Festival, a landmark of the genre.
  • Monterey Pop (1968), directed by D. A. Pennebaker, and Woodstock (1970), which won the Academy Award for Best Documentary.
  • Gimme Shelter (1970) — the Rolling Stones' 1969 US tour and the Altamont disaster.
  • The Last Waltz (1978) — Martin Scorsese's film of The Band's farewell concert, often called the greatest concert film.
  • Stop Making Sense (1984) — Jonathan Demme's film of Talking Heads, widely acclaimed for its staging and editing.

Recent era

High-definition multi-camera production made concert films a routine part of album and tour releases, distributed on DVD/Blu-ray, streaming platforms, and as theatrical event cinema. Recent blockbuster examples include Beyoncé's Homecoming (2019), Taylor Swift: The Eras Tour (2023) — one of the highest-grossing concert films ever — and Renaissance: A Film by Beyoncé (2023).

Style and production

Concert films are shot with many cameras covering stage, audience, and detail angles; sound is recorded on multitrack and mixed afterwards, often with studio overdubs correcting or enhancing performances (a longstanding and debated practice). Directors work to convey the energy of a live event within a fixed frame, balancing wide spectacle with intimate close-ups.

Distinctions

A concert film differs from a music documentary (which surveys a career or scene) and from a filmed stage musical or an opera broadcast, although boundaries blur in hybrid works.
