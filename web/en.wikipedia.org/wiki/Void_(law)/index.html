Void (law)

Void (from the Old French voide, meaning empty) is a legal concept meaning null, of no value or legal effect. An act, document, or transaction that is void is treated in law as though it had never existed or had never been completed. A void contract, for example, is not merely "cancelled" — it is regarded as if it had never been formed at all.

Void and voidable

The distinction between something that is void and something that is merely voidable is fundamental in contract law and administrative law. A void agreement is a complete nullity from the outset (void ab initio): it confers no rights and imposes no obligations, and generally cannot be ratified or enforced by either party. A voidable agreement, by contrast, is valid until the party entitled to avoid it elects to set it aside; until that election is made, the transaction may be fully effective.

Typical grounds on which a contract or legal act may be void include illegality, lack of legal capacity, impossibility of performance, and certain fundamental mistakes. Statutes frequently declare specified transactions void, for instance agreements in restraint of marriage or, in some jurisdictions, usurious contracts.

Void judgments and orders

In procedural law, a judgment or court order may be void where the court lacked jurisdiction over the subject matter or the parties, or where the judgment was entered in violation of due process — for example without proper service of process. A void judgment is vulnerable to attack at any time and in any court, unlike a merely erroneous (voidable) judgment, which must be challenged on appeal within prescribed time limits.

Void marriages

Many jurisdictions distinguish void marriages, which are treated as never having existed (such as bigamous marriages or marriages within prohibited degrees of relationship), from voidable marriages, which are valid until annulled by decree. The distinction affects property rights, legitimacy of children, and the need for formal annulment proceedings.
