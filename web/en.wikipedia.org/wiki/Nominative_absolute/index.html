Nominative absolute

The nominative absolute is a grammatical construction in English and other languages consisting of a noun phrase in the nominative case joined with a participle (and often other modifiers), standing apart from — "absolute" from, i.e. absolved of — the grammatical structure of the main clause. It modifies the whole sentence, typically providing background information such as cause, time, or attendant circumstances.

Examples

In the sentence "The weather being fine, we went for a walk", the phrase "the weather being fine" is a nominative absolute: "weather" is a nominative noun followed by the present participle "being". Other examples:

  • "The meeting over, the delegates left the hall." (past participle implied "being" omitted)
  • "His hands trembling, he opened the letter."
  • "All things considered, the plan worked well."

Related constructions

Similar absolute constructions occur in many Indo-European languages: the ablative absolute in Latin, the genitive absolute in Ancient Greek, and the locative absolute in Sanskrit. English's nominative absolute corresponds functionally to these, using the nominative rather than an oblique case.

A common error related to the nominative absolute is the dangling modifier, in which a participial phrase lacking its own subject is attached to a main clause whose subject it does not logically modify ("Walking to the station, the rain started"). In a proper absolute construction, the participle has its own explicit subject, so no dangling occurs.

Use

The construction is most common in formal and literary English. Some fixed expressions survive in everyday usage: "weather permitting", "all things considered", "barring accidents", and "God willing".
