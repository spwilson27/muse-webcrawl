Saenghwang

ClassificationFree-reed mouth organ (aerophone)
OriginKorea (derived from the Chinese sheng)
Pipes17 bamboo pipes
Related instrumentsSheng (China), shō (Japan), khaen (Laos/Thailand)

The saenghwang (Korean: 생황) is a Korean free-reed mouth organ. It consists of 17 bamboo pipes fitted with metal free reeds, set vertically into a wind chest traditionally made from a gourd (today often of wood or metal). The player blows through a mouthpiece and stops holes in the pipes with the fingers to sound two- or three-note chords, which gives the instrument a prominent harmonic role in ensembles.

History

The saenghwang derives from the Chinese sheng, which reached Korea during the Goryeo period; in Korea it was classified among the hyangak instruments in court music. Unlike its Japanese relative, the shō, which is used mainly for sustained chords, the saenghwang historically also played melodic lines in small chamber ensembles. Paintings and treatises from the Joseon dynasty, including the Akhak gwebeom (1493), document its early form.

Construction and playing

Each bamboo pipe contains a flexible metal tongue (reed). Air blown or drawn through the wind chest vibrates a pipe's reed only when the finger hole in that pipe is closed. Most pipes sound single notes, but some produce notes both on blowing and drawing. Because chords of two or three pipes can be sounded together, the saenghwang was traditionally paired with instruments such as the danso or yanggeum in small wind ensembles, and it is also used in court music such as yeominrak.

Modern use

By the 20th century the saenghwang had become rare, but it has been revived by Korean traditional performers and is now also used in contemporary and crossover compositions. It remains one of a small number of Korean instruments capable of playing harmony.
