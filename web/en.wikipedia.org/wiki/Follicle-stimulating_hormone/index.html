Follicle-stimulating hormone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) is a gonadotropin produced by the gonadotropic cells of the anterior pituitary gland. Together with luteinizing hormone (LH), it regulates the function of the ovaries and testes and is essential for puberty, fertility and the menstrual cycle.

Structure and regulation

FSH is a glycoprotein heterodimer: an alpha subunit shared with LH, TSH and hCG, and a unique beta subunit that confers its specific activity. Its release is stimulated by gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus and modulated by feedback from sex steroids and inhibin B. Secretion is pulsatile and varies across the menstrual cycle, rising in the early follicular phase and peaking, with LH, just before ovulation.

Function

In women, FSH stimulates the growth and maturation of ovarian follicles and the production of oestrogen by granulosa cells. In men, it acts on Sertoli cells in the testes to support spermatogenesis. FSH levels rise after the menopause, when ovarian feedback is lost, and are low in disorders of the hypothalamus or pituitary.

Clinical use

Measuring blood FSH helps evaluate infertility, menstrual disorders, precocious or delayed puberty, and menopause. Purified or recombinant FSH (follitropin) is widely used in assisted reproduction to stimulate controlled ovarian hyperstimulation, and in some men to treat hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.
