Spacetime interval

In physics, the spacetime interval is the invariant distance between two events in spacetime. It is a central concept of the theory of relativity: unlike spatial distance or time difference taken separately, the interval between two events is the same for all inertial observers.

Definition

For two events separated by a time difference Δt and spatial differences Δx, Δy, Δz, the squared interval in flat (Minkowski) spacetime is

Δs² = −c²Δt² + Δx² + Δy² + Δz²

(using the −+++ metric signature; the opposite convention is also common). Here c is the speed of light.

Classification of intervals

Depending on its sign, an interval is classified as timelike (the events can be causally connected and a reference frame exists in which they occur at the same place), spacelike (no signal can connect them, and a frame exists in which they are simultaneous), or lightlike or null (connected only by a light signal). This classification underlies the light-cone structure of causality in relativity.

Proper time

For a timelike interval, the square root of the interval (divided by c, with sign conventions) gives the proper time experienced by an observer travelling between the two events. The invariance of the interval explains effects such as time dilation and length contraction as different observers decompose the same interval into space and time parts differently.
