Rodolphe Töpffer (31 January 1799 – 8 June 1846) was a Swiss teacher, author, painter and cartoonist. He is widely regarded as the father of the comic strip: his satirical picture-stories (histoires en estampes), created from 1827 onward, combined sequential drawings with handwritten captions in a form that anticipated the modern comic, and he theorised the medium in his Essai de physiognomonie (1845).

Life

Töpffer was born in Geneva, the son of the painter Wolfgang Adam Töpffer. An eye ailment prevented him from following his father into fine art; instead he studied in Paris and became a schoolmaster, founding a boarding school in Geneva in 1824. For the amusement of his pupils he drew whimsical "picture stories" — sequences of lively, scratchy drawings, each with a line or two of ironic caption beneath. Encouraged by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, who saw and praised some of the stories, Töpffer published them from 1837 using autolithography.

He later became professor of rhetoric and belles-lettres at the Academy of Geneva and wrote travel narratives (the Voyages en zigzag, accounts of walking tours with his schoolboys), short stories, and art criticism, including early appreciations of Dutch landscape painting.

Picture stories

Töpffer's albums include Histoire de M. Jabot (1833), Monsieur Crépin (1837), Les Amours de Monsieur Vieux Bois (1837; published in the United States in 1842 as The Adventures of Mr. Obadiah Oldbuck — often called the first comic book published in America), Monsieur Pencil (1840) and Histoire d'Albert (1845). Their heroes blunder through chains of escalating absurdities; Töpffer's loose, improvisational line and mastery of visual slapstick and timing were revolutionary. In the Essai de physiognomonie he argued that the picture-story was a distinct art — a mixed form in which drawings and text each contribute what the other cannot — an analysis still quoted by comics theorists such as Scott McCloud.

Legacy

Töpffer's influence ran through Wilhelm Busch's picture stories in Germany to the European bande dessinée tradition and, through pirated editions like Obadiah Oldbuck, to early American comics. Comics historians regard him as the first comics artist to build full narratives from sequential panels.
