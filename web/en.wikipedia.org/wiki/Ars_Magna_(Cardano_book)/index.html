The Ars Magna (The Great Art), full title Artis Magnae, Sive de Regulis Algebraicis Liber Unus ("Book One on the Great Art, or the Rules of Algebra"), is a mathematical treatise by the Italian Renaissance scholar Gerolamo Cardano, published in 1545. It is famous as the first printed work to give general solutions of cubic and quartic equations.

Contents

The book presents methods for solving all cases of the cubic equation, extending the solution for the depressed cubic first found by Scipione del Ferro and Niccolò Tartaglia, and the solution of the quartic equation discovered by Cardano's student Lodovico Ferrari. In working through cases where the cubic formula produced square roots of negative numbers, Cardano encountered what we now call complex numbers, remarking on their strangeness. The treatise also contains early work on systems of equations and the binomial expansion.

The Tartaglia controversy

Tartaglia had disclosed his cubic method to Cardano in 1539 under an oath of secrecy. When Cardano learned that del Ferro had found the method earlier, he argued the oath no longer bound him and published in 1545, crediting both men. Tartaglia responded with fury; the ensuing feud culminated in a public mathematical contest between Tartaglia and Ferrari in Milan in 1548, which Ferrari won.

Significance

Historian of mathematics Carl Boyer called the Ars Magna the greatest scientific treatise of the early 16th century; it marks the beginning of modern algebra and stimulated the work of Bombelli, Viète and others.
