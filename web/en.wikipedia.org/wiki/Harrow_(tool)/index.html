Harrow (tool)

In agriculture, a harrow is a farm implement used for surface tillage. It is used after ploughing for the purpose of breaking up and smoothing out the surface of the soil. The purpose of harrowing is generally to break up clods (lumps of soil) and to provide a finer finish, a good tilth or soil structure that is suitable for seeding and planting operations.

Uses

Harrowing is a shallower operation than ploughing and is usually performed as part of seedbed preparation. Coarse harrowing may be used to remove weeds and to cover seeds after sowing; finer harrowing levels the surface and breaks crusts. Harrowing of a roughly ploughed field is sometimes called "dragging", and the implement a "drag". In modern mechanised farming, harrows are pulled by tractors, and in some systems several implements are combined so that ploughing, harrowing, and seeding occur in fewer passes to reduce soil compaction and cost.

Types

  • Disc harrows use concave steel discs set at an angle to roll through the soil, cutting and turning it; they include heavy disc harrows used as primary tillage tools in some systems.
  • Tine harrows (including spring-tooth and spike-tooth harrows) use many flexible or rigid teeth that rake the surface.
  • Chain harrows consist of a heavy mat of linked steel, used for levelling, spreading manure, and pasture maintenance.
  • Power harrows have vertically rotating tines driven by the tractor's power take-off and produce a fine seedbed without inverting the soil.
  • Roller harrows and combination implements incorporate rollers that firm the seedbed.

History

Harrows have been used since ancient times; early forms were little more than dragged branches or wooden frames fitted with pegs. Animal-drawn iron harrows became common in the medieval and early modern periods, and the industrial era introduced steel disc and spring-tooth designs. Today harrowing is often integrated into reduced-tillage and conservation agriculture systems.
