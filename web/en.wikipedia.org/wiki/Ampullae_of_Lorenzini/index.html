Ampullae of Lorenzini

Ampullae of Lorenzini
SystemSensory (electroreception)
Found inCartilaginous fishes (sharks, rays, chimaeras)
Described byStefano Lorenzini (1678)
FunctionDetection of electric fields; possibly temperature and geomagnetic sensing

The ampullae of Lorenzini are electroreceptor organs found in cartilaginous fishes — sharks, skates, rays, and chimaeras — that allow them to detect minute electric fields in the water. They appear as small pores scattered over the head and underside of the snout, each pore leading through a jelly-filled canal (ranging from a few millimetres to tens of centimetres long) to a bulb-shaped sac, the ampulla, lined with sensory cells connected to nerves.

Function

The canals are filled with a mucopolysaccharide gel with electrical properties similar to a semiconductor, and the system can detect voltage gradients as small as a few nanovolts per centimetre — sensitive enough to pick up the bioelectric fields of prey buried in sand, even when no other sense can detect them. Experiments in the 1960s–70s, notably by Adrianus Kalmijn, established the organs' role in prey detection and suggested they may also help sharks navigate using Earth's magnetic field. The organs are named for the Italian physician and ichthyologist Stefano Lorenzini, who described them in 1678, though their function remained unknown until the 20th century.

Distribution

Similar electroreceptors occur in some bony fishes, lungfish, and even monotreme mammals (the platypus), but the ampullae of Lorenzini proper are characteristic of elasmobranchs and other chondrichthyans.
