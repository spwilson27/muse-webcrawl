Baháʼu'lláh

Baháʼu'lláh (born Mírzá Ḥusayn ʿAlí Núrí, 12 November 1817 – 29 May 1892) was the founder of the Baháʼí Faith. A follower of the Báb, he claimed in 1863 to be the figure foretold by the Báb, "He whom God shall make manifest," and a Manifestation of God in his own right. His teachings centre on the oneness of humanity, the unity of religion, and the need for a global civilization; his writings, including the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (Most Holy Book) and the Kitáb-i-Íqán (Book of Certitude), form the core scripture of the Baháʼí Faith.

Life and exiles

Born in Tehran to a noble family in government service, Baháʼu'lláh became a prominent Bábí and survived the persecutions and massacres that followed the Báb's execution; his property was confiscated and he was imprisoned in the subterranean Síyáh-Chál dungeon in 1852, where Baháʼís believe he received the first intimations of his revelation. He was exiled successively to Baghdad, where in 1863 he declared his claim in the Garden of Ridván; to Constantinople and Adrianople (Edirne); and finally, in 1868, to the prison-city of Acre (Akko) in Ottoman Palestine, where he spent the rest of his life, eventually living at the Mansion of Bahjí.

Teachings and legacy

From exile, Baháʼu'lláh addressed letters to the rulers of the world — including Queen Victoria, Napoleon III, Kaiser Wilhelm I, Tsar Alexander II, and Pope Pius IX — calling them to justice and collective security. He abolished holy war, ordained a system of elected councils (the precursor of the Baháʼí Administrative Order), and prescribed institutions culminating in the Universal House of Justice. He died in 1892 at Bahjí, where his shrine, the faith's holiest spot and point of prayer (Qiblih), stands today. His son ʿAbdu'l-Bahá succeeded him as head of the faith by appointment in his will.
