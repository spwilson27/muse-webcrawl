Chi-squared test

Chi-squared distributions for different degrees of freedom

A chi-squared test (also chi-square or χ² test) is a statistical hypothesis test used in the analysis of contingency tables when the sample sizes are large. In simpler terms, this test is primarily used to examine whether two categorical variables are independent in influencing the test statistic. The test was first investigated by Karl Pearson in 1900.

Definition

The chi-squared statistic is computed as

χ² = Σ (Oi − Ei)² / Ei

where Oi are the observed frequencies and Ei the expected frequencies under the null hypothesis. Under the null hypothesis, the statistic approximately follows a chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom determined by the dimensions of the table.

Common applications

  • Pearson's chi-squared test of independence — tests whether two categorical variables are independent.
  • Goodness-of-fit test — tests whether an observed frequency distribution differs from a theoretical one.
  • Test for homogeneity — tests whether different populations have the same distribution of a categorical variable.

Assumptions

The test assumes random sampling and adequate sample size; a common rule of thumb is that expected frequencies should be at least 5 in most cells. For small samples, alternatives such as Fisher's exact test are preferred.
