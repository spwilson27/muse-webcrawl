Motorola 68000 series

The Motorola 68000 series (also known as the 680x0 family, m68k, or 68k) is a family of 32-bit complex instruction set computer (CISC) microprocessors from Motorola (later Freescale and NXP). Introduced with the Motorola 68000 in 1979, the architecture is noted for its regular, orthogonal instruction set, eight 32-bit data registers and eight address registers, and a flat 24-bit (later 32-bit) address space — features that made it a favourite of programmers and a dominant processor in workstations, personal computers, arcade machines, and embedded systems from the mid-1980s into the 1990s.

Members

  • 68000 (1979) — 16-bit external data bus, 24-bit addressing; powered the original Apple Macintosh, Amiga, Atari ST, Sega Genesis/Mega Drive, and many Unix workstations.
  • 68010 (1982) — minor improvements, support for virtual memory.
  • 68020 (1984) — full 32-bit buses, instruction cache, coprocessor support; used in the Macintosh II and Amiga 1200's predecessors and Sun-3 workstations.
  • 68030 (1987) — on-chip memory management unit and data cache.
  • 68040 (1990) — integrated floating-point unit, larger caches, over a million transistors.
  • 68060 (1994) — superscalar, the last general-purpose member of the line.

Cost-reduced and embedded derivatives (68008, 68EC020, the 683xx microcontrollers, CPU32, and the DragonBall processors of early Palm handhelds) sold in enormous numbers.

Design

The 68000 programming model treats registers as genuinely 32-bit while maintaining a compact, memory-to-memory instruction encoding with rich addressing modes. Programs were not segmented in the manner of contemporary x86 processors, and the clean architecture made the family a common teaching example. The instruction set was extended in later generations with full IEEE floating-point support in the 68881/68882 coprocessors and the integrated FPU of the 68040.

Decline and legacy

In the 1990s, Apple, Sun, and others migrated to RISC architectures (notably PowerPC, developed partly by Motorola itself), and the 68000 line lost the desktop and workstation markets. The architecture survives in the ColdFire embedded family and in long-lived industrial, avionics, and arcade applications; its software lives on in retro-computing and emulation communities centred on the Amiga, Atari ST, and classic Macintosh.
