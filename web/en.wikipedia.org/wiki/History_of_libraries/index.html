The history of libraries begins with the first archives of clay tablets in Mesopotamia and runs through the great Library of Alexandria, monastic scriptoria, the chained libraries of medieval universities, the national and public libraries of the modern age, and today's digital collections.

From clay tablets to cloud storage, libraries have preserved recorded knowledge for over four thousand years.

Ancient libraries

The earliest libraries were archives of cuneiform tablets; the royal library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BC) held some 30,000 tablets, including the Epic of Gilgamesh. In Egypt, temple and palace collections were kept on papyrus. The Greeks and Romans maintained both private and public libraries: the Library of Alexandria, founded under the Ptolemies in the 3rd century BC, was the ancient world's greatest centre of scholarship, and its catalogue, the Pinakes of Callimachus, pioneered bibliography. Rome gained public libraries from the time of Augustus, housed in temples and baths.

Medieval and Islamic libraries

In Europe, after Rome's fall, monasteries preserved classical and Christian texts in scriptoria; books were valuable manuscripts, often chained in institutional collections. In the Islamic world, libraries flourished: the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, mosque and hospital libraries, and the great collections of Córdoba made books widely available, with catalogues and lending. In China, imperial libraries and the spread of woodblock printing expanded collections from the Tang dynasty onward.

Renaissance to the 19th century

Printing with movable type (from the 1440s) multiplied books and libraries: the Vatican Library, Bodleian Library (1602) and the royal collections that became national libraries. The Enlightenment brought subscription and circulating libraries. The 19th century saw the public library movement: Britain's Public Libraries Act of 1850 and, in the United States, philanthropist Andrew Carnegie's funding of more than 2,500 libraries. Melvil Dewey's Decimal Classification (1876) and the founding of library schools professionalised librarianship.

20th century and digital age

National libraries such as the Library of Congress and the British Library (separated from the British Museum in 1973) grew into vast research institutions. Card catalogues gave way to computerised OPACs; the MARC format standardised records. From the 1990s the internet, digitisation projects such as Google Books, and open repositories transformed libraries into hybrid physical-digital services, while public libraries expanded into community hubs offering internet access and programming.
