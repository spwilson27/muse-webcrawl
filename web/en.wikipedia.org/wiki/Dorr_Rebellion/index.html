Dorr Rebellion

Date1841–1843
LocationRhode Island, United States
LeaderThomas Wilson Dorr
ResultRebellion suppressed; new constitution adopted in 1843 expanding suffrage

The Dorr Rebellion (1841–1843) was an attempt by residents of Rhode Island to force broader democracy in the state. Rhode Island in the 1840s still governed itself under its colonial charter of 1663, which restricted voting to landowners and their eldest sons, disenfranchising a majority of adult white men in the growing industrial towns. Led by lawyer Thomas Wilson Dorr, reformers held an extralegal convention, drafted a "People's Constitution," and elected a rival state government with Dorr as governor in 1842.

Thomas Wilson Dorr, leader of the rebellion, was convicted of treason against the state but later pardoned.

Course of events

Two governments — Dorr's and the charter government of Governor Samuel Ward King — claimed legitimacy in the spring of 1842. President John Tyler recognised the charter government and threatened federal intervention. In May 1842 Dorr's supporters attempted to seize the state arsenal in Providence; their cannon misfired and the attack collapsed. Martial law was declared, Dorr fled the state, and the rebellion dissolved, though a last small armed gathering at Chepachet in June dispersed quickly.

Aftermath

Though the rebellion failed militarily, it largely achieved its aims: a new state constitution adopted in 1843 extended the vote to most native-born adult men regardless of property. Dorr was convicted of treason in 1844 and sentenced to life imprisonment at hard labour, but public sympathy led to his release in 1845 and the restoration of his civil rights in 1854, the year of his death. The episode prompted the U.S. Supreme Court case Luther v. Borden (1849), which established the political question doctrine regarding which state government was legitimate.
