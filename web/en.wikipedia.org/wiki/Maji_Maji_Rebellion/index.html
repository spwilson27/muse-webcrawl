The Maji Maji Rebellion (German: Maji-Maji-Aufstand) was an armed rebellion of Africans against German colonial rule in German East Africa (modern-day Tanzania). The war was triggered by German colonial policies designed to force the indigenous population to grow cotton for export, and it was one of the largest colonial wars in Africa.

Background

German administration of the colony relied on coercive cotton schemes, head taxes payable in labour, and local agents (akidas) whose abuses bred deep resentment. In July 1905 the Matumbi people of the Nandete area refused to work on the cotton plantations, and the revolt spread rapidly across the south of the colony.

The spirit medium and "maji"

The rebellion drew unity and courage from the spirit medium Kinjikitile Ngwale, who distributed sacred water — maji in Swahili — said to turn German bullets into water. Fighters from many ethnic groups, traditionally divided, joined under the medicine's promise. Kinjikitile was captured and hanged in August 1905, but the rising continued under new leaders.

Course and suppression

Rebels attacked German posts, missions and plantations; the attack on Mahenge in August 1905 failed with heavy losses. Germany reinforced its Schutztruppe with askari troops and naval infantry. German forces conducted a scorched-earth campaign, burning villages and crops, which caused a catastrophic famine (remembered as the Great Hunger). By 1907 the rebellion was crushed.

Consequences

Estimates of African deaths range from 75,000 to 300,000, most caused by famine. The rebellion led to reforms in German colonial administration under Bernhard Dernburg, and in Tanzania it is remembered as an early, unifying chapter of national resistance — a forerunner of the later independence movement.
