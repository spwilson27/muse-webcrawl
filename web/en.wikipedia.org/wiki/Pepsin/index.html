Pepsin is an endopeptidase that breaks down proteins into smaller peptides and is produced in the gastric chief cells of the stomach lining. It is one of the main digestive enzymes in the digestive systems of humans and many other animals, where it helps digest the proteins in food. Pepsin is an aspartic protease, using a catalytic aspartate in its active site.

It is one of three principal proteases in the human digestive system, the other two being chymotrypsin and trypsin, which act in the intestine. Pepsin was one of the first enzymes to be discovered, identified in 1836 by Theodor Schwann, and in 1930 John Northrop showed that it could be crystallised — work that helped establish that enzymes are proteins.

Production and activation

Chief cells secrete pepsin as an inactive zymogen, pepsinogen, whose extra N-terminal segment blocks the active site and prevents the enzyme from digesting the cells that make it. Hydrochloric acid secreted by parietal cells lowers the gastric pH, unfolding pepsinogen and triggering autocatalytic cleavage to active pepsin; the active enzyme then activates more pepsinogen. Pepsin works best in the strongly acidic environment of the stomach (pH 1.5–2.5) and is irreversibly denatured when the pH rises above about 6.5, as occurs when chyme enters the duodenum.

Function and specificity

Pepsin cleaves peptide bonds preferentially adjacent to hydrophobic and aromatic amino acids such as phenylalanine, tryptophan and tyrosine, chopping dietary proteins into peptides that intestinal proteases can finish reducing to amino acids. Its activity accounts for roughly 10–20% of total protein digestion.

Uses

Commercial pepsin, traditionally extracted from porcine stomach lining, has been used in digestive remedies, in the manufacture of foods, and historically in the drink Pepsin-containing products, notably as an early ingredient and namesake of Pepsi-Cola. In the laboratory pepsin is used in antibody fragmentation (producing F(ab')2 fragments) and in assays of protein digestibility.
