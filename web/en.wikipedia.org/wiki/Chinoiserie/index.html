Chinoiserie

A European porcelain vase in chinoiserie style

Chinoiserie (from the French chinois, "Chinese") is the European interpretation and imitation of Chinese and other East Asian artistic traditions, especially in the decorative arts, garden design, architecture, literature and music. It flourished from the late 17th century through the 18th century, and revived in various forms later.

European fascination with East Asia was fueled by trade goods — porcelain, silk, lacquer and tea — arriving via the Dutch and British East India Companies. Because few Europeans had seen China, chinoiserie blended Chinese, Japanese, Indian and entirely imaginary motifs: pagodas, dragons, phoenixes, mandarin figures, latticed railings and fantastical landscapes into a playful fantasy of "Cathay."

Applications

Chinoiserie appeared in Meissen and Sèvres porcelain, japanned furniture, silk and wallpaper, silverware, and in whole rooms such as the Chinese House at Sanssouci and the Chinese Pavilion at Drottningholm. In architecture it inspired pagodas such as the one at Kew Gardens (1762), and elements of the English landscape garden — winding paths, irregular lakes, borrowed views — drew on descriptions of Chinese gardens. The style peaked with the Rococo, fell from favour under Neoclassicism, and returned in revived forms in the 19th and 20th centuries, notably in the Brighton Pavilion's interiors and later in Hollywood Regency design.
