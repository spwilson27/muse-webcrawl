The Oseberg style is the initial phase of Viking Age art, attested from about 775 to 875 AD, and named after the richly decorated grave goods of the Oseberg ship burial near Tønsberg in Vestfold, Norway, where the ship was buried in 834. Together with the Borre style, with which it overlaps and is often combined in the "Borre–Oseberg" grouping, it forms the earliest of the generally recognised phases of Viking art, preceding the Jelling, Mammen, Ringerike and Urnes styles.

Characteristics

The hallmark motif of the Oseberg style is the "gripping beast": a plump, sinuous animal, often with a lappet (a backward-swept lock) on its head, whose paws literally grip the borders of the design field, its own body, or neighbouring beasts. The carving of the Oseberg finds — the ship itself, sledges, a cart, bedposts and five carved head posts — displays a new repertoire also including sinuous interlace and animals organised in carpet-like patterns. Scholars have noted influences from both Insular (Anglo-Saxon and Celtic) art and Carolingian art of the Continent, fused into a distinctly Scandinavian idiom.

The Oseberg burial

The ship burial, excavated in 1904, contained the remains of two women and an extraordinary assemblage of textiles, wooden objects and household goods. The five animal-head posts, carved in differing grades of the style, are interpreted as having ritual significance, and the carvings are attributed to several hands, including a conservative "academic" master and a more innovative carver sometimes called the "Baroque" master.
