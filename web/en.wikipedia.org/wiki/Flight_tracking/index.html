Flight tracking

Data sourcesADS-B, multilateration, radar feeds, airline data
Major servicesFlightradar24, FlightAware

Flight tracking is the real-time monitoring of aircraft positions, made publicly available through websites and apps that display live air traffic on a map. It combines official air navigation data with signals broadcast by the aircraft themselves, and is used by travellers, plane spotters, airlines, journalists and researchers.

Technology

The backbone of modern tracking is ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast): aircraft continuously broadcast their GPS-derived position, altitude, speed and identity on 1090 MHz. Networks of volunteer-operated ground receivers — as pioneered by Flightradar24, founded in Sweden in 2006 — collect these signals and feed them to central servers. Where ADS-B coverage is absent, positions are estimated by multilateration of transponder signals (MLAT) or taken from air navigation service provider radar feeds, sometimes subject to a five-minute delay, as with the FAA data distributed by FlightAware. Satellite-based ADS-B reception, introduced commercially in the late 2010s, extended coverage over oceans and polar regions where no ground receivers exist.

Uses and controversies

Tracking services verify flight status, investigate accidents and diversions, and monitor emissions and noise; during events such as the disappearance of Malaysia Airlines Flight 370 and the 2022 tracking of sanctioned oligarchs' jets, they became significant open-source intelligence tools. Public tracking of private jets has prompted privacy debates and programmes such as the FAA's LADD, which allows owners to limit the display of their aircraft data on some services — though raw ADS-B broadcasts remain receivable by anyone.
