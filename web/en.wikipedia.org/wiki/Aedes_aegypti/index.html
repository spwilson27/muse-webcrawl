Aedes aegypti, commonly known as the yellow fever mosquito, is a mosquito that can spread dengue fever, chikungunya, Zika fever, Mayaro and yellow fever viruses, and other disease agents. The mosquito can be recognized by black and white markings on its legs and a marking in the form of a lyre on the upper surface of its thorax. This mosquito originated in Africa, but is now found in tropical, subtropical and temperate regions throughout the world.

Biology

Aedes aegypti is a small, dark mosquito approximately 4 to 7 millimetres long. Females require blood meals to produce eggs and bite preferentially during the daytime, with peak activity in the early morning and late afternoon. The species is strongly associated with human habitation: it breeds in small collections of standing water such as discarded tyres, flowerpots, water-storage jars and blocked gutters. Eggs can survive desiccation for months and hatch when flooded.

The life cycle from egg to adult takes roughly one to two weeks depending on temperature. Adults typically remain within a few hundred metres of their breeding site, which makes the mosquito a poor natural disperser but an efficient household-level vector.

Role as a vector

The species is the primary vector of dengue, the most common arboviral disease of humans, and was central to historic urban epidemics of yellow fever in the Americas. In 2015–2016 it transmitted the Zika virus during the epidemic in the Americas, which was linked to congenital Zika syndrome including microcephaly. Control strategies include source reduction, larviciding, insecticide spraying, and newer approaches such as releasing mosquitoes infected with the bacterium Wolbachia or genetically modified males.

Distribution and history

Native to Africa, A. aegypti spread to the Americas during the era of the transatlantic slave trade, travelling in water barrels aboard ships, and later to Asia and the Pacific through trade. Mid-20th-century eradication campaigns in the Americas, led by the Pan American Health Organization, eliminated it from many countries, but the species subsequently reinfested most of its former range.

Genomics

The genome of Aedes aegypti was sequenced in 2007; at roughly 1.38 billion base pairs it is about five times the size of the genome of Drosophila melanogaster, largely because of repetitive elements. The species has become a model organism for the study of mosquito genetics and vector biology.
