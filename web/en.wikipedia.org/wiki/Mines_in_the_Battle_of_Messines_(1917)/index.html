The mines in the Battle of Messines were a series of underground explosive charges placed beneath German front-line positions along the Messines–Wytschaete ridge in Flanders, Belgium, and detonated at 03:10 on 7 June 1917 to open the Battle of Messines. Dug by Royal Engineer tunnelling companies — many of them civilian miners, colliers and "clay-kickers" — together with Canadian, Australian and New Zealand tunnellers, the mines were among the largest non-nuclear explosions ever made: 19 mines containing about 455 tonnes of ammonal exploded almost simultaneously, killing an estimated 10,000 German soldiers and utterly destroying the ridge defences.

The Spanbroekmolen crater, today preserved as the Pool of Peace.

Background

The Messines ridge, held by the Germans since 1914, overlooked the Ypres Salient from the south. General Herbert Plumer's Second Army planned to seize the ridge as a preliminary to the Third Battle of Ypres. Tunnelling began as early as 1915–1916; galleries were driven at depths of up to 30 metres into the blue clay to escape German counter-mining, in an underground war fought with listening devices, camouflets and flooding. The largest mine, at St Eloi, contained 43,400 kg of ammonal; the Spanbroekmolen mine held 41,000 kg at a depth of 27 metres.

Explosion

At zero hour, after the artillery fell silent, the mines were fired. Plumer remarked to his staff the evening before: "Gentlemen, we may not make history tomorrow, but we shall certainly change the geography." The explosions were reportedly heard in London and Dublin, and the ridge was taken within hours. Two charged mines were not fired: one at La Petite Douve Farm (its gallery had been damaged) and one near Ploegsteert.

Unexploded mines

The abandoned mines remained buried after the war. In 1955 a lightning strike detonated one near Ploegsteert, leaving a crater but no casualties. At least one fully charged mine — containing some 22,000 kg of explosive — is still believed to lie beneath La Petite Douve Farm, its exact position uncertain; it is sometimes described as one of the largest unexploded landmines in the world. Several craters survive as ponds and memorials, including the Pool of Peace at Spanbroekmolen and the Caterpillar and Hill 60 craters.
