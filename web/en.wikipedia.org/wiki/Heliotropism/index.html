Heliotropism

Heliotropism, a form of tropism, is the directional growth or movement of a plant in response to sunlight. The term derives from the Greek helios (sun) and tropos (turn). Heliotropic plants track the motion of the sun across the sky from east to west; the best-known examples are young sunflowers, whose flower heads follow the sun during the day and reset toward the east at night.

Sunflower heads orienting toward the sun

Mechanism

In heliotropic flowers, specialised motor cells in the pulvinar or stem alter turgor in response to signals from light-sensitive photoreceptor proteins called phototropins. Tracking is also governed by the plant's internal circadian clock, so that movements continue with a roughly 24-hour rhythm even under constant light. Research on sunflowers has shown that differential growth of opposite sides of the stem — promoted by the hormone auxin and by circadian regulation of growth — produces the daily east-to-west motion and the night-time re-orientation toward the east.

Adaptive value

Sun tracking increases light interception for photosynthesis, warms the flower, which can attract pollinators and speed seed development, and may discourage insect herbivores. Solar-tracking behaviour is found in many plant families, including species of the Arctic and alpine environments where flowers act as small solar furnaces that attract pollinating insects.
