Commutative algebra

Commutative algebra, first known as ideal theory, is the branch of algebra that studies commutative rings, their ideals, and modules over such rings. Both algebraic geometry and algebraic number theory build on commutative algebra. Prominent examples of commutative rings are polynomial rings; rings of algebraic integers, including the ordinary integers; and p-adic integers.

Overview

The subject grew out of the work of Richard Dedekind and others on ideals in rings of algebraic integers, created to salvage unique factorization after Ernst Kummer's introduction of ideal numbers. Emmy Noether laid the modern foundations in the 1920s, proving the ascending chain condition for ideals and the primary decomposition theorem (the Lasker–Noether theorem). David Hilbert's basis theorem and Nullstellensatz connected commutative algebra to the geometry of algebraic varieties.

Main concepts

Central notions include prime and maximal ideals, localization, Noetherian and Artinian rings, integral extensions, dimension theory (the Krull dimension), completions, and the spectra of rings (Spec R), which form the basis of the modern, scheme-theoretic approach to algebraic geometry developed by Alexander Grothendieck.

Importance

Because polynomial rings and their quotients correspond to algebraic varieties, commutative algebra supplies the local algebraic tools of algebraic geometry; because rings of integers in number fields are Dedekind domains, it is equally fundamental to algebraic number theory. Classic texts include Atiyah and Macdonald's Introduction to Commutative Algebra and Matsumura's Commutative Ring Theory.
