County of Foix

The County of Foix (French: Comté de Foix) was a medieval fief in the south of France, in the foothills of the Pyrenees, roughly corresponding to the modern department of Ariège in the region of Occitanie. Its capital was the town of Foix, dominated by the counts' castle, the Château de Foix. The counts of Foix were among the most powerful feudal lords of the Languedoc and played a prominent part in the politics of southern France, Aragon, and Navarre.

Origins

The county emerged in the early eleventh century when Roger, a younger son of Roger I of Carcassonne, inherited lands around Foix and founded the comital dynasty. Through marriage, inheritance, and military success, his descendants accumulated further territories, including the viscounties of Béarn, Marsan, and Gabardan, and lands in Catalonia.

The Albigensian Crusade

During the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), Count Raymond-Roger of Foix was a leading defender of the southern nobility against the crusaders led by Simon de Montfort. Although the county sheltered many Cathars, the counts negotiated a settlement that allowed them to retain most of their lands, and Foix survived as a largely autonomous principality.

Later history

In the fourteenth century, Gaston III, Count of Foix, known as Gaston Fébus, made the court of Foix and Béarn a celebrated centre of chivalric culture; his treatise on hunting, the Livre de chasse, remains famous. The counts also acquired the crown of Navarre through marriage in the fifteenth century. When Henry of Navarre became King Henry IV of France in 1589, the Foix inheritance passed to the French crown, and the county was formally incorporated into the royal domain in 1607. The title of Count of Foix was retained in the styles of later French monarchs.
