Rule of Saint Benedict

A Benedictine cloister; the Rule ordered monastic life for centuries

The Rule of Saint Benedict (Latin: Regula Sancti Benedicti) is a book of precepts written in Latin c. 530 by St Benedict of Nursia for monks living communally under the authority of an abbot. Comprising a prologue and 73 chapters, it became the most influential rule of Western monasticism and remains the guide of Benedictine, Cistercian, and Trappist communities today.

Content

The Rule balances prayer, work, and reading in a daily round — summed up in the motto ora et labora ("pray and work"). It prescribes the Liturgy of the Hours, meals in common with reading aloud, silence, manual labour, care of guests ("let all guests be received as Christ"), and private ownership of nothing. Monks take vows of stability, conversion of life, and obedience. Discipline is graduated and pastoral: the abbot is portrayed as a wise physician and father who must "so order all things that the strong may still have something to strive after and the weak may not be driven away."

Sources and character

Benedict drew substantially on the earlier anonymous Rule of the Master, the rule of Basil, the sayings of the Desert Fathers, and John Cassian, shaping them with his own moderation — the Rule calls itself "a little rule for beginners" and famously permits wine, adequate clothing by climate, and a balance between rigour and mercy.

Influence

Promoted by figures such as Gregory the Great, whose Dialogues recount Benedict's life, and standardised under Charlemagne and Benedict of Aniane in the 9th century, the Rule shaped the monasteries that preserved learning and reformed agriculture across medieval Europe. Monte Cassino, Benedict's own foundation, remains its symbolic home.
