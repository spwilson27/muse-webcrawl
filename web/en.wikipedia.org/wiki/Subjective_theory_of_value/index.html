Subjective theory of value

The subjective theory of value is an economic theory of value which advances the idea that the value of a good is not determined by any inherent property of the good, by the labour needed to produce it, or by its scarcity alone, but instead by the importance an individual places on it for the achievement of their desired ends. It is the core of the marginalist revolution in economics and of the Austrian School.

Development

Ideas of subjective value appear in medieval scholastics and in Ferdinando Galiani, Étienne Bonnot de Condillac, and Daniel Bernoulli, who resolved the paradox of value (why diamonds cost more than water) by noting that utility depends on circumstances. The theory was formalised independently in 1871 by William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger, and Léon Walras through the concept of marginal utility: price reflects the value of the least important use satisfied, so abundant water has low marginal value despite high total utility. Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk and Friedrich von Wieser developed Menger's account; Ludwig von Mises built his praxeology upon it.

Contrast with labour theory

The subjective theory displaced the classical labour theory of value held by Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and Karl Marx, under which exchange value derives from embodied labour. Subjectivists argue that labour itself derives its value from the expected value of its product, reversing the direction of causation.
