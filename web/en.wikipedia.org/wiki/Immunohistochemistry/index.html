Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining: brown chromogen marks cells expressing the target antigen.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is a form of immunostaining. It involves the process of selectively identifying antigens (proteins) in cells and tissue by exploiting the principle of antibodies binding specifically to antigens in biological tissues. IHC is widely used in diagnostic pathology and in research to localise proteins within tissue architecture.

Method

Tissue sections — typically formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, or frozen — are first subjected to antigen retrieval to unmask epitopes altered by fixation. The section is then incubated with a primary antibody against the target protein. In the direct method the antibody itself carries a label; in the more common indirect method, a labelled secondary antibody binds the primary, amplifying the signal. Labels are commonly enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase (HRP), which converts the chromogen DAB into a brown precipitate, or alkaline phosphatase, which produces a red product. Sections are counterstained, often with haematoxylin, and examined by light microscopy.

Applications

In surgical pathology, panels of antibodies help classify tumours of uncertain origin, distinguish benign from malignant lesions, and identify prognostic and predictive markers. Examples include HER2 testing in breast cancer, PD-L1 scoring to guide immunotherapy, mismatch repair protein staining in colorectal cancer screening for Lynch syndrome, and lineage markers such as cytokeratins, CD20 and S100. IHC is also used to detect infectious organisms in tissue.

History

The technique grew from Albert Coons's fluorescent antibody work in the 1940s. Enzyme-labelled antibody methods in the 1960s and 1970s made staining compatible with ordinary light microscopy and archival tissue, and heat-induced antigen retrieval in the early 1990s greatly expanded the range of usable antibodies.

Limitations

Results depend on antibody specificity, fixation, and protocol quality. Cross-reactivity, background staining, and variable expression can complicate interpretation, so controls and correlation with morphology are essential.
