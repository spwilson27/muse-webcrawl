The Ethiopian wolf (Canis simensis), also called the Simien jackal or Simien fox, is a canid native to the Ethiopian Highlands. It is one of the world's rarest and most endangered canids and Africa's most endangered carnivore. About the size of a coyote, it has a distinctive rufous coat with white underparts, a long narrow skull and widely spaced teeth adapted to its highly specialised diet.

Ecology

Unlike most social wolves, the Ethiopian wolf forages alone by day for rodents — principally giant mole-rats, grass rats and other afroalpine species, which make up the great majority of its diet — yet lives and breeds in tight family packs that jointly defend a territory and rear pups communally. It is restricted to isolated pockets of grassland and moorland above roughly 3,000 m in the Bale Mountains, Simien Mountains and a few other highland areas, a distribution likened to that of an island species on "sky islands".

Threats and conservation

The species is threatened by habitat loss to subsistence farming at high altitude, overgrazing, road kills, hybridisation with domestic dogs, and diseases — especially rabies and canine distemper — transmitted from dogs, which have periodically killed large fractions of local populations. Conservation measures led by the Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme include oral and injectable rabies vaccination of wolves, vaccination of domestic dogs, community education and monitoring. Its total population is estimated at fewer than 500 adults, concentrated mainly in the Bale Mountains.
