Antiprotozoal

Antiprotozoal agents are a class of pharmaceuticals used in the treatment of protozoan infections. Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic parasites responsible for diseases including malaria, amoebic dysentery, giardiasis, leishmaniasis, trichomoniasis, toxoplasmosis, and African sleeping sickness, which together cause substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions.

Antiprotozoal drugs target parasites such as Plasmodium, Entamoeba, and Giardia

Examples by disease

Malaria

Antimalarial drugs—the largest group of antiprotozoals—include chloroquine, artemisinin-based combination therapies, quinine, and primaquine.

Amoebiasis and giardiasis

The nitroimidazoles metronidazole and tinidazole are mainstays for infections by Entamoeba histolytica, Giardia lamblia, and Trichomonas vaginalis. After a tissue-active agent, a luminal agent such as paromomycin or diloxanide furoate may be given for amoebiasis to eradicate cysts in the bowel.

Leishmaniasis and trypanosomiasis

Pentavalent antimonials, amphotericin B, miltefosine, and pentamidine are used against Leishmania species. Human African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) is treated with drugs such as suramin, pentamidine, melarsoprol, eflornithine, and the oral drug fexinidazole, while Chagas disease is treated with benznidazole and nifurtimox.

Other infections

Toxoplasmosis is treated with pyrimethamine combined with sulfadiazine; cryptosporidiosis with nitazoxanide; and Pneumocystis pneumonia (caused by a fungus, treated with similar agents) with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole.

Mechanisms

Because protozoa are eukaryotes like their hosts, selective toxicity can be difficult to achieve, and some antiprotozoals have significant adverse effects. Mechanisms include disruption of parasite DNA (nitroimidazoles), inhibition of folate synthesis, interference with heme detoxification (chloroquine), and generation of toxic free radicals (artemisinins).
