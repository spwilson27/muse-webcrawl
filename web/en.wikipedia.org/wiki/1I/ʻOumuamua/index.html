1I/ʻOumuamua

1I/ʻOumuamua is the first known interstellar object detected passing through the Solar System. Formally designated 1I/2017 U1, it was discovered by Robert Weryk using the Pan-STARRS telescope at Haleakalā Observatory, Hawaii, on 19 October 2017, about 40 days after it passed its closest point to the Sun. The name, chosen by the Pan-STARRS team, is Hawaiian for "scout" or "messenger from afar arriving first".

ʻOumuamua appears to be a highly elongated object, roughly 100–1,000 metres long with a width and thickness perhaps only a tenth of that, and has a red colour similar to objects in the outer Solar System. It showed no sign of a coma or tail despite passing close to the Sun. Its trajectory is strongly hyperbolic, with an eccentricity of about 1.2, confirming that it is not gravitationally bound to the Sun and originated from interstellar space.

Observations

Observations with the Very Large Telescope and other facilities showed that ʻOumuamua's brightness varied dramatically, by a factor of about ten, suggesting an extreme axial ratio and a tumbling rotation. In 2018, astronomers reported a small non-gravitational acceleration in its outbound trajectory, most plausibly explained by outgassing, although no dust or gas was directly detected. The object's nature — asteroid-like or comet-like — remains debated, and its composition and origin continue to be studied.

Significance

The "1I" designation established a new class of astronomical object naming for interstellar visitors. A second interstellar object, the comet 2I/Borisov, was discovered in 2019. ʻOumuamua is now receding from the Sun and is too faint for further observation with existing telescopes.
