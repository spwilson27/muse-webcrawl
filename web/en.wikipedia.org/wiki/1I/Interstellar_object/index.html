Interstellar object

Type: Astronomical object on a hyperbolic trajectory

First confirmed: 1I/2017 U1 ʻOumuamua (2017)

Known examples: ʻOumuamua (1I), Borisov (2I), ATLAS (3I)

An interstellar object is an astronomical body, such as an asteroid or comet, that is located in interstellar space and is not gravitationally bound to a star. Such objects pass through other stellar systems on hyperbolic trajectories, never to return. The term is also applied to objects on interstellar trajectories within a planetary system, such as extrasolar visitors passing through the Solar System.

Discovery

The first confirmed interstellar object detected passing through the Solar System was 1I/2017 U1, discovered on 19 October 2017 by Robert Weryk using the Pan-STARRS telescope. It was given the name ʻOumuamua, Hawaiian for "scout" or "a messenger from afar arriving first," and received the first-ever "I" designation for interstellar objects. Its strongly hyperbolic orbit (eccentricity about 1.2) proved it was not bound to the Sun.

A second interstellar object, the comet 2I/Borisov, was discovered by amateur astronomer Gennadiy Borisov on 30 August 2019. Unlike the enigmatic ʻOumuamua, Borisov behaved like an ordinary comet with a visible coma and tail. A third, 3I/ATLAS, was discovered in 2025.

Characteristics

Interstellar objects are presumably ejected from their home planetary systems during their formation, through gravitational interactions with giant planets or passing stars. Estimates based on the detection rate of ʻOumuamua suggest that a large population of such objects drifts through the Milky Way, and that at any given moment many may be passing through the inner Solar System undetected.

Hypothetical missions

Because interstellar objects offer a direct sample of material from other star systems, various mission concepts, such as the ESA Comet Interceptor and the proposed Project Lyra, have been studied to intercept and study a future visitor up close.
