1I/ʻOumuamua is the first known interstellar object detected passing through the Solar System. Discovered by Robert Weryk using the Pan-STARRS telescope at Haleakalā Observatory, Hawaii, on 19 October 2017, it was already outbound and receding from the Sun. Its name, chosen by the Pan-STARRS team, is Hawaiian for "scout" or "messenger from afar arriving first".

Discovery and trajectory

ʻOumuamua travelled on a strongly hyperbolic trajectory with an eccentricity of about 1.2, proving it is not bound to the Sun. It passed perihelion at 0.256 AU on 9 September 2017 and came within about 0.16 AU of Earth in October. Its incoming direction was close to the solar apex in the constellation Lyra.

Physical characteristics

Observations indicated a highly elongated or flattened body — early analyses suggested dimensions on the order of several hundred metres long but only tens of metres thick — tumbling with a rotation period of roughly 7–8 hours. Its spectrum was reddish, similar to some outer Solar System bodies, and it showed no visible coma despite passing close to the Sun.

Classification debate

ʻOumuamua displayed a small non-gravitational acceleration away from the Sun without an observed dust or gas outflow. Explanations proposed include outgassing of exotic ices (such as hydrogen or nitrogen ice), radiation pressure on an unusually thin or low-density body, and — speculatively, most famously by Harvard astronomer Avi Loeb — artificial origin as a light sail. The mainstream consensus favours a natural object, most likely an exotic comet fragment, though no single model explains all observations.

Origin

The object's velocity relative to the local standard of rest was small, suggesting it may not have travelled far or fast before reaching the Solar System. A 2021 interstellar comet, 2I/Borisov, behaved like an ordinary comet, making ʻOumuamua's oddities stand out further. ʻOumuamua is now beyond the orbit of Neptune and too faint to observe.
