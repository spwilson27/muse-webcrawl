Astronomical survey

Modern surveys map billions of objects across the whole sky.
Astronomical survey
PurposeSystematic mapping of a region of sky
OutputsCatalogues, images, light curves, spectra
ExamplesSDSS, Gaia, 2MASS, Pan-STARRS, ZTF, Legacy Survey of Space and Time (Rubin)

An astronomical survey is a systematic observation programme that maps a large area of the sky rather than targeting individual objects, producing catalogues and images that serve as general resources for astronomy. Surveys have defined the field since the star catalogues of antiquity; today they are conducted across the electromagnetic spectrum and increasingly in the time domain.

Notable surveys

The Bonner Durchmusterung (1859–1903) catalogued some 324,000 stars visually; photographic surveys culminated in the Palomar Observatory Sky Survey. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey (from 2000) delivered imaging and spectra of millions of galaxies and quasars. ESA's Gaia mission (2013–2025) measured positions, parallaxes and motions of nearly two billion stars. Infrared (2MASS, WISE), radio (NVSS, FIRST) and X-ray (ROSAT) surveys map the sky at other wavelengths. Time-domain surveys such as Pan-STARRS, the Zwicky Transient Facility and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time repeatedly scan the sky to catch supernovae, asteroids and other transients.

Uses

Survey data underpin studies of galactic structure, cosmology (baryon acoustic oscillations, weak lensing), planetary defence (near-Earth asteroid inventories) and the discovery of rare objects, from the most distant quasars to interstellar visitors.
