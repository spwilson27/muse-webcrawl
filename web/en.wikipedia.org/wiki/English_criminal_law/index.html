English criminal law

English criminal law concerns offences — acts or omissions harmful to society — prosecuted in the name of the Crown in England and Wales. It is a mixture of common law developed by judges over centuries and statute law enacted by Parliament. The prosecution must prove both the prohibited conduct (actus reus) and a culpable state of mind (mens rea) beyond reasonable doubt.

Classification of offences

Offences are summary (tried in magistrates' courts), indictable (tried in the Crown Court before a judge and jury), or either-way. Serious offences such as murder, rape and robbery are indictable only.

General principles

Defendants benefit from the presumption of innocence. Recognised defences include self-defence, duress, necessity, insanity and automatism. Liability may also arise through aiding, abetting, counselling or procuring an offence, and through inchoate offences such as attempt and conspiracy.

Major offences

Homicide comprises murder (unlawful killing with intent to kill or cause grievous bodily harm, carrying a mandatory life sentence) and manslaughter. Other major statutes include the Theft Act 1968, the Offences against the Person Act 1861, the Fraud Act 2006 and the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971.
