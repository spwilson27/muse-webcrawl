Dopamine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dopamine is a neurotransmitter — a chemical messenger — made in several areas of the brain, including the substantia nigra and the ventral tegmental area. It plays several important roles in the brain and body: in movement, motivation, reinforcement and reward, mood, attention and learning, and as a hormone regulating prolactin release. Popular culture calls it the "pleasure chemical", but research increasingly frames dopamine as a signal of motivation and prediction — it spikes in anticipation of reward and when outcomes are better than expected ("reward prediction error"), teaching the brain what to pursue.

Systems and disease

Four major dopamine pathways are described: the mesolimbic pathway (reward; implicated in addiction — cocaine and amphetamines act by boosting dopamine signalling), the mesocortical (cognition and executive function), the nigrostriatal (movement), and the tuberoinfundibular (hormonal control). Loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra causes the movement symptoms of Parkinson's disease, treated by the dopamine precursor L-DOPA and by dopamine agonists. Excess or dysregulated dopamine signalling is implicated in schizophrenia — most antipsychotic drugs block dopamine D2 receptors — and dopamine dysfunction features in ADHD and depression research. The pathways operate through synapses across the nervous system.
