East African drought

RegionHorn of Africa and East Africa
Countries affectedSomalia, Ethiopia, Kenya, Djibouti, Uganda
Major events2011 drought and famine; 2020–2023 drought

Drought in East Africa is a recurrent natural hazard affecting the Horn of Africa and surrounding countries, where rain-fed agriculture and pastoralism make livelihoods highly sensitive to failed rainy seasons. Severe multi-year droughts periodically produce food crises, livestock losses, displacement and famine, most notoriously in 2011 and during the prolonged drought of 2020–2023, the worst in the region in about forty years.

Causes

Rainfall in the region depends on the seasonal migration of the Intertropical Convergence Zone, sea-surface temperatures in the Indian and Pacific Oceans, and phenomena such as La Niña and the Indian Ocean Dipole, which can suppress the region's two rainy seasons (the long rains and short rains). Climate change is increasing temperatures and evaporation and appears to be making droughts more severe, while conflict, poverty and weak infrastructure turn meteorological drought into humanitarian disaster.

Major droughts

The 2011 East African drought, caused by two consecutive failed rainy seasons, affected more than 13 million people; famine was declared in parts of southern Somalia, where roughly a quarter of a million people died, half of them children under five. Earlier crises included the Ethiopian famines of 1983–1985, in which drought combined with civil war and government policy to kill hundreds of thousands.

From late 2020 the region experienced five consecutive failed rainy seasons, driven by a rare triple-dip La Niña. By 2023 tens of millions of people in Somalia, Ethiopia and Kenya faced acute food insecurity, millions of livestock had died, and over a million people had been displaced in Somalia alone, even as parts of the region then suffered destructive flooding when rains finally returned.

Responses

Responses include humanitarian food aid, cash transfers, famine early-warning systems such as FEWS NET, drought-resistant crops, water infrastructure and regional initiatives on climate adaptation and pastoralist livelihoods. Agencies have repeatedly warned that aid tends to arrive only after famine conditions develop, despite increasingly accurate seasonal forecasts.
