The Fair Housing Act is Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968 in the United States. It protects people from discrimination when they are renting or buying a home, getting a mortgage, seeking housing assistance, or engaging in other housing-related activities. The Act prohibits discrimination in housing based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability.

The legislation followed the civil rights movement and long-standing demands to end practices such as redlining and racially restrictive covenants. Earlier, the Civil Rights Act of 1866 had prohibited racial discrimination in the sale or rental of property, a right affirmed in Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. (1968), but comprehensive fair-housing legislation stalled in Congress until the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. on April 4, 1968, and the riots that followed, spurred final passage; President Johnson signed the Act on April 11, 1968.

Prohibited conduct

The Act makes it unlawful to refuse to sell or rent a dwelling to any person because of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin; to discriminate in the terms, conditions, or privileges of sale or rental; to make or publish discriminatory advertisements; to falsely represent that a dwelling is unavailable; and to engage in blockbusting — persuading owners to sell cheaply by suggesting that people of a particular background are moving into the neighborhood. It also covers discrimination in mortgage lending, homeowners insurance, and real-estate brokerage services.

Amendments

The Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 added sex to the list of protected characteristics. The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, signed by President Ronald Reagan, added disability and familial status (the presence of children under 18), substantially expanded HUD's enforcement powers, and provided for administrative law judges and Justice Department pattern-or-practice suits. A notable exemption, the so-called "Mrs. Murphy exemption," covers owner-occupied buildings with no more than four units.

Enforcement

Individuals who believe they have experienced housing discrimination may file a complaint with HUD within one year, or file suit in federal court within two years. HUD investigates complaints and may refer matters to the Department of Justice. The Act has also been applied through the doctrine of disparate impact, upheld by the Supreme Court in Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project (2015), which allows challenges to policies that disproportionately harm protected groups even without proof of intent. The 2021 executive action extending Bostock v. Clayton County reasoning led HUD to treat sexual orientation and gender identity discrimination as sex discrimination under the Act.

Legacy

Although the Act outlawed overt housing discrimination, residential segregation in the United States declined only slowly, and debates continue over enforcement, zoning, and the federal obligation to "affirmatively further fair housing," a mandate included in the 1968 law and given regulatory form in rules issued in 2015 and 2023, with contested changes in between.
