Prebiotic

A prebiotic is a compound in food that induces the growth or activity of beneficial microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi in the gastrointestinal tract. The most common examples are non-digestible dietary fibres, including inulin, fructooligosaccharides (FOS) and galactooligosaccharides (GOS), which are fermented by the gut microbiota to produce short-chain fatty acids.

Definition

The concept was introduced in 1995 by Glenn Gibson and Marcel Roberfroid. A widely used modern definition, from the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (2016), describes a prebiotic as "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit". Unlike a probiotic, which consists of live microorganisms, a prebiotic is not itself alive.

Sources and effects

Prebiotics occur naturally in chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus and bananas, and are also manufactured for use in supplements and functional foods. Their fermentation by gut bacteria is associated with improved mineral absorption, modulation of the immune system and other proposed health effects, though the strength of evidence varies by compound and endpoint.
