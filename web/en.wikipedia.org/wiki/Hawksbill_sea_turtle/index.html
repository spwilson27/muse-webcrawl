Hawksbill sea turtle

The hawksbill sea turtle (Eretmochelys imbricata) is a critically endangered sea turtle belonging to the family Cheloniidae. It is the only extant species in the genus Eretmochelys. The species has a worldwide distribution, with two distinct subspecies in the Atlantic and Indo-Pacific regions.

The hawksbill's appearance is similar to that of other marine turtles. Its body is generally flattened, with a protective carapace, flipper-like limbs adapted for swimming in the open ocean, and a sharp, curving upper beak that resembles a hawk's bill — the feature from which its common name derives.

Description

Adult hawksbills typically grow to about 1 metre (3 ft) in length and weigh around 80 kilograms (180 lb). The carapace has an amber background patterned with an irregular combination of light and dark streaks and striking overlapping scutes. The shell's edges are serrated toward the rear. Two pairs of prefrontal scales on the head and two claws on each foreflipper distinguish it from other sea turtles.

Distribution and habitat

The Atlantic subspecies (E. i. imbricata) is found chiefly in the western Atlantic, including the Caribbean, while the Indo-Pacific subspecies (E. i. bissa) ranges across the Indian and Pacific Oceans. Hawksbills are strongly associated with coral reefs, rocky coasts, lagoons and mangrove estuaries, where they forage among crevices.

Diet and ecology

Hawksbills are omnivorous but feed predominantly on sea sponges — an unusual specialisation among marine animals. Some of the sponges they consume are highly toxic and siliceous, lethal to most other organisms. They also feed on sea anemones, jellyfish, molluscs, algae and other invertebrates. By cropping sponges, hawksbills help maintain the health and species balance of coral reefs.

Conservation

The species is listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN. Hawksbills have been hunted for centuries for their beautiful carapace — the principal source of commercial "tortoiseshell" used in jewellery, combs and ornaments. International trade is banned under CITES, but poaching, egg collection, coastal development, fisheries bycatch and reef degradation continue to threaten populations, which have declined by more than 80% over the last century in many regions.

Life history

Like other sea turtles, females return to beaches to nest, laying clutches of around 140 eggs in pits dug in the sand. Hatchlings emerge after about two months and make their way to the sea, facing heavy predation. Hawksbills reach maturity at roughly 20 to 40 years of age.
