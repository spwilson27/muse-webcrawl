Sufism in India refers to the development of Sufi Islam — the mystical dimension of Islam — in the Indian subcontinent from roughly the twelfth century onward. Sufi saints (pirs) and their orders (tariqas) played a central role in the spread of Islam in South Asia, and their shrines (dargahs) remain major pilgrimage sites for Muslims and Hindus alike.

History

Early Sufis arrived with and after the establishment of Muslim rule in northern India. Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti settled at Ajmer around 1192 and founded the Chishti order in India; his successors, including Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki and Nizamuddin Auliya of Delhi, made the Chishtiyya the most influential order in the subcontinent, noted for its emphasis on love, generosity, devotional music (sama and qawwali), and distance from royal courts. The Suhrawardi order was prominent in Multan and Sindh, while the Qadiri and the more sharia-minded Naqshbandi orders spread in later centuries.

Practices and culture

Indian Sufism is characterised by the veneration of saints, annual urs festivals marking a saint's death anniversary, and the composition of devotional poetry in Persian, Urdu, Punjabi, and other regional languages. Figures such as Amir Khusrau and Bulleh Shah shaped the region's literary and musical culture, and Sufi ideas interacted with the Bhakti movement, contributing to a shared devotional culture that many historians describe as syncretic.
