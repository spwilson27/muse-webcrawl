European polecat

Scientific name: Mustela putorius

Family: Mustelidae

Conservation status: Least Concern

The European polecat (Mustela putorius) is a species of mustelid native to western Eurasia and North Africa. It is a solitary, nocturnal carnivore with dark brown fur, a lighter underfur that shows through to give a grizzled appearance, and a distinctive pale face crossed by a dark bandit-like mask. Males are considerably larger than females, weighing roughly 1–1.5 kg.

Distribution and habitat

Polecats occur across most of Europe east to the Ural Mountains, though they were driven to extinction across much of Britain by trapping and persecution in the nineteenth century, surviving in Wales and recolonising England during the twentieth. They favour lowland habitats including farmland, marshes, riverbanks, woodland edge and farm buildings, and often den in rabbit burrows. Prey includes rabbits, rats, mice, frogs, toads and birds; amphibian toxins are tolerated, and polecats are known to cache paralysed frogs in larders.

Domestication

The domestic ferret (Mustela furo) is generally considered to descend from the European polecat, possibly with some contribution from the steppe polecat. Wild polecats hybridise readily with escaped ferrets, producing polecat–ferret hybrids that can be difficult to distinguish from pure polecats, a matter of conservation concern in Britain. The black-footed ferret of North America and the mink are relatives within the same family.
