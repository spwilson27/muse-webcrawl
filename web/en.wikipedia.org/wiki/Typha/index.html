Typha is a genus of about 30 species of monocotyledonous flowering plants in the family Typhaceae. These plants have a variety of common names, principally cattail (in North America) and bulrush or reedmace (in Britain). The genus is largely distributed in the Northern Hemisphere, where it is found in a variety of wetland habitats.

Description

The plants are aquatic or semi-aquatic, rhizomatous, perennial herbs with long, strap-like leaves. The flowers are minute and densely packed into a cylindrical spike: dark brown male flowers form the upper, thinner part of the spike, while the female flowers form the dense, sausage-shaped lower portion. The tiny seeds are dispersed by wind with the aid of fluffy hairs.

Ecology

Cattails are often among the first wetland plants to colonize areas of newly exposed wet mud, and they can spread aggressively, sometimes crowding out other vegetation. They provide food and cover for marsh animals; muskrats eat the rhizomes, and many birds nest among the stems. Common species include the broadleaf cattail (Typha latifolia) and the narrowleaf cattail (Typha angustifolia), which readily hybridize.

Uses

Many parts of the plant are edible: the rhizomes are starchy, the young shoots can be eaten like asparagus, and the pollen is used as flour. The fluffy seed heads were historically used as tinder and stuffing, and the leaves woven into mats, baskets, and chair seats. Cattails are also widely used in constructed wetlands for wastewater treatment and phytoremediation.
