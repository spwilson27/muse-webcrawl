Daji (Chinese: 妲己; pinyin: Dájǐ) was the favourite consort of King Zhou of Shang, the last king of the Chinese Shang dynasty. In Chinese historiography and folklore she is remembered as a classic femme fatale whose beauty and cruelty hastened the fall of the dynasty. She stands alongside Mo Xi of Xia and Bao Si of Zhou as one of the legendary women blamed for the ruin of a dynasty.

Legend

According to traditional accounts, Daji was the daughter of the nobleman Su Hu, given to King Zhou following a conflict between the Su clan and the king. The king became infatuated with her, and later histories accuse her of devising cruel punishments — most famously the "Cannon Burning Punishment", in which victims were forced to walk on a greased, heated bronze pillar over a pit of burning charcoal — and of delighting in torture at the Deer Terrace Pavilion, with its legendary "pool of wine and forest of meat".

In Fengshen Yanyi

In the Ming-dynasty novel Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen Yanyi), Daji's story is supernatural: the goddess Nüwa, offended by King Zhou's lustful poem at her temple, sends a thousand-year-old nine-tailed fox spirit to possess Daji's body and bring about the king's downfall. After the Shang fall to King Wu of Zhou, the fox-Daji is executed — in the novel, by Jiang Ziya, since ordinary executioners are too enchanted by her beauty to strike.

Historicity and legacy

Daji was likely a real historical person, a consort of King Zhou from the Yousu clan, but the lurid details of her cruelty are widely regarded by historians as later embellishments, part of a historiographical pattern of blaming dynastic collapse on women. She remains a popular figure in Chinese opera, film, television and video games.
