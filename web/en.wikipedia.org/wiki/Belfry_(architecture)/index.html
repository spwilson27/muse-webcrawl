Belfry (architecture)

A belfry is a structure enclosing bells, most often the upper part of a bell tower or steeple in which the bells are rung, or a free-standing tower built to house them. The word derives from the Old French berfrei, originally a movable siege tower, and is unrelated to "bell" in origin, though the association reshaped its spelling. Belfries are found in churches, civic buildings and defensive towers across Europe and beyond; the great civic belfries of Flanders and northern France, symbols of municipal independence, are protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Structure and function

A belfry chamber typically has pairs of tall arched openings, often fitted with louvres (abat-sons) that direct the sound outward while keeping out the weather. The bells hang from a timber or steel frame (the bell frame) within the chamber, and may be rung by swinging, by a chiming mechanism, or as part of a carillon played from a keyboard. In church towers the belfry sits above the ringing chamber, where the ringers stand, and below the spire if there is one.

History

Separate campaniles appeared in early medieval Italy, while in the Low Countries wealthy cloth towns built monumental belfries — such as those of Bruges, Ghent and Ypres — to hold their charters, treasury and alarm bells, proclaiming civic liberty against feudal and ecclesiastical authority. Belfries also survive in secular contexts from China, where bell towers marked the centres of cities such as Xi'an, to colonial churches in the Americas.
