Hausdorff measure

In mathematics, the Hausdorff measure is a generalisation of the traditional notions of area and volume to non-integer dimensions, and in particular to fractal sets. Introduced by Felix Hausdorff in 1918, it assigns a size to subsets of a metric space at any non-negative real dimension s, and is the foundation of the Hausdorff dimension. (Note: this article concerns the measure; for the topology concept, see Hausdorff space.)

Definition

Let (X, d) be a metric space. For a subset U, let diam(U) denote its diameter. For s ≥ 0 and δ > 0, one defines an approximate measure by covering a set E with countably many sets of diameter at most δ and summing the s-th powers of their diameters:

Hsδ(E) = inf { Σi (diam Ui)s : E ⊆ ∪ Ui, diam Ui ≤ δ }.

The Hausdorff s-dimensional measure is then the limit as δ → 0: Hs(E) = limδ→0 Hsδ(E). The construction is an outer measure, and its restriction to Borel sets yields a genuine measure.

Properties

For integer dimensions, Hausdorff measure agrees (up to a normalising constant) with familiar quantities: H0 is counting measure, H1 measures length of curves, H2 is proportional to area, and in ℝn the n-dimensional Hausdorff measure equals Lebesgue measure. For fixed E, Hs(E) is non-increasing in s, and there is a critical value — the Hausdorff dimension — at which the measure jumps from infinity to zero. Hausdorff measures are metric outer measures, invariant under isometries, and scale predictably under similarities, which makes them the natural tool for measuring fractals such as the Cantor set, whose Hs measure at s = log 2 / log 3 is finite and positive.

Applications

Hausdorff measure is central to geometric measure theory, where it is used to study rectifiable sets, minimal surfaces, and the fine structure of sets of partial differential equations, and to fractal geometry, where it quantifies the size of irregular sets that Lebesgue measure cannot distinguish.
