Siege of the International Legations

The foreign legations in Beijing, besieged during the Boxer Rebellion.

The Siege of the International Legations took place in Beijing during the summer of 1900, at the height of the Boxer Rebellion. For 55 days, the foreign diplomatic legations in the Legation Quarter were besieged by forces of the Qing dynasty and the Boxers (Yihetuan), a Chinese militia movement opposed to foreign influence. Roughly 900 foreign nationals — diplomats, soldiers, missionaries and civilians — together with several thousand Chinese Christians, held out behind improvised defences until an international relief expedition of the Eight-Nation Alliance fought its way into the city on 14 August 1900.

Background

By 1900, anti-foreign sentiment in northern China had coalesced in the Boxer movement, whose members attacked missionaries, Chinese Christians and foreign property. As violence spread toward Beijing, the foreign legations called in small detachments of guards in late May and early June 1900. On 19 June the Qing court, effectively controlled by the Empress Dowager Cixi, delivered an ultimatum ordering all foreigners to leave the capital within 24 hours. The next day the German minister, Clemens von Ketteler, was murdered on his way to the Zongli Yamen, and the siege began in earnest.

The siege

The defence of the Legation Quarter was coordinated by the British minister, Sir Claude MacDonald. The defenders built barricades from furniture, sandbags and carts, and held a perimeter anchored on the British legation. Chinese Christian refugees were sheltered largely in the grounds of the Fu, the palace of Prince Su. The besiegers subjected the legations to rifle and artillery fire and attempted to set them ablaze, but Qing forces were divided: some commanders, notably Ronglu, appear to have restrained their attacks, and periods of truce and negotiation interrupted the fighting. A relief column under Admiral Seymour had been forced back in June; a second, much larger expedition advanced from Tianjin in August.

Relief and aftermath

On 14 August 1900 the troops of the Eight-Nation Alliance — Britain, the United States, Japan, Russia, France, Germany, Italy and Austria-Hungary — entered Beijing and lifted the siege. The Qing court fled to Xi'an. The subsequent Boxer Protocol of 1901 imposed a large indemnity on China, stationed foreign troops in the legation quarter, and punished officials held responsible for the uprising. The siege became famous in the West through memoirs, novels and films, notably the 1963 film 55 Days at Peking.
