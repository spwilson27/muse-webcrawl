Spam musubi

Spam musubi
TypeSnack
Place of originUnited States (Hawaii)
Main ingredientsSpam, rice, nori
VariationsWith furikake, egg, teriyaki glaze, or shiso

Spam musubi is a snack and lunch food composed of a slice of grilled Spam sandwiched on or in a block of rice, wrapped together with nori (dried seaweed) in the tradition of Japanese omusubi. Inexpensive and portable, it is a fixture of convenience stores, lunch counters and school cafeterias across Hawaii, where it is sold at virtually every corner store and gas station.

History

Spam, introduced by Hormel in 1937, reached Hawaii in quantity during the Second World War, when it fed soldiers and, under wartime restrictions on fishing and food supply, became a staple for local residents — including the large Japanese-American population, who combined it with the rice-ball format of their own cuisine. The musubi form is commonly credited to Barbara Funamura of Kauai, who in the 1980s popularised the now-standard rectangular moulded version and sold it at her Joni-Hana restaurant; similar items had circulated earlier.

Preparation and popularity

A slice of Spam is fried, often glazed with soy sauce and sugar or teriyaki sauce, placed on a block of rice pressed in a mould (frequently the emptied Spam can in home preparation), optionally sprinkled with furikake, and wrapped with a strip of nori. Hawaii consumes more Spam per capita than any other US state, and the musubi is also widely sold in Guam, the Northern Marianas, and increasingly on the US mainland and in musubi specialty shops abroad. The McDonald's menu in Hawaii has featured Spam breakfast items, underscoring the ingredient's local status.
