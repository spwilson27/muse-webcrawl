Stable isotope ratio

The term stable isotope ratio refers to the ratio of the abundances of two stable isotopes of an element in a material, usually expressed relative to an international standard. Because physical, chemical and biological processes fractionate isotopes — slightly favouring the lighter or heavier form — these ratios carry information about the origin and history of a sample.

An isotope ratio mass spectrometer

Notation

Ratios are most often reported in delta (δ) notation, in parts per thousand (per mille, ‰), as δ = (Rsample/Rstandard − 1) × 1000, where R is the ratio of the heavy to the light isotope (for example 13C/12C or 18O/16O). Standards include VPDB for carbon, VSMOW for oxygen and hydrogen, and atmospheric nitrogen for δ15N.

Measurement

Stable isotope ratios are measured by isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS), in which a sample is converted to a simple gas (CO2, N2, H2, SO2) and the masses of its isotopologues are compared with those of reference gases.

Applications

Applications include reconstructing past climates from ice cores and marine sediments, tracing water and carbon cycling in ecosystems, determining diets and migration in archaeology and ecology, authenticating food and detecting adulteration, forensic provenancing of human remains and materials, and detecting banned substance use in sport through carbon isotope testing of steroids.
