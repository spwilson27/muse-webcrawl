Price mechanism

The price mechanism is the means by which the forces of supply and demand determine the prices of goods and services and allocate resources in a market economy. Prices act simultaneously as signals, as incentives and as a rationing device: a rising price tells producers that a good is becoming scarcer or more desired, encourages them to produce more, and rations the existing supply to those willing to pay most; a falling price does the reverse.

Functions

Economists usually assign prices three functions. The signalling function conveys information about relative scarcity — Friedrich Hayek argued in "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945) that prices compress dispersed local knowledge into a single number that anyone can act on. The incentive function rewards producers who respond to signals with profit and penalises those who ignore them. The rationing function allocates goods among consumers without queues or administrators. Together these coordinate millions of decentralised decisions without central direction — the phenomenon Adam Smith associated with the "invisible hand".

Equilibrium and adjustment

If price is above the level at which quantity supplied equals quantity demanded, a surplus accumulates and sellers cut prices; if below, shortages push prices up. This adjustment toward equilibrium is the core of the mechanism, though real markets adjust at varying speeds: financial and commodity markets reprice in seconds, while wages and rents can be sticky for years.

Interventions and failures

Governments frequently override the mechanism with price ceilings (such as rent controls), price floors (such as minimum wages and farm supports), taxes and subsidies — creating surpluses, shortages or wedges between what buyers pay and sellers receive. Critics of unrestricted pricing point to market failures: externalities like pollution are not priced at all, monopoly restricts output, and in emergencies price spikes for essentials strike many as unjust, prompting anti-gouging laws. Defenders answer that suppressing prices in a crisis discourages supply precisely when it is most needed. Economists continue to debate how far the mechanism should rule in areas such as healthcare, housing and organ donation.
