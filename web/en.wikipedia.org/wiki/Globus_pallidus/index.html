Globus pallidus

The globus pallidus (Latin for "pale globe"), also known as the pallidum, is a subcortical structure of the brain. It is one of the nuclei that make up the basal ganglia, and with the putamen it forms the lentiform nucleus. The globus pallidus is a major component of the circuitry that regulates voluntary movement, and it lies medial to the putamen and lateral to the internal capsule.

Structure

A sheet of myelinated fibres, the lateral medullary lamina, divides the globus pallidus into two functionally distinct segments:

  • The external segment (GPe), which receives inhibitory input from the striatum and sends inhibitory projections to the subthalamic nucleus and the internal segment — a key node in the "indirect pathway" of movement control.
  • The internal segment (GPi), together with the substantia nigra pars reticulata, the principal output nucleus of the basal ganglia, sending tonic inhibitory (GABAergic) projections to the thalamus. A pause in this inhibition "releases" thalamocortical activity and permits movement.

Function

Within the direct and indirect pathway model of the basal ganglia, the pallidum helps select desired movements and suppress competing ones. Its neurons fire tonically at high frequency, and the balance between its segments shapes motor output, procedural learning and aspects of cognition and motivation.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the globus pallidus is implicated in Parkinson's disease, dystonia and Huntington's disease. The GPi is a principal target for deep brain stimulation and for the older lesion procedure of pallidotomy in movement disorders, and bilateral pallidal damage — classically from carbon monoxide poisoning — produces distinctive parkinsonism and cognitive impairment.
