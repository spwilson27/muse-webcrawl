Young's modulus

SymbolE
DefinitionRatio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the linear elastic region
SI unitPascal (Pa), usually reported in GPa
Named afterThomas Young (1773–1829)
Example valuesSteel ≈ 200 GPa; aluminium ≈ 69 GPa; rubber ≈ 0.01–0.1 GPa

Young's modulus (also called the elastic modulus or modulus of elasticity) is a mechanical property that measures the stiffness of a solid material. It is defined as the ratio of tensile stress (force per unit area) to tensile strain (proportional deformation) in the region where Hooke's law holds: E = σ / ε.

Interpretation

A high Young's modulus means a material stretches very little under load — diamond and tungsten carbide are extreme examples — while compliant materials such as rubber have very low values. Within the elastic limit the deformation is recoverable; beyond it, plastic deformation or fracture occurs.

Measurement and use

Young's modulus is measured in tensile tests, by ultrasonic methods, or from bending experiments. Engineers use it to predict deflections of beams and shafts, natural frequencies of structures, and buckling loads, and it enters most finite-element simulations of mechanical parts.

Related moduli

For isotropic materials, Young's modulus is related to the shear modulus G and bulk modulus K through Poisson's ratio ν, e.g. E = 2G(1 + ν) = 3K(1 − 2ν). Anisotropic materials such as wood and single crystals require a stiffness tensor instead of a single value.
