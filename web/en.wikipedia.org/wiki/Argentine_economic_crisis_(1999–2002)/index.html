Argentine economic crisis (1999–2002)

Protests in Buenos Aires during the crisis of December 2001.

The Argentine economic crisis of 1999–2002, known in Argentina as la Crisis or the Corralito crisis, was a severe economic depression that devastated the Argentine economy and destabilised the country's politics. GDP fell by about 28% between 1998 and 2002, unemployment exceeded 20%, and poverty rose above 50%. The crisis ended Argentina's decade-long convertibility regime, which had pegged the peso one-to-one to the US dollar, and led to the largest sovereign debt default in history at the time.

Causes

The Convertibility Law of 1991 under President Carlos Menem and economy minister Domingo Cavallo had ended hyperinflation by fixing the peso to the dollar under a currency-board-like system. Over the 1990s, however, the strengthening dollar, Brazil's 1999 devaluation, persistent fiscal deficits and mounting foreign debt made the peg increasingly untenable. A recession beginning in late 1998 deepened into a bank run in 2001.

Collapse

In December 2001, the government of Fernando de la Rúa imposed the corralito, freezing bank withdrawals to stop the run on deposits. Mass protests and looting (the Argentinazo) forced de la Rúa to resign on 20 December 2001; Argentina cycled through five presidents in two weeks. In January 2002 interim president Eduardo Duhalde abandoned convertibility; the peso fell to nearly four to the dollar, savings were forcibly converted ("pesificación"), and Argentina defaulted on roughly $100 billion of debt — restructured in 2005 and 2010 after protracted conflict with holdout creditors.

Recovery

From 2003, under President Néstor Kirchner, a competitive exchange rate, soaring soy prices and expansionary policies produced rapid growth averaging around 8–9% a year until 2007, sharply reducing poverty and unemployment, though the crisis left lasting scars on trust in banks and economic institutions.
