Defence mechanism

In psychoanalytic theory, a defence mechanism (American spelling: defense mechanism) is an unconscious psychological operation that functions to protect a person from anxiety-producing thoughts and feelings related to internal conflicts and external stressors. According to this theory, healthy people normally use different defences throughout life. Defence mechanisms may result in healthy or unhealthy consequences depending on the circumstances and frequency with which the mechanism is used.

The concept originated with Sigmund Freud, who described several ego defences in his writings beginning in the 1890s. His daughter Anna Freud systematized the theory in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936), identifying ten classic mechanisms. Later theorists, notably George Eman Vaillant, organized defences into a hierarchy ranging from pathological and immature defences to neurotic and mature ones.

Common mechanisms

Frequently described defences include repression (excluding distressing thoughts from awareness), denial (refusing to acknowledge reality), projection (attributing one's own unacceptable feelings to others), displacement (redirecting an impulse toward a safer target), regression (reverting to earlier behavior), rationalization (constructing plausible but false explanations), reaction formation (expressing the opposite of an unacceptable impulse), intellectualization, and sublimation (channeling unacceptable impulses into socially valued activities, generally classed as a mature defence).

Classification

Vaillant's hierarchy groups defences into levels: pathological (such as delusional projection), immature (acting out, passive aggression), neurotic (intellectualization, repression, displacement), and mature (humor, sublimation, altruism, suppression, anticipation). The fourth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) included a provisional Defensive Functioning Scale based on a similar hierarchy.

Criticism

Defence mechanisms are difficult to test empirically, and their measurement relies on clinical judgment or self-report instruments, leading to debate about their scientific status. Nevertheless, the concept remains influential in psychodynamic psychotherapy and in everyday psychological vocabulary.
