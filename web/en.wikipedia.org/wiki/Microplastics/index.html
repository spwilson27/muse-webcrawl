Microplastics

Microplastics are pieces of plastic less than 5 millimetres (0.20 in) in length, down to about 1 micrometre. They are an increasingly prominent form of marine and freshwater debris and have been detected in ocean water, sediments, soil, air, drinking water, food and human tissues. The term was popularised after Richard Thompson and colleagues reported accumulating microscopic plastic fragments in British beach sediments in 2004.

Sources

Primary microplastics are manufactured at small size, including industrial resin pellets (nurdles), abrasive microbeads once used in cosmetics and cleansers, and plastic powders. Secondary microplastics arise from the breakdown of larger items — bottles, bags, fishing gear and textiles — by sunlight, abrasion and wave action. Washing synthetic clothing releases large numbers of fibres, and wear of vehicle tyres is estimated to be among the largest sources to the environment.

Distribution

Microplastics are found from Arctic sea ice and deep-ocean sediments to remote mountain air and agricultural soils amended with sewage sludge. They concentrate in ocean gyres but are by no means confined to them; rivers are major pathways from land to sea.

Effects

Small organisms ingest microplastics, which have been found throughout marine food webs; whether particles routinely biomagnify is debated. Plastics can sorb persistent organic pollutants and contain chemical additives such as plasticisers and flame retardants, which may leach. Studies since 2022 have reported microplastics in human blood, lung tissue and placentas; the health significance for humans remains under investigation.

Responses

Several jurisdictions have banned rinse-off cosmetic microbeads (including the United States' Microbead-Free Waters Act of 2015 and similar UK and EU measures), and the European Union adopted restrictions on intentionally added microplastics in 2023. Mitigation also includes improved waste management, washing-machine filters and product redesign.
