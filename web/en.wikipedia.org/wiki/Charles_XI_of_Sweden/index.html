Charles XI (Swedish: Karl XI; 1655–1697) was King of Sweden from 1660 until his death. Coming to the throne as a small child, he assumed personal rule after a regency dominated by the high aristocracy, survived the Scanian War against Denmark, and then carried out sweeping reforms — the Great Reduction and the establishment of near-absolute monarchy — that reshaped the Swedish state.

Charles XI, King of Sweden

Regency and accession

Charles became king at age four on the death of his father, Charles X Gustav. A regency council led by Chancellor Magnus Gabriel De la Gardie governed Sweden, accumulating debts and granting away crown lands to the nobility. Charles was declared of age in 1672, but real power followed only from the emergencies of war.

Scanian War

In 1675 Denmark invaded Scania to recover the provinces lost in 1658, beginning the Scanian War (1675–1679). The young king proved himself a capable field commander, winning the bloody Battle of Lund in December 1676, one of the bloodiest battles in Scandinavian history, and the Battle of Landskrona. Peace settlements at Lund and Saint-Germain restored the status quo.

The Reduction and absolute monarchy

Blaming the regency aristocracy for Sweden's near-collapse, Charles used the Riksdag of 1680 to launch the Great Reduction (reduktionen), reclaiming for the crown vast estates previously alienated to the nobility. Successive Riksdags declared that the king was bound by no authority but God, effectively creating an absolute monarchy. With the recovered revenues Charles rebuilt the military through the allotment system (indelningsverket), reformed the administration and judiciary, and restored state finances while avoiding foreign wars for the rest of his reign.

Death and legacy

Charles XI died in 1697 of stomach cancer, having concealed his illness. He left his fifteen-year-old son, Charles XII, a solvent, well-armed, and tightly governed kingdom — though one whose absolutist settlement and Baltic empire would be tested almost immediately in the Great Northern War.
