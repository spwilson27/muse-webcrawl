Recklessness (law)

Recklessness is a state of mind in law in which an individual proceeds with a course of action despite knowing, or — in some formulations — despite the fact that they ought to know, that the action creates an unjustifiable risk of harm. In criminal law it is a form of mens rea less culpable than intention but more culpable than negligence.

Subjective and objective tests

Under the subjective test, the defendant must personally have foreseen the risk and unjustifiably taken it. The classic English formulation came from R v Cunningham (1957). In Metropolitan Police Commissioner v Caldwell (1982) the House of Lords adopted an objective test for criminal damage — a defendant was reckless if he failed to consider an obvious risk — which was widely criticised for convicting those incapable of foreseeing risks, and was overruled in R v G (2003), restoring subjectivity, in a case involving two boys who set fire to newspapers behind a shop.

United States

The Model Penal Code defines recklessness as conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk, involving a gross deviation from the standard of conduct of a law-abiding person. It is the default mens rea for offences such as reckless driving, reckless endangerment, and some forms of manslaughter.

Tort law

In tort, recklessness or "wilful and wanton" conduct sits between negligence and intentional wrongdoing and may defeat certain immunities and support punitive damages.
