The Year of the Five Emperors was AD 193, in which five men claimed the title of Roman emperor. The year opened a period of civil war that ended the Antonine dynasty's orderly succession and brought the Severan dynasty to power. The five were Pertinax, Didius Julianus, Pescennius Niger, Clodius Albinus and Septimius Severus.

Sequence of events

After the assassination of the tyrannical Commodus on 31 December 192, the Senate proclaimed the elderly city prefect Pertinax emperor. Pertinax's attempts to restore discipline and the treasury alienated the Praetorian Guard, who murdered him after only 86 days. The Guard then notoriously auctioned the empire to the highest bidder: the wealthy senator Didius Julianus outbid Pertinax's father-in-law by offering 25,000 sesterces per soldier. Public outrage followed, and three provincial commanders were proclaimed emperor by their armies: Septimius Severus in Pannonia, Pescennius Niger in Syria and Clodius Albinus in Britain.

Civil war and Severan victory

Severus, nearest to Rome, marched on the capital; Julianus was abandoned and executed after a reign of 66 days. Severus recognised Albinus as Caesar temporarily, then defeated Niger in the east (decisively at Issus in 194) and finally crushed Albinus at the Battle of Lugdunum in 197, one of the largest battles ever fought between Roman armies. Severus thus secured the throne and founded the Severan dynasty, ruling until 211.

Significance

The year exposed the fatal weakness of imperial succession without a settled mechanism and previews the third-century crisis: military power now openly made emperors. Edward Gibbon treated 193 as the turning point after the age of the "five good emperors".
