Percival (also spelled Perceval, Parsifal, Parzival) is one of the most famous knights of King Arthur's Round Table in Arthurian literature. He is best known for his role in the quest for the Holy Grail, of which he is the original hero in the earliest Grail romance, Chrétien de Troyes' unfinished Perceval, the Story of the Grail (c. 1181–1190). In later tradition he is sometimes displaced by Galahad as the knight who achieves the Grail, but he remains one of its principal achievers in many versions.

Origins

Percival is often identified with the Welsh hero Peredur, protagonist of the Welsh tale Peredur son of Efrawg in the Mabinogion, which shares many incidents with Chrétien's poem. Chrétien mentions Percival only briefly in his earlier romance Erec and Enide before making him the hero of the Grail story. His name is sometimes folk-etymologised as "pierce the valley" (percer val), an explanation already offered by medieval authors.

The Grail quest

Raised in the Welsh forest by his widowed mother, who hoped to keep him from the fate of his father and brothers, Percival grows up ignorant of knighthood. His journey to Arthur's court, his education by Gornemant, his love for Blancheflor, and his failure to ask the healing question at the castle of the Fisher King form the core of Chrétien's poem. Wolfram von Eschenbach's Middle High German Parzival (c. 1200–1210) completed the story: Parzival returns to the Grail castle, asks the question, heals King Anfortas and succeeds him as Grail king. In the French prose cycles and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Percival, together with Galahad and Bors, achieves the Grail at Sarras.

Later portrayals

Richard Wagner's final opera Parsifal (1882) made the "pure fool, enlightened by compassion" the centre of a religious drama. Percival has since appeared in countless novels, films and games, usually embodying innocence progressing toward wisdom and spiritual maturity.
