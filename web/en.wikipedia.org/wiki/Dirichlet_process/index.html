Dirichlet process

In probability theory, Dirichlet processes (after Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet) are a family of stochastic processes whose realizations are probability distributions. In other words, a Dirichlet process is a probability distribution whose range is itself a set of probability distributions. It is often used in Bayesian inference to describe prior knowledge about the distribution of random variables — how likely it is that random variables are drawn from one distribution or another.

Definition

A Dirichlet process is specified by a base distribution H and a positive real number α called the concentration parameter. A random probability measure G is distributed according to the Dirichlet process, written G ~ DP(α, H), if for every finite measurable partition of the sample space, the vector of probabilities assigned to the parts follows a Dirichlet distribution with parameters given by α times the base measure of each part.

Properties

Draws from a Dirichlet process are discrete probability distributions with probability one, even if the base distribution is continuous. This discreteness is what makes the Dirichlet process useful for clustering: repeated samples from a single draw G can coincide, naturally grouping observations into clusters. The concentration parameter α controls how strongly draws resemble the base distribution: larger α gives draws closer to H and more distinct values among samples.

Representations

Two constructive representations are widely used:

  • The stick-breaking process, in which the weights of the atoms of G are formed by repeatedly breaking fractions off a unit-length stick according to a beta distribution.
  • The Chinese restaurant process, which describes the clustering of a sequence of samples marginally, without explicitly constructing G: customers entering a restaurant sit at occupied tables with probability proportional to their occupancy, and at a new table with probability proportional to α.

Applications

The Dirichlet process is the foundation of Dirichlet process mixture models, which allow mixture models with a potentially infinite number of components, and it plays a central role in Bayesian nonparametric statistics more generally. Extensions include the hierarchical Dirichlet process, used for sharing clusters across related groups of data, as in topic modeling.
