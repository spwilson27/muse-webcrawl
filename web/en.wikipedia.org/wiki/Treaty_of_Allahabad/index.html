Treaty of Allahabad
Signed12 August 1765 (with the Mughal emperor); 16 August 1765 (with Awadh)
LocationAllahabad, India
PartiesBritish East India Company; Mughal emperor Shah Alam II; Nawab of Awadh
Key resultGrant of the Diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa to the Company

The Treaty of Allahabad was concluded in August 1765 between the British East India Company, represented by Robert Clive, and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II and the Nawab of Awadh, following the Company's victory at the Battle of Buxar (1764) and its earlier success at Plassey (1757).

Terms

By the treaty, Shah Alam II granted the Company the Diwani — the right to collect the land revenue — of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, in return for an annual tribute of 26 lakh rupees and the districts of Kora and Allahabad. The Nawab of Awadh, Shuja-ud-Daula, paid a war indemnity of 50 lakh rupees and accepted a Company subsidiary force. The Nawab of Bengal retained formal judicial and police functions (the Nizamat), producing the "dual system" of administration in Bengal.

Significance

The treaty is regarded as the legal foundation of British rule in India: it transformed a trading company into the revenue-collecting sovereign of India's richest province, giving it the income with which to finance its armies and its trade, while leaving a façade of Mughal authority in place.
