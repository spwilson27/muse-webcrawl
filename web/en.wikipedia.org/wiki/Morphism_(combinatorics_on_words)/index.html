Morphism (combinatorics on words)

In combinatorics on words and formal language theory, a morphism is a mapping from a free monoid to another free monoid that respects concatenation. Formally, given alphabets Σ and Γ, a morphism is a function h : Σ* → Γ* such that h(uv) = h(u)h(v) for all words u, v, and h(ε) = ε, where ε is the empty word. A morphism is completely determined by its values on the individual letters of Σ.

Special classes of morphisms arise throughout the field. A morphism is uniform (or k-uniform) if every letter maps to a word of the same length k; it is non-erasing if no letter maps to the empty word. A morphism can be iterated on a starting letter to generate a fixed point — an infinite word obtained as the limit of successive applications — provided the morphism is prolongable on that letter. Such morphic sequences include the Thue–Morse sequence, generated by 0 → 01, 1 → 10, and the Fibonacci word, generated by a → ab, b → a.

Morphisms underlie the theory of automatic and morphic sequences, D0L systems (Lindenmayer systems), and the study of word equations and avoidability of patterns, all central topics in combinatorics on words.
