Esek Hopkins

Esek Hopkins (April 26, 1718 – February 26, 1802) was the first and only commander-in-chief of the Continental Navy during the American Revolutionary War.

Esek Hopkins (April 26, 1718 – February 26, 1802) was an American naval officer who served as the first — and only — Commander in Chief of the Continental Navy during the American Revolutionary War. A Rhode Island sea captain and slave trader with forty years of maritime experience, he led the young navy's first expedition, the successful raid on Nassau in 1776, but his later inactivity and quarrels with Congress led to his dismissal in 1778.

Early life

Hopkins was born in Scituate, Rhode Island, into a prominent family; his brother Stephen Hopkins would sign the Declaration of Independence. Esek went to sea at twenty and rose to captain merchant vessels trading around the Atlantic world, including voyages in the transatlantic slave trade — notably as captain of the brig Sally in 1764–65, a disastrous slaving voyage for the Brown family of Providence in which 109 of 196 captive Africans died. During the French and Indian War he commanded privateers with success and profit.

Commander in chief

In December 1775 Congress appointed Hopkins commander in chief of the Continental Navy, senior to all its captains. Ordered to attack British forces in the Chesapeake and the Carolinas, he judged those missions impracticable and instead sailed for the Bahamas, where on 3–4 March 1776 his squadron landed Continental Marines — their first amphibious operation — and captured Nassau, seizing badly needed gunpowder and cannon, though the bulk of the powder had been spirited away. On the return voyage his ships fought HMS Glasgow inconclusively, an action for which several captains were criticised.

Decline and dismissal

Thereafter Hopkins's fleet was largely blockaded in Narragansett Bay by superior British forces, and Congress grew impatient with his failure to put to sea, his disregard of orders, and his outspoken contempt — he called Congress "a pack of damned fools" in correspondence that reached his enemies. Suspended in March 1777, he was dismissed from the navy on 2 January 1778, the only holder of his title. A resulting libel suit by one of his officers, midshipman James Sellers, prompted Congress to pass America's first whistleblower protection law in 1778. Hopkins served in the Rhode Island legislature and assembly into old age and died at his farm in North Providence in 1802. The US Navy has named destroyers for him.
