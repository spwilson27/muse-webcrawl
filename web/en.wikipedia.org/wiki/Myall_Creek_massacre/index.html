Myall Creek massacre

Location: Myall Creek, near Bingara, New South Wales, Australia
Date: 10 June 1838
Victims: At least 28 unarmed Aboriginal Australians
Perpetrators: 12 stockmen led by John Russell

The Myall Creek massacre was the killing of at least 28 unarmed Aboriginal Australians by 12 settlers on 10 June 1838 at Myall Creek, near Bingara in northern New South Wales, Australia. It is notable as one of the few frontier massacres in Australia for which white perpetrators were tried and executed: seven of the men were convicted of murder and hanged.

The massacre

A party of Aboriginal people from the Wirrayaraay band had been camped peacefully at Myall Creek station for some weeks at the invitation of the station manager. On 10 June 1838, a group of twelve stockmen arrived, tied the Aboriginal people together with rope, led them to a gully and murdered them with swords and guns. The victims were mostly women, children and older men.

Trials and executions

The station manager reported the killings, and the Attorney-General of New South Wales, John Plunkett, prosecuted the men. A first trial ended in acquittal, but a second jury convicted seven of the twelve of the murder of one child, and they were hanged on 18 December 1838. The ringleader, John Russell, was never brought to trial for the massacre.

Legacy

The case is remembered as a landmark in Australian colonial legal history because it demonstrated, unusually for the period, that the law could protect Aboriginal people. A memorial at the site, opened in 2001, commemorates the victims and the reconciliation efforts that followed.
