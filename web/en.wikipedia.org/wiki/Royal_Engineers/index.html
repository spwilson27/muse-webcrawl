The Corps of Royal Engineers, usually called the Royal Engineers (RE) and commonly known as the Sappers, is the engineering arm of the British Army. It provides military engineering and technical support — bridging, demolitions, water and power supply, route clearance, construction, surveying and explosive ordnance disposal — to enable the Army to live, move and fight.

History

Military engineers served the English Crown from Norman times, but the modern corps traces its origins to the Board of Ordnance and the Corps of Engineers formed in 1716 at Woolwich; the rank and file, the Royal Military Artificers, followed in 1772. The corps received the "Royal" title in 1787, and officers and other ranks were amalgamated in 1856. Royal Engineers built railways, docks and fortifications across the Empire, ran the early military ballooning and aviation (predecessor of the Royal Flying Corps), the Army postal and signals services, tunnelling companies in the First World War, and Mulberry harbours and Bailey bridges in the Second.

The corps today

Regiments are based at Chatham, Wimbish, Ripon, Perham Down and elsewhere, with trades ranging from combat engineer and plant operator to draughtsman, electrician and bomb disposal specialist. The Royal Engineers Museum is at Gillingham, and the corps' motto Ubique — granted by William IV in 1832 in place of individual battle honours — reflects service in virtually every campaign.
