Visual snow

Simulated visual snow over a scene

Visual snow is a visual disturbance in which people see persistent white, black or transparent dots — resembling the static of an untuned television — across the whole visual field, in both eyes, even in darkness. When accompanied by additional symptoms such as palinopsia (afterimages), enhanced entoptic phenomena, photophobia and impaired night vision (nyctalopia), it is termed visual snow syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

The static is continuous and unrelenting for sufferers of the syndrome, persisting for months or years. Associated features frequently include migraine, tinnitus, tremor, floaters and trails behind moving objects. Standard ophthalmological examinations — visual acuity, fundoscopy and visual fields — are usually normal, which historically led to the condition being dismissed or misdiagnosed as migraine aura, anxiety or a persistent perceptual disturbance following drug use.

Causes and mechanisms

The cause is unknown. Current hypotheses invoke hyperexcitability of the visual cortex and dysfunction of higher-order visual processing, including the lingual gyrus and thalamocortical pathways; PET imaging studies have reported hypermetabolism in the right lingual gyrus in patients. Visual snow syndrome overlaps strongly with migraine and with hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD), but occurs in many patients with neither history.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnostic criteria proposed by Schankin and colleagues require dynamic, continuous dots across the entire visual field lasting more than three months plus at least two additional visual symptoms, with symptoms not consistent with typical migraine aura and not better explained by another disorder. There is no established cure; management includes tinted (e.g. FL-41) lenses, treatment of comorbid migraine and anxiety, and avoidance of triggers. Some medications used for migraine, such as lamotrigine, have been reported to help a minority of patients.
