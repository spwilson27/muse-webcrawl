The Church of the Holy Cross (Armenian: Սուրբ Խաչ եկեղեցի, Surb Khach yekeghetsi; Turkish: Akdamar Kilisesi) is a medieval Armenian Apostolic cathedral on Akdamar Island in Lake Van, eastern Turkey. Built between 915 and 921 by the architect-monk Manuel for King Gagik I Artsruni of Vaspurakan, it is celebrated as a masterpiece of medieval Armenian art for the elaborate relief carvings covering its exterior walls.

[Image: The Church of the Holy Cross on Akdamar Island, Lake Van]

History

The church formed part of a palace and monastic complex built when Gagik I made Akdamar his royal residence. It later became the seat of the Catholicosate of Aghtamar, an Armenian catholicosate that persisted until 1895. The monastery remained active until 1915, when its monks were killed and the buildings plundered during the Armenian genocide. The church thereafter stood abandoned and deteriorating for decades.

Architecture and sculpture

The church is a domed, centrally planned building of pink-red tuff, about 15 by 12 metres. Its exterior is sheathed in bands of high-relief sculpture unique in medieval Christian art: scenes from the Old and New Testaments — Jonah cast into the sea, David and Goliath, Adam and Eve, Daniel in the lions' den, Samson, the Three Hebrews in the furnace — intertwine with vine scrolls, animals, court figures and a portrait of King Gagik presenting a model of the church to Christ.

Restoration and present status

After decades of advocacy, the Turkish government restored the church in 2005–2006 at a cost of about 1.4 million US dollars, reopening it in March 2007 as a museum. Since 2010 an annual Armenian liturgy has been permitted in the building, the first in 95 years, though its status as a secular museum rather than an active church remains contested by Armenian religious authorities. A cross was installed on the dome in October 2010.
