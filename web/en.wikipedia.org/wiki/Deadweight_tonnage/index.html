Deadweight tonnage

[Image: Bulk carrier loaded to its Plimsoll mark, showing deadweight capacity in use]

Deadweight tonnage or DWT (also known as deadweight; abbreviation DWAT, DWT, TDW, or dwt) is a measure of how much weight a ship can carry. It is the sum of the weights of cargo, fuel, fresh water, ballast water, provisions, passengers, and crew, and is defined as the difference between the ship's displacement when loaded to its summer load line (maximum permitted draft) and its lightship displacement (the weight of the ship itself with no cargo or stores).

Definition and use

Deadweight tonnage is a measure of weight, not volume, and is expressed in tonnes (metric tons), long tons, or short tons depending on convention. It is the standard commercial measure used to describe the size of cargo ships: tankers and bulk carriers are commonly classified by ranges of deadweight, such as Handysize (roughly 15,000–35,000 DWT), Panamax, Capesize, and Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs, roughly 200,000–320,000 DWT). The largest vessels ever built, the Seawise Giant and the Batillus-class supertankers, exceeded 550,000 DWT.

DWT should not be confused with gross tonnage or net tonnage, which are measures of a ship's internal volume used for registration and port dues, nor with displacement tonnage, used mainly for warships.
