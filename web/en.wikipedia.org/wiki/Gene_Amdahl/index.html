Gene Myron Amdahl (November 16, 1922 – November 10, 2015) was an American computer architect and high-tech entrepreneur, chiefly known for his work at IBM and for Amdahl's law, a fundamental principle of parallel computing. At IBM he was a principal architect of the System/360 family of mainframe computers, one of the most influential computer designs in history.

Career

Born in Flandreau, South Dakota, Amdahl earned his doctorate in theoretical physics from the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1952, designing an early computer, the WISC, for his thesis. At IBM he worked on the IBM 704 and the Stretch project before leading the architecture of System/360, launched in 1964. In 1970 he left IBM to found the Amdahl Corporation, which competed directly with IBM by selling plug-compatible mainframes that were faster and cheaper; its first machine, the Amdahl 470V/6 (1975), was a landmark of the plug-compatible industry.

Amdahl's law

In a 1967 paper, Amdahl argued that the speedup from parallelising a computation is limited by the fraction that must remain serial. If a fraction s of a task is inherently sequential, the maximum speedup from any number of processors is bounded by 1/s. The argument, initially controversial, became a standard framework for evaluating parallel architectures and remains a staple of computer science education.
