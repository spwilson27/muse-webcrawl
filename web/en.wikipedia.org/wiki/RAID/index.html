RAID

RAID (originally Redundant Array of Inexpensive Disks, later Redundant Array of Independent Disks) is a data storage virtualization technology that combines multiple physical disk drive components into one or more logical units for the purposes of data redundancy, performance improvement, or both. The concept was defined in a 1987 paper by David Patterson, Garth Gibson, and Randy Katz at the University of California, Berkeley.

Standard levels

  • RAID 0 — striping: data is split across drives for performance, with no redundancy.
  • RAID 1 — mirroring: data is duplicated on two or more drives.
  • RAID 5 — striping with distributed parity: tolerates one drive failure; requires at least three drives.
  • RAID 6 — striping with double distributed parity: tolerates two drive failures.
  • RAID 10 — a stripe of mirrors, combining performance and redundancy.

Implementation

RAID can be implemented in dedicated hardware controllers, in software by the operating system, or in firmware/driver-based ("fake RAID") form. Hardware controllers may include battery-backed cache to protect data in flight. Rebuilding a failed array member can take many hours on large drives, during which the array is vulnerable or degraded; this has motivated parity schemes beyond RAID 5 for large disks.

Limitations

RAID protects against drive failure but is not a backup: it does not protect against deletion, corruption, malware, or site disasters. Modern alternatives and successors include erasure coding in distributed storage systems and filesystems such as ZFS with RAID-Z.
