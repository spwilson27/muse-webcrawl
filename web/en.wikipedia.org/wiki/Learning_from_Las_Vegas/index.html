Learning from Las Vegas
Authors: Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, Steven Izenour
Published: 1972 (MIT Press)
Subject: Postmodern architecture

Learning from Las Vegas is a 1972 book by Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour that became a foundational text of postmodern architecture. It grew out of a 1968 Yale School of Architecture studio in which the authors and their students studied the Las Vegas Strip.

Argument

The book argues that the commercial strip—with its signs, parking lots, and low, sprawling casino buildings—embodies a legitimate architectural language suited to the automobile age, and that architects should learn from the existing landscape rather than impose heroic modernist ideals upon it. Its most famous distinction is between the "duck", a building that is itself a symbol (named after the Long Island Duckling, a duck-shaped shop), and the "decorated shed", a conventional building with ornament and signage applied to its front. The authors contend that most successful buildings, including many admired monuments, are decorated sheds, whereas modernist architecture, in denying ornament while striving for sculptural expression, produced expensive and dysfunctional ducks.

Influence

Alongside Venturi's earlier Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966), the book challenged orthodox modernism and influenced generations of architects. A revised edition, Learning from Las Vegas: The Forgotten Symbolism of Architectural Form, appeared in 1977. Critics initially attacked the book as an apologia for sprawl and commercial vulgarity, but it is now widely regarded as a landmark of 20th-century architectural theory.
