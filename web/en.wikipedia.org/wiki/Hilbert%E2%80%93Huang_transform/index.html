Hilbert–Huang transform

The Hilbert–Huang transform (HHT) is a signal-processing method designed to analyze nonlinear and non-stationary data. It was developed by Norden E. Huang and colleagues at NASA in the late 1990s, and consists of two parts: empirical mode decomposition (EMD) and Hilbert spectral analysis.

Method

EMD adaptively decomposes a signal into a finite set of intrinsic mode functions (IMFs) — oscillatory components derived from the data itself rather than from a fixed basis such as sines (Fourier) or wavelets — through an iterative "sifting" process based on local extrema and envelope interpolation. Applying the Hilbert transform to each IMF yields instantaneous amplitude and frequency, giving a sharp time–frequency–energy representation called the Hilbert spectrum.

Applications and issues

HHT has been widely used in ocean engineering, seismology, biomedical signals, structural health monitoring, and finance. Because it is adaptive and local, it suits data generated by nonlinear, non-stationary processes. Criticisms focus on its largely empirical character: mode mixing, end effects, and sensitivity to noise and sampling. Ensemble EMD (EEMD) and related variants were proposed to mitigate mode mixing, and mathematical foundations for EMD remain an active research topic.
