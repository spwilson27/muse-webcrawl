Postmillennialism

In Christian eschatology, postmillennialism is the interpretation of chapter 20 of the Book of Revelation holding that Christ's second coming will occur after (Latin post-) a "Millennium" — a golden age in which the gospel progressively triumphs, Christian ethics prosper, and the world is broadly Christianized. The Millennium need not be a literal thousand years; it is an extended era of righteousness and peace brought about through preaching, conversion, and the gradual transformation of society, at whose climax Christ returns to raise the dead and judge the world.

History

Elements appear in Eusebius and Augustine's heirs, but the view crystallized in post-Reformation Protestantism, notably in the works of Daniel Whitby (1703) and the American Great Awakening theologian Jonathan Edwards, who expected revival to usher in the latter-day glory. Postmillennial optimism fueled nineteenth-century missions and reform movements — abolitionism, temperance, and the Social Gospel — and was the majority view among American Presbyterians and many others until the devastation of the World Wars drove many toward premillennialism and amillennialism.

Modern forms

Loraine Boettner's The Millennium (1957) defended the view in the twentieth century, while Rousas John Rushdoony and Christian Reconstructionists gave it a theonomic form expecting biblical law to shape civil society. A "partial preterist" reading — locating many prophecies in the first century — is common among contemporary postmillennialists such as R. C. Sproul and Kenneth Gentry.

Distinctions

Unlike premillennialism, postmillennialism expects conditions to improve rather than deteriorate before Christ's return, and unlike amillennialism, it expects a distinct, future golden age rather than identifying the Millennium with the present church age.
