Old Norse (Old Norse: dǫnsk tunga, "the Danish tongue"; norrœnt mál, "the northern speech") was a North Germanic language spoken by inhabitants of Scandinavia and their overseas settlements during and after the Viking Age, from about the 8th century to the 15th century. It is the ancestor of the modern North Germanic languages: Icelandic, Faroese, Norwegian, Danish, Swedish, and Elfdalian.

Dialects and development

Old Norse developed from Proto-Norse around the 8th century and gradually split into Old West Norse (Norway, Iceland, the Faroes, and the Norse Atlantic) and Old East Norse (Denmark and Sweden), with Old Gutnish on Gotland a third branch. Conservative Icelandic has changed so little that modern Icelanders can read the sagas with modest effort, while Danish and Swedish underwent far greater simplification. Around the 15th century the continental languages entered their modern stages, ending the Old Norse period.

Literature

Old Norse literature is extraordinarily rich for a medieval vernacular. It includes the mythological and heroic poems of the Poetic Edda, the skaldic court poetry, Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda and Heimskringla, and above all the Icelandic sagas — prose narratives of kings, families, and outlaws written mainly in the 13th and 14th centuries. Runic inscriptions, from brief memorial formulas to long texts such as the Rök stone, provide the earliest records.

Features

Old Norse had four noun cases, three genders, and a suffixed definite article (e.g. hestr "horse", hestrinn "the horse"), a feature typical of Scandinavian languages. It lent many words to English through Viking settlement in the Danelaw — among them sky, egg, window, they, their, and law itself — and place-name elements such as -by and -thorpe survive across northern England.
