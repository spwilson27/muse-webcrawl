Second Battle of Artois

Date9 May – 18 June 1915
LocationArtois, France
BelligerentsFrance and the United Kingdom vs. the German Empire
ResultGerman defensive success; limited Allied gains

The Second Battle of Artois (French: Deuxième bataille de l'Artois) was a battle on the Western Front of the First World War fought from 9 May to 18 June 1915. The main effort was made by the French Tenth Army under General Victor d'Urbal, part of Joseph Joffre's spring offensive intended to break the German line in Artois and advance into the Douai plain, coordinated with a British attack by the First Army at Aubers Ridge and Festubert. Its central objective was Vimy Ridge, the commanding heights north of Arras.

The offensive

After a six-day bombardment, the French attacked on 9 May 1915. In the centre, the Moroccan Division and the French Foreign Legion broke through the German lines with remarkable speed, advancing about 4.5 kilometres (2.8 mi) in an hour and a half and reaching the crest of Vimy Ridge near the village of Givenchy-en-Gohelle — the deepest Allied penetration of 1915. The success could not be exploited: reserves were held too far back, communications broke down, and the flanks lagged behind, leaving the troops on the ridge exposed to enfilade fire. German reserves under Crown Prince Rupprecht of Bavaria counter-attacked, and the French were driven off most of the crest over the following days.

The British attacks at Aubers Ridge (9 May) and Festubert (15–25 May) gained little ground at heavy cost, the former failing almost completely within a single day, partly through a shortage of shells — the resulting "shell scandal" in Britain helped bring down the Asquith government and create a Ministry of Munitions.

Outcome

Fighting continued into mid-June around Souchez, Neuville-Saint-Vaast (the "Labyrinth"), and Ablain-Saint-Nazaire, where the ruined church became a famous landmark. The Allies gained a strip of territory roughly 3 kilometres deep on a narrow front but failed to take Vimy Ridge, which remained in German hands until the Canadian Corps captured it in April 1917. Casualties were severe: French losses were on the order of 100,000 men, British about 27,000, and German about 75,000. The battle demonstrated both the possibility of breaking the trench line and the near-impossibility, with 1915 methods, of converting a breakthrough into a strategic advance.
