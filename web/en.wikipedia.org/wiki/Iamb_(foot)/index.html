An iamb (or iambus) is a metrical foot used in various types of poetry. In classical Greek and Latin quantitative metre, an iamb consists of a short syllable followed by a long syllable (˘ ¯). In accentual-syllabic verse in English and similar languages, it consists of an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable — as in the words "a-BOVE", "at-TEMPT" and "be-LOW".

Use

The iamb is the most common foot in English verse. Five iambs in a row form iambic pentameter, the dominant metre of English poetry from Chaucer through Shakespeare's plays and sonnets to Milton and beyond. Other iambic measures include tetrameter (four feet) and the trimeter of ballads. Greek iambic trimeter was the standard metre of dialogue in tragedy and comedy, and the genre of "iambic" poetry in archaic Greece was associated with blame and invective — the word is traditionally linked to Iambe, a figure in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, and possibly to the verb iambizein, to lampoon.

Related feet

The reverse pattern (stressed–unstressed) is the trochee; other disyllabic and trisyllabic feet include the spondee, pyrrhic, anapaest and dactyl. A line of verse may substitute other feet for an iamb for emphasis or variety, a practice especially common at the start of a line.
