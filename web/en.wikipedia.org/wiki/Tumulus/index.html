Tumulus

A tumulus (plural tumuli) is a mound of earth and stones raised over a grave or graves. "Tumulus" is Latin for a small hill; in British archaeology the same structures are commonly called barrows, while similar mounds worldwide include the Japanese kofun and the burial mounds of North America's mound-building cultures.

Forms

Tumuli range from a few metres to over 80 metres across. They may cover a single central burial, multiple graves, cremations in urns, or timber or stone chambers; some, like the long barrows of Neolithic Europe, are elongated, while round barrows of the Bronze Age dominate much of the British landscape. The internal structure can include kerbs of stones, ditches from which the mound material was quarried, and secondary burials inserted later.

Examples

Notable tumuli include the ship-burial mound at Sutton Hoo in Suffolk; the great tumulus of Alyattes of Lydia in Turkey; the Etruscan tumuli of Cerveteri; Viking Age mounds in Scandinavia such as those at Borre and Birka; the royal tombs of Silla at Gyeongju, Korea; and the keyhole-shaped kofun of Japan, the largest of which, the Daisen Kofun attributed to Emperor Nintoku, is among the largest tombs in the world.

Archaeology

Because tumuli often preserved rich grave goods, they were prime targets of early antiquarians and treasure hunters; modern excavation emphasises their value as evidence for social hierarchy, ritual, and cosmology as much as for artefacts.
