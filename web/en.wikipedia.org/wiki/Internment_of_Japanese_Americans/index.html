Internment of Japanese Americans

The internment of Japanese Americans in the United States during World War II was the forced relocation and incarceration of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of whom lived on the Pacific Coast. Notably, about two-thirds were US citizens. These actions were ordered by President Franklin D. Roosevelt via Executive Order 9066, issued on 19 February 1942, shortly after the Imperial Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor.

Manzanar, in California's Owens Valley, held more than 10,000 incarcerees.

Background

Decades of anti-Japanese sentiment, alien land laws, and the 1924 Immigration Act preceded the war. After Pearl Harbor, military commander John L. DeWitt and political leaders pressed for mass removal, claiming military necessity, despite intelligence assessments finding no evidence of organized disloyalty. Japanese Americans were first subjected to curfews and "voluntary evacuation", then to assembly centers (often fairgrounds and racetracks) before transfer to ten War Relocation Authority camps in remote inland locations: Manzanar and Tule Lake (California), Poston and Gila River (Arizona), Topaz (Utah), Amache (Colorado), Heart Mountain (Wyoming), Minidoka (Idaho), and Jerome and Rohwer (Arkansas).

Life in the camps

Families lived in tar-paper barracks with communal facilities, under armed guard. Incarcerees worked for token wages, organized schools, newspapers, and sports, and farmed the surrounding land. A controversial 1943 "loyalty questionnaire" divided communities; some 5,500 renounced US citizenship under duress. Meanwhile the all-Nisei 442nd Regimental Combat Team became one of the most decorated US units, and other Japanese Americans served as linguists in the Military Intelligence Service. Draft resisters from Heart Mountain were prosecuted, and Tule Lake became a segregation center for those deemed "disloyal".

Legal challenges and release

The Supreme Court upheld the curfew in Hirabayashi v. United States (1943) and the exclusion order in Korematsu v. United States (1944), decisions now widely condemned; in Ex parte Endo (1944) the Court held that concededly loyal citizens could not be detained. The exclusion orders were rescinded in December 1944, and the last camp, Tule Lake, closed in March 1946.

Redress

The Commission on Wartime Relocation concluded in 1983 that the incarceration resulted from "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership". The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 provided a formal apology and $20,000 payments to surviving incarcerees. The wartime convictions of Korematsu, Hirabayashi and Yasui were vacated in the 1980s via coram nobis proceedings after evidence emerged that the government had suppressed exculpatory intelligence reports. Several camp sites are now National Historic Sites.
