Lewy body

Lewy bodies are abnormal aggregates of protein that develop inside neurons in certain neurodegenerative diseases. They are composed mainly of the protein alpha-synuclein associated with ubiquitin, neurofilament proteins, and other components. Lewy bodies are the pathological hallmark of Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, collectively termed Lewy body diseases.

History

They are named after the German neurologist Friedrich H. Lewy, who described them in 1912 while studying Parkinson's disease in the laboratory of Alois Alzheimer in Munich. For much of the 20th century they could be identified only in brainstem nuclei such as the substantia nigra; the discovery in 1997 that alpha-synuclein is their main constituent, together with immunohistochemistry, revealed their widespread cortical distribution.

Appearance and distribution

Classical brainstem Lewy bodies are spherical, eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions 5–25 micrometres across with a dense core and pale halo that displace the nucleus. Cortical Lewy bodies are less well defined and lack the halo. Abnormal filamentous aggregates in neuronal processes are called Lewy neurites. The Braak staging scheme describes a progression of Lewy pathology from the lower brainstem through the substantia nigra to the cerebral cortex.

Role in disease

Lewy bodies are thought to arise when misfolded alpha-synuclein accumulates beyond the capacity of cellular clearance systems. Whether the mature bodies are themselves toxic or are protective sequestration of more toxic oligomers remains debated. Their presence in neurons correlates with cell loss, and synucleinopathies also include multiple system atrophy, whose inclusions occur in glia.
