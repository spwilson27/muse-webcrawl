Disk encryption theory

Disk encryption theory is the study of cryptographic methods for encrypting data on storage devices sector by sector, as in full disk encryption. Unlike file encryption, disk encryption must operate within strict constraints: data must be accessible in fixed-size blocks (typically 512 bytes or 4 KiB) at arbitrary positions, the ciphertext can occupy no more space than the plaintext (there is nowhere to store an initialization vector or authentication tag per sector), and access must be fast and random.

Tweakable block ciphers

The standard solution is the tweakable block cipher, in which the sector address serves as a public "tweak" that diversifies the encryption without requiring extra storage. The dominant constructions are XTS, standardized in IEEE 1619-2007 and used by BitLocker, FileVault, and dm-crypt, and its predecessor XEX (XOR–encrypt–XOR). XTS combines two keys with AES and multiplies a tweak value in the finite field GF(2128) for each block within a sector. Earlier modes such as CBC with sector-derived IVs suffered from watermarking attacks and predictable-IV weaknesses, and LRW (Liskov–Rivest–Wagner) was largely displaced by XTS after concerns over key collisions across sectors.

Security notions and limitations

Disk encryption provides confidentiality against an adversary who obtains the powered-off device, but standard sector modes are length-preserving and unauthenticated: they are malleable, leak equality of ciphertext blocks moved between tweaks, and do not detect tampering. They also do not protect data while the system is running and unlocked. Wide-block modes such as AES-SIV derivatives and the Adiantum construction (used on low-end Android devices) aim to improve diffusion so that changing one plaintext bit garbles an entire sector. Cold boot attacks, evil maid attacks, and DMA attacks target keys in memory rather than the cipher itself and are mitigated with hardware key storage and pre-boot authentication.
