Caroline Amelia Elizabeth of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel (17 May 1768 – 7 August 1821) was the wife of King George IV of the United Kingdom from their marriage on 8 April 1795 until her death in 1821. She was Princess of Wales from 1795 to 1820. The marriage was a notorious failure: George detested Caroline on sight, and the couple separated after the birth of their only child, Princess Charlotte, in 1796. Caroline spent much of her later life abroad, chiefly in Italy.

Marriage and separation

Caroline was a daughter of Charles William Ferdinand, Duke of Brunswick, and Princess Augusta of Great Britain, making her a first cousin of her husband. The marriage was arranged so that the Prince of Wales could settle his enormous debts with an increased parliamentary allowance. The couple reportedly took an instant mutual dislike to one another at their first meeting, and George spent the wedding night drunk. After Charlotte's birth they formally separated, and Caroline's conduct became the subject of the 1806 "Delicate Investigation" into alleged adultery, which found evidence of impropriety but not of the gravest charges.

The Queen Caroline affair

On George's accession in January 1820, Caroline returned to England to claim her rights as queen consort. The government, at the King's insistence, introduced the Pains and Penalties Bill 1820 in the House of Lords to annul the marriage and deprive her of her title, based on evidence of adultery with her Italian servant Bartolomeo Pergami gathered by the so-called Milan Commission. The resulting trial was a public sensation: Caroline enjoyed massive popular support as a wronged woman, while the King was deeply unpopular. The Bill passed its third reading by only nine votes and was abandoned by Lord Liverpool's government. Caroline was nevertheless barred from the coronation at Westminster Abbey in July 1821 and died less than three weeks later, reportedly of an intestinal obstruction. Her funeral procession sparked serious rioting in London.

Legacy

The affair badly damaged the prestige of the monarchy and the government, and Caroline remains a byword for a royal marriage gone spectacularly wrong. Her only child, Charlotte, heir presumptive to the throne, had died in childbirth in 1817.
