Mammalian diving reflex

The mammalian diving reflex, or diving response, is a set of physiological responses to immersion that overrides the basic homeostatic reflexes. It is found in all air-breathing vertebrates studied, and is most strongly developed in aquatic mammals such as seals, dolphins, and whales, though it also occurs in humans, particularly infants.

Components

The reflex is triggered principally by cold water contacting the face (via the trigeminal nerve) and by breath-holding (apnea). Its three main components are:

  • Bradycardia — a marked slowing of the heart rate, which in humans may fall by 10–30% and in seals by far more.
  • Peripheral vasoconstriction — constriction of blood vessels in the limbs and peripheral tissues, redirecting blood to the heart, lungs, and brain.
  • Blood shift — in deep-diving animals, blood plasma fills the thoracic cavity's blood vessels, preventing collapse of the lungs under pressure.

Splenic contraction, which releases stored red blood cells into circulation, also occurs in some species.

Function

By reducing heart rate and restricting blood flow to oxygen-tolerant tissues, the reflex conserves the oxygen stored in the blood for the heart and brain, extending the time an animal can remain submerged. In humans the reflex contributes to the survival of some victims of cold-water drowning after prolonged submersion, and it can be invoked clinically — splashing cold water on the face is a vagal maneuver used to terminate some supraventricular tachycardias.
