Urban decay

Vacant and deteriorating buildings are the visible symptom of urban decay

Urban decay (also known as urban rot, urban death, or urban blight) is the process by which a previously functioning city or neighbourhood falls into disrepair and decrepitude. It is characterized by depopulation, abandoned and derelict buildings, high unemployment and crime, fragmented families, and a deteriorating built environment and tax base. Urban decay has no single cause; it typically results from interacting economic, demographic, and policy factors over decades.

Causes

Common contributors include deindustrialization and the loss of manufacturing employment; suburbanization and white flight, which drew wealthier residents and retail to the suburbs; highway construction and urban renewal programmes that demolished or divided neighbourhoods; redlining and disinvestment, which starved districts of credit; depopulation as households shrank or left declining regions (as in the American Rust Belt or post-socialist cities in eastern Germany); and changes in transport and technology that made old industrial locations obsolete.

Effects

Visible effects include vacant housing and "broken windows", failing infrastructure, reduced municipal services as the tax base erodes, and concentrated poverty. The broken windows theory proposed that visible signs of disorder encourage further crime and neglect, though the size and direction of that effect remain debated. Abandoned structures attract vandalism, squatting, and arson, further depressing neighbouring property values in a self-reinforcing cycle.

Responses

Responses have included urban renewal (often criticized for demolishing viable communities), historic preservation, gentrification (with its own controversy over displacement), community development corporations, land banks that return tax-delinquent property to productive use, and "right-sizing" strategies in shrinking cities such as Detroit, which declared the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history in 2013. A related phenomenon is shrinking cities, where long-term population loss is treated as a planning condition rather than a temporary downturn.
