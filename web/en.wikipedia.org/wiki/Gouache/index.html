Gouache

Gouache (from Italian guazzo, "water paint") is a type of opaque water-based paint, consisting of pigment, water and a binding agent such as gum arabic, modified with a white filler — chalk or white pigment — that gives it a matte, velvety finish and allows it to be painted in flat, solid layers over dark grounds. Closely related to watercolour but with a higher pigment-to-binder ratio and added opacity, gouache has been used since the Middle Ages for manuscript illumination and in the 20th century became a standard medium for illustrators, poster artists and designers ("poster paint").

Properties and technique

Unlike transparent watercolour, gouache can be worked from dark to light, covers underlying layers, and dries to a uniform matte surface that photographs and scans evenly — qualities that made it the medium of choice for commercial illustration before digital art. It remains water-soluble after drying and is prone to value shifts as it dries (dark colours dry lighter, light colours darker), and thick layers may crack. Because it is applied on paper or board, gouache works are usually framed under glass.

History

Opaque watercolour (bodycolour) appears in medieval Persian, Indian and European miniatures; the term gouache gained currency in 18th- and 19th-century France. Artists such as J. M. W. Turner used bodycolour extensively, and in the 20th century gouache served illustrators, animation background painters and poster designers; modern "acrylic gouache" binds the pigment in acrylic polymer for a waterproof matte finish.
