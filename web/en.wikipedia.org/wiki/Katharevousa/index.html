Katharevousa (Greek: Καθαρεύουσα, "purifying [language]") is a conservative form of the Modern Greek language conceived in the late 18th century as a compromise between Ancient Greek and the contemporary spoken vernacular, Demotic. It was the official language of the Greek state from independence until 1976 and stands at the center of the long-running Greek language question.

Origins

The scholar Adamantios Korais (1748–1833) proposed a "middle way": cleansing the vernacular of foreign (especially Turkish) loanwords and restoring ancient morphology and vocabulary where feasible, without attempting a wholesale return to classical Attic. After Greek independence, successive governments adopted progressively more archaizing versions of Katharevousa for administration, law, education, and the press.

Characteristics

Katharevousa retained ancient features absent from Demotic, including archaic case endings, infinitives in formal usage, learned vocabulary, and ancient participles, while its syntax and phonology remained largely modern. In practice it existed on a spectrum from Korais's moderate compromise to a heavily archaized "extreme" form used by later purists.

The language question

For nearly two centuries Greek public life was split between supporters of Katharevousa (associated with the state, church, and classicism) and Demoticists (associated with literature and progressive politics). The dispute at times turned violent, as in the 1901 Gospel riots in Athens over a Demotic translation of the New Testament. Educational reform in 1917 and 1964 introduced Demotic into schools, but the military junta of 1967–1974 reimposed Katharevousa. After the restoration of democracy, Law 309 of 1976 made Demotic-based Standard Modern Greek the official language, ending Katharevousa's official status — though its vocabulary and learned forms remain deeply embedded in modern Greek.
