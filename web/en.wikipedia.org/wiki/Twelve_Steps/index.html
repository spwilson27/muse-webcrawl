The Twelve Steps are a set of spiritual principles that outline a course of action for recovery from alcoholism, drug addiction and other compulsive behaviours. They were first published in 1939 in the book Alcoholics Anonymous (the "Big Book"), written primarily by AA co-founder Bill Wilson. The steps describe admitting powerlessness over the addiction, placing one's life in the care of a higher power, taking a searching moral inventory, admitting wrongs, making amends to those harmed, and carrying the message to others who suffer. The programme has been adapted by dozens of fellowships, including Narcotics Anonymous, Al-Anon, Gamblers Anonymous and Sexaholics Anonymous.

The steps

  1. We admitted we were powerless over alcohol — that our lives had become unmanageable.
  2. Came to believe that a Power greater than ourselves could restore us to sanity.
  3. Made a decision to turn our will and our lives over to the care of God as we understood Him.
  4. Made a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves.
  5. Admitted to God, to ourselves, and to another human being the exact nature of our wrongs.
  6. Were entirely ready to have God remove all these defects of character.
  7. Humbly asked Him to remove our shortcomings.
  8. Made a list of all persons we had harmed, and became willing to make amends to them all.
  9. Made direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others.
  10. Continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it.
  11. Sought through prayer and meditation to improve our conscious contact with God as we understood Him.
  12. Having had a spiritual awakening as the result of these steps, we tried to carry this message to alcoholics, and to practice these principles in all our affairs.

History

Wilson drafted the steps in late 1938, synthesising ideas from the Oxford Group (a Christian movement in which early AA members had participated), evangelist practices of confession and restitution, and the psychology of William James, whose The Varieties of Religious Experience Wilson credited with the concept of spiritual experience as a route to recovery. The number twelve was settled as the steps were written; Wilson later said he wrote them in about thirty minutes, though they were edited through group discussion before publication in April 1939. The companion Twelve Traditions, governing the fellowship's unity and anonymity, were adopted in 1950.

Practice

Members typically work through the steps with a sponsor — an experienced member who guides them — while attending meetings. The steps are explicitly "suggested" rather than compulsory in AA's literature, and the phrase "as we understood Him" is intended to let members define their higher power flexibly, including secular interpretations. Step work is combined with fellowship practices such as the Serenity Prayer, meeting attendance slogans, and anniversary chips.

Effectiveness and criticism

Research on twelve-step effectiveness is mixed but increasingly supportive for alcohol use disorder: a 2020 Cochrane review found AA and twelve-step facilitation produced abstinence rates as good as or better than other established treatments, including cognitive behavioural therapy, with lower healthcare costs. Criticisms include the programme's spiritual and theistic vocabulary (challenged in some court rulings on coerced attendance), its emphasis on powerlessness, variable success for substances other than alcohol, and the lack of professional oversight in peer-led groups.
