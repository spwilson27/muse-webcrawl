The counties of England are territorial divisions used for several different purposes. The historic counties — 39 in number, such as Yorkshire, Kent and Devon — developed from the Anglo-Saxon shires and remained the standard geography of England for roughly a thousand years. Today the term most often refers to the 48 ceremonial counties, areas to which a lord-lieutenant is appointed, and to the various counties and county-level areas used in local government.

Historic counties

Many English counties originated as shires administered by a sheriff (shire-reeve) on behalf of the crown. Their boundaries were largely fixed by the 16th century and remained stable until the later 19th century. They remain widely used for cultural, sporting and postal-address purposes: county cricket, for example, is still organised around historic county clubs.

Ceremonial counties

Since 1997 England has been divided into 48 ceremonial counties under the Lieutenancies Act 1997. They range from County Durham, Rutland (the smallest) and the City of London, up to North Yorkshire, the largest by area. Greater London and the metropolitan counties each form a single ceremonial county.

Local government

Administrative geography is more complex: as of the 2020s England has a patchwork of two-tier county and district councils, unitary authorities, metropolitan boroughs and the Greater London Authority. The six metropolitan counties (such as Greater Manchester and the West Midlands) lost their county councils in 1986 but persist as ceremonial and statistical areas. Postal counties were abolished by Royal Mail in 1996.
