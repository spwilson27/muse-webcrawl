Traditional Chinese medicine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is an alternative medical practice drawn from traditional medicine in China. Founded on more than 2,500 years of practice, it includes various forms of herbal medicine, acupuncture, cupping, moxibustion, massage (tui na), exercise (qigong), and dietary therapy. A core premise is that the body's vital energy (qi) circulates through channels called meridians, which have branches connected to bodily organs and functions.

Theoretical framework

TCM theory is rooted in texts such as the Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon). Health is understood as balance among the complementary forces of yin and yang and the interactions of the five phases (wuxing: wood, fire, earth, metal, water). Diagnosis relies on observation, listening and smelling, inquiry, and palpation — including pulse diagnosis and examination of the tongue — and patterns are described in terms such as the eight principles (interior/exterior, hot/cold, excess/deficiency, yin/yang).

Practices

Acupuncture inserts thin needles at acupoints along meridians; moxibustion burns dried mugwort near the skin; herbal medicine prescribes formulae from a large materia medica of plant, mineral, and animal ingredients; cupping and tui na massage are widely used for musculoskeletal complaints.

Scientific evaluation

Scientific consensus is that most TCM concepts, such as qi and meridians, have no counterpart in anatomy or physiology, and the efficacy of most TCM treatments beyond acupuncture for some pain conditions is not supported by rigorous evidence. Some TCM-derived compounds have proved valuable: artemisinin, isolated from sweet wormwood (qinghao) by Tu Youyou's team during a search through traditional remedies, became a frontline antimalarial and earned the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Concerns include adulteration of herbal products, heavy-metal contamination, and the use of endangered species.
