Sicilian Baroque

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sicilian Baroque is the distinctive form of Baroque architecture that developed on the island of Sicily, then part of the Kingdom of Sicily under Spanish rule, in the 17th and 18th centuries. The style is characterized by exuberant ornament, theatrical use of light and shadow, curved facades, and elaborate carved decoration — including putti, masks, and grotesques — executed in the local golden limestone.

The style reached its height during the great rebuilding that followed the devastating earthquake of 1693, which destroyed cities across the Val di Noto in southeastern Sicily, including Catania, Noto, Ragusa, Modica, and Syracuse. Entire towns were rebuilt on new plans with broad streets and monumental churches and palaces.

Characteristics

Sicilian Baroque buildings often feature undulating or convex facades, external staircases of dramatic design, bell towers integrated into church fronts, and richly carved balconies supported by sculpted corbels with faces and figures. Leading architects included Rosario Gagliardi, Giovanni Battista Vaccarini, Andrea Palma, and Stefano Ittar.

Recognition

In 2002, UNESCO inscribed the "Late Baroque Towns of the Val di Noto (South-Eastern Sicily)" on the World Heritage List, recognizing eight towns as outstanding examples of the style and of large-scale post-earthquake reconstruction.
