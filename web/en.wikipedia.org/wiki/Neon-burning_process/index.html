Neon-burning process

The neon-burning process is a stage of nuclear fusion in massive stars (at least about 8 solar masses) that follows carbon burning and precedes oxygen burning. Unlike earlier burning stages, neon burning is not initiated by direct fusion of neon nuclei: at temperatures around 1.2 billion kelvin, high-energy photons photodisintegrate neon-20 nuclei, and the liberated alpha particles are captured to build heavier elements, principally oxygen-16 and magnesium-24.

Reactions

The key sequence begins with photodisintegration:

  • 20Ne + γ → 16O + 4He

The alpha particle released is then captured by another neon nucleus:

  • 20Ne + 4He → 24Mg + γ

Further captures and photodisintegrations produce nuclei such as 23Na, 23Mg, and 28Si. The net effect converts the carbon-burning ashes (mainly 20Ne, 16O, and 23Na) into an oxygen–magnesium core.

Conditions and duration

Neon burning requires core temperatures of roughly 1.2 × 109 K and densities above about 4 × 106 g/cm³. Because energy losses to neutrinos are severe at these temperatures, the stage is brief — lasting only on the order of a year in typical models of massive stars. As neon is exhausted in the core, contraction raises the temperature until oxygen burning begins.

Context in stellar evolution

Neon burning is one of the sequence of advanced burning stages — carbon, neon, oxygen, silicon — that build an "onion-skin" structure of concentric burning shells in a massive star, culminating in an iron core whose collapse triggers a core-collapse supernova.
