Hollow Nickel Case

The Hollow Nickel Case (also known as the Hollow Coin Case) was a Cold War espionage case in the United States that began in 1953 when a Brooklyn newspaper boy discovered a hollowed-out nickel containing a coded microfilm message. The investigation eventually led the FBI to the Soviet spy Rudolf Abel, whose arrest and 1957 trial became one of the most famous espionage prosecutions of the era.

Discovery

On 22 June 1953, Jimmy Bozart, a fourteen-year-old Brooklyn paperboy collecting payments, dropped a nickel that split open, revealing a tiny piece of microfilm inside. The microfilm bore a grid of numbers in a ciphered format. Bozart's family reported the find, and the nickel and microfilm passed through the New York Police Department to the FBI, which spent years attempting to identify the sender and decipher the message.

Investigation and arrest

The break came in 1957 when Reino Häyhänen, a Soviet intelligence officer acting as Abel's assistant in New York, defected to the United States in Paris and identified his superior. Häyhänen explained the cipher system — a straddling checkerboard (the VIC cipher) used with one-time pads — enabling the FBI to decode the hollow nickel's message, which turned out to be a personal communication from Moscow Center to Häyhänen. On 21 June 1957, the FBI arrested Rudolf Abel (born William August Fischer), a Soviet intelligence colonel operating under deep cover in Brooklyn, where he had worked as an artist and photographer.

Trial and exchange

Abel was defended by James B. Donovan and convicted of conspiracy to commit espionage in federal court. He was sentenced to 30 years in prison but famously avoided the death penalty. In February 1962, Abel was exchanged on the Glienicke Bridge in Berlin for captured American U-2 pilot Francis Gary Powers — an exchange negotiated by Donovan and dramatized decades later in the film Bridge of Spies (2015).
