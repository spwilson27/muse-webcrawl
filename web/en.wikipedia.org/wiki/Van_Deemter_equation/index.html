Van Deemter equation

The van Deemter equation in chromatography, named for Jan van Deemter, relates the variance per unit length of a separation column (the height equivalent to a theoretical plate, HETP) to the linear velocity of the mobile phase, taking into account the physical processes of diffusion and mass transfer that cause band broadening. It was introduced in a 1956 paper by van Deemter, Zuiderweg, and Klinkenberg on the longitudinal diffusion and resistance to mass transfer in gas chromatography.

H = A + B/u + C·u

where:

  • H is the plate height (HETP), a measure of column efficiency;
  • u is the linear velocity of the carrier gas or mobile phase;
  • A is the eddy diffusion term, arising from multiple flow paths through the packed column;
  • B is the longitudinal diffusion coefficient, describing diffusion of analyte bands along the column axis;
  • C is the resistance to mass transfer coefficient, describing the finite rate of equilibration of analyte between the stationary and mobile phases.

Interpretation

The equation predicts a characteristic curve of plate height against flow rate with a clear minimum, representing the optimum velocity at which column efficiency is greatest. At low flow rates, the B/u term dominates because longitudinal diffusion has more time to broaden the bands. At high flow rates, the C·u term dominates because the analyte molecules cannot equilibrate between phases quickly enough. The A term is independent of flow rate in the classical treatment and depends on particle size and packing uniformity.

Applications

The van Deemter equation is fundamental to the design and optimization of gas chromatography (GC) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) columns. It explains why smaller, more uniform packing particles improve efficiency, and it guides the selection of operating flow rates. Later refinements, such as the Golay equation for open tubular (capillary) columns — in which the eddy diffusion term vanishes — build directly on the van Deemter framework.
