Shale gas in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shale gas in the United States is an available source of unconventional natural gas. Beginning in the late 1990s and accelerating in the 2000s, the combination of horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing made it economically feasible to extract gas from low-permeability shale formations on a large scale. The resulting "shale revolution" transformed the United States from an expected major importer of natural gas into the world's largest producer and, later, a leading exporter of liquefied natural gas.

History

Gas was produced from shallow shale wells in the Appalachian Basin from the nineteenth century, but modern shale development began in the Barnett Shale of Texas, where Mitchell Energy refined slickwater hydraulic fracturing in the 1990s. Techniques then spread to the Fayetteville, Haynesville, Marcellus, Bakken (for associated gas), Eagle Ford, Permian and Utica plays. Shale gas grew from about 1 percent of US gas production in 2000 to more than half by the mid-2010s, and dry shale gas has since accounted for the large majority of total US production.

Economic impact

Abundant shale gas sharply reduced domestic natural gas prices, reviving US petrochemical and fertiliser industries, displacing coal in electricity generation, and contributing to declines in US carbon dioxide emissions from power generation. It also generated substantial employment and royalty income in producing regions, while exposing the industry to boom-and-bust price cycles.

Environmental controversy

Shale development has been controversial because of concerns about groundwater contamination, wastewater disposal and induced seismicity, methane leakage, air pollution, and land and community impacts. Regulation is shared between federal statutes — several of which contain exemptions for oil and gas operations — and state regulators; some states and municipalities, including New York State in 2014, have banned high-volume hydraulic fracturing.
