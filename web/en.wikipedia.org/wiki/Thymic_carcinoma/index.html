Thymic carcinoma is a rare type of thymus gland cancer that usually exhibits more aggressive behaviour than its more common counterpart, thymoma. It is a cancer originating from the epithelial cells of the thymus and is clearly malignant under the microscope, lacking the immature T-cell population typical of thymoma.

Signs and symptoms

Early thymic carcinoma often has no symptoms and may be found incidentally on imaging. As it grows it can cause chest pain, cough, and shortness of breath, and it frequently invades neighbouring structures or metastasises to the pleura, lungs, liver, bone and brain. Unlike thymoma, it is rarely associated with paraneoplastic syndromes such as myasthenia gravis.

Diagnosis and staging

Diagnosis is by imaging (CT, MRI, PET) and biopsy. Tumours are staged with the Masaoka–Koga system and the TNM classification. The most common histological subtype is squamous cell carcinoma.

Treatment and prognosis

Complete surgical resection offers the best chance of control where feasible, usually combined with radiotherapy and platinum-based chemotherapy for advanced or incompletely resected disease. Prognosis is substantially worse than for thymoma, with five-year survival in many series around 30–55 percent depending on stage.
