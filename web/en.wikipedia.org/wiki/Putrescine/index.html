Putrescine is an organic compound with the formula H2N(CH2)4NH2, a foul-smelling diamine produced by the breakdown of amino acids in living and dead organisms. Together with cadaverine, it is largely responsible for the odour of putrefying flesh. Putrescine was first isolated in 1885 by the Berlin physician Ludwig Brieger from decaying matter, hence the name (from Latin putrescere, "to rot"). Despite its grim associations, it is also an essential metabolite in all living cells: it is the biosynthetic precursor of the polyamines spermidine and spermine, which are required for cell growth and division.

Biosynthesis and metabolism

In animals and many bacteria, putrescine is formed by decarboxylation of the amino acid ornithine by the enzyme ornithine decarboxylase — a rate-limiting, tightly regulated step in polyamine synthesis. Bacteria also produce it from arginine via agmatine. Gut microbes generate substantial putrescine, contributing to its presence (and smell) in faeces and bad breath. Cells convert putrescine to spermidine and then spermine by successive addition of aminopropyl groups from decarboxylated S-adenosylmethionine.

Occurrence and significance

Putrescine occurs in many foods, especially fermented products and aged cheese, and accumulates in spoiled protein-rich food, where it serves (with cadaverine and histamine) as an indicator of freshness. In plants it accumulates under stress and participates in development. Industrially, putrescine is produced by fermentation or chemical synthesis as a monomer for polyamides such as nylon-4,6 (from putrescine and adipic acid). Elevated polyamine synthesis in tumours has made ornithine decarboxylase a drug target; its inhibitor eflornithine (DFMO) is used against sleeping sickness and hirsutism and has been studied in cancer chemoprevention.

Toxicity

Putrescine itself has relatively low acute toxicity, though it can potentiate the effects of histamine in food poisoning.
