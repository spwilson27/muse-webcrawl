My Lai massacre

The My Lai massacre (Vietnamese: Thảm sát Mỹ Lai) was a mass murder of unarmed South Vietnamese civilians carried out by United States Army soldiers on 16 March 1968 during the Vietnam War. Between 347 and 504 unarmed civilians — including women, children, and elderly people — were killed by soldiers of Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry Regiment, in the hamlets of Mỹ Lai and Mỹ Khê in Quảng Ngãi Province.

Events

Charlie Company had been operating in the area, known to soldiers as "Pinkville", believed to be a Viet Cong stronghold, and had suffered casualties from mines and booby traps. On the morning of 16 March 1968 the company assaulted the hamlet of Mỹ Lai expecting enemy forces; instead they found only civilians preparing breakfast. Over the next hours, soldiers rounded up and murdered villagers, committed rapes, and destroyed houses, livestock, and food stores.

The killing was halted in part by Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson Jr., a helicopter pilot who landed his aircraft between soldiers and fleeing villagers, ordered his crew to train their guns on the Americans, and evacuated survivors; his crew members Glenn Andreotta and Lawrence Colburn also helped rescue civilians.

Cover-up and exposure

The operation was initially reported as a victory. The Army suppressed the truth until former soldier Ronald Ridenhour, who had heard accounts from participants, wrote letters to officials in 1969. Investigative journalist Seymour Hersh broke the story in November 1969, and photographs by Army photographer Ronald Haeberle appeared in Life magazine, provoking worldwide outrage and fuelling opposition to the war.

Trials

Twenty-six soldiers were charged. Only Platoon leader Lieutenant William Calley was convicted — of the premeditated murder of 22 villagers — and sentenced to life imprisonment in 1971. After intervention by President Richard Nixon, Calley served about three and a half years, mostly under house arrest, before being paroled. The Peers Commission inquiry criticized failures of leadership and the cover-up up the chain of command.

Legacy

My Lai became a symbol of the moral costs of the Vietnam War. A memorial and museum stand at the site in Vietnam, and the massacre remains a standard case study in military ethics and the law of armed conflict.
