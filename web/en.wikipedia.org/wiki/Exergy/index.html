Exergy

Exergy analysis traces the maximum useful work obtainable as a system comes into equilibrium with its environment.

Exergy, often called "available energy" or "useful energy", is the maximum useful work possible during a process that brings the system into equilibrium with a heat reservoir, reaching thermal, mechanical, and chemical equilibrium. Exergy is the energy that is available to be used. After the system and surroundings reach equilibrium, the exergy is zero. Determining exergy was also the first goal of thermodynamics.

Definition

Energy is never destroyed during a process; it changes from one form to another (see First law of thermodynamics). In contrast, exergy accounts for the irreversibility of a process due to increase in entropy (see Second law of thermodynamics) and is always destroyed when a process is irreversible, for example loss of heat to the environment. Exergy is always evaluated relative to a reference environment defined by a temperature, pressure, and chemical composition.

Applications

Applying exergy analysis (also called second-law analysis) to energy conversion systems such as power plants, chemical processes, and buildings identifies where the greatest losses of useful work occur, often in places different from those highlighted by simple energy balances. Exergy is also used in industrial ecology and life-cycle assessment to quantify resource consumption in common units.

History

The concept derives from the work of Sadi Carnot and Rudolf Clausius in the nineteenth century. The term "exergy" was coined in 1956 by Zoran Rant, from the Greek ex and ergon meaning "from work". Related earlier notions include J. Willard Gibbs's "available energy" of 1873.
