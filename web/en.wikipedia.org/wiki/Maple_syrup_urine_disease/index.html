Maple syrup urine disease

Maple syrup urine disease (MSUD), also called branched-chain ketoaciduria, is an inherited metabolic disorder in which the body cannot properly break down the branched-chain amino acids leucine, isoleucine and valine. It is named for the sweet, burnt-sugar or maple-syrup odour of affected infants' urine and sweat, caused by the accumulation of branched-chain keto acids.

Cause

MSUD is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by deficiency of the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex, due to mutations in the genes BCKDHA, BCKDHB, DBT or related genes. It is particularly common in Old Order Mennonite populations, where carrier rates are high.

Signs and course

In the classic form, healthy-seeming newborns deteriorate within days: poor feeding, lethargy, abnormal movements and odour, progressing to seizures, coma and death if untreated. Milder intermittent and intermediate forms present later, often during illness.

Diagnosis and treatment

MSUD is detected by newborn screening (tandem mass spectrometry measuring leucine/isoleucine levels) in many countries. Lifelong management requires a diet severely restricted in branched-chain amino acids with specialised medical foods, monitoring of blood levels, and emergency treatment during metabolic crises. Liver transplantation can essentially normalise amino acid tolerance.
