Algorithmic randomness
FieldAlgorithmic information theory; computability theory
Key notionsKolmogorov complexity, Martin-Löf randomness, Schnorr randomness
FoundersAndrey Kolmogorov, Per Martin-Löf, Claus Schnorr, Gregory Chaitin

Algorithmic randomness is the study of what it means for an individual sequence — typically an infinite binary sequence — to be random, using tools from computability theory and algorithmic information theory rather than probability alone. Intuitively, a sequence is random if it has no description substantially shorter than itself and passes every effective statistical test for randomness.

Three approaches

Three classical characterisations turn out to coincide for the strongest notion. Incompressibility: a sequence is random if all its initial segments have high Kolmogorov complexity (the approach of Kolmogorov, Chaitin and Levin). Typicality: a sequence is Martin-Löf random if it avoids every effectively null set, i.e. passes all Martin-Löf tests. Unpredictability: a sequence is random if no computable betting strategy (martingale) can win unboundedly against it. Weaker notions, such as Schnorr randomness and computable randomness, arise by restricting the tests or betting strategies allowed.

Consequences and applications

Martin-Löf random sequences display the standard statistical regularities demanded by probability theory: they satisfy the strong law of large numbers and contain every finite string infinitely often. Almost all sequences (in the sense of Lebesgue measure) are Martin-Löf random, yet no single such sequence can be exhibited computably. The theory connects randomness with computability, effective dimension, and the foundations of statistical mechanics and information theory.
