German wine

German wine is primarily produced in the west of Germany, along the river Rhine and its tributaries, with the oldest plantations going back to the Roman era. About 60 percent of German wine is produced in the state of Rhineland-Palatinate, where 6 of the 13 regions (Anbaugebiete) for quality wine are situated. Germany has about 103,000 hectares (252,000 acres, or 1,030 square kilometres) of vineyard, which is around one tenth of the vineyard surface in Spain, France or Italy. The total wine production is usually around 10 million hectolitres, corresponding to 1.3 billion bottles, which places Germany as the eighth-largest wine-producing country in the world. White wine accounts for almost two thirds of the total production.

History

Viticulture in Germany has a long history. The Romans brought vines to the Moselle valley, and the oldest recorded vineyard, the Niersteiner Glöck, dates to around AD 742. During the Middle Ages, monasteries — particularly Cistercian monks — expanded viticulture and refined vineyard practices. The famous Riesling grape was first documented in 1435 near Rüsselsheim. The Spätlese (late harvest) style was famously discovered at Schloss Johannisberg in 1775, when a delayed courier carrying picking permission led to grapes affected by noble rot being harvested, producing unusually fine sweet wine.

Wine regions

The 13 defined regions for quality wine are Ahr, Baden, Franconia (Franken), Hessische Bergstraße, Mittelrhein, Mosel, Nahe, Pfalz, Rheingau, Rheinhessen, Saale-Unstrut, Sachsen (Saxony) and Württemberg. Rheinhessen is the largest region by area, while the Mosel is famous for its steep slate slopes and Rieslings, and the Rheingau for historically prestigious estates. Saale-Unstrut and Sachsen are the northernmost and easternmost regions, reunified into German production after 1990.

Grape varieties

Riesling is the flagship grape, covering roughly a quarter of vineyard area, prized for its acidity, aromatic precision and capacity to express terroir across sweetness levels. Müller-Thurgau (Rivaner), a cross of Riesling and Madeleine Royale bred in 1882, was long the most planted variety. Among red varieties, Spätburgunder (Pinot noir) leads — Germany is the world's third-largest producer of Pinot noir — followed by Dornfelder. Silvaner dominates in Franconia, where it is bottled in the distinctive flattened Bocksbeutel flask.

Classification

German wine law classifies wines by ripeness at harvest (must weight) under the Prädikat system: Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein. Below these sit Qualitätswein (quality wine from a specified region) and Landwein and Deutscher Wein. Dry styles are labelled trocken or halbtrocken. The private VDP association classifies top vineyards with its own pyramid culminating in Grosse Lage and Grosses Gewächs dry wines.

Styles

German wines range from bone-dry to lusciously sweet. Cool-climate conditions yield wines of moderate alcohol, high acidity and intense aromatics. Sekt, German sparkling wine, is produced in large quantities, mostly by the Charmat method, with premium Riesling Sekt made by traditional bottle fermentation.
