Census in Canada

The Canadian census is conducted every five years by Statistics Canada.

The census in Canada is a national enumeration of the Canadian population conducted every five years by Statistics Canada, an agency of the federal government. It provides a statistical portrait of the country and its people, and its results are used to set electoral district boundaries, allocate federal transfer payments, and plan public services. The most recent completed census was held on May 11, 2021, recording a population of 36,991,981.

History

The first census in what is now Canada was conducted in 1666 by Jean Talon, intendant of New France, counting 3,215 settlers. The British North America Act of 1867 required a decennial census, and the first Dominion census was taken in 1871. Since 1956 Canada has held a census every five years, alternating between mid-decade and decade censuses. The 1871 count recorded 3.69 million people.

Questionnaires

All households receive a short-form questionnaire covering name, age, sex, marital status, household relationships and mother tongue. A sample of households — historically one in five, and one in four in 2021 — receives a detailed long-form questionnaire covering education, labour, income, housing, ethnicity, language, mobility, and Indigenous identity.

The 2011 controversy

In 2011 the mandatory long-form census was replaced by the voluntary National Household Survey, a change criticised by statisticians, businesses and provinces and followed by the resignation of the Chief Statistician, Munir Sheikh. Response rates and data quality fell. The incoming government restored the mandatory long-form census for 2016, which achieved the highest response rate in decades.

Uses

Census counts determine the allocation of seats in the House of Commons among provinces, feed the formulas for equalization and health and social transfers, and provide small-area data relied on by municipalities, businesses and researchers. Under the Statistics Act, completing the census is mandatory.
