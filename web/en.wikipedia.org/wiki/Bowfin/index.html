The bowfin, a living fossil of North American fresh waters.

The bowfin (Amia calva) is a bony fish native to North America. It is the sole surviving member of the order Amiiformes and the family Amiidae, groups that were diverse and widespread during the Mesozoic era; together with the gars it belongs to the Holostei. The bowfin is often called a "living fossil" because of its many primitive features.

Description

Bowfin have a cylindrical body covered with rounded scales, a flattened head with a mouth full of sharp teeth, and a characteristically long dorsal fin running along most of the back, which undulates to propel the fish gently forward. Adults are olive to brown, and males develop a prominent dark eyespot at the base of the tail. They commonly reach 50–70 cm in length. Bowfin possess a swim bladder connected to the oesophagus that functions like a lung, letting them gulp air at the surface and survive in warm, oxygen-poor water where other fish cannot.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges from the St. Lawrence River and Great Lakes south through the Mississippi basin to the Gulf Coast and Florida. It favours slow-moving, heavily vegetated waters — swamps, bayous, sloughs and lake margins.

Behaviour and relationship to humans

Bowfin are ambush predators of fish, crayfish and amphibians. In spring, males build circular nests in shallow vegetation, guard the eggs fiercely and shepherd the fry in a tight ball after hatching — unusual parental care among fishes. Bowfin eggs are sold as a cheap caviar substitute ("Cajun caviar"), but the flesh is soft and generally considered poor eating; anglers prize the fish for its hard fight. It is also kept in large aquariums.
