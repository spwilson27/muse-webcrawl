Bittern

FamilyArdeidae (herons)
SubfamilyBotaurinae
GeneraBotaurus, Ixobrychus, Zebrilus
HabitatReed beds and marshes

Bitterns are wading birds in the subfamily Botaurinae of the heron family, Ardeidae. Unlike most herons, which advertise themselves by their size and open habits, bitterns are secretive birds of dense reed beds and marshes, relying on streaked brown camouflage and stillness to escape detection.

Description and behaviour

Bitterns have thickset bodies, relatively short necks and dagger-like bills. When alarmed, many species adopt the "bitterning" posture, pointing the bill vertically skyward so that the streaked throat plumage blends with upright reed stems. The large species, such as the Eurasian bittern (Botaurus stellaris) and American bittern (Botaurus lentiginosus), are famous for the males' far-carrying booming calls, produced by inflating the oesophagus and likened to a distant foghorn or a blowing across a bottle top.

Species

The group comprises about fourteen species. The four large Botaurus bitterns are distributed across Eurasia, the Americas and Australasia. The smaller least bittern and its relatives, formerly placed in Ixobrychus, inhabit marshes worldwide, while the zigzag heron of Amazonia is sometimes included in the subfamily.

Diet and breeding

Bitterns feed on fish, frogs, eels and large invertebrates, hunting by slow stalking or by standing motionless at the reed edge. They nest on platforms of dead reeds above shallow water; several large species are polygynous, males booming to defend territories containing multiple females.

Conservation

Drainage of wetlands has made several species scarce. The Eurasian bittern, once extinct as a breeding bird in Britain, has recovered under reed-bed restoration, though it remains vulnerable to habitat loss and harsh winters.
