Systemic inflammatory response syndrome

Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) is the body's whole-body inflammatory response to a variety of severe clinical insults. The concept was introduced in 1992 at a consensus conference of the American College of Chest Physicians and the Society of Critical Care Medicine. SIRS is defined by the presence of two or more of four criteria:

  • Body temperature greater than 38 °C (100.4 °F) or less than 36 °C (96.8 °F)
  • Heart rate greater than 90 beats per minute
  • Respiratory rate greater than 20 breaths per minute, or arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) less than 32 mmHg
  • White blood cell count greater than 12,000/µL, less than 4,000/µL, or more than 10% immature (band) forms

SIRS can be triggered by infection — in which case, when accompanied by organ dysfunction, it was traditionally classified along the spectrum of sepsis, severe sepsis, and septic shock — or by non-infectious insults such as trauma, burns, pancreatitis, major surgery, and hemorrhage.

Criticism and the Sepsis-3 definitions

The SIRS criteria were widely criticized for being overly sensitive: many hospitalized patients who are not seriously ill meet two criteria, and the criteria do not by themselves indicate a dysregulated, life-threatening response. In 2016 the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) moved away from SIRS as the defining framework for sepsis, instead defining sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, operationalized with the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and its simplified bedside variant, qSOFA. SIRS nonetheless remains in use for screening and in the definition of sepsis in pediatric and some other settings.
