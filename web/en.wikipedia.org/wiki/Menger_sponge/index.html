Menger sponge

A Menger sponge after several iterations

In mathematics, the Menger sponge (also known as the Menger cube) is a fractal curve. It is a three-dimensional generalization of the one-dimensional Cantor set and two-dimensional Sierpinski carpet. It was first described by Karl Menger in 1926, in his studies of the concept of topological dimension.

Construction

The construction of a Menger sponge can be described as follows: begin with a cube; divide every face of the cube into nine squares, as in a Rubik's Cube, subdividing the cube into 27 smaller cubes; remove the smaller cube in the middle of each face and the smaller cube in the very centre of the larger cube, leaving 20 smaller cubes. Repeat the process indefinitely for each remaining cube. The first iteration gives a shape like a cube with square tunnels through it; the limiting object is the Menger sponge.

Properties

With each iteration the volume is multiplied by 20/27, so in the limit the sponge has zero volume, while its surface area grows without bound and becomes infinite. Its Hausdorff dimension is log 20 / log 3 ≈ 2.727. The Menger sponge is a universal curve: every one-dimensional topological space can be embedded in it, a remarkable result of Menger's work on dimension theory.

Physical models

Large physical Menger sponges have been built as art and education projects, most famously a level-3 model assembled from some 66,000 folded business cards, coordinated by Jeannine Mosely. The fractal has also inspired designs in engineering, such as shock-absorbing and lightweight structures.
