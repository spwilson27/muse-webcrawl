Augury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Augury is the practice of interpreting signs and omens in ancient Roman and Etruscan religion, especially the flight patterns, calls and feeding behaviour of birds, in order to divine the will of the gods. The priest who performed this task was an augur, a member of one of Rome's major priestly colleges.

An augur taking the auspices, marked by the curved staff (lituus).

Practice

The augur marked out a templum — a consecrated region of the sky — and observed signs within it. Birds flying in from certain directions, the cries of ravens and owls, and the feeding of sacred chickens were all interpreted. Nothing of public importance — elections, legislation, war, the founding of colonies — was supposed to proceed without the auspices (auspicia). Unfavourable signs could suspend public business; the act of "watching the sky" (servare de caelo) was famously used by Bibulus in 59 BC in an attempt to block the legislation of Julius Caesar.

History

Roman tradition traced augury to Romulus, who was said to have confirmed Rome's founding site through a contest of birds with Remus. The college of augurs, originally three members, grew to nine and eventually sixteen under Caesar. The Etruscan discipline (disciplina Etrusca) also included haruspicy — reading entrails — which was related but distinct from augury proper.
