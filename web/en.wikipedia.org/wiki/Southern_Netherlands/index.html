Southern Netherlands

The Southern Netherlands, also called the Catholic Netherlands, were the parts of the Low Countries belonging mostly to modern-day Belgium and Luxembourg, together with at times adjoining parts of the Netherlands and France. The region was successively part of the Spanish Netherlands, the Austrian Netherlands, and (after annexation by France and the Congress of Vienna) the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, before Belgian independence in 1830.

Spanish Netherlands (1556–1714)

When the Habsburg Netherlands passed to the Spanish branch of the dynasty under Philip II, revolt broke out in 1568 (the Dutch Revolt). The northern provinces declared independence as the Dutch Republic, while the south — including Flanders, Brabant, Hainaut, Artois, and Luxembourg — was reconquered by Spain under Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, by 1585. The reconquest drove tens of thousands of Protestants and merchants north, shifting economic primacy from Antwerp and Ghent to Amsterdam. The Spanish Netherlands were governed from Brussels by governors-general such as Archduke Albert VII and the Infanta Isabella.

Austrian Netherlands (1714–1795)

After the War of the Spanish Succession, the Treaty of Utrecht and the Peace of Rastatt transferred the territory to the Austrian Habsburgs. The Austrian Netherlands experienced enlightened-absolutist reforms under Charles VI and Joseph II, whose ecclesiastical and administrative changes provoked the Brabant Revolution of 1789–1790, which briefly proclaimed a United States of Belgium. France annexed the region in 1795.

Aftermath

After Napoleon's defeat the Southern Netherlands were united with the north in the United Kingdom of the Netherlands (1815–1830), from which Belgium seceded in the Belgian Revolution of 1830. The term "Southern Netherlands" remains a common historical designation for the region in the Habsburg centuries.
