Strasbourg Cathedral

Strasbourg Cathedral (French: Cathédrale Notre-Dame de Strasbourg; German: Liebfrauenmünster zu Straßburg) is a Catholic cathedral in Strasbourg, Alsace, France. Built principally in the Gothic style between 1176 and 1439 from red sandstone of the Vosges, it is famous for its single soaring spire, its astronomical clock, and its richly sculpted west front. From 1647 until 1874 — a span of 227 years — its 142-metre (466 ft) spire made it the tallest building in the world.

History

Construction of the present Gothic cathedral began in the late 12th century on the foundations of an earlier Romanesque church. The architect Erwin von Steinbach, who began the west front in 1277, gave the facade its distinctive openwork tracery; after his death in 1318 his sons continued the work. The spire, completed by Ulrich von Ensingen in 1439, is the cathedral's only one — the second planned tower was never built — and remains the emblem of the city.

Features

The cathedral's treasures include the Pillar of Angels (c. 1230), the rose window of the west front, and the Renaissance astronomical clock (the third on the site, built 1838–1843), which displays processions of automata at midday. The building is constructed from red sandstone that changes colour with the light, a feature celebrated by Victor Hugo and Goethe, who praised Erwin's facade in his essay on German architecture.
