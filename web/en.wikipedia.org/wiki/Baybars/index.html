Baybars

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The lion emblem of Baybars carved on the Lion Tower at Tripoli, Lebanon]

Baybars (19 July 1223 or 1228 – 1 July 1277), al-Malik al-Zahir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari, was the fourth Mamluk sultan of Egypt and Syria (1260–1277), the true founder of the Mamluk sultanate's power. A Kipchak Turk sold into slavery and risen through the Bahri mamluks of al-Salih Ayyub, he was a commander at Mansurah against Louis IX (1250) and the architect, with Sultan Qutuz, of the victory over the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260) — after which he murdered Qutuz and took the throne. His seventeen-year reign drove the Crusader states to a coastal fringe (Antioch fell 1268), broke the Assassins of Syria, held the Ilkhanate's armies at the Euphrates, and rebuilt Egypt and Syria's defences, mosques and postal relay into a military state.

Ain Jalut and the throne

When Hulagu withdrew from Syria in 1260, his lieutenant Kitbuqa's demand for Egypt's submission met Qutuz's defiance; at Ain Jalut (3 September 1260) Baybars commanded the vanguard whose feigned retreat drew the Mongols into the trap — the battle that first proved Mongol armies could be destroyed. On the return march Baybars, promised Aleppo and refused, assassinated Qutuz at a hunt (24 October 1260) and was proclaimed sultan. Legitimacy he manufactured brilliantly: in 1261 he installed an Abbasid refugee as caliph in Cairo, receiving investiture from the caliphate he thus owned.

The sultanate built

Baybars campaigned almost annually: against the Crusaders (Caesarea, Arsuf, Safed, Jaffa, Antioch), Armenian Cilicia, the Nubian frontier, and the Ilkhanate (Elbistan, 1277, a raid into Anatolia weeks before his death). At home he rebuilt the fleet, the citadels and the barid postal system, endowed the Zahiriya madrasa-library in Damascus (where he is buried), and ruled through constant motion between Cairo and Damascus. In Arabic popular memory he became the hero of the epic Sirat al-Zahir Baybars, a folk romance larger than the history; in the history itself he is the prototype of the Mamluk sultan — slave, soldier, usurper, state-builder.
