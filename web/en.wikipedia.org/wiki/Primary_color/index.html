A primary color is a colour that cannot be created by mixing other colours in a given colour model, and from which other colours can be produced by mixing. The choice of primaries depends on the medium: light is mixed additively, while pigments and inks are mixed subtractively.

Additive primaries

For light, the primaries are red, green and blue (RGB) — the system used in screens and displays. Combining all three at full intensity produces white light; combining pairs yields cyan, magenta and yellow. The choice reflects human trichromatic vision: the eye's three cone types are most sensitive to long (reddish), medium (greenish) and short (bluish) wavelengths, a principle formalised in the Young–Helmholtz theory.

Subtractive primaries

For printing, the primaries are cyan, magenta and yellow (CMY, extended to CMYK with black, "key") — each absorbs one additive primary. Traditional art education taught red, yellow and blue (RYB) as pigment primaries, a model dating to the 18th and 19th centuries that remains common in schools though it produces a smaller gamut of mixtures than CMY.

Limitations

No finite set of real primaries can reproduce every perceivable colour; every mixing system covers only a gamut within the full colour space. "Primary" is thus a property of a system, not of the colours themselves. Secondary colours result from mixing two primaries, and tertiary colours from mixing a primary with a neighbouring secondary.
