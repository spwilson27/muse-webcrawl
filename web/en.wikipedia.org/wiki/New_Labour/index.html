New Labour

New Labour is the name given to a period in the history of the British Labour Party from the mid-1990s to 2010, when the party, under leaders Tony Blair and Gordon Brown, moved toward the political centre, rebranding itself to appeal beyond its traditional working-class and trade-union base. The project combined acceptance of the market economy with commitments to social justice, public service investment, and constitutional reform, under an ideology often described as the "Third Way" between capitalism and traditional socialism.

Origins

After four consecutive general election defeats (1979–1992) and the sudden death of leader John Smith in 1994, Blair won the leadership and accelerated modernisation. The symbolic centrepiece was the 1995 rewriting of Clause IV of the party constitution, which had committed Labour to common ownership of the means of production. The party adopted a new red rose branding, a disciplined media operation built around press secretary Alastair Campbell, and a policy programme promising not to raise income tax rates, to keep to Conservative spending plans for two years, and to be "tough on crime, tough on the causes of crime".

In government, 1997–2010

Labour won a landslide in the 1997 general election, ending 18 years of Conservative government. The New Labour governments introduced the national minimum wage, devolution to Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland, the Human Rights Act, greatly increased spending on health and education, tax credits and Sure Start for families, and civil partnerships. The Bank of England was given operational independence in 1997. The period was also defined by the rivalry between Blair and Chancellor Gordon Brown, by Britain's participation in the 2003 invasion of Iraq — the most controversial decision of the era — and by the 2008 financial crisis, after which Brown's government bailed out the banks. Labour lost power in the 2010 general election. Debates continue over whether New Labour represented necessary modernisation or a dilution of Labour's founding purposes; a later generation of garden-city and reform thought can be contrasted with the mill-town model community of New Lanark.
