Mozi (c. 470 – c. 391 BCE), born Mo Di, was a Chinese philosopher who founded the school of Mohism during the Hundred Schools of Thought period of the late Zhou dynasty. Of humble, possibly artisan, origins, he organised his followers into a disciplined order noted for expertise in defensive fortification and for opposing wars of aggression.

Teachings

Mohism's core doctrine is jiān'ài, usually translated "impartial care" or "universal love": one should care for all people without the graded distinctions of Confucian family ethics. Mozi argued doctrines should be tested by benefit — whether they enrich the state and increase population and order — and judged by the "three gauges" of precedent, evidence and practical use. He condemned offensive warfare, elaborate funerals and musical extravagance as wasteful, taught belief in Heaven (Tian) as a moral standard and in ghosts and spirits who reward and punish, and promoted meritocracy ("exalting the worthy") over hereditary privilege.

Legacy

Mohism rivalled Confucianism for centuries before fading under the Han dynasty, whose state orthodoxy was Confucian. The later Mohist chapters contain remarkable work in logic, definitions, optics and mechanics — including an early statement of the camera obscura effect. Modern scholars compare Mohist impartial care, in some respects, to Western Consequentialism and utilitarianism.
