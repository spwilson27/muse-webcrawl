Deism

Deism is the philosophical position and rationalistic theology that generally rejects revelation as a source of divine knowledge and asserts that empirical reason and observation of the natural world are exclusively logical, reliable, and sufficient to determine the existence of a Supreme Being as the creator of the universe. More simply, deism is the belief in the existence of God solely based on rational thought without any reliance on revealed religions or religious authority.

Deism emphasises a natural religion and, particularly during the Enlightenment, was often expressed through the metaphor of the divine watchmaker: God created the universe and its laws but does not intervene in its daily operation through miracles or providence.

History

The term derives from the Latin deus ("god"). The movement took shape in 17th-century England with writers such as Edward Herbert, 1st Baron Herbert of Cherbury, whose De Veritate (1624) set out common notions of natural religion. English deists including John Toland and Matthew Tindal influenced French thinkers such as Voltaire. Deism strongly influenced several American Founding Fathers, among them Thomas Jefferson, Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Paine, whose The Age of Reason (1794) popularised deist ideas.

Beliefs

Deists typically hold that God created the universe but does not interfere with human affairs; reject miracles, prophecy and revealed scripture as authoritative; and maintain that morality flows from reason and nature rather than religious command. Modern forms range from classical deism to pandeism, which identifies God with the universe itself.
