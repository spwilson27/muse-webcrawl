Exodus of the Israelites

The Exodus (Hebrew: יציאת מצרים, Yetsi'at Mitzrayim) is the founding myth of the Israelites in the Hebrew Bible: their slavery in ancient Egypt, departure under Moses, miraculous crossing of the sea, wandering in the Sinai wilderness, and covenant at Mount Sinai, as narrated chiefly in the Book of Exodus.

The Exodus
Source: Book of Exodus
Leader: Moses
Destination: Canaan

Narrative

God inflicts Ten Plagues on Pharaoh, institutes Passover, leads Israel through the parted sea, provides manna, and gives the Torah and Ten Commandments at Sinai. After forty years' wandering the generation dies and their children approach Canaan.

Historicity

Mainstream archaeologists find no evidence for an exodus of the biblical scale or a mass Sinai wandering, and regard the story as composed in the 7th–5th centuries BCE, possibly preserving memories of smaller groups, Egyptian rule in Canaan, or the Hyksos expulsion. It remains central to Judaism (Passover), Christianity and Islam, and to later liberation movements.
