Roman festival

Roman festivals were religious celebrations held throughout the year in ancient Rome. Rooted in the agricultural cycle and in the worship of Rome's gods, they filled a large part of the Roman calendar: by the late Republic well over a hundred days a year were marked as festival days (feriae), on which legal business was suspended and public rituals, games, feasting, and processions took place.

The calendar

The early Roman calendar, attributed by tradition to Romulus and reformed by Numa Pompilius, assigned fixed festivals (feriae stativae) to particular dates; other observances were movable (feriae conceptivae), proclaimed annually by priests or magistrates. The fasti — inscribed calendars such as the Fasti Praenestini — listed these days alongside market days and days lawful for assemblies and courts. Our knowledge of many festivals comes above all from Ovid's poem the Fasti, which treats the first six months of the year.

Major festivals

The Saturnalia (from 17 December) honoured Saturn with feasting, gift-giving, gambling, and a temporary reversal of social order in which slaves were served by their masters. The Lupercalia (15 February) was an archaic fertility and purification rite in which priests called Luperci ran around the Palatine striking bystanders with goat-skin thongs. The Parilia (21 April) celebrated the founding of Rome and the welfare of flocks. The Floralia honoured Flora with games and licence; the Cerealia honoured Ceres, goddess of grain; the Vestalia honoured Vesta, whose temple was opened to married women.

Other important observances included the Lemuria and Parentalia, concerned with the spirits of the dead; the Quinquatrus for Minerva; the Neptunalia in the heat of July; the Consualia and Opiconsivia for harvest storage gods; the Vulcanalia; and the great Ludi Romani, games for Jupiter featuring chariot racing and theatrical performances. Under the Empire, imperial birthdays and deified emperors added still more festivals, and the army observed its own calendar, as documented by the Feriale Duranum from Dura-Europos.

Games and public spectacle

Many festivals included ludi — public games such as chariot races in the Circus Maximus, theatrical shows, and, increasingly in the late Republic and Empire, gladiatorial combats (originally funeral offerings, munera). Festival days were occasions of eating and drinking in public, with sacrificial animals providing meat for the population and benefactors funding banquets.

Later influence

Roman festivals shaped the later European calendar: some scholars connect the timing of Christmas with the Saturnalia season and the festival of Sol Invictus on 25 December, and several Roman observances have echoes in modern folk customs. The study of Roman festivals illuminates the agricultural year, Roman religion, and the social fabric of the ancient city.
