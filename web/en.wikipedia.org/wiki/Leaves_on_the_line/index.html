Leaves on the line

Leaves on the line is a common cause of disruption to railway services in the United Kingdom and other countries during the autumn. Fallen leaves settle on the rails and are crushed by passing train wheels into a hard, slippery mulch layer that coats the railhead, dramatically reducing adhesion between wheel and rail. Trains may then have difficulty accelerating and, more seriously, braking, so services are slowed or delayed as a precaution.

The phrase has entered British popular culture as a shorthand for an implausible-sounding excuse for poor service, and is frequently cited alongside "the wrong type of snow" in lists of notorious excuses given by British train operators.

The problem

When leaves fall on the track they are compressed by the enormous pressure of steel wheels into a thin black layer of leaf mulch. This layer is extremely slippery — comparable to ice or Teflon — and greatly increases braking distances. It can also insulate the rail electrically, interfering with track circuits used to detect the position of trains. Lines with heavy lineside tree cover, and routes through cuttings, are the most affected.

Countermeasures

Railway infrastructure managers such as Network Rail employ a range of measures during the autumn season:

  • Rail Head Treatment Trains (RHTTs), which blast the railhead with high-pressure water jets and apply Sandite, a mixture of sand, antifreeze and steel shot, to improve grip;
  • Stationary and train-mounted sanding equipment;
  • Laser and dry-ice cleaning systems trialled to remove the leaf layer;
  • Vegetation management programmes to cut back lineside trees;
  • Special autumn timetables with additional journey time allowed on affected routes.
