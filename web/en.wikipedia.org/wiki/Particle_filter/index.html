Particle filter

Particle filters, or sequential Monte Carlo methods, are a set of Monte Carlo algorithms used to solve filtering problems in signal processing and Bayesian statistical inference. The filtering problem consists in estimating the internal states of a dynamical system from a sequence of noisy observations. A particle filter represents the posterior distribution of the state by a set of random samples ("particles") with associated weights.

Algorithm

The best-known variant, the bootstrap filter of Gordon, Salmond and Smith (1993), cycles through three steps: propagate each particle through the state-transition model; weight each particle by the likelihood of the new observation; and resample the particle set — duplicating high-weight particles and discarding low-weight ones — to combat sample degeneracy, the tendency for all weight to concentrate on a single particle.

Applications

Because they impose no linearity or Gaussianity assumptions, particle filters are used in robot localisation and SLAM, target tracking, econometrics, navigation, and bioinformatics. They generalise earlier approaches such as the Kalman filter, which is exact only for linear-Gaussian systems. The number of particles required can grow exponentially with state dimension, a form of the curse of dimensionality that motivates variants such as the Rao–Blackwellised particle filter.
