Broadway theatre

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The bright marquees of Broadway theatres lighting up Times Square at night]
The bright marquees of Broadway theatres lighting up Times Square at night

Broadway theatre — commonly called simply Broadway — refers to the theatrical performances presented in the 41 professional theatres with 500 or more seats located in the Theater District and the Lincoln Center area along Broadway, in Manhattan, New York City. Together with London's West End theatre, Broadway represents the highest level of commercial theatre in the English-speaking world, and its annual Tony Awards are the industry's highest honour.

History

New York theatre grew from the eighteenth century; the Theater District migrated uptown to Times Square around 1900 as the 'Great White Way' of electric lights. The American musical was forged here — Show Boat (1927), Rodgers and Hammerstein's Oklahoma! (1943), West Side Story (1957), the Sondheim era — alongside straight plays from Eugene O'Neill to Arthur Miller and Tennessee Williams. The Shubert, Nederlander and Jujamcyn organisations own most houses.

Industry today

Broadway grosses run to roughly US$1.5–1.8 billion per season in strong years, with attendance around 12–14 million, heavily touristed; shows must earn back multi-million-dollar capitalisations at premium prices. Long-runners define the modern era: The Phantom of the Opera (1988–2023, the longest run), Chicago, The Lion King and Hamilton (2015), a phenomenon that reached global audiences via streaming. The 2020–21 COVID closure — 18 months dark — was the industry's gravest crisis.
