Social Darwinism

Social Darwinism is the study and implementation of various pseudoscientific theories and social policies that attempt to apply biological concepts of natural selection and survival of the fittest to sociology, economics and politics. The term emerged in the late 19th century and was popularised in the 1940s by the historian Richard Hofstadter.

Origins

The phrase "survival of the fittest" was coined by the philosopher Herbert Spencer, who drew parallels between his economic theories and Charles Darwin's theory of biological evolution; Darwin later adopted the phrase in his own work. Writers such as William Graham Sumner applied such reasoning to argue against government intervention and poor relief, while other thinkers used evolutionary language to justify imperialism, racism and eugenics.

Criticism

Historians and biologists have long noted that social Darwinism misrepresents Darwin's theory: it commits the naturalistic fallacy by deriving moral and political "oughts" from natural processes, and Darwin himself resisted many of these applications for human societies. The label is applied so loosely that scholars debate whether it names a coherent doctrine at all. Nevertheless, ideas under its banner influenced laissez-faire economic policy, colonial expansion and, in the 20th century, eugenic programmes in several countries.
