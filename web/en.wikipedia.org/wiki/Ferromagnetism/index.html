Ferromagnetism

Ferromagnetism is a property of certain materials (such as iron) that results in a significant, observable magnetic permeability, and in many cases, a significant magnetic coercivity, allowing the material to form a permanent magnet. Ferromagnetic materials are noticeably attracted to a magnet, which is a consequence of their substantial magnetic permeability.

Ferromagnetism is the strongest type of magnetism and is responsible for the common phenomenon of magnets encountered in everyday life. It is one of the several forms of magnetism distinguished from paramagnetism, diamagnetism, antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism.

Explanation

In ferromagnetic materials, the unpaired electron spins within small regions called magnetic domains align parallel to one another because of a quantum mechanical effect known as the exchange interaction. When an external field is applied, the domains align with the field, producing a strong net magnetisation that persists, in part, after the field is removed — the basis of the permanent magnet. The material exhibits hysteresis: its magnetisation depends on its magnetic history, traced out by the hysteresis loop.

Curie temperature

Above a characteristic temperature called the Curie temperature (1043 K for iron), thermal agitation destroys the spontaneous alignment and the material becomes paramagnetic.

Materials

Only a few elements are ferromagnetic at room temperature: iron, cobalt, nickel, and (just below room temperature) gadolinium. Many alloys and compounds, such as steel, alnico, and neodymium–iron–boron, are strongly ferromagnetic; neodymium magnets are the strongest permanent magnets commercially available.

Applications

Ferromagnetic materials are used in electric motors, generators, transformers, electromagnets, magnetic recording media, and magnetic resonance imaging equipment.
