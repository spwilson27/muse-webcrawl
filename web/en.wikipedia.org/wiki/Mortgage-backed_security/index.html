Mortgage-backed security

A mortgage-backed security (MBS) is a type of asset-backed security that is secured by a collection of mortgages. The mortgages are aggregated and sold to a group of individuals (a government agency or investment bank) that "securitizes", or packages, the loans together into a security that investors can buy. The cash flows paid by borrowers — principal and interest — are passed through to the security holders, typically monthly.

Securitization pools individual mortgages and sells claims on their cash flows to investors.

History

The modern MBS market began in the United States in 1970, when the Government National Mortgage Association (Ginnie Mae) guaranteed the first mortgage pass-through security. Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae followed with their own programmes, creating a deep market in "agency" MBS whose credit risk was backed by government-sponsored enterprises. Private-label MBS, issued by banks without agency guarantees, grew rapidly in the 2000s.

Types

The simplest form is the pass-through, in which collections from the pool are passed directly to investors pro rata. More complex structures, called collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs), slice the cash flows into tranches with different maturities and risk profiles. Investors in MBS bear prepayment risk — the risk that borrowers refinance when rates fall — as well as credit risk in non-agency securities.

Role in the 2008 financial crisis

During the US housing bubble, lenders originated large volumes of subprime and Alt-A mortgages that were packaged into private-label MBS and CDOs and sold worldwide with high credit ratings. When house prices fell and defaults surged in 2007–2008, the value of these securities collapsed, inflicting huge losses on banks and investors and precipitating the 2008 financial crisis. In September 2008 Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were placed into federal conservatorship, and the Federal Reserve later bought over a trillion dollars of agency MBS under quantitative easing.
