High-yield debt

In finance, a high-yield bond (non-investment-grade bond, speculative-grade bond, or junk bond) is a bond rated below investment grade by the credit rating agencies — below Baa3 by Moody's or below BBB− by Standard & Poor's and Fitch. These bonds carry a higher risk of default but pay higher yields than better-quality bonds in order to compensate investors, and they constitute the asset class known as high-yield debt.

High-yield bonds trade at wider credit spreads than investment-grade debt

Issuers and uses

High-yield issuers include companies with heavy debt burdens, young or speculative businesses without long track records, and "fallen angels" — large firms whose ratings have been downgraded from investment grade. Companies use high-yield borrowing to finance expansion, acquisitions, and leveraged buyouts; the debt is typically unsecured, often with maturities of seven to ten years, and frequently callable after a few years.

History

The modern high-yield market was created in the late 1970s and 1980s by the investment bank Drexel Burnham Lambert and its head of high-yield bonds, Michael Milken, who argued that a diversified portfolio of below-investment-grade bonds earned returns that more than compensated for defaults. The market financed corporate raiders, leveraged buyouts, and new industries such as cable television and mobile telephony, but it collapsed in 1989–90 amid defaults, scandal, and Drexel's bankruptcy; Milken was imprisoned for securities violations. The market recovered in the 1990s and grew into a global asset class worth on the order of two trillion dollars, including a European market that developed after the introduction of the euro.

Risk and performance

The central risk of high-yield debt is credit risk: historically, speculative-grade issuers default at rates of several percent per year, rising sharply in recessions, while recovery rates on defaulted bonds average well under half of face value. Prices of high-yield bonds therefore behave partly like equities in market stress. Investors manage the risk through diversification, credit analysis, and loan covenants, and the segment sits alongside leveraged loans and private credit in the broader market for below-investment-grade corporate borrowing.
