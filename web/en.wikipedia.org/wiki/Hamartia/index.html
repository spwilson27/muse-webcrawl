Hamartia (from Ancient Greek ἁμαρτία, literally "missing the mark" or "error") is a term used in literary criticism for the flaw or mistake that leads to the downfall of a tragic hero. It derives from Aristotle's Poetics, where the ideal tragic protagonist falls from prosperity to adversity not through vice or depravity but through some hamartia.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether Aristotle meant a moral character flaw — the popular notion of the "tragic flaw" — or an error in judgment made in ignorance. Classic examples include Oedipus, whose relentless pursuit of truth and earlier killing of a stranger at a crossroads bring about the catastrophe in Oedipus Rex; Hamlet's hesitation; and Macbeth's ambition. Because the hero is "better than ordinary" yet errs, the audience experiences pity and fear, producing catharsis.

Other uses

In the Greek New Testament, hamartia is the word most often translated as "sin", again in the sense of failing to hit the intended mark. In medicine, the unrelated term hamartoma refers to a benign, disorganised overgrowth of tissue, from the same root idea of an error of development.
