Lebesgue decomposition theorem

In measure theory, the Lebesgue decomposition theorem states that for any two σ-finite signed measures μ and ν on a measurable space, there exists a unique decomposition of ν into a part that is absolutely continuous with respect to μ and a part that is singular with respect to μ. It is a fundamental companion to the Radon–Nikodym theorem and generalises the decomposition of a function of bounded variation into continuous and jump parts.

Statement

Theorem. Let (X, Σ) be a measurable space and let μ and ν be σ-finite signed measures on it. Then there exist unique σ-finite signed measures νac and νs such that:
  • ν = νac + νs;
  • νac is absolutely continuous with respect to μ (νac ≪ μ);
  • νs is singular with respect to μ (νs ⊥ μ), i.e. it is concentrated on a set of μ-measure zero.

Relation to distribution functions

Applied to Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures on the real line, the theorem corresponds to the decomposition of a distribution function into three parts: an absolutely continuous part (given by an integral of a density), a singular continuous part (such as the Cantor function), and a discrete jump part. The Lebesgue decomposition is therefore the measure-theoretic basis for classifying probability distributions into discrete, absolutely continuous, and singular continuous types.

Proof and refinements

The standard proof constructs νac by restricting ν to a maximal set on which it is dominated by a multiple of μ and applying the Radon–Nikodym theorem; what remains is singular. A refinement due to Henri Lebesgue himself decomposes with respect to Lebesgue measure specifically, and the result extends to signed and complex measures under σ-finiteness hypotheses.
