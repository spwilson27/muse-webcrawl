Closed-end fund

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A closed-end fund's market price often trades at a discount or premium to its net asset value

A closed-end fund (CEF) is a collective investment fund that raises a fixed amount of capital through an initial public offering (IPO) and then trades its shares on a stock exchange, much like an individual stock. Unlike open-end mutual funds, which continuously issue and redeem shares at net asset value (NAV), a closed-end fund issues shares once; investors buy and sell from each other in the secondary market.

Price vs. net asset value

Because supply and demand set the market price, CEF shares frequently trade at a discount to NAV, and sometimes at a premium. Persistent discounts are a long-studied puzzle in finance and have led activist investors to push for buybacks, tenders, or conversion to open-end status.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • The manager is not forced to sell assets to meet redemptions, suiting illiquid strategies (municipal bonds, emerging markets, private assets).
  • Funds can use leverage to enhance returns — and risk.
  • Investors may pay a premium, or be unable to exit at NAV.

Comparison with ETFs

Exchange-traded funds also trade on exchanges, but authorised participants create and redeem shares in kind, keeping market price close to NAV — a mechanism closed-end funds lack.
