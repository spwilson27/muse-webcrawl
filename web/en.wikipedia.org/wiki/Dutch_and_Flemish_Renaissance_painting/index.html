Dutch and Flemish Renaissance painting
Period: c. 1400–1600
Region: Low Countries
Predecessor: Early Netherlandish painting

Dutch and Flemish Renaissance painting represents the response in the Low Countries to Italian Renaissance art, beginning in the late 15th century and lasting to about 1600, alongside the culmination of Early Netherlandish painting.

History

Artists such as Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden had pioneered oil painting and meticulous naturalism in the 15th century. From about 1500, Antwerp became a major centre; painters including Quentin Matsys, Joachim Patinir — inventor of the world landscape — and Pieter Bruegel the Elder absorbed Italian perspective and classical motifs while retaining northern detail.

Romanists and later developments

The Romanists, including Jan Gossaert and Frans Floris, travelled to Italy and introduced Mannerist anatomy and mythological subjects. Bruegel developed peasant scenes and landscape into major genres. Religious upheaval, iconoclasm in 1566, and the Dutch Revolt split the region: the Catholic south (Flanders) and Protestant north diverged, leading in the 17th century to Flemish Baroque under Rubens and Dutch Golden Age painting in the Republic.

Characteristics

Detailed surface description, landscape backgrounds, moralising symbolism, and the rise of independent genres — landscape, still life and peasant scenes — mark the period, as does the growth of art markets and printmaking.
