Müllerian duct

The Müllerian ducts (or paramesonephric ducts) are paired embryonic structures that, in female development, give rise to the fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix and the upper part of the vagina. They are named after Johannes Peter Müller, who described them in his 1830 work on the development of the genitals. In males, the ducts regress under the influence of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH).

Development

The Müllerian ducts form in embryos of both sexes around the sixth week of gestation, as invaginations of the coelomic epithelium lateral to the Wolffian (mesonephric) ducts, whose presence is required as a guide for their elongation. Each duct has three parts: a cranial portion that opens into the coelom (the future abdominal ostium of the fallopian tube), a horizontal portion crossing the Wolffian duct, and a caudal portion that fuses with its counterpart in the midline. The fused caudal segment becomes the uterus and upper vagina; the unfused cranial and horizontal parts become the fallopian tubes.

Sex differentiation

In male embryos, Sertoli cells of the developing testis secrete anti-Müllerian hormone from about week 8, causing the Müllerian ducts to regress; small remnants persist as the appendix testis and the prostatic utricle. In female embryos, the absence of AMH allows the ducts to develop, while the Wolffian ducts regress for lack of testosterone. Genes including WNT4, WNT7A, PAX2 and the HOXA cluster pattern the developing ducts.

Clinical significance

Congenital anomalies of Müllerian development (Müllerian duct anomalies) include uterine didelphys, bicornuate and septate uterus, unicornuate uterus, and the absence of the uterus and upper vagina in Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser (MRKH) syndrome. Defective regression in males causes persistent Müllerian duct syndrome, usually due to mutations in AMH or its receptor, in which a male develops a uterus and tubes — often discovered during surgery for undescended testes or hernia.
