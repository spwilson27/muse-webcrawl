Zeila (Somali: Saylac; Arabic: زيلع‎) is a historic port city in the Awdal region of western Somaliland, on the southern coast of the Gulf of Aden near the border with Djibouti. For centuries it was one of the principal commercial ports of the Horn of Africa, linking the Ethiopian highlands and the Somali interior with Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean world.

Early history

Zeila has been identified with the ancient Avalites of the first-century Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a market town trading in spices, ivory and tortoiseshell. Islam arrived early; the town's al-Qiblatain mosque, with its two mihrabs — one facing Mecca and one attributed to an earlier orientation toward Jerusalem — is traditionally dated to the first decades of Islam, making Zeila one of the oldest centres of the faith in Africa.

Medieval golden age

Under the Ifat and Adal sultanates Zeila flourished as an emporium for gold, coffee, ivory, slaves and frankincense. The traveller Ibn Battuta visited in about 1331 and described a large, bustling city. In the sixteenth century it was the seaward base of Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi's campaigns against Ethiopia, and was raided by Portuguese fleets; in 1517 and again in 1528 the Portuguese attacked and burned the town.

Later history

Zeila passed under Ottoman influence, was administered from Mocha in Yemen, and in 1875 was occupied by Egypt. In 1884 it became part of British Somaliland. Its trade steadily lost ground to Djibouti (city) and later to Berbera, and a tsunami following the 1945 Makran earthquake damaged the town. Modern Zeila is a small settlement amid impressive ruins, its colonial-era buildings and old mosques standing as reminders of its former importance. In 2024, a memorandum between Ethiopia and Somaliland concerning sea access revived attention on Zeila and nearby Lughaya as possible port sites.
