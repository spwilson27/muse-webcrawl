Eskimo

Eskimo is a term used to refer to two closely related Indigenous peoples of the circumpolar Arctic: the Inuit, inhabiting northern Canada, Greenland and northern Alaska (Iñupiat), and the Yupik of western Alaska and the Russian Far East. Together with the more distantly related Aleut (Unangan), they speak languages of the Eskimo–Aleut family. The broader term sometimes used by linguists and anthropologists is "Eskimoan peoples".

Terminology

The origin of the word "Eskimo" is disputed; it was long popularly (and probably wrongly) explained as meaning "eaters of raw meat", while a leading scholarly proposal derives it from an Innu-aimun (Montagnais) word referring to snowshoes or to speaking a foreign language. In Canada and Greenland, "Eskimo" is widely considered offensive and has been replaced in official use by "Inuit"; Canada's Constitution Act of 1982 recognises the Inuit as a distinctive group of Aboriginal Canadians. In Alaska, where both Inuit (Iñupiat) and Yupik peoples live, "Eskimo" remains in some common and legal use because "Inuit" does not include the Yupik, though many prefer specific names or "Alaska Native".

Culture and history

Eskimoan peoples descend from the Thule culture that spread across the Arctic after about 1000 CE. Traditional life centred on hunting sea mammals, caribou and fish, with dog sleds, kayaks and umiaks, igloos in some regions, and tailored skin clothing suited to extreme cold. Population across the circumpolar region is roughly 180,000.
