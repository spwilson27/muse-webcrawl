Tudor conquest of Ireland

The Tudor conquest of Ireland (also called the reconquest) took place under the Tudor dynasty of England in the 16th century. Following a failed rebellion by the Earl of Kildare's heir in 1534, English rulers sought to complete the conquest that had begun with the Norman invasion of 1169 but had stalled outside the Pale. By 1603, after the Nine Years' War, England exercised control over the whole island for the first time, transforming Ireland politically, socially and religiously.

Background

In 1541 Henry VIII was declared King of Ireland by the Irish Parliament, upgrading the old lordship into a kingdom in personal union with England. His government pursued "surrender and regrant", under which Gaelic and Hiberno-Norman lords surrendered their lands and received them back under English law with English titles. The policy was undermined by the English Reformation: Ireland remained overwhelmingly Catholic while the crown imposed Protestantism, fusing religious and national resistance.

Wars and plantations

Under Elizabeth I, rebellions in Munster (the Desmond Rebellions, 1569–83) and the crushing of the Shane O'Neill in Ulster were followed by confiscations and the first plantations — settlements of English colonists in Laois and Offaly, and later Munster. The final and greatest challenge came with the Nine Years' War (1593–1603), led by Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone, and Red Hugh O'Donnell. Spanish troops landed at Kinsale in 1601 but were defeated; O'Neill submitted at Mellifont in March 1603, days after Elizabeth's death.

Consequences

The conquest destroyed the Gaelic lordships' independence; the Flight of the Earls in 1607 opened the way to the Plantation of Ulster. Irish landholding, law (the Brehon laws were suppressed), language and Catholic landownership all entered long declines, and the sectarian geography planted in the 17th century shaped Irish history into the modern era.
