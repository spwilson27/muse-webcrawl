The wave (audience)

Also known asMexican wave
PopularisedEarly 1980s, North America
Typical speedAbout 20 seats per second

The wave (known outside North America as the Mexican wave) is an example of a metachronal rhythm in a crowd, especially at sporting events: successive groups of spectators briefly stand, yell and raise their arms, producing a ripple that travels around the stadium. Each person rises as their neighbours do, so the wave propagates without any organiser.

Origins

The wave was popularised in the United States in the early 1980s. Claims to invention include cheerleader Krazy George Henderson, who led one at an Oakland Athletics baseball game in October 1981, and the University of Washington, whose marching band tradition under Robb Weller produced a wave the same month. It gained worldwide fame at the 1986 FIFA World Cup in Mexico — the source of its international name — and has since become a fixture of large stadiums, and occasionally of controversy when it distracts from play or occurs at solemn venues.

Physics

Studies of crowd waves modelled them as an excitable medium, like a chemical reaction or forest fire: a small number of instigators (a few dozen) can trigger one, the wave usually travels clockwise at about 12 metres (20 seats) per second, and it maintains a roughly constant width of 6–15 metres. Researchers have used the model to think about crowd behaviour and safe stadium design.
