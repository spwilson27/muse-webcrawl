Roman governor

A Roman governor was an official appointed to administer a Roman province, exercising within it military command (imperium), jurisdiction and supervision of taxation. Under the Republic, provinces were allotted to ex-magistrates — propraetors and proconsuls — typically for one year. Governors were notorious at times for extortion, against which provincials could prosecute them on their return, as Cicero famously prosecuted Verres, governor of Sicily.

Under the Empire

Augustus divided provinces between the Senate and himself. Senatorial provinces were governed by proconsuls chosen by lot, usually for a year; imperial provinces, where legions were stationed, were governed by the emperor's deputies, legati Augusti pro praetore, serving at his pleasure. Smaller or sensitive provinces such as Judaea were administered by equestrian prefects or procurators — Pontius Pilate was prefect of Judaea. Egypt, the emperor's personal domain, was ruled by an equestrian prefect, and senators were barred from entering without permission.

Duties and staff

The governor toured his province holding assize courts (conventus), commanded its garrison, supervised local councils and tax collection, and maintained order and the imperial cult. He was assisted by quaestors, legates and a small official staff; routine administration was largely left to the cities.
