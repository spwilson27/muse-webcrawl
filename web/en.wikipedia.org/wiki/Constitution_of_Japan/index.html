Constitution of Japan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Constitution of Japan is the supreme law of Japan, promulgated on 3 November 1946 and in force from 3 May 1947. Drafted during the Allied occupation (largely by US officials of MacArthur's headquarters in February 1946, after the Japanese government's own draft proved too conservative, then negotiated with the Diet), it replaced the Meiji Constitution of 1889 and transformed the state: sovereignty moved from the emperor to the people; the emperor became “the symbol of the State and of the unity of the People” (Article 1); and fundamental human rights were proclaimed eternal and inviolate.

Its most famous provision is Article 9, renouncing war and the maintenance of “war potential” — interpreted by governments to permit the Self-Defense Forces (established 1954) and, controversially, a 2014–15 reinterpretation allowing limited collective self-defence. The constitution has never been amended — the world's oldest unamended constitution — despite revision procedures (two-thirds of both Diet houses plus a referendum) and persistent LDP ambition to revise Article 9 and other clauses, checked by public ambivalence and coalition arithmetic. Rights jurisprudence is restrained (the Supreme Court has struck down few statutes), though landmark rulings exist on equality and apportionment.
