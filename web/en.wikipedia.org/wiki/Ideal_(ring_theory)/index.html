Ideal (ring theory)

In ring theory, a branch of abstract algebra, an ideal of a ring is a special subset of its elements. Ideals generalise certain subsets of the integers, such as the even numbers or the multiples of 3, and play a central role in the study of rings: the quotient of a ring by an ideal is again a ring, just as the quotient of a group by a normal subgroup is again a group.

Definitions

Let R be a ring. A nonempty subset I of R is a left ideal if it is an additive subgroup of R and for every r in R and every x in I, the product rx lies in I. A right ideal is defined analogously with the product xr. A subset that is both a left and a right ideal is a two-sided ideal, usually called simply an ideal (the case of interest for commutative rings, where the two notions coincide).

History

Ideals were introduced by Richard Dedekind in 1871 as "ideal numbers", extending Ernst Kummer's earlier notion designed to rescue unique factorisation in rings of algebraic integers: numbers might fail to factor uniquely, but ideals always factor uniquely into prime ideals in Dedekind domains. Emmy Noether later gave the modern axiomatic treatment.

Types of ideals

  • Principal ideal: generated by a single element a, written (a).
  • Prime ideal: a proper ideal P such that if ab ∈ P then a ∈ P or b ∈ P; the quotient is an integral domain.
  • Maximal ideal: a proper ideal contained in no other proper ideal; the quotient is a field.
  • Primary, radical and irreducible ideals refine the decomposition theory of ideals.

Operations

Ideals can be added, multiplied and intersected; the sum of two ideals is the smallest ideal containing both, while their product is generated by all products of an element of one with an element of the other. The quotient ring R/I inherits addition and multiplication from R, and I is prime (respectively maximal) exactly when the quotient is a domain (respectively a field).
