Worker cooperative

A worker cooperative is a cooperative business owned and self-managed by its workers. Worker-members typically govern on a one-member-one-vote basis, elect their managers or boards, and share the surplus according to rules agreed in the co-operative's statutes — often in proportion to hours worked rather than capital invested. Worker cooperatives form part of the wider cooperative movement codified in the Rochdale Principles (1844) and the International Co-operative Alliance's Statement on the Co-operative Identity (1995).

Characteristics

Defining features generally include democratic member control, limited return on capital, indivisible reserves in some legal traditions, and the subordination of capital to labour — the inverse of the conventional firm, in which capital hires labour. Internal capital accounts (as at Mondragon) may track each member's stake while preserving collective ownership. Legal forms vary: France's SCOP, Italy's cooperative societies under the 1947 law and the Marcora Law (1985) funding worker buyouts, Spain's sociedades laborales and Basque cooperatives, and various US state cooperative corporation statutes.

Notable examples

The Mondragon Corporation in the Basque Country of Spain, founded in 1956 under the inspiration of the priest José María Arizmendiarrieta, is the world's largest worker cooperative federation, employing roughly 70,000–80,000 people across industrial firms such as Fagor (whose appliance arm failed in 2013), the Eroski retail chain and the Caja Laboral bank. Other prominent examples include the John Lewis Partnership in the UK (an employee trust rather than a pure cooperative), Argentina's empresas recuperadas — factories occupied and restarted by workers after the 2001 crisis, such as the FaSinPat (ex-Zanon) ceramics plant — and thousands of small cooperatives in Emilia-Romagna, Italy, and in the French SCOP sector.

Performance and critique

Empirical studies generally find worker cooperatives have survival rates comparable to or better than conventional firms, more stable employment across recessions (adjusting pay and hours rather than jobs), and narrower pay differentials between managers and shop floor. Critics argue that cooperatives face difficulties raising investment capital, may under-invest or "degenerate" toward conventional hierarchies as they scale, and remain a small share of most economies — under 1% of employment in the US and UK, though larger in France, Italy and Spain.
