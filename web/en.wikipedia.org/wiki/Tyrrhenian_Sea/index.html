The Tyrrhenian Sea (Italian: Mar Tirreno; French: Mer Tyrrhénienne) is part of the Mediterranean Sea off the western coast of Italy. It is bounded by the islands of Corsica and Sardinia to the west, the Italian peninsula to the east, and Sicily to the south. Its name derives from the Tyrrhenians — the Greek name for the Etruscans, whose civilisation flourished along its shores.

Geography

The Tyrrhenian is roughly triangular. It connects to the Ligurian Sea to the northwest through the Corsica Channel, to the wider Mediterranean to the southwest, and to the Ionian Sea through the Strait of Messina in the southeast. Its coasts include the Tuscan Archipelago (Elba, Giglio and others), the Pontine Islands, Capri and Ischia in the Bay of Naples, and the volcanic Aeolian (Lipari) Islands north of Sicily, among them the active volcanoes Stromboli and Vulcano. Major ports include Naples, Salerno, Palermo, Cagliari, Civitavecchia, and Livorno.

Geology

The Tyrrhenian basin is geologically young. It formed over roughly the last 10 million years as a back-arc basin behind the Apennine subduction zone, where the African plate slides beneath the Eurasian plate and the overriding crust has stretched and thinned, in places to true oceanic crust — one of the youngest patches of ocean floor in the Mediterranean. The basin contains the Marsili Seamount, a huge submarine volcano in its southeastern deep plain, and its active margins explain Italy's volcanism (Vesuvius, Etna, Stromboli) and frequent earthquakes.

Oceanography and fauna

Waters circulate generally cyclonically (counterclockwise). The Tyrrhenian is an important habitat and migration corridor for Mediterranean cetaceans, including fin whales and sperm whales, and its rocky coasts and island archipelagos support protected marine areas. Fishing for tuna and swordfish has a long history in the straits and island waters.
