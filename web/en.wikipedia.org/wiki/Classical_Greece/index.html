Classical Greece

Classical Greece was a period of around 200 years (the 5th and 4th centuries BC) in Greek culture, following the Archaic period and preceding the Hellenistic period. This period had a powerful influence on the Roman Empire and was foundational to much of Western civilization, including art, architecture, philosophy, theatre, mathematics, and politics.

The Parthenon, built 447–432 BC, the supreme monument of Classical Greece.

History

The period conventionally begins with the Athenian revolution of 508 BC and the reforms of Cleisthenes, and witnessed Athens and Sparta jointly repel two Persian invasions (490 and 480–479 BC, with battles at Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis, and Plataea). Athens converted the Delian League into an empire and, under Pericles, built the Parthenon and developed radical democracy — while also producing the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides and the comedies of Aristophanes. The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) ended Athenian supremacy; Sparta and then Thebes held hegemony in the 4th century, while Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle transformed philosophy. The period ends with Philip II of Macedon's victory at Chaeronea (338 BC) and the death of Alexander the Great (323 BC).

Culture

Classical Greek sculpture moved from the balanced contrapposto of Polykleitos to the grace of Praxiteles; red-figure vase painting, Doric and Ionic temple architecture, the histories of Herodotus and Thucydides, Hippocratic medicine, and the mathematics of Euclid and the Pythagoreans all shaped later civilization.
