Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg

Theobald Theodor Friedrich Alfred von Bethmann Hollweg (29 November 1856 – 1 January 1921) was a German politician who served as Chancellor of the German Empire from 1909 to 1917. A career civil servant without a party base, he steered Germany through the July Crisis of 1914 and most of the First World War, pursuing domestic reform at home and a policy of attempted moderation between the military high command and the Reichstag parties abroad. British Prime Minister H. H. Asquith's reported description of Bethmann Hollweg's phrase about the Belgian neutrality treaty — a "scrap of paper" — made him notorious in Allied propaganda.

Early career

Born into a Frankfurt banking family ennobled through his grandfather August von Bethmann-Hollweg, a noted jurist and Prussian minister, Bethmann Hollweg studied law at Strasbourg, Leipzig and Berlin and entered the Prussian civil service. He rose steadily — district administrator, then president of the province of Brandenburg — earning a reputation as a conscientious, liberal-minded administrator. In 1905 he became Prussian Minister of the Interior and in 1907 Imperial Secretary of State for the Interior and Vice-Chancellor.

Chancellorship

Appointed Chancellor in July 1909 after the fall of Bernhard von Bülow, Bethmann Hollweg tried to pursue a "policy of the diagonal": balancing left and right while reforming the Prussian three-class franchise and seeking a naval understanding with Britain. His government passed constitutional reform for Alsace-Lorraine and social insurance codification. In the July Crisis following the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, he authorised the "blank cheque" of support to Austria-Hungary and, hoping to localise the conflict, gambled on a short war — calculations that collapsed as Russia, France and Britain entered the conflict. His remark to the British ambassador on 4 August 1914 that the 1839 treaty guaranteeing Belgian neutrality was a "scrap of paper" was widely cited as proof of German cynicism.

During the war Bethmann struggled to hold the middle against annexationists, the army high command of Hindenburg and Ludendorff, and advocates of unrestricted submarine warfare, which he resisted until January 1917, when the military prevailed — a decision that brought the United States into the war. In July 1917, after the Reichstag's Peace Resolution and a crisis engineered by the high command, Wilhelm II dismissed him; he was replaced by Georg Michaelis.

Later life and assessment

Bethmann Hollweg retired to Hohenfinow and wrote his memoirs, Betrachtungen zum Weltkriege (Reflections on the World War), defending his record and admitting a share of German guilt. He died of pneumonia on New Year's Day 1921. Historians remain divided: Fritz Fischer's school emphasised his responsibility for the Septemberprogramm of annexationist war aims, while others portray him as a moderate overwhelmed by military and institutional forces he could not control.
