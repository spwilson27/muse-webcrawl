LargestRoss Ice Shelf, Antarctica (~472,000 km²)
DistributionAntarctica (~75% of coastline), Greenland, northern Canada

An ice shelf is a large floating platform of ice that forms where a glacier or ice sheet flows down to a coastline and onto the ocean surface. Ice shelves are found mainly in Antarctica, where they surround about 75% of the coastline, but also in Greenland and northern Canada.

Characteristics

Ice shelves can be tens to hundreds of metres thick. They are fed by glaciers flowing off the land and by snowfall, and they lose mass by calving icebergs at their seaward front and by melting from below where they contact ocean water. Because the shelf is already floating, its melting or calving does not directly raise sea level, but shelves buttress the grounded ice behind them; their loss accelerates glacier flow and thus sea-level rise.

Major ice shelves

The largest is the Ross Ice Shelf, roughly the size of France, followed by the Filchner–Ronne Ice Shelf. Others include the Larsen, Amery, Getz, and Thwaites-associated shelves in Antarctica, and the Petermann shelf in Greenland.

Collapse and climate change

Several shelves have disintegrated rapidly after warming: the Larsen A shelf collapsed in 1995 and the Larsen B in 2002, each in a matter of weeks, following the appearance of surface meltwater ponds that fractured the ice. Warm ocean water thinning shelves from below is a key concern for the stability of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet and future sea-level rise.
