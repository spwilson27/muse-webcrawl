Error of law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An error of law is a mistake in the application or interpretation of law by a decision-maker — a trial court's misstatement of the governing rule, admission or exclusion of evidence under a wrong legal standard, or an agency's misreading of its statute. Errors of law are the classic ground of appeal: they are reviewed de novo (without deference) in the usual taxonomy of Standard of review, and, if prejudicial under the applicable harmless-error test, require reversal or remand.

The contrast category is error of fact (findings unsupported by the evidence, reviewed deferentially as “clearly erroneous”), and the line between them — the mixed question of law and fact — is itself one of appellate law's organising puzzles (a question such as “was this search reasonable?” being assigned de novo review in US doctrine since Ornelas, 1996). French cassation and its descendants institutionalise the distinction: the cour de cassation quashes only for errors of law, remanding the facts to lower courts. Administrative law adds the ultra vires family — error of law on the face of the record, jurisdictional error, Wednesbury unreasonableness and proportionality — as the grounds of judicial review.
