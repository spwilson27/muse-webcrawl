Futurism (Christianity)

In Christian eschatology, futurism is an approach to interpreting the prophecies of the books of Daniel and Revelation which holds that most of their events lie in the future, shortly before and surrounding the Second Coming of Christ. It is one of four main interpretive systems, alongside historicism, preterism, and idealism. Futurism is the interpretive backbone of modern dispensationalism and of most contemporary evangelical teaching on the end times.

Beliefs

Futurists generally hold that prophecies such as the rise of the Antichrist, the Great Tribulation, the Battle of Armageddon, and the millennial reign of Christ await future fulfilment. Most futurists insert a long "gap" — now approaching two thousand years — between the 69th and 70th "weeks" of Daniel's prophecy (Daniel 9:24–27), placing the seventieth week in the final seven years before Christ's return. In its dispensational form, futurism expects the rapture of the church before or during that tribulation period, followed by Christ's visible return and a literal thousand-year kingdom.

History

The futurist reading was first systematised by the Spanish Jesuit Francisco Ribera around 1590 in his commentary on Revelation, partly as a Counter-Reformation response to the Protestant historicist identification of the papacy with the Antichrist. Futurist ideas were later developed by Cardinal Robert Bellarmine and, among Protestants, by Samuel Roffey Maitland in the 1820s. The view was popularised in the English-speaking world by John Nelson Darby and the Plymouth Brethren, and in the 20th century by the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), whose notes embedded futurist dispensationalism in popular evangelicalism. Best-selling works such as Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth (1970) and the Left Behind novels carried futurist scenarios to a mass audience.

Criticism

Critics from preterist and historicist camps argue that futurism removes Revelation's relevance to its original first-century audience, note that the New Testament repeatedly describes the events as "near" or "soon" for its first readers, and question the scriptural basis for a gap in Daniel's seventy weeks. Futurists respond that prophetic perspective can compress distant events and that many details — a restored Israel, a revived global power, a rebuilt temple — could only find meaning in the modern era.
