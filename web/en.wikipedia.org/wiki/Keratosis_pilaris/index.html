Keratosis pilaris

Keratosis pilaris (also known as follicular keratosis, lichen pilaris, or colloquially chicken skin) is a common, autosomal-dominant, genetic condition of the hair follicles characterised by the appearance of small, rough folliculocentric keratotic papules. The condition most often appears on the backs and outer sides of the upper arms, and can also occur on the thighs, buttocks, and cheeks.

Signs and symptoms

Keratosis pilaris presents as clusters of tiny, rough bumps — often described as resembling gooseflesh or sandpaper — centred on hair follicles. The bumps may be skin-coloured, red (keratosis pilaris rubra), or brownish in darker skin. The condition is usually asymptomatic, though it may occasionally itch, especially in dry winter conditions. It commonly begins in childhood, becomes more pronounced during adolescence, and often improves with age; it is estimated to affect a large fraction of adolescents and a substantial proportion of adults worldwide.

Cause

The condition is caused by excess production of keratin, the protein that forms the outer layer of skin, which plugs the hair follicles instead of exfoliating normally. It is associated with mutations in the filaggrin gene in some families and frequently co-occurs with atopic dermatitis (eczema), ichthyosis vulgaris, and dry skin.

Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be managed. Treatment focuses on moisturising and keratolytic agents that help remove the keratin plugs, including creams containing urea, lactic acid, salicylic acid, or topical retinoids. Gentle exfoliation and avoiding harsh soaps may help. Laser treatments are sometimes used for persistent redness. Most cases improve substantially by adulthood.
