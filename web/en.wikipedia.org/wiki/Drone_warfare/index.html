Drone warfare is a form of warfare using unmanned combat aerial vehicles (UCAVs) and weaponized commercial unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). It encompasses long-endurance armed drones that launch precision-guided munitions under remote human control, smaller tactical drones used for reconnaissance and artillery spotting, and one-way "kamikaze" or loitering munitions that destroy themselves on their targets.

History

Unmanned aircraft were used for reconnaissance in the Vietnam War and by Israel in the 1973 Yom Kippur War and the 1982 Lebanon War, where Israeli drones helped dismantle Syrian air defenses in the Bekaa Valley. The decisive shift came with the American RQ-1 Predator, introduced for surveillance over the Balkans in the 1990s and armed with Hellfire missiles in 2001. After the September 11 attacks, the United States used armed drones extensively in Afghanistan, Pakistan's tribal areas, Yemen, and Somalia for counterterrorism "targeted killings," a practice that drew sustained legal and ethical criticism over civilian casualties and sovereignty. The larger MQ-9 Reaper became the workhorse of these campaigns.

Proliferation

Turkey's Bayraktar TB2 demonstrated the impact of mid-priced UCAVs in Libya, Syria, and decisively in the 2020 Nagorno-Karabakh war, where Azerbaijani drones destroyed Armenian armor and air defenses. China (Wing Loong series) and Iran (Shahed series) became major exporters, and dozens of states now operate armed drones.

Small drones and the war in Ukraine

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 transformed drone warfare at the tactical level. Both sides deploy thousands of cheap first-person-view (FPV) drones—many adapted from racing and commercial quadcopters such as DJI models—to strike trenches, vehicles, and individual soldiers, alongside Iranian-designed Shahed-136 loitering munitions used by Russia against Ukrainian infrastructure. Electronic warfare and jamming have become central countermeasures, and production has scaled into the millions of units per year, making small drones responsible for a large share of battlefield losses.

Debate

Supporters argue drones offer persistence, precision, and reduced risk to pilots. Critics cite civilian harm, the psychological toll on targeted populations and operators alike, lowered thresholds for the use of force, and the prospect of fully autonomous weapons, which campaigns such as the Campaign to Stop Killer Robots seek to ban. No binding international treaty specifically regulates armed drones or lethal autonomous weapons.
