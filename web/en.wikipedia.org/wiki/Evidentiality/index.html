Evidentiality

Evidentiality is a linguistic concept referring to the indication of the nature of evidence for a given statement; that is, whether evidence exists for the statement and if so what kind of evidence it is. An evidential (also called a verificational) is the formal grammatical element — typically a morpheme, auxiliary verb, clitic, or particle — required in some languages to mark the source of information.

Overview

An evidential indicates for example whether a statement is based on direct visual evidence, inferred from indirect evidence, or hearsay. Evidentiality is a central feature of the grammars of languages of the Amazon, the Andes, the Himalayas, and many Indigenous languages of North America. In Quechua, for instance, suffixes distinguish information the speaker witnessed (-mi), information inferred (-cha), and reported speech (-si). Turkish contrasts the direct past -di with the inferential/hearsay past -miş.

Distribution

According to the typological work of Alexandra Aikhenvald, roughly a quarter of the world's languages have grammatical evidentiality. In most European languages evidential meanings are expressed optionally, through adverbials such as "allegedly", "reportedly", and "apparently", rather than by obligatory morphology.

Relation to epistemic modality

Evidentiality is often discussed alongside epistemic modality, which encodes the speaker's degree of commitment to the truth of a proposition. Some linguists treat evidentiality as a subcategory of modality; others argue the two are conceptually distinct, since a speaker may report hearsay (evidential) without doubting it (modal).
