Polycarpic

Most perennial trees are polycarpic, flowering and fruiting many times during their lives

Polycarpic plants are those that flower and set seed many times during their lifetime. The term derives from the Greek words poly, "many", and karpos, "fruit". Plants that flower, set seed, and then die are called monocarpic; the distinction corresponds closely, though not exactly, to the difference between perennial plants and annual or biennial plants that die after reproduction.

Characteristics

Polycarpic plants are generally perennial: they survive for multiple growing seasons and reproduce in successive years once they reach maturity. Nearly all woody plants—trees and shrubs—are polycarpic, as are many herbaceous perennials. Iteroparity in animal biology is the analogous reproductive strategy, in which an organism reproduces repeatedly rather than in a single event (semelparity).

Contrast with monocarpic plants

Monocarpic plants, such as annual cereals, many bamboos, and the century plant (Agave americana), invest all their resources in a single reproductive episode and die afterwards. Polycarpic plants, by contrast, must balance the energy costs of reproduction in any one season against survival and future reproduction, and typically produce smaller seed crops relative to their body size in a given year. A plant that flowers repeatedly but dies within one season because of frost is still, by botanical definition, classified by its behaviour in its native climate; the terms describe the plant's intrinsic life-history strategy rather than the outcome in a particular environment.
