Leadscrew

Leadscrew
TypePower transmission mechanism
Common thread formsAcme/trapezoidal, square, buttress, ball screw
ApplicationsLathes, vises, jacks, linear actuators, 3D printers, CNC machines

A leadscrew (or lead screw), also known as a power screw or translation screw, is a screw used as a linkage in a machine to translate turning motion into linear motion. Because of the large area of sliding contact between their male and female members, screw threads have larger frictional energy losses compared to other linkages, so they are not typically used for continuous power transmission but for intermittent positioning and clamping duties.

Common applications include the leadscrew of a lathe, which drives the carriage at a precise rate for turning and thread cutting; screw jacks and vises; valve actuators; and the Z-axis drives of many 3D printers and CNC machines. A ball screw is a close relative that replaces sliding with rolling contact for much higher efficiency.

Properties

The lead of a screw is the linear distance travelled per revolution. A leadscrew converts torque into thrust: for a given torque, a finer lead gives greater force but slower travel. Most plain leadscrews are self-locking — the load cannot back-drive the screw — because the lead angle is smaller than the friction angle, a property exploited in jacks and vises. Efficiency of an Acme screw is typically 20–40%, depending on lead and lubrication.

Thread forms

Square threads are most efficient but difficult to manufacture; Acme and metric trapezoidal threads are stronger and easier to cut and are the usual choice; buttress threads are used where thrust is predominantly in one direction. Multi-start threads give a larger lead for the same pitch, trading force for speed.
