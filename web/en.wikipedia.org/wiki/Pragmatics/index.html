Pragmatics

Pragmatics is a subfield of linguistics and semiotics that studies how context contributes to meaning — in particular, how speakers use language to do more than literally encode information, and how listeners infer intended meaning beyond the words used. It contrasts with semantics, which studies meaning encoded in expressions themselves.

Pragmatics explains how an utterance such as "It's cold in here" can function as a request to close a window.

Key concepts

  • Speech acts: following J. L. Austin and John Searle, utterances perform actions — asserting, requesting, promising, apologising — with locutionary, illocutionary and perlocutionary dimensions.
  • Implicature: H. P. Grice proposed that conversation is governed by a cooperative principle and maxims (quantity, quality, relation, manner); speakers exploit these to imply more than they say.
  • Presupposition: information an utterance takes for granted, as "Jane has stopped smoking" presupposes that Jane smoked.
  • Deixis: expressions such as I, here and tomorrow whose reference depends on the context of utterance.
  • Politeness: Brown and Levinson's theory of face and politeness strategies in interaction.

History and related frameworks

The term was coined by Charles Morris (1938) in his division of semiotics into syntax, semantics and pragmatics. Austin's lectures How to Do Things with Words (published 1962), Searle's Speech Acts (1969) and Grice's William James lectures (1967) founded the modern field. Later frameworks include relevance theory (Sperber and Wilson), which recasts pragmatic inference as a search for optimal relevance, and neo-Gricean theories of Horn and Levinson. Pragmatics interacts with sociolinguistics, discourse analysis, cognitive linguistics and the philosophy of language.
