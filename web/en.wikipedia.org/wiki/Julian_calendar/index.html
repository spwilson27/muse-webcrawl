The Julian calendar is a solar calendar of 365 days in every year with an additional leap day every fourth year (without exception). The Julian calendar is still used as a liturgical calendar in parts of the Eastern Orthodox Church and in parts of Oriental Orthodoxy as well as by the Amazigh people of North Africa. [Image: Roman calendar inscription (fasti) showing the months of the Julian calendar]

The Julian calendar was proposed in 46 BC by (and takes its name from) Julius Caesar, as a reform of the earlier Roman calendar, which was largely a lunisolar one. It took effect on 1 January 45 BC, by his edict. Caesar's calendar became the predominant calendar in the Roman Empire and subsequently in most of the Western world for more than 1,600 years, until 1582 when Pope Gregory XIII promulgated a refined version, the Gregorian calendar, which most of the world has since adopted as the civil standard.

Motivation

The ordinary Roman calendar consisted of 12 months, for a total of 355 days. Because it was based on a lunar cycle, it required the periodic insertion of an intercalary month, the mensis intercalaris, by the pontifices to keep the calendar aligned with the seasons. By the late Republic this intercalation had become irregular and subject to political manipulation, so that by 46 BC the calendar was about three months ahead of the solar year. Caesar, advised by the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes, reformed the calendar to a purely solar system.

Design

The reformed calendar has a regular year of 365 days divided into 12 months, and a leap day is added to February every four years, giving a mean year of 365.25 days. The month lengths inherited by the modern calendar were fixed under the Julian reform:

The names Quintilis and Sextilis were later renamed July (Iulius) and August (Augustus) in honour of Julius Caesar and Augustus. To bring the calendar into alignment, 46 BC was extended to 445 days — the "last year of confusion".

Leap year error

Although Greek astronomers had known since Hipparchus that the tropical year is slightly shorter than 365.25 days, the Julian calendar did not compensate for this difference. As a result, the calendar gains about three days every 400 years compared to the observed equinox times. By 1582 this accumulated error had reached ten days, so that the spring equinox fell on 11 March rather than 21 March, the date fixed by the Council of Nicaea for computing Easter.

Replacement by the Gregorian calendar

Pope Gregory XIII introduced a modification in 1582: century years are leap years only if divisible by 400, reducing the mean year to 365.2425 days. Catholic countries adopted it quickly; Protestant and Orthodox countries adopted the Gregorian calendar over the following centuries, Greece being the last European country to adopt it for civil use in 1923. The difference between the calendars, 13 days in the 20th and 21st centuries, explains why the Russian "October Revolution" of 1917 is commemorated in November in the Gregorian calendar.

Modern usage

The Julian calendar remains in use for liturgical purposes in several Eastern Orthodox churches, and the Revised Julian calendar, proposed in 1923, is used by some national churches for fixed feasts. The Amazigh (Berber) calendar of North Africa is a survival of the Julian calendar used for agricultural purposes.
