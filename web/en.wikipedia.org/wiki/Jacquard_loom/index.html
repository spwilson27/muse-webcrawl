Invention

The Jacquard loom is a power loom fitted with a Jacquard head that uses a chain of punched cards to control the lifting of individual warp threads, making complex figured fabrics — brocade, damask, matelassé — weavable by a single operator. The French inventor Joseph Marie Jacquard (1752–1834), a Lyon silk weaver, demonstrated his device in 1801 and patented it in 1804, building on the card mechanisms of Basile Bouchon (1725), Jean-Baptiste Falcon, and Jacques de Vaucanson. Napoleon granted the patent to the city of Lyon in 1805, making Jacquard a pensioner of the state.

Mechanism

Each card in the laced chain corresponds to one pass of the shuttle. Spring-loaded needles press against the card: where a hole is punched, the corresponding hooks remain upright and are lifted by the rising griffe, raising their warp threads; where the card is blank, the hook is pushed aside and the thread stays down. Patterns of thousands of cards thus encode elaborate designs, and changing a design means simply changing the card chain.

Impact

Despite initial resistance from Lyon's silk workers, who feared unemployment (Jacquard was nearly lynched and his looms smashed), by 1812 some 11,000 Jacquard looms operated in France. The loom revolutionised the silk industry of Lyon and later the cotton and carpet trades of Britain, enabling intricate patterned textiles at industrial speed.

Influence on computing

The Jacquard loom's punched cards are a celebrated ancestor of computer programming: they inspired Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, were championed by Ada Lovelace ("the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves"), and resurfaced in Herman Hollerith's census tabulators and the punched-card data processing of the twentieth century.
