Ecclesiastical Latin

Ecclesiastical Latin, also called Church Latin or Liturgical Latin, is a form of Late Latin developed to discuss Christian thought and used in the documents, liturgy and administration of the Latin Church. It remains the official language of the Holy See, and documents of the Roman Catholic Church, including the acts of ecumenical councils and papal encyclicals, continue to be issued in it.

Characteristics

Ecclesiastical Latin is based on the Latin of late antiquity as found in authors such as Jerome, Augustine and Boethius, rather than on classical Ciceronian Latin, though the two are mutually intelligible in writing. It incorporates Greek and Hebrew loan-words mediated through the Bible (ecclesia, baptisma, pascha), new vocabulary for Christian concepts, and simplified constructions influenced by the Vulgate. Its traditional pronunciation, modelled on Italian, differs from reconstructed classical pronunciation: for example, c before e or i is pronounced "ch", and gn as "ny".

Use

Church Latin was the language of Western scholarship and worship throughout the Middle Ages. The Roman Rite Mass was celebrated in Latin everywhere until the reforms following the Second Vatican Council (1962–65) permitted widespread use of vernacular languages, though Latin remains the normative language of the Roman liturgy and its use continues in chant, seminaries, canon law and Vatican administration. The standard edition of the Bible in Church Latin is the Nova Vulgata.
