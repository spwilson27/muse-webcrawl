Ivan Wyschnegradsky

Ivan Alexandrovich Wyschnegradsky (4 May 1893 – 29 September 1979) was a Russian composer who settled in Paris and became a pioneer of microtonal music, particularly the use of quarter tones. He developed the concept of "pansonority" and a theory of sound continua in which pitch is treated as a continuum divisible into ever smaller intervals.

Life and work

Born in St Petersburg, Wyschnegradsky studied law and composition; an ecstatic concert experience of Scriabin's music in his youth, which he later described as an "awakening", turned him toward microtonality. After the Russian Revolution he emigrated to Paris in 1920. There he designed quarter-tone pianos (built by Pleyel, with three keyboards) and wrote works such as the 24 Preludes in quarter tones for two pianos tuned a quarter tone apart, the oratorio The Day of Existence, and numerous string quartets using sixth-tones and third-tones.

Legacy

Largely neglected during his lifetime, Wyschnegradsky's music was revived from the 1970s, with concerts and recordings in Europe and Canada, and he is now regarded alongside Alois Hába and Julián Carrillo as a founder of microtonal composition. He died in Paris in 1979.
