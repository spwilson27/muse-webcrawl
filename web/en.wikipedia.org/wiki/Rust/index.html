Rust

Rust is an iron oxide, a usually reddish-brown oxide formed by the reaction of iron and oxygen in the catalytic presence of water or air moisture. Rust consists of hydrous iron(III) oxides (Fe₂O₃·nH₂O) and iron(III) oxide-hydroxide (FeO(OH)), and is typically associated with the corrosion of refined iron. Unlike the protective oxide layers on aluminium or copper, rust is porous and flaky: it does not shield the underlying metal, so corrosion continues until the iron is entirely consumed.

Chemistry

Rusting is an electrochemical process. At anodic sites iron atoms lose electrons and enter solution as Fe²⁺ ions, while at cathodic sites dissolved oxygen is reduced; the ions combine and oxidise further to form hydrated iron(III) oxides. Water is essential, and dissolved salts — as in seawater or road de-icing — accelerate the reaction by increasing conductivity. Acids, high temperature, and contact with less reactive metals (galvanic corrosion) also speed rusting.

Economic impact and prevention

Corrosion of iron and steel costs industrial economies several percent of GDP annually in replacement, maintenance, and failures, including bridge and pipeline collapses. Prevention methods include protective coatings (paint, galvanising with zinc, powder coating), alloying — stainless steel contains chromium, which forms a passive chromium oxide layer — cathodic protection with sacrificial anodes, corrosion inhibitors, and control of exposure to moisture. Rust converters based on tannic or phosphoric acid transform rust into a more stable, paintable surface.

Other uses of the term

"Rust" also names a group of plant diseases caused by rust fungi (order Pucciniales), such as wheat stem rust, and was borrowed for the colour rust, a reddish-brown, and for the programming language Rust.
