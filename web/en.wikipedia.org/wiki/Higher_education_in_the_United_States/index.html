Higher education in the United States

Higher education in the United States is an optional stage of formal learning following secondary education, delivered by some 4,000 degree-granting institutions including public and private universities, liberal arts colleges, community colleges, and for-profit institutions. Around 19 million students are enrolled. American universities dominate global research rankings and attract the world's largest number of international students.

History

The first colleges, beginning with Harvard (1636), were founded in the colonial era to train clergy and civil leaders. The Morrill Land-Grant Acts of 1862 and 1890 created public universities focused on agriculture and the mechanical arts, while the GI Bill of 1944 massively expanded access after the Second World War. The Higher Education Act of 1965 established large-scale federal student aid.

Structure

Undergraduate study typically lasts four years leading to a bachelor's degree, following a liberal-arts or major-based curriculum. Graduate study leads to master's and doctoral degrees, and professional schools award degrees in law (JD), medicine (MD), and business (MBA). Two-year community colleges award associate degrees and serve as transfer pathways. Institutions are accredited by regional and national accrediting bodies recognised by the US Department of Education.

Funding and costs

Funding comes from tuition, state appropriations (for public institutions), endowments, and federal research grants. Rising tuition and student debt — totalling more than $1.6 trillion — are major public policy concerns. Financial aid includes Pell Grants, federal student loans, and institutional scholarships.
