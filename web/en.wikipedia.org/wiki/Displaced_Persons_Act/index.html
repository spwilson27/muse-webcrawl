Displaced Persons Act

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Displaced Persons Act of 1948 was a United States federal law that authorised the admission of displaced persons (DPs) from Europe to the United States following the Second World War. Signed by President Harry S. Truman on 25 June 1948, the act allowed roughly 200,000 displaced persons to enter over two years, with the numbers charged against future immigration quotas of their countries of origin – a mechanism critics called "mortgaging" the quotas.

Background

At the end of the war, millions of Europeans were living in displaced persons camps in Germany, Austria, and Italy, including Holocaust survivors, former forced labourers, and people who had fled or been expelled from Eastern Europe and the Soviet sphere. Truman had already directed, in December 1945, that existing quotas be used preferentially for displaced persons, but a dedicated statute was needed to admit larger numbers.

Provisions and controversy

The 1948 act set eligibility dates and occupational preferences that in practice disadvantaged Jewish survivors: it required applicants to have entered the DP camps by 22 December 1945 and reserved a large share of visas for agricultural workers and for people from territories annexed by the Soviet Union, notably the Baltic states. Truman signed it reluctantly, publicly calling some of its provisions discriminatory and flagrantly unjust to Jewish displaced persons.

Amendment of 1950

The act was amended on 16 June 1950, extending the programme, moving the eligibility cutoff date forward, expanding the total number of visas to more than 400,000, and removing several of the provisions that had disadvantaged Jewish applicants. Under the original act and its amendments, roughly 400,000 displaced persons were admitted to the United States by 1952, alongside separate provisions for war orphans and other special categories.
