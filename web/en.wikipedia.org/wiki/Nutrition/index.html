Nutrition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nutrition is the process by which organisms obtain and use food — and the science that studies it. Nutrients divide into macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins and fats, supplying energy and building blocks) and micronutrients (vitamins and minerals, needed in small amounts for specific biochemical roles), plus water and fibre. Human nutritional science connects diet to health outcomes from deficiency diseases to the chronic diseases of excess.

Deficiency classics mark its history: scurvy (vitamin C), beriberi (B1), pellagra (niacin), rickets (vitamin D), goitre (iodine). Today the global burden is double-edged — undernutrition and stunting in poorer regions alongside epidemics of obesity, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease driven by energy-dense, ultra-processed diets.

Dietary patterns

Evidence consistently favours dietary patterns rich in vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts and fish (Mediterranean-style), with limited salt, sugar, processed meat and trans fats. Guidance is issued as national food guides and WHO recommendations; individual nutrient fads cycle faster than the underlying evidence.

Metabolism

Energy from food is captured as ATP through cellular respiration; intake beyond expenditure is stored as glycogen and fat. Basal metabolic rate, activity and the thermic effect of food set requirements, which vary with age, sex, pregnancy and illness.
