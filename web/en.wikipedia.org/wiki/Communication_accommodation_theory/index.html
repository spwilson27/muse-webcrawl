Communication accommodation theory

[Image: Interpersonal communication]

Communication accommodation theory (CAT) is a theory of communication developed by the social psychologist Howard Giles and his colleagues. It argues that "when speakers interact they adjust their speech, their vocal patterns and their gestures to accommodate to others." The theory explores the various reasons why individuals accentuate or minimise the social differences between themselves and their interlocutors through verbal and non-verbal communication.

Convergence and divergence

The theory identifies two main processes. Convergence is a strategy where individuals adapt to each other's communicative behaviours in terms of a wide range of linguistic and paralinguistic features including speech rate, pausal phenomena, utterance length, accent and vocabulary, in order to seek approval, increase communicative efficiency or signal shared identity. Divergence is the process by which speakers accentuate the speech and identity differences between themselves and their interlocutors, often to maintain social distance or assert group identity. A third pattern, maintenance, involves persisting in one's original style without adjusting.

Development

CAT grew out of Giles's earlier speech accommodation theory of the 1970s, which focused on accent and speech style, and was broadened in the 1980s and 1990s to encompass all aspects of communication behaviour, intergroup relations, and contexts such as intergenerational, intercultural, organisational and health communication. It draws on social identity theory and attribution theory: listeners evaluate convergence positively when they attribute good intentions to the speaker, while perceived over-accommodation — patronising talk, such as "elderspeak" directed at older adults — is judged negatively.

Applications

The theory has been applied to police–citizen encounters, doctor–patient communication, courtroom interaction, second-language learning, media language and computer-mediated communication, including how people adjust their language in emails, social media and interactions with artificial agents.
