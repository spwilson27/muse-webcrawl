Columbidae is a bird family consisting of pigeons and doves — the only family in the order Columbiformes. The family contains roughly 350 species in about 50 genera, distributed nearly worldwide, with the greatest diversity in the Indomalayan and Australasian realms. Members range from the tiny common ground dove (about 22 g) to the crowned pigeons of New Guinea (up to 3–4 kg); the extinct dodo and Rodrigues solitaire were also columbids, giant flightless island descendants of pigeons.

The rock dove (Columba livia) is the ancestor of domestic and feral pigeons.

Characteristics

Columbids are stout-bodied birds with short necks, small heads, and short slender bills with a fleshy cere. They feed mainly on seeds, fruits, and some invertebrates. Uniquely among birds, both sexes produce "crop milk", a nutritious secretion of the crop lining, to feed their nestlings (squabs). They drink by suction, immersing the bill and swallowing without raising the head. Nests are typically flimsy platforms of sticks holding one or two white eggs.

Relationship with humans

The domestic pigeon, bred from the rock dove for at least 5,000 years, has been used for food, sport (racing), and message-carrying — homing pigeons served in both World Wars. The passenger pigeon, once among the most numerous birds on Earth, was hunted to extinction by 1914. Doves are near-universal symbols of peace and love, associated with the Holy Spirit in Christianity and with goddesses such as Aphrodite and Venus; the white dove released at ceremonies is usually a domestic rock dove.

Evolution

Molecular studies place Columbiformes near the flamingos/grebes (Mirandornithes) and the sandgrouse and mesites within the clade Columbimorphae. The family's fossil record extends to the early Miocene of France, though its origins are probably much older.
