Women in mathematics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The field of mathematics has historically been dominated by men, and women have faced substantial barriers to education and professional recognition; nevertheless, women have made fundamental contributions in every era, often working against institutional exclusion. Since the late 20th century their participation has grown steadily, though women remain underrepresented among research mathematicians and prizewinners.

Notable figures include Hypatia of Alexandria (5th century), Maria Gaetana Agnesi, Sophie Germain, Sofia Kovalevskaya — the first woman to obtain a doctorate in mathematics by research — and Emmy Noether, whose theorem linking symmetry and conservation laws Albert Einstein's contemporaries called her "the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced since the higher education of women began". In 2014 Maryam Mirzakhani became the first woman to receive the Fields Medal; Karen Uhlenbeck became the first woman to receive the Abel Prize in 2019.

History

Access to universities was closed to women in most countries until the late 19th century, so early women mathematicians typically learned through family connections — fathers, brothers or husbands in the field — and published under male names or posthumously. Sophie Germain corresponded with Gauss as "Monsieur Le Blanc".

Antiquity to the 18th century

Hypatia (c. 350–415 AD), head of the Neoplatonic school in Alexandria, wrote commentaries on Diophantus and Apollonius and was murdered by a Christian mob. In the 18th century Maria Gaetana Agnesi published the first calculus textbook covering both differential and integral calculus (1748), and Émilie du Châtelet produced the standard French translation and commentary of Newton's Principia.

19th and early 20th centuries

Ada Lovelace wrote what is regarded as the first algorithm intended for a machine. Sofia Kovalevskaya (1850–1891) earned a doctorate from Göttingen (in absentia) and became the first woman full professor in northern Europe, at Stockholm. Emmy Noether revolutionised abstract algebra and theoretical physics despite Göttingen initially refusing her a paid post; David Hilbert reportedly protested that the faculty was "not a bathhouse".

Modern era

Julia Robinson contributed decisively to solving Hilbert's tenth problem. Maryam Mirzakhani (1977–2017), a Stanford professor, won the Fields Medal for work on the dynamics of Riemann surfaces. Ukrainian analyst Maryna Viazovska won the Fields Medal in 2022 for the sphere-packing problem in eight dimensions. Ingrid Daubechies transformed signal processing with wavelets.

Representation and initiatives

Organisations such as the Association for Women in Mathematics (founded 1971) and European Women in Mathematics promote mentoring and visibility. The International Day of Women in Mathematics is celebrated on 12 May, Mirzakhani's birthday.
