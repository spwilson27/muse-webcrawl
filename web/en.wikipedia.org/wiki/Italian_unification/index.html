Italian unification

Italian unification (Italian: Unità d'Italia), also known as the Risorgimento ("Resurgence"), was the 19th-century political and social movement that in 1861 united the states of the Italian Peninsula into the Kingdom of Italy, and was completed with the capture of Rome in 1870. The process combined the statecraft of the Kingdom of Sardinia under Count Camillo Benso of Cavour, the diplomacy and wars of Victor Emmanuel II, and the popular military expeditions of Giuseppe Garibaldi, within an intellectual movement inspired by figures such as Giuseppe Mazzini.

Background

After the Congress of Vienna (1815), Italy was a patchwork: the Austrian-dominated Kingdom of Lombardy–Venetia, the Habsburg duchies of Tuscany, Parma and Modena, the Papal States, the Kingdom of Sardinia (Piedmont), and the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. Secret societies such as the Carbonari staged failed revolts in 1820–21 and 1830–31. Mazzini's Young Italy (founded 1831) preached a democratic, republican nation; others favoured a federation under the Pope or leadership by Piedmont.

Wars of independence and Cavour

The First Italian War of Independence (1848–49) ended in defeat by Austria at Custoza and Novara, but Piedmont kept its liberal constitution, the Statuto Albertino. As prime minister from 1852, Cavour modernised Piedmont, joined the Crimean War to gain a diplomatic voice, and in 1858 made the secret Plombières agreement with Napoleon III of France. In the Second War of Independence (1859), Franco-Piedmontese victories at Magenta and Solferino won Lombardy; Tuscany, Parma, Modena and Romagna then voted for annexation to Piedmont (1860), while France took Nice and Savoy.

Garibaldi and the Expedition of the Thousand

In May 1860 Giuseppe Garibaldi sailed from Genoa with about a thousand red-shirted volunteers, landing in Sicily. Victories at Calatafimi and Palermo, then on the mainland, collapsed the Bourbon kingdom; Garibaldi handed the south to Victor Emmanuel II at Teano in October 1860. Plebiscites confirmed annexation, and on 17 March 1861 the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed with Turin as capital (Florence from 1865).

Completion: Venetia and Rome

Italy allied with Prussia in the Austro-Prussian War of 1866 and gained Venetia despite defeats at Custoza and Lissa. Rome remained protected by a French garrison until the Franco-Prussian War withdrew it; on 20 September 1870 Italian troops breached Porta Pia, and Rome became the capital in 1871. The "Roman Question" with the papacy persisted until the Lateran Treaty of 1929. Remaining Italian-speaking territories under Austria — Trentino and Trieste, the terre irredente — were gained only after the First World War.
