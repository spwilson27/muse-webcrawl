German unification

The unification of Germany into a single nation-state was achieved on 18 January 1871, when the German Empire (Deutsches Kaiserreich) was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, with Wilhelm I of Prussia as German Emperor. The process was driven by Prussia under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck through a series of calculated wars and skillful diplomacy.

Anton von Werner's depiction of the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles, 18 January 1871

Background

The Holy Roman Empire, dissolved in 1806, left some 39 German states loosely joined in the German Confederation (Deutscher Bund). The revolutions of 1848 attempted liberal unification through the Frankfurt Parliament, but the "small German" (kleindeutsch) versus "greater German" (großdeutsch) question — whether to include Austria — proved unresolvable, and the Frankfurt offer of the crown was refused by Frederick William IV of Prussia.

Bismarck's wars

  • Second Schleswig War (1864): Prussia and Austria seized Schleswig-Holstein from Denmark.
  • Austro-Prussian War (1866): Prussia defeated Austria at Königgrätz, dissolved the German Confederation, and formed the Prussian-dominated North German Confederation — the kleindeutsch solution excluding Austria.
  • Franco-Prussian War (1870–71): Bismarck's edited Ems Dispatch provoked France into declaring war; the German states rallied to Prussia, Napoleon III was captured at Sedan, and the southern German states joined the confederation.

Proclamation and consequences

The empire united 25 states under Prussian leadership, excluding Austria. Bismarck's "blood and iron" realpolitik created Europe's most powerful land empire, shifting the continental balance of power. The exclusion of Austria ("Lesser Germany") shaped Central European politics until 1918, while the new empire's rapid industrialization made it a colonial and naval rival to Britain.
