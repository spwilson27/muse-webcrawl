Fictionalism

Fictionalism treats some discourse as a useful fiction
FieldPhilosophy; metaphysics; philosophy of language
Key figuresHans Vaihinger, Hartry Field, Gideon Rosen, Stephen Yablo

Fictionalism is a philosophical position holding that certain areas of discourse — for example talk about numbers, possible worlds, moral values, or unobservable theoretical entities — need not be taken as literally true or as referring to real objects, even though the discourse is useful or even indispensable. Instead, such talk is treated as a kind of fiction: statements within it are accepted, asserted, or used without commitment to their literal truth.

Varieties

Modal fictionalism, developed by Gideon Rosen, holds that talk of possible worlds is a useful fiction that need not commit us to the real existence of other worlds. Mathematical fictionalism, associated with Hartry Field, holds that mathematical statements are literally false (because there are no abstract mathematical objects) but useful as a representational aid. Fictionalist positions have also been defended in ethics (moral fictionalism), in the philosophy of science regarding theoretical entities, and with respect to talk of colours, free will, and religious language.

Background and criticism

The approach descends in part from Hans Vaihinger's The Philosophy of 'As if' (1911), which argued that humans construct fictions and behave "as if" they were true. Fictionalism is often contrasted with realism about the subject matter and with error theory, which likewise denies the literal truth of the discourse but treats continued use of it less favourably. Critics ask whether fictionalists can explain the usefulness and apparent indispensability of the discourse, and whether the distinction between literal belief and fictional acceptance can be maintained in practice.
