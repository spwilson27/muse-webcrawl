Credit union

Image: A local credit union branch office (placeholder)

A credit union is a member-owned, not-for-profit financial cooperative that provides savings, credit and other financial services to its members. Unlike commercial banks, a credit union is owned and governed by the people who use its services: each member typically holds one vote regardless of the size of their deposits, and earnings are returned to members in the form of lower loan rates, higher savings rates and lower fees, or reinvested in the institution.

History

The modern credit union movement originated in 19th-century Germany, with the people's banks of Hermann Schulze-Delitzsch and Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen serving townspeople and farmers respectively. The first North American credit union (caisse populaire) was founded by Alphonse Desjardins in Lévis, Quebec, in 1900; Desjardins later helped organise credit unions in the United States, where the Federal Credit Union Act (1934) established a federal charter system. In the US, deposits are insured by the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA, created 1970) up to the same limits as FDIC bank insurance.

Membership and structure

Membership is normally restricted by a "field of membership" — a common bond such as an employer, occupation, association, faith or community — although community charters have broadened eligibility in many places. Credit unions range from tiny volunteer-run institutions to some of the largest financial cooperatives in the world; Navy Federal Credit Union, the largest in the US, has tens of millions of members and hundreds of billions of dollars in assets. Worldwide, the World Council of Credit Unions reports hundreds of millions of members in more than 100 countries.

Differences from banks

Because they are not-for-profit and member-owned, credit unions in many jurisdictions enjoy tax exemptions — a longstanding point of contention with the banking industry, which argues the exemption gives large credit unions an unfair advantage. Credit unions are also generally barred from commercial lending beyond set limits and cannot raise capital on stock markets, relying instead on retained earnings.
