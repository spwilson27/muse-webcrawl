The Sudbury Basin, also known as the Sudbury Structure or the Sudbury Nickel Irruptive, is a major geological structure in Ontario, Canada. It is the third-largest known impact structure on Earth, as well as one of the oldest. The basin lies on the Canadian Shield in the city of Greater Sudbury, Ontario. The structure was formed by the impact of an asteroid or comet approximately 10–15 km (6.2–9.3 mi) in diameter about 1.85 billion years ago, during the Paleoproterozoic era.

Formation and geology

The impact created a crater perhaps 250 km in diameter; subsequent geological processes have deformed it into the present smaller, oval basin of 62 × 30 km. The impact melted the crust, forming the Sudbury Igneous Complex — a layered body of norite, gabbro, and granophyre — and shattered the surrounding rock into breccias. Shatter cones and shocked quartz confirm the impact origin, which was only widely accepted in the 1960s and 1970s. Debris from the impact (ejecta) has been found as far away as Minnesota.

Mining

The basin is one of the world's richest mining districts. The impact concentrated nickel, copper, platinum, palladium, gold, and other metals in the ores along the margin of the igneous complex. Nickel was discovered during railway construction in 1883, and mining companies such as Inco (now Vale) and Falconbridge (now Glencore) built the economy of Sudbury on these deposits. The Creighton Mine is among the deepest nickel mines in the world and hosts the SNOLAB underground physics laboratory.
