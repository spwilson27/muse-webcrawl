Scuba diving

Scuba diving is a mode of underwater diving whereby divers use a breathing apparatus (scuba set) that is largely independent of surface air supply. The name "scuba" is an acronym of "self-contained underwater breathing apparatus". Unlike free diving, which relies on breath-holding, scuba divers carry their own source of breathing gas, usually compressed air, allowing them to remain underwater for extended periods.

History

The modern scuba era began with the Aqua-Lung, developed in 1943 by Jacques Cousteau and Émile Gagnan, which combined a demand regulator with high-pressure cylinders and allowed divers to swim freely. Military frogmen used earlier closed-circuit rebreathers during the Second World War. Recreational diving grew rapidly from the 1950s with the formation of training agencies such as PADI and NAUI.

Equipment

Standard equipment includes a mask, fins, an exposure suit (wetsuit or drysuit), a cylinder and regulator set, a buoyancy compensator device (BCD), weights, and instruments such as a pressure gauge, depth gauge or dive computer. Technical divers may use mixed gases such as nitrox, trimix and heliox, and rebreathers for deeper or longer dives.

Safety

Risks include decompression sickness ("the bends"), nitrogen narcosis, oxygen toxicity, barotrauma and drowning. Training emphasises dive planning, gas management, controlled ascents and the buddy system. Recreational limits are commonly set at 40 metres, with decompression diving reserved for technical training.

Applications

Besides recreation, scuba is used in scientific diving, commercial diving, military operations, underwater archaeology, search and recovery, and media production.
