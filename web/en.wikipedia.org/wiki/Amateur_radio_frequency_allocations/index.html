Amateur radio frequency allocations are the portions of the radio spectrum assigned to the amateur service by the International Telecommunication Union and implemented, with regional variations, by national regulators. Allocations vary between the ITU's three regions and between countries: some bands are exclusive to amateurs, others are shared with other services on a primary or secondary basis, and privileges within a band may depend on licence class.

Principal bands

Amateurs enjoy bands across the spectrum. On low and medium frequencies lie the 2200-metre (135.7–137.8 kHz) and 630-metre bands and the 160-metre "top band". The high-frequency (HF) bands — 80, 60, 40, 30, 20, 17, 15, 12, and 10 metres — support intercontinental communication via ionospheric propagation and are the heart of DXing and contesting; the 20-metre band (14.000–14.350 MHz) is the most popular. The 6-metre "magic band", the 2-metre band (144–148 MHz in the Americas), and the 70-centimetre band (420–450 MHz) dominate local VHF/UHF activity via repeaters, satellites, and weak-signal work, while microwave allocations extend from 23 centimetres up to 250 GHz for experimentation.

Band plans and regulation

Within allocated bands, voluntary band plans coordinated by national societies and the IARU divide frequencies by mode — CW and digital at the low end, single-sideband telephony higher up, with segments for beacons, satellites, and repeaters — to minimise interference. Power limits, emission standards, and licence conditions are set nationally; allocations are periodically reviewed at World Radiocommunication Conferences, where amateur representatives defend the service's spectrum against commercial pressure.
