Semi-automatic firearm

A semi-automatic firearm, also called a self-loading firearm, is a firearm that automatically chambers a fresh cartridge after each shot is fired, but requires a separate pull of the trigger to fire each individual round. This distinguishes it from a fully automatic firearm, which continues to fire as long as the trigger is held, and from manually operated firearms such as bolt-action rifles, which require the shooter to work an action between shots.

Operation

Semi-automatic mechanisms harness part of the energy of the fired cartridge — through recoil, blowback, or tapped gas — to eject the spent case, cock the firing mechanism, and load the next cartridge from a magazine. Common types include semi-automatic pistols, rifles, and shotguns. The concept dates to the late 19th century, with early designs by Ferdinand Mannlicher and the Mauser C96 pistol; the M1 Garand was among the first semi-automatic rifles issued as a standard infantry arm.

Legal status

Semi-automatic firearms are widely owned for sport, hunting, and self-defence in many countries, but some jurisdictions restrict particular models, magazine capacities, or features. They are frequently at the centre of public debate about firearms regulation, partly because some have been used in mass shootings.
