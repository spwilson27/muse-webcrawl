Life insurance

Life insurance (or life assurance, especially in the Commonwealth of Nations) is a contract between an insurance policy holder and an insurer, where the insurer promises to pay a designated beneficiary a sum of money upon the death of an insured person. Depending on the contract, other events such as terminal illness or critical illness can also trigger payment. The policy holder typically pays a premium, either regularly or as one lump sum.

History

Early methods of spreading risk existed in ancient Rome, where burial clubs pooled contributions to pay funeral costs, and among medieval guilds. The first recorded life insurance policy was issued in London in 1583, on the life of William Gibbons. In 1762 the Equitable Life Assurance Society was founded in London — the first mutual insurer to price policies scientifically using mortality tables and age-based premiums. In the United States, the Presbyterian Church's Corporation for Relief of Poor and Distressed Widows and Children of Presbyterian Ministers (1759) was an early precursor, and the industry expanded rapidly in the 19th century alongside industrialisation.

Types of policy

Term insurance

Term life insurance provides coverage for a specified period — 10, 20, or 30 years are common — and pays only if the insured dies within the term. It is generally the cheapest form per unit of coverage and builds no cash value.

Permanent insurance

Whole life, universal life, and variable life policies combine a death benefit with a savings or investment component (cash value) and remain in force for the insured's lifetime so long as premiums are paid. Permanent policies are substantially more expensive than term cover and are used in estate planning and wealth transfer as well as protection.

Group and industrial policies

Employers and associations often provide group life insurance as an employee benefit, typically with limited underwriting. Historically, small "industrial" policies with weekly premiums collected door-to-door served working-class households, mainly to cover funeral expenses.

Underwriting and taxation

Insurers price policies using mortality tables, medical underwriting, and classification by factors such as age, sex, smoking status, and occupation. In many jurisdictions the death benefit is paid free of income tax, and policies enjoy favourable treatment in estate planning; the industry is regulated at state level in the United States (under the McCarran–Ferguson Act framework) and by solvency regimes such as Solvency II in the European Union. Policies generally require an insurable interest in the life insured, to distinguish insurance from wagering.
