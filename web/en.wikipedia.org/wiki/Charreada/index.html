Charreada

CountryMexico (also practised in the United States)
VenueLienzo charro (arena)

The charreada (Spanish: [tʃareˈaða]) is a competitive Mexican rodeo event and the formal competition of charrería, the traditional equestrian culture of the Mexican horseman, the charro. Recognised as Mexico's national sport, the charreada consists of a sequence of scored events (suertes) demonstrating horsemanship, roping and cattle-handling skills derived from the working practices of colonial haciendas. In 2016 charrería was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The events

A charreada is held in a lienzo charro, a long arena with a lane leading into a circular ring, between teams (equipos) of charros in elaborate embroidered traditional dress. The nine men's suertes are: the cala de caballo (tests of the horse's training and control), piales en el lienzo (roping a mare's hind legs), colas en el lienzo (bull tailing), bull riding, the terna en el ruedo (team roping of a bull), mare riding, manganas a pie and manganas a caballo (roping feats on foot and on horseback), and the dramatic paso de la muerte ("pass of death"), in which a rider leaps from his horse onto a bareback running mare. Women's teams, the escaramuzas, perform synchronised, side-saddle riding routines.

History

Charrería grew from the cattle work of Spanish colonial estates from the 16th century onward, when indigenous and mestizo horsemen became central to ranching in New Spain. After the Mexican Revolution, charros organised formally to preserve the tradition; the Federación Mexicana de Charrería was founded in 1933. Charreadas are now held throughout Mexico and in the southwestern United States. Some events have drawn criticism from animal-welfare organisations, and certain manoeuvres are restricted in some jurisdictions.
