The van der Waals equation is a thermodynamic equation of state that generalizes the ideal gas law to account for the finite size of molecules and the attractive forces between them. Proposed by the Dutch physicist Johannes Diderik van der Waals in his 1873 doctoral thesis, it was one of the first equations of state to describe the behaviour of real fluids, including the liquid–gas phase transition and the existence of a critical point.

The equation

For one mole of a fluid, the van der Waals equation reads:

(p + a/Vm2)(Vm − b) = RT

where p is the pressure, Vm the molar volume, T the absolute temperature, and R the gas constant. The two substance-specific parameters are a, which measures the strength of intermolecular attraction, and b, the excluded volume per mole, roughly four times the actual volume of the molecules themselves.

The term a/Vm2, sometimes called the internal or cohesion pressure, corrects the measured pressure upward because molecules near the wall of a container are pulled back by their neighbours, reducing the force of their collisions with the wall. The constant b reduces the available volume, reflecting that molecules are not point particles.

Critical point

Unlike the ideal gas law, the van der Waals equation predicts isotherms with a maximum and a minimum below a critical temperature, corresponding to the coexistence of liquid and gas. At the critical point the isotherm has an inflection point, which fixes the critical constants in terms of a and b: Vc = 3b, pc = a/(27b2), and Tc = 8a/(27Rb). The predicted critical compressibility factor, Zc = 3/8 = 0.375, is the same for all van der Waals fluids; real fluids typically give values near 0.27–0.29, showing the equation is only qualitatively correct.

Law of corresponding states

Expressed in reduced variables (pressure, volume and temperature divided by their critical values), the equation takes a universal form containing no substance-specific constants. This is the law of corresponding states: fluids at the same reduced temperature and pressure have approximately the same reduced volume. The principle remains a useful engineering approximation even though the van der Waals equation itself has been superseded by more accurate equations of state such as the virial expansion and cubic equations like the Redlich–Kwong and Peng–Robinson equations.

History and significance

Van der Waals presented the equation in his 1873 Leiden dissertation, Over de Continuïteit van den Gas- en Vloeistoftoestand ("On the Continuity of the Gaseous and Liquid States"). James Clerk Maxwell championed the work in Britain, and the equation's prediction of a continuous transition between gas and liquid was a milestone in nineteenth-century physics. Van der Waals received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1910. Although quantitatively inaccurate, the equation introduced the essential physics of molecular size and attraction and laid the groundwork for the modern theory of fluids and phase transitions.
