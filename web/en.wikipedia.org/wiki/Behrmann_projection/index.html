The Behrmann projection is a cylindrical equal-area map projection with standard parallels at 30° N and S, described by Walter Behrmann in 1910. It belongs to the family of cylindrical equal-area projections whose members differ by the choice of standard parallels; the Behrmann is the case with parallels at 30°.

Formulas

For longitude λ and latitude φ (in radians), the projection is:

x = λ cos 30° = (√3/2) λ;   y = sin φ / cos 30° = (2/√3) sin φ

Because areas are preserved, landmasses keep their correct relative sizes — unlike on the Mercator projection — but shapes are increasingly sheared toward the poles, so continents appear vertically stretched near the equator and compressed at high latitudes.

Relationship to other projections

The cylindrical equal-area family also includes the Lambert cylindrical equal-area projection (standard parallel at the equator) and the Gall–Peters projection (standard parallels at 45°). The Behrmann's 30° standard parallels produce a map with a π:2 width-to-height ratio and less extreme shape distortion than the equatorial case. The projection is sometimes used for thematic world maps where accurate area comparison matters, such as distributions of population or climate.
