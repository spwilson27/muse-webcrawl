Ester

In chemistry, an ester is a compound derived from an acid (organic or inorganic) in which at least one hydroxyl group (–OH) is replaced by an alkoxy group (–OR). Most commonly, esters are carboxylic acid derivatives with the general structure RCOOR′, formed by the reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol — a condensation reaction known as esterification, which also produces water.

Structure and naming

The ester group consists of a carbonyl carbon double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to another oxygen linking two carbon groups. Ester names derive from the parent alcohol and acid, as in ethyl acetate (from ethanol and acetic acid) or methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen). Cyclic esters are called lactones.

Properties

Esters are polar but cannot donate hydrogen bonds, so their boiling points are lower than those of comparable carboxylic acids. Lower esters are volatile liquids, many with pleasant fruity odours: isoamyl acetate smells of banana, ethyl butyrate of pineapple, and octyl acetate of oranges. Such esters are widely used as flavourings and fragrances. Fats and oils are triesters of glycerol with long-chain fatty acids (triglycerides).

Reactions

Esters are hydrolysed by water — slowly, but rapidly under acid or base catalysis. Base-promoted hydrolysis, called saponification, yields a carboxylate salt (soap, when applied to fats) and an alcohol. Esters also undergo transesterification (exchange of the alkoxy group, important in biodiesel production), reduction to alcohols with lithium aluminium hydride, and Claisen condensation to form β-keto esters.

Applications

Polyesters such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET) are among the most produced plastics and fibres. Other important esters include cellulose acetate, nitrocellulose, aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid, an ester of salicylic acid), and phosphate esters, which form the backbone of DNA and RNA.
