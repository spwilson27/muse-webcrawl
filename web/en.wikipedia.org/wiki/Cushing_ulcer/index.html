Cushing ulcer


A gastric erosion of the type seen in stress ulceration (placeholder image)

Specialty: Gastroenterology, neurosurgery
Named after: Harvey Cushing

A Cushing ulcer, named after the American neurosurgeon Harvey Cushing, is a type of stress-related mucosal injury: a gastric ulcer associated with elevated intracranial pressure caused by head injury, brain tumor, stroke or neurosurgery.

Mechanism

Unlike other stress ulcers, the Cushing ulcer is thought to arise from direct stimulation of the vagus nerve nuclei by raised intracranial pressure. Vagal overstimulation increases gastric acid secretion through the parietal cells of the stomach, and the resulting hyperacidity injures the mucosa of the stomach, duodenum or esophagus. The lesions tend to be single, deep and prone to perforation.

Clinical features and treatment

Patients — typically critically ill after traumatic brain injury — may develop gastrointestinal bleeding. Management centers on treating the underlying intracranial problem, acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors or H2-receptor antagonists, and endoscopic or surgical intervention if bleeding or perforation occurs. Prophylactic acid suppression is commonly given to patients with severe head injury.
