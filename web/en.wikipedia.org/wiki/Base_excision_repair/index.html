Base excision repair (BER) is a cellular mechanism that repairs damaged DNA throughout the cell cycle. It is responsible primarily for removing small, non-helix-distorting base lesions — such as oxidised, alkylated, deaminated and misincorporated bases — and for sealing many single-strand breaks. An estimated tens of thousands of such lesions arise in each human cell every day, mostly from normal metabolism and spontaneous decay, and BER is the cell's main defence against them.

Mechanism

Repair begins when a DNA glycosylase recognises and removes a damaged base, cutting the bond between the base and the sugar backbone and leaving an abasic (AP) site. Different glycosylases specialise in different lesions — for example, OGG1 removes 8-oxoguanine caused by oxidative stress, and UNG removes uracil arising from cytosine deamination. The AP endonuclease APE1 then incises the backbone, and one of two sub-pathways completes the repair. In short-patch BER, DNA polymerase β inserts a single correct nucleotide and its associated lyase activity removes the remaining sugar fragment, after which the nick is sealed by the ligase III–XRCC1 complex. In long-patch BER, polymerases δ/ε synthesise a flap of two to ten nucleotides that is trimmed by FEN1 before ligation by ligase I.

Medical significance

Defects in BER genes cause or contribute to disease: biallelic mutations in the glycosylase MUTYH cause MUTYH-associated polyposis, a hereditary colorectal cancer syndrome, because adenines mispaired with 8-oxoguanine go uncorrected. Because cancer cells often depend on BER to survive chemotherapy and radiotherapy that damage DNA, inhibitors of BER components such as PARP — a sensor of single-strand breaks that recruits repair factors — have become important anticancer drugs, particularly for tumours already deficient in homologous recombination.
