The lysogenic cycle is one of the two cycles of viral reproduction (the other being the lytic cycle) and is characterised by the integration of the viral genome into the host cell's DNA. In this state the viral nucleic acid, called a prophage when the virus is a bacteriophage, is replicated along with the host chromosome each time the cell divides, without destroying the host. A bacterium harbouring a prophage is called a lysogen, and the condition is termed lysogeny.

Mechanism

After a temperate bacteriophage injects its DNA into a bacterium, the viral genome may circularise and integrate into the bacterial chromosome by site-specific recombination, mediated by a viral integrase enzyme. In the classic example, lambda phage of Escherichia coli, the choice between the lysogenic and lytic pathways is governed by regulatory proteins: the cI repressor maintains lysogeny by switching off lytic genes, while the Cro protein promotes the lytic pathway. While integrated, the prophage is passively copied with every host cell division, potentially for many generations.

Induction

A prophage can exit the chromosome and enter the lytic cycle in a process called induction. Triggers include environmental stress, ultraviolet radiation, and certain chemicals — signals that activate the host SOS response, leading to cleavage of the viral repressor and excision of the prophage. The virus then directs synthesis of new virions, which are released by lysis of the host cell.

Lysogenic conversion

Prophages can carry genes that change the phenotype of their host, a phenomenon known as lysogenic conversion. Several bacterial toxins are encoded by prophages, including diphtheria toxin (Corynebacterium diphtheriae), cholera toxin (Vibrio cholerae, via the CTXφ phage), Shiga toxin (E. coli O157:H7), and some streptococcal toxins. Such conversions can increase bacterial virulence, making lysogeny medically significant.

Animal viruses

Some animal viruses exhibit an analogous latent state — for example herpesviruses, which persist in host cells with only limited gene expression — although latency in animal viruses does not usually involve integration in the same manner (retroviruses such as HIV, by contrast, integrate as proviruses).
