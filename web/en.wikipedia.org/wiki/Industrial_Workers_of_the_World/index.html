The Industrial Workers of the World (IWW), whose members are nicknamed Wobblies, is an international labour union founded in Chicago in 1905. It advocates industrial unionism — organising all workers in an industry into "One Big Union" — and, in its classic programme, the abolition of the wage system and the eventual organisation of production by workers themselves. Its motto is "An injury to one is an injury to all."

Founding

The IWW was founded by figures including Big Bill Haywood, Eugene V. Debs, Mary Harris "Mother" Jones, Daniel De Leon, and Lucy Parsons. Its preamble declared that "the working class and the employing class have nothing in common" and rejected the conservative craft unionism of the American Federation of Labor, seeking instead to organise the unskilled, immigrant, migratory, and non-white workers the AFL largely excluded.

Campaigns and repression

The union led landmark strikes such as the Lawrence textile strike of 1912 (the "Bread and Roses" strike) and the Paterson silk strike of 1913, and fought celebrated "free speech fights" in western cities. During and after the First World War it was subjected to intense repression: federal raids on its halls in 1917, mass trials, criminal syndicalism laws, deportations, and vigilante violence such as the Everett and Centralia massacres and the Bisbee deportation. The songwriter Joe Hill was executed in Utah in 1915 after a controversial murder conviction. Membership declined sharply in the 1920s.

Modern IWW

The IWW survives today as a small but active union, organising workers in retail, food service, delivery, and other sectors, and maintaining its democratic, rank-and-file structure with low dues and elected, recallable officers.
