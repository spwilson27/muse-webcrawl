Gerard Manley Hopkins

Gerard Manley Hopkins SJ (28 July 1844 – 8 June 1889) was an English poet and Jesuit priest, whose posthumously published poetry — written in an innovative "sprung rhythm" and dense with alliteration, coined words, and religious intensity — established him as one of the leading Victorian poets. His major works include "The Windhover", "Pied Beauty", "God's Grandeur", and the "terrible sonnets" of his desolate final years in Ireland.

Life

Hopkins was educated at Highgate School and Balliol College, Oxford, where he studied classics and was influenced by John Henry Newman. In 1866 he converted to Roman Catholicism, a decision that estranged him from his Anglican family, and in 1868 he entered the Jesuit novitiate, burning his early poems on deciding to give up poetry for religion. He resumed writing in 1875, composing The Wreck of the Deutschland to commemorate five Franciscan nuns drowned in the wreck of the Deutschland. After ordination in 1877 he worked as a parish priest and preacher in industrial cities including Sheffield, Liverpool, and Glasgow. In 1884 he became professor of Greek and Latin at University College Dublin, where he suffered depression and ill health, dying of typhoid fever in 1889.

Poetic technique

Hopkins's poetry is built on sprung rhythm, a metre counting stresses rather than syllables, and on his theological-aesthetic concepts of inscape (the unique essence of a thing) and instress (the energy that perceives it). Almost none of his mature work was published in his lifetime; his friend Robert Bridges issued the first edition of the Poems only in 1918, and Hopkins's reputation grew steadily through the twentieth century, influencing poets such as W. H. Auden and Dylan Thomas.
