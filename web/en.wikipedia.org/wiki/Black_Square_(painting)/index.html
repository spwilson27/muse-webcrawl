Black Square (Russian: Чёрный квадрат) is a 1915 oil painting by the Russian avant-garde artist Kazimir Malevich. It depicts a black quadrilateral on a white field and is regarded as one of the most famous and influential works of abstract art, the founding icon of the Suprematist movement.

History

Malevich first exhibited Black Square at the Last Futurist Exhibition of Paintings 0.10 in Petrograd in December 1915, hanging it high across a corner of the room — the position traditionally reserved for a religious icon in Russian homes, a deliberate statement of the work's spiritual ambition. Malevich described it as the "zero point of form" and the starting point of Suprematism, an art of pure feeling freed from depicting the visible world. Malevich painted several later versions of the square, including one around 1923 and one in the late 1920s; a fourth version was discovered in 1993.

Condition

The original 1915 canvas is severely cracked, its black surface webbed with craquelure. X-ray analysis has revealed that Malevich painted the square over earlier compositions, including a Cubo-Futurist work and a proto-Suprematist sketch, suggesting the idea developed quickly from his earlier explorations. Research published in 2015 reported an inscription beneath the paint layer, in Malevich's hand, referring jokingly to an earlier "battle of negroes in a dark cave" — a nod to nineteenth-century monochrome humour pieces.

Legacy

Black Square is widely considered a watershed between representational and non-objective art. Critics have called it the "zero point of painting," and it has been endlessly quoted, parodied, and exhibited — including in major retrospectives at the Tate Modern and the State Russian Museum.
