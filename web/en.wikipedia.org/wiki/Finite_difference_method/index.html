In numerical analysis, finite-difference methods (FDM) are a class of numerical techniques for solving differential equations by approximating derivatives with finite differences. The domain is discretised into a grid of points, the derivatives at each point are replaced by algebraic differences of neighbouring values, and the resulting system of equations is solved numerically.

Derivation

From Taylor's theorem, the first derivative can be approximated by the forward difference f′(x) ≈ (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h with error O(h); central differences such as (f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h improve the accuracy to O(h²). Second derivatives are commonly approximated by (f(x−h) − 2f(x) + f(x+h)) / h².

Time-dependent problems

For evolution equations such as the heat equation, schemes may be explicit (e.g. the FTCS method), implicit, or semi-implicit (Crank–Nicolson). Explicit schemes are simple but conditionally stable — for advection problems the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition must be satisfied — while implicit schemes are usually unconditionally stable at greater computational cost per step. The Lax equivalence theorem states that for a consistent scheme, stability is necessary and sufficient for convergence.

Applications

Finite differences are widely used in computational fluid dynamics, heat transfer, weather prediction, seismology and quantitative finance — the Black–Scholes equation is routinely solved by finite-difference grids. They complement the finite element and finite volume methods, against which they trade geometric flexibility for simplicity on structured grids.
