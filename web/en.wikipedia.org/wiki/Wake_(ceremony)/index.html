A wake is a social gathering associated with death, held before or after a funeral. Traditionally, a wake takes place in the house of the deceased or of a close relative, with the body present, and consists of a vigil kept over the dead from death until burial — the English word derives from the Old English wacu, "a watch or vigil". Wakes allow mourners to view the body, pay their respects, console the family, and share memories of the dead.

History

The practice of watching over the corpse is ancient and widespread, serving both spiritual purposes — guarding the soul and body from evil spirits — and the practical purpose of confirming death in eras before reliable medical certification (reflected in folk tales of premature burial). In Ireland and parts of Scotland and Wales, wakes developed a distinctive character: alongside prayer and keening (improvised lament), traditional Irish wakes included food, drink, tobacco, storytelling, music, games, and practical joking into the night, a mixture of grief and celebration that both clergy and authorities periodically attempted to suppress. The custom travelled with emigration to North America and Australia.

Modern practice

Today, in North America, a "wake" or funeral "viewing"/"visitation" is often held at a funeral home on the evening before the funeral service — a term influenced also by the rosary service held by Roman Catholic families. Open-casket viewings remain common; contemporary wakes may include photo displays, videos, and memorial objects. Similar vigils or lying-in customs exist worldwide: Jewish shemira (watching over the body), the Filipino nine-night pasiyam, Korean and Chinese funerary vigils, and the Māori tangihanga held on marae over several days.
