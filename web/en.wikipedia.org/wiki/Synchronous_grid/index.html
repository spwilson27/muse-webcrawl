Synchronous grid

A synchronous grid is a regional electric grid in which all alternating-current generators rotate in synchrony, so that the whole interconnected network shares a common electrical frequency — 50 Hz in most of the world and 60 Hz in the Americas and parts of Asia. The largest synchronous grid by area is the Continental Europe grid, serving over 400 million people in 24 countries; in North America there are four major interconnections: the Eastern, Western, Texas (ERCOT) and Quebec interconnections.

Properties

Within a synchronous grid, power flows according to the impedances of the transmission network, and any imbalance between generation and consumption shows up immediately as a frequency deviation everywhere in the grid. Operators maintain frequency within tight bounds by balancing supply and demand in real time. Grids that are not synchronized with each other can exchange power only through high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) links, which act as firewalls against cascading failures.

Stability and failures

Synchrony is normally maintained by the inertia of rotating machines. Large disturbances can split a grid into islands or cause cascading blackouts, as in the 2003 North America blackout and the 2003 Italy blackout. The increasing share of inverter-based renewable generation, which adds little physical inertia, is an active engineering challenge for grid operators.
