Fludeoxyglucose (18F)

Fludeoxyglucose [18F] (INN), or fluorodeoxyglucose, commonly abbreviated 18F-FDG, is a radiopharmaceutical used in medical imaging with positron emission tomography (PET). Chemically, it is 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose, a glucose analogue in which the normal hydroxyl group at the 2' position has been replaced by the positron-emitting radionuclide fluorine-18.

Whole-body PET scan using 18F-FDG

Mechanism

After injection, FDG is taken up by metabolically active tissues in proportion to their glucose consumption and is phosphorylated by hexokinase, like ordinary glucose. Unlike glucose-6-phosphate, however, FDG-6-phosphate cannot proceed through glycolysis and remains trapped in the cell, while the fluorine-18 decays (half-life about 110 minutes) emitting positrons that the scanner detects. Tissues with high glucose uptake — the brain, many cancers, and sites of inflammation or infection — appear as "hot spots".

Applications

FDG PET is used chiefly in oncology, for detecting cancers, staging, and monitoring response to treatment, and also in cardiology (assessing myocardial viability) and neurology (evaluating dementia and epilepsy).

Production and distribution

Because fluorine-18 has a half-life of only about 110 minutes, FDG must be produced in a cyclotron and delivered quickly, often from regional production facilities to hospitals the same day — an unusual distribution model for a pharmaceutical.

History

FDG was first synthesised in the 1970s by Ido and co-workers at Brookhaven National Laboratory, and the first human images were obtained in 1976 by Abass Alavi's group at the University of Pennsylvania.
