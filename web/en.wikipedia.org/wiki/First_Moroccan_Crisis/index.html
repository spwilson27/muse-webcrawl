First Moroccan Crisis

The First Moroccan Crisis (also known as the Tangier Crisis) was an international crisis between March 1905 and May 1906 over the status of Morocco. Germany wanted to challenge France's growing control over Morocco, aggravating tensions between the two countries, with the prospect of war. The crisis was resolved by the Algeciras Conference of 1906, a conference of mostly European countries that affirmed French predominance in Morocco while nominally preserving Moroccan independence and equal commercial rights for all nations.

Background

Under the Entente Cordiale of 1904, Britain had recognized France's sphere of influence in Morocco in exchange for French recognition of Britain's position in Egypt. France moved to extend its control through loans and police arrangements with Sultan Abdelaziz. Germany, which had commercial interests in Morocco and had not been consulted, decided to intervene.

The Kaiser's visit

On 31 March 1905, Kaiser Wilhelm II landed at Tangier and declared Germany's support for Moroccan independence and integrity, demanding an international conference. The dramatic visit was intended to test the Entente and to assert Germany's claim to a voice in colonial settlements. Under German pressure, France's foreign minister Théophile Delcassé resigned, and France accepted a conference.

Algeciras Conference

The conference at Algeciras, Spain (January–April 1906), left France and Spain in control of Moroccan police in the ports while confirming the open-door policy for trade and the sovereignty of the Sultan. Only Austria-Hungary supported Germany. The outcome strengthened the Anglo-French Entente and pushed Germany and Austria-Hungary closer together; tensions resurfaced in the Agadir Crisis of 1911.
