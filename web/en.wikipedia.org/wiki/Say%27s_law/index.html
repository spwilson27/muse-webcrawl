Say's law

Say's law of markets, or simply Say's law, is a principle of classical economics named after the French economist Jean-Baptiste Say (1767–1832). It is often summarised as "supply creates its own demand", although Say's own formulation was that production of goods creates the income with which other goods are bought, so that a general glut — sustained overproduction of everything — is impossible, even though particular goods may be overproduced relative to demand.

History and interpretation

Say introduced the idea in his Traité d'économie politique of 1803. Variations were defended by David Ricardo and John Stuart Mill, while Thomas Malthus and later John Maynard Keynes disputed it. Keynes's critique, in The General Theory (1936), argued that aggregate demand could fall short of the level needed for full employment, making Say's law a central target of the Keynesian revolution.

Versions

Economists distinguish stronger and weaker forms: an identity version holding that supply and demand are necessarily equal in aggregate, and an equilibrium version holding that markets tend toward equality. Modern supply-side economists invoke Say's law in support of policies favouring production and investment, while critics regard it as neglecting the independent role of money and effective demand.
