Greedy algorithm

A greedy algorithm is an algorithm that finds a solution to a problem by making, at each step, the choice that looks best at the moment — a locally optimal choice — in the hope that these choices will lead to a globally optimal solution. Greedy algorithms are simple and efficient, but for many problems they do not guarantee an optimal answer.

When greed works

A problem can be solved greedily when it has optimal substructure (an optimal solution contains optimal solutions to subproblems) and the greedy choice property (a locally optimal choice can be part of a global optimum). The abstract structure guaranteeing correctness is the matroid. Classic successes include Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm, Kruskal's and Prim's minimum spanning tree algorithms, and Huffman coding.

When it fails

The greedy strategy for the knapsack problem or for making change with arbitrary coin denominations can fail: with coins of 1, 3 and 4, changing 6 greedily gives 4+1+1 (three coins) instead of the optimal 3+3 (two coins). In such cases dynamic programming or other methods are needed, though greedy methods may still serve as useful approximation algorithms and heuristics — for example, for the travelling salesman problem or set cover, where greedy achieves a logarithmic approximation guarantee.
