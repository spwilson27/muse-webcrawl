Sound barrier

The sound barrier is the sudden increase in aerodynamic drag as an aircraft approaches the speed of sound, accompanied by loss of lift and handling problems in early aircraft. The term came into use during World War II, when some fighters began to experience the effects of compressibility, and was popularly — but misleadingly — believed to be a physical barrier preventing supersonic flight. It was first "broken" in controlled level flight by Chuck Yeager in 1947.

Physics

As an aircraft accelerates toward Mach 1, airflow over parts of the wing reaches supersonic speed while the aircraft itself is subsonic. Shock waves form, causing a sharp rise in drag (the drag-divergence Mach number), buffeting, and "Mach tuck". Swept wings, thin airfoils, all-flying tails, and the area rule were developed to manage these transonic effects.

History

Propeller aircraft of World War II encountered compressibility in dives, sometimes fatally. On 14 October 1947, US Air Force test pilot Chuck Yeager flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 to Mach 1.06 at 45,000 ft over the Mojave Desert, the first confirmed controlled supersonic flight. George Welch may have exceeded Mach 1 in a dive in the XP-86 days earlier, though this remains disputed. Sonic booms produced by supersonic flight led to bans on routine overland supersonic flight, limiting aircraft such as Concorde.

Other uses

The tip of a bullwhip cracks because it exceeds the speed of sound, making the whip arguably the first human-made device to break the sound barrier.
