Prostacyclin

Prostacyclin (also called prostaglandin I2, or PGI2) is a prostaglandin member of the eicosanoid family of lipid molecules. It is produced by endothelial cells from arachidonic acid via prostaglandin H2 and the enzyme prostacyclin synthase. Prostacyclin is a potent vasodilator and the most potent endogenous inhibitor of platelet aggregation known.

Function

Prostacyclin acts on the IP receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor on vascular smooth muscle cells and platelets, raising intracellular cyclic AMP. In smooth muscle this produces relaxation and vasodilation; in platelets it prevents activation and aggregation. Its effects functionally oppose those of thromboxane A2, and the balance between the two mediators helps regulate local blood flow and clot formation. Prostacyclin has a very short half-life in blood — on the order of minutes — and breaks down to the largely inactive 6-keto-PGF1α.

Medical use

The synthetic analogue epoprostenol is used as a drug, most importantly in the treatment of pulmonary arterial hypertension, where continuous intravenous infusion improves symptoms and survival. Other analogues used clinically include iloprost, treprostinil, and beraprost; the IP-receptor agonist selexipag is also used for this indication. Epoprostenol has also been used to prevent platelet clotting in extracorporeal circuits such as dialysis.

History

Prostacyclin was discovered in 1976 by a team led by Salvador Moncada and John Vane, work that contributed to Vane's share of the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
