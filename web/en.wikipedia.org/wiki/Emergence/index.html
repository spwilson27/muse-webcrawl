Emergence

[Hero image placeholder: starling murmuration]

In philosophy, systems theory, science, and art, emergence occurs when a complex entity has properties or behaviours that its parts do not have on their own, and which emerge only when they interact in a wider whole. Emergent properties are central to many sciences and philosophies: examples include the wetness of water (not a property of individual H2O molecules), consciousness arising from neural activity, the flocking patterns of birds, ant colonies, traffic jams, and the behaviour of markets.

Weak and strong emergence

Philosophers commonly distinguish weak emergence, in which macro-level properties are unexpected given the parts but are in principle deducible from them (for example, the patterns in Conway's Game of Life or the temperature of a gas), from strong emergence, in which the higher-level properties are claimed to be irreducible even in principle and to exert novel causal powers ("downward causation"). Strong emergence is associated with British emergentists such as C. D. Broad and Samuel Alexander in the early 20th century, and remains controversial; weak emergence is widely accepted in complexity science.

History

The idea descends from Aristotle and John Stuart Mill's distinction between "homopathic" and "heteropathic" laws, and was developed by George Henry Lewes (who coined the term "emergent" in 1875), Samuel Alexander, C. Lloyd Morgan, and C. D. Broad. Interest waned under the rise of reductionism in the mid-20th century and revived with the growth of systems theory, cybernetics, nonlinear dynamics, and complexity science, including work at the Santa Fe Institute. Philip W. Anderson's influential 1972 essay "More Is Different" argued that at each level of complexity new laws and concepts are needed.

Examples

  • Physics and chemistry — superconductivity, phase transitions, and chemical properties of compounds
  • Biology — life itself, multicellularity, eusocial colony behaviour, ecosystems
  • Mind — consciousness as an emergent property of the brain (emergentism in philosophy of mind)
  • Society and technology — language, money, cities, prices in markets, and large-scale behaviours of computer networks and AI systems

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that "emergence" sometimes functions as a label for ignorance rather than an explanation, and strong emergence faces Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument: if physical causes fully explain an event, emergent mental causes appear redundant. Defenders reply that emergence is a heuristic and explanatory framework — concerning patterns, levels, and computational irreducibility — rather than a claim of magic.
