Rachel Carson (1907–1964) was an American marine biologist and author whose book Silent Spring (1962) documented the environmental harm caused by synthetic pesticides and ignited the modern {L('Environmentalism','environmental movement')}.

A quiet government scientist and bestselling nature writer before fame, Carson is credited with helping to launch environmental protection as a public cause in the United States and beyond.

Life and earlier work

Carson studied zoology and worked for the US Bureau of Fisheries and its successor the Fish and Wildlife Service, rising to editor-in-chief of its publications. Her lyrical books about the sea — Under the Sea Wind (1941), The Sea Around Us (1951), a bestseller that won the National Book Award, and The Edge of the Sea (1955) — made her financially able to write full-time.

Silent Spring

Prompted by reports of birds killed by DDT spraying, Carson spent four years documenting how insecticides, sprayed indiscriminately, accumulate through food chains, thin birds' eggshells and threaten human health, and how chemical companies and regulators suppressed the evidence. Serialised in The New Yorker in 1962, Silent Spring provoked furious industry attacks — she was smeared as hysterical and unpatriotic — but a presidential science panel vindicated her findings.

Carson, already ill with the cancer that killed her in 1964, testified before Congress in 1963. Within a decade the United States had created the {L('United_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency','Environmental Protection Agency')}, banned DDT for agriculture (1972), and passed the foundational air and water laws of the environmental era. Bald eagles and other birds of prey, freed from DDT's eggshell thinning, famously recovered.
