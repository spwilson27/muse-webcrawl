The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō

The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (Japanese: Tōkaidō Gojūsan-tsugi no uchi) is a series of Japanese ukiyo-e woodblock prints by the artist Utagawa Hiroshige, published in 1833–34 by Hōeidō. The series depicts the fifty-three post stations (shukuba) along the Tōkaidō road between Edo (Tokyo) and Kyoto, together with prints of the two termini — fifty-five images in all.

Background

The Tōkaidō was the most important of the Five Routes of Edo-period Japan, linking the shogun's capital with the imperial capital. Hiroshige is said to have travelled the road in 1832 with an official procession conveying horses from the shogunate to the imperial court, sketching the scenery along the way; the journey inspired the series.

The prints

Each print shows a station in a characteristic mood or season: travellers crossing the Nihonbashi bridge at dawn in Edo, porters fording rivers, inns in snow at Kanbara, and the arrival at the Sanjō Ōhashi bridge in Kyoto. The Hōeidō edition was an enormous commercial success that made Hiroshige's reputation, and he returned to the subject in many later series in different formats. The images are celebrated for their atmospheric weather effects and lyrical treatment of everyday travel.

Legacy

The series influenced European artists of the nineteenth century, including Vincent van Gogh, who copied Hiroshige compositions, and it remains the most famous landscape series in Japanese art. Complete sets are held by major museums worldwide.
