Duck curve

The duck curve is a graph of electricity production over the course of a day that illustrates the timing imbalance between peak demand and solar power generation. Popularised by the California Independent System Operator (CAISO) around 2012–2013, the chart of net load — total demand minus variable renewable generation — takes the shape of a duck: a deep midday belly as solar output surges, followed by a steep evening ramp ("the duck's neck") as the sun sets just as demand peaks.

Implications

As solar penetration grows, the midday belly deepens, risking overgeneration and curtailment of renewable output, while the evening ramp becomes steeper, requiring dispatchable generators to start and climb very quickly over about three hours. The pattern also undermines the economics of baseload plant, which cannot cycle cheaply.

Mitigation

Strategies to "flatten the duck" include energy storage (especially batteries charged at midday), demand response and time-of-use pricing to shift load into solar hours, orienting panels westward, regional interconnection to smooth production, flexible loads such as electric vehicle charging and water heating, and curtailment as a last resort. In California the curve has grown more extreme since the term was coined, with spring midday net loads falling close to zero or below; similar curves have appeared in Hawaii, Arizona, Australia, and other high-solar systems.
