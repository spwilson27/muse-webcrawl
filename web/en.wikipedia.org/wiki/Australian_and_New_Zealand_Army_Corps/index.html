The Australian and New Zealand Army Corps (ANZAC) was a First World War army corps of the Mediterranean Expeditionary Force, formed in Egypt in December 1914 from the First Australian Imperial Force and the New Zealand Expeditionary Force. Commanded by Lieutenant-General Sir William Birdwood, it landed at Gallipoli on 25 April 1915 and gave rise to the immortalised term "Anzac."

Formation

Australian and New Zealand volunteers, originally bound for the Western Front, were diverted to Egypt for training when the Ottoman Empire entered the war. There the two national contingents were combined into a single corps — the clerks' abbreviation "A. & N.Z.A.C." stamped on documents is the usual story of the name's origin. The corps comprised the 1st Australian Division and the New Zealand and Australian Division.

Gallipoli

On 25 April 1915 the corps landed at Z Beach — soon called Anzac Cove — about two kilometres north of the intended beach, beneath steep scrub-covered ridges. The troops hung on to a tiny, precarious perimeter for eight months against determined Ottoman defence directed locally by Mustafa Kemal and overall by Otto Liman von Sanders. In August the corps spearheaded the offensive toward the Sari Bair range, briefly gaining the summit of Chunuk Bair, and covered the simultaneous Landing at Suvla Bay. The Anzac position was evacuated, almost without loss, on the nights of 19–20 December 1915.

After Gallipoli and legacy

Reorganised in Egypt, the corps' divisions went on to fight on the Western Front as I and II ANZAC Corps, while the mounted troops served in Sinai and Palestine. More than 10,000 Anzac soldiers died at Gallipoli — some 8,700 Australians and 2,700 New Zealanders. The landing's anniversary, Anzac Day (25 April), became the nations' central day of remembrance, and "Anzac" — protected by law in Australia — stands for ideals of courage, mateship, and endurance.
