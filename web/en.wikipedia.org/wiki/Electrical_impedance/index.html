In electrical engineering, impedance is the opposition to alternating current presented by the combined effect of resistance and reactance in a circuit. Quantitatively, impedance is the complex ratio of the voltage to the current in an alternating current (AC) circuit. It extends the concept of resistance to AC circuits and possesses both magnitude and phase, unlike resistance, which has only magnitude.

Impedance can be represented as a complex number, with the same units as resistance — the ohm (Ω). Its symbol is usually Z and it may be represented by writing its magnitude and phase in the form |Z|∠θ. The concept was introduced by Oliver Heaviside in the 1880s, and the phasor formalism that makes impedance analysis tractable was developed by Arthur Kennelly and Charles Proteus Steinmetz in the 1890s.

Definition

The impedance of a circuit element is the ratio of the phasor voltage across the element to the phasor current through the element:

Z = V / I = R + jX

where R is the resistance (the real part) and X is the reactance (the imaginary part); j is the imaginary unit. When the circuit contains only resistance, impedance reduces to ordinary resistance and voltage and current are in phase.

The magnitude of the impedance is |Z| = √(R² + X²), and the phase angle θ = arctan(X/R) gives the phase difference between voltage and current. The reciprocal of impedance is admittance, measured in siemens.

Device impedances

Resistor

An ideal resistor has impedance Z = R, purely real: the voltage and current are in phase.

Inductor

An ideal inductor has impedance Z = jωL, where ω is angular frequency and L is inductance. Its reactance X = ωL is positive and increases with frequency; the current lags the voltage by 90°.

Capacitor

An ideal capacitor has impedance Z = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC). Its reactance is negative and decreases in magnitude with frequency; the current leads the voltage by 90°.

Combining impedances

Impedances combine like resistances. In series, impedances add: Zeq = Z₁ + Z₂ + ⋯. In parallel, the reciprocals add: 1/Zeq = 1/Z₁ + 1/Z₂ + ⋯. A series combination of inductance and capacitance can exhibit resonance, at which the reactances cancel and the impedance is minimal (for series) and purely resistive; this principle underlies tuned radio circuits.

Measurement and applications

Impedance is measured with impedance analyzers and LCR meters, often over a range of frequencies in a technique called impedance spectroscopy, used in electrochemistry and battery diagnostics. In audio engineering and radio-frequency work, impedance matching between source and load maximizes power transfer and minimizes reflections; characteristic impedances of 50 Ω and 75 Ω are standard for coaxial cables. In medicine, bioelectrical impedance analysis estimates body composition from the impedance of body tissues.
