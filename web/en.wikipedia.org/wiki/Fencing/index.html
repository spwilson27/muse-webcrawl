Fencing
Governing body: FIE (Fédération Internationale d'Escrime)
Olympic since: 1896
Weapons: foil, épée, sabre

Fencing is a combat sport in which two athletes fight with bladed weapons — the foil, épée or sabre — scoring points by striking valid target areas on their opponent. It is one of only five sports contested at every modern Olympic Games since 1896. Fencers compete on a 14-metre strip called a piste, wearing protective clothing including a mask, jacket and glove, and modern scoring is registered electrically.

Weapons

The foil is a light thrusting weapon; valid target is the torso, and conventions of "right of way" award the touch to the fencer who correctly initiated the attack. The épée is a heavier thrusting weapon derived from the duelling sword; the whole body is target and there is no right of way, so simultaneous hits can both score. The sabre, descendant of the cavalry sword, scores with the edge as well as the point; target is everything above the waist, and right of way applies.

Technique

Fencing footwork centres on the advance, retreat, lunge and flèche; blade actions include thrusts, parries (such as quarte and sixte), ripostes, disengages, beats and flicks. Bouts are typically fenced to 15 touches in direct elimination (or 5 in pool rounds), with individual and team events for each weapon.

History

Swordsmanship was codified in European fencing schools from the Renaissance — Domenico Angelo's London school and his 1763 treatise shaped the modern sport — and the French school's terminology remains standard. The Fédération Internationale d'Escrime (FIE) was founded in 1913. Women's foil joined the Olympics in 1924, women's épée in 1996 and women's sabre in 2004, completing the programme.
