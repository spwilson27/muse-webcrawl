Calcitonin is a 32-amino-acid peptide hormone secreted by the parafollicular cells (C cells) of the thyroid gland in humans and other chordates. It acts to lower blood calcium (Ca²⁺) levels, opposing the effects of parathyroid hormone (PTH).

Function

Calcitonin is released when blood calcium rises. It inhibits the activity of osteoclasts, the cells that break down bone, thereby reducing the release of calcium from the skeleton, and it modestly reduces calcium reabsorption in the kidney. In adult humans its physiological role in calcium homeostasis appears small — patients who lose the thyroid gland maintain normal calcium levels — but the hormone is pharmacologically useful.

The CALCA gene also gives rise, by alternative RNA splicing, to calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), a potent vasodilator and neurotransmitter important in migraine — hence CGRP-blocking medicines.

Clinical uses

Salmon calcitonin, which is considerably more potent than the human hormone, has been used to treat Paget's disease of bone, hypercalcaemia, and postmenopausal osteoporosis, though its osteoporosis use has declined because of weak efficacy and a small associated cancer signal that led European regulators to withdraw the nasal spray in 2012. Serum calcitonin is also an important tumour marker for medullary thyroid carcinoma, a cancer of the C cells, and for screening families with MEN 2 syndromes.

History

The hormone was discovered in 1962 by Douglas Harold Copp and B. Cheney at the University of British Columbia, who demonstrated a calcium-lowering factor perfused from the thyroid-parathyroid glands of dogs; it was soon localised to the thyroid's C cells and named for its role in regulating the "tone" of calcium in blood.
