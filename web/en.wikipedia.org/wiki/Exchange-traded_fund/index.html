An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a type of investment fund that is also an exchange-traded product, that is, it is traded on stock exchanges. ETFs own financial assets such as stocks, bonds, currencies, debts, futures contracts, and/or commodities such as gold bars. Many ETFs are index funds that track a market index such as the S&P 500, but the category also includes actively managed funds and funds focused on specific sectors, factors or asset classes.

Structure

Unlike mutual funds, which are priced once per day after markets close, ETF shares trade throughout the trading day at market prices that may differ slightly from net asset value. Large institutional investors known as authorised participants create and redeem ETF shares in large blocks ("creation units") in kind, a mechanism that keeps market prices close to the value of the underlying holdings and makes ETFs comparatively tax-efficient in the United States.

History

The first successful ETF in the United States, the SPDR S&P 500 Trust (ticker: SPY), was launched in 1993 and became one of the most heavily traded securities in the world. ETFs subsequently expanded to bonds, commodities, international markets and, in the 2020s, spot cryptocurrency funds.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages commonly cited include low expense ratios, diversification, transparency of holdings and intraday liquidity. Disadvantages and risks include bid–ask spreads, tracking error, possible premiums or discounts to net asset value, and, for leveraged and inverse ETFs, returns that can diverge sharply from the underlying index over periods longer than a day.
