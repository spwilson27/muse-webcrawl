Lied

Lied (German for "song"; plural Lieder) is a term in classical music for a song — typically a setting of a German poem for solo voice and piano. The Lied flourished in the 19th century as a central genre of Romantic music, in which the piano accompaniment is not mere support but an equal partner that illustrates and deepens the text.

History

The modern Lied emerged in the late 18th century from earlier German song traditions, and rose to artistic maturity with Franz Schubert, who composed more than 600 songs, including the cycles Die schöne Müllerin (1823) and Winterreise (1827) to poems by Wilhelm Müller, and the famous "Erlkönig" after Goethe. Schubert's successors included Robert Schumann, whose cycles Dichterliebe and Frauen-Liebe und Leben date from his "year of song" 1840; Johannes Brahms; Hugo Wolf, known for his intense, text-sensitive settings; and Gustav Mahler, who carried the Lied into the orchestral realm with works such as Das Lied von der Erde. Richard Strauss was among the last great composers of the genre in its classic form.

Forms

Lieder may be strophic (the same music for each verse), through-composed (new music throughout), or in modified strophic form. A group of songs unified by a poet, theme, or narrative is called a song cycle (Liederkreis). The genre is closely related to the French mélodie and to art songs in other languages, and the term "Lied" is sometimes used loosely for art song in general.

Performance tradition

Lieder are performed in song recitals by a singer and pianist. Celebrated interpreters of the 20th century include Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau, Elisabeth Schwarzkopf, and Fritz Wunderlich, and collaborative pianists such as Gerald Moore helped establish the accompanist as an equal artistic partner.
