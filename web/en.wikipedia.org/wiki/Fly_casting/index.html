Fly casting

Fly casting is the technique used in fly fishing to propel an artificial fly to the target using the weight of a specially designed fly line rather than the weight of the lure. Because an artificial fly is nearly weightless, the thick, heavy fly line itself is cast: the angler accelerates the flexible fly rod and stops it abruptly, causing the line to form a moving loop that unrolls toward the target, carrying the fine leader and fly with it.

Technique

The basic overhead cast consists of a back cast and a forward cast. Starting with the rod low and line on the water, the angler lifts and accelerates the rod backward, stopping near the vertical so the line straightens behind; then, after a pause timed to the amount of line out, drives the rod forward and stops it high, letting the loop unroll and the fly settle. Repeating false casts with more line drawn from the reel ("shooting" line on the final delivery) increases distance. Key faults include dropping the rod tip too far (opening the loop) and starting the forward cast before the back cast has straightened (which can crack the fly off like a whip).

Specialised casts

Variations include the roll cast (used when there is no room behind for a back cast), the double haul (pulling the line with the free hand to increase line speed and distance, developed for saltwater fishing), the Spey casts used with two-handed rods on rivers, and presentation casts such as the reach cast, parachute cast and curve casts used to avoid drag on flowing water. Competitive casting events judge accuracy and distance; in the related sport of casting (Casting Sport), casters using fly tackle achieve distances well over 40 metres.

Equipment

Casting performance depends on the match between rod action and line weight (standardised by AFTMA numbers), the line's taper profile (weight-forward, double taper, shooting heads), and a tapered leader that transfers the loop's energy to the fly.
