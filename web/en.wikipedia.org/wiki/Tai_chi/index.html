Tai chi

Tai chi (Chinese: 太極; pinyin: tàijí), short for tàijí quán (太極拳; "great polarity boxing"), is an internal Chinese martial art practiced for self-defense and health. Known for its slow, flowing movements and deep breathing, tai chi is practiced worldwide, often for its perceived benefits in balance, relaxation, and stress reduction. In 2020, it was included on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

History

Tai chi's origins are traced to Chen Village (Chenjiagou) in Henan province, where Chen Wangting (1580–1660) is widely credited with developing the art in the 17th century, combining earlier martial traditions with Taoist daoyin exercises and concepts of yin and yang. Yang Luchan later brought the art to Beijing and founded the Yang style. The five major traditional family styles are Chen, Yang, Wu (Hao), Wu, and Sun.

Philosophy

The art takes its name from the taiji concept — the supreme ultimate, the unity of opposites from which yin and yang arise. Its classics teach yielding rather than meeting force with force, using softness to overcome hardness: "four ounces deflect a thousand pounds."

Practice

Practice centres on solo forms (taolu) — choreographed sequences of slow continuous movements — pushing hands (tui shou), qigong breathing, and, in traditional schools, weapons forms (sword, sabre, staff, spear) and sparring. Modern studies suggest modest benefits for balance and fall prevention in older adults.
