Herbert Chapman

Herbert Chapman (19 January 1878 – 6 January 1934) was an English football player and manager. He is widely regarded as one of the most influential figures in the early history of association football, credited with modernising many aspects of the game during his managerial career with Huddersfield Town and Arsenal.

Playing career

Chapman was born in Kiveton Park, near Sheffield, the son of a coal miner. He studied mining engineering at Sheffield Technical College and played as an inside forward for a string of clubs including Ashton North End, Stalybridge Rovers, Rochdale and Grimsby Town. He played in the Football League for Burton Swifts, Lincoln City and Notts County, and in the Southern League for Northampton Town, Sheffield United and Tottenham Hotspur. His playing career was modest, and he never won a major honour as a player.

Managerial career

Northampton Town and Leeds City

Chapman became player-manager of Northampton Town in 1907, leading them to the Southern League title in 1908–09. He moved to Leeds City in 1912 and improved the club's fortunes in the Second Division, but the club was expelled from the Football League in 1919 over illegal payments to players during the First World War, and Chapman was briefly banned from football management before being cleared on appeal.

Huddersfield Town

Joining Huddersfield Town in 1921, Chapman built one of the dominant sides of the decade. Huddersfield won the FA Cup in 1922 and the First Division championship in 1923–24 and 1924–25, the start of a run of three consecutive titles completed under his successor after his departure.

Arsenal

In 1925 Chapman was lured to Arsenal by chairman Sir Henry Norris with a salary then unprecedented for a manager. He rebuilt the side around players such as Alex James and Cliff Bastin, won the FA Cup in 1930 — the club's first major trophy — and league championships in 1930–31 and 1932–33, laying the foundations for Arsenal's dominance of English football in the 1930s.

Innovations

Chapman was a restless innovator. After the 1925 change to the offside law, he developed the "WM" formation, withdrawing the centre-half into defence and pulling the inside forwards back into midfield, which became the standard system in English football for a generation. He advocated floodlit matches, numbered shirts, and the use of a white ball, promoted the idea of European club competition, and insisted on measures from physiotherapy and massage to pre-match team talks and journalists' access. At Arsenal he also oversaw the rebuilding of Highbury's stands and the renaming of the local Underground station to "Arsenal".

Death and legacy

Chapman died suddenly of pneumonia in January 1934, aged 55, after catching a cold while scouting players. A bust of him stood for decades in the marble halls of Highbury, and a statue of Chapman was unveiled outside the Emirates Stadium in 2011. He is remembered as the first modern football manager, who transformed the role from a trainer subordinate to directors into the controlling figure in a club's playing affairs.
