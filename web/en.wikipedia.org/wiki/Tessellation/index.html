Tessellation

A tessellation is the covering of a surface, often a plane, using one or more geometric shapes called tiles, with no overlaps and no gaps. In mathematics, tessellations can be generalised to higher dimensions and a variety of geometries. A periodic tiling has a repeating pattern; aperiodic tilings, such as Penrose tilings, do not repeat.

Regular tessellations

Only three regular polygons can tile the plane by themselves: the equilateral triangle, the square, and the regular hexagon. Eight further semiregular (Archimedean) tilings combine two or more kinds of regular polygon while keeping the same arrangement at every vertex.

History

Tessellations were used by the Sumerians around 4000 BC in clay wall decorations, and elaborate geometric tilings are a hallmark of Islamic art and architecture, as in the Alhambra palace in Granada. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher became famous in the 20th century for his intricate tessellations of interlocking animals and figures. In 1974 Roger Penrose discovered aperiodic sets of tiles that force non-periodic tilings of the plane.

In nature

Honeycombs, basalt columns such as the Giant's Causeway, mud cracks and the scales of fish and reptiles all exhibit natural tessellation-like patterns.
