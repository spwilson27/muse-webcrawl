Megali Idea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Megali Idea (Greek: Μεγάλη Ιδέα, "Great Idea") was the core concept of Greek nationalism that expressed the goal of establishing a Greek state that would encompass all historically ethnic Greek-inhabited areas, including the large Greek populations that were still under Ottoman rule after Greek independence in 1830, and ultimately with Constantinople (Istanbul) as its capital — a revival, in effect, of the Byzantine Empire.

Origins

The term was coined in 1844 by Ioannis Kolettis, who declared in the National Assembly that the Kingdom of Greece was "not the whole of Greece, but only a part, the smallest and poorest part", and that a Greek was not only one who lived within the kingdom but also those in Ioannina, Thessaly, Serres, Adrianople, Constantinople, and other regions historically associated with Greek history and the Greek race.

Expansion and conflicts

The Megali Idea drove Greek foreign policy through the 19th and early 20th centuries. Territories added to Greece included the Ionian Islands (1864), Thessaly (1881), and — following the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 — Epirus, Macedonia, Crete and the eastern Aegean islands. Under Prime Minister Eleftherios Venizelos, Greece obtained a mandate over Smyrna (İzmir) and its hinterland under the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) after the First World War.

Collapse

The project collapsed in the Greco-Turkish War of 1919–1922. The Greek army was decisively defeated by Turkish nationalist forces under Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk); Smyrna was burned in September 1922, and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) settled the conflict, followed by a compulsory population exchange between Greece and Turkey that uprooted roughly 1.5 million people. The catastrophe — remembered in Greece as the Mikrasiatiki Katastrophi (Asia Minor Disaster) — effectively ended the Megali Idea as a programme of territorial expansion.
