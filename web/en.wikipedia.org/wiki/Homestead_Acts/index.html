Homestead Acts

The Homestead Acts were several laws in the United States by which an applicant could acquire ownership of government land or the public domain, typically called a homestead. In all, more than 160 million acres (650,000 km²) of public land, or nearly 10 percent of the total area of the United States, were given away free to 1.6 million homesteaders; most homesteads were found west of the Mississippi River.

An extension of the homesteading principle in law, the Homestead Acts were an expression of the "Free Soil" policy of Northerners who wanted individual farmers to own and operate their own farms, as opposed to Southern slave owners who wanted to buy up large tracts of land and use slave labor, thereby shutting out free white farmers.

The Homestead Act of 1862

The first of the acts, the Homestead Act of 1862, was signed into law by President Abraham Lincoln on May 20, 1862. It opened up millions of acres of land in the West to settlers. Any adult citizen, or intended citizen, who had never borne arms against the United States could claim 160 acres of surveyed government land. Claimants were required to "prove up" their claim by living on the land, building a dwelling, and cultivating the land for five years, after which they received title for a nominal registration fee.

The act was notable for its inclusiveness: women and immigrants who had applied for citizenship were eligible to file claims, making it one of the era's most progressive land measures. Daniel Freeman, a Union Army scout, is often cited as the first person to file a claim under the act, doing so on January 1, 1863, near Beatrice, Nebraska.

Subsequent acts

Lawmakers supplemented the 1862 act with later legislation, including the Timber Culture Act of 1873, the Desert Land Act of 1877, the Enlarged Homestead Act of 1909 (which doubled the allotment to 320 acres in dry western states), and the Stock-Raising Homestead Act of 1916 (640 acres for ranching). Homesteading continued in Alaska until 1986, and the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 officially ended homesteading in the contiguous states.

Impact and criticism

Homesteading shaped the settlement of the Great Plains and the American West, but it also accelerated the dispossession of Native American peoples, as the "public land" distributed by the government was largely territory taken from Indigenous nations through treaties and warfare. Fraud was also common, with speculators and corporations using dummy entrymen to acquire land. Of roughly 4 million claims filed, about 1.6 million were successfully completed.
