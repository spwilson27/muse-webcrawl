Psychedelic therapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Modern psychedelic therapy pairs a supervised dosing session with preparation and integration psychotherapy.

Psychedelic therapy is the use of psychedelic substances—such as psilocybin, LSD, MDMA, or ketamine—as adjuncts to psychotherapy in the treatment of mental disorders. In contemporary practice it usually involves a small number of supervised dosing sessions embedded in a course of non-drug preparation and integration therapy, with attention to "set and setting": the patient's mindset and the physical and social environment.

History

Between the 1950s and mid-1960s, thousands of patients were treated with LSD and mescaline in research programmes such as Humphry Osmond's work on alcoholism in Saskatchewan and the Spring Grove studies in Maryland. Prohibition-era drug policy ended most research: the classic psychedelics were placed in the most restrictive schedules, and clinical work largely ceased by the early 1970s. Research resumed in the 1990s and accelerated after 2000 with studies at institutions including Johns Hopkins, Imperial College London, and NYU, and with advocacy and funding from organisations such as MAPS (the Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies).

Conditions studied

Randomised trials have investigated psilocybin for major depressive disorder and for depression and anxiety in life-threatening illness, MDMA-assisted therapy for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and psychedelics for addiction. Phase 3 trials of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD reported substantial benefit, although in 2024 the US Food and Drug Administration declined approval and requested further study. Australia became the first country to permit authorised psychiatrists to prescribe MDMA and psilocybin for specified conditions, in 2023.

Risks and debate

Adverse effects can include acute anxiety, transient increases in blood pressure, and—in vulnerable individuals—precipitation of psychosis; trials therefore screen out people with personal or family histories of psychotic disorders. Methodological challenges include the near-impossibility of true double-blinding, expectancy effects, and questions about the role of the subjective "mystical-type" experience that often correlates with outcomes.
