Brooks' theorem

In graph theory, Brooks' theorem, proved by Rowland Leonard Brooks in 1941, states a relationship between the maximum degree of a graph and its chromatic number. It sharpens the simple greedy colouring bound for almost all graphs.

Statement

For any connected undirected graph G with maximum degree Δ, the chromatic number χ(G) satisfies χ(G) ≤ Δ, unless G is a complete graph or an odd cycle, in which case χ(G) = Δ + 1.

The greedy colouring algorithm shows that any graph can be coloured with at most Δ + 1 colours. Brooks' theorem says the extra colour is needed only in the two exceptional cases: complete graphs, where every vertex is adjacent to every other, and cycles of odd length, which are 2-regular but need three colours.

Proof idea

A standard proof (simplified by László Lovász in 1975) orders the vertices so that each vertex has a neighbour later in the ordering, then colours greedily in reverse; careful choice of the ordering — often based on a spanning tree rooted at a vertex whose neighbours are not all adjacent — ensures that no vertex ever sees all Δ colours among its already-coloured neighbours.

Extensions

Brooks' theorem has been generalised in several directions, including to list colouring (the list colouring conjecture for line graphs relates to it), to the Borodin–Kostochka conjecture on colouring graphs with large maximum degree, and to algorithmic versions: a Δ-colouring of a Brooks-exception-free graph can be found in polynomial, and even linear, time.
