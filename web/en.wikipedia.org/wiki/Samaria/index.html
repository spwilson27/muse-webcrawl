Samaria (Hebrew: שֹׁמְרוֹן, Shomron) is the ancient and biblical name used for the central region of the southern Levant, roughly corresponding to the northern part of the modern West Bank. The name derives from the ancient city of Samaria, founded as the capital of the northern Kingdom of Israel around 880 BC. The region lies between Galilee to the north and Judea to the south, bounded by the Mediterranean coastal plain to the west and the Jordan Valley to the east, and is characterised by fertile hills and valleys around modern Nablus (ancient Shechem).

History

According to the Hebrew Bible, King Omri purchased the hill of Samaria from Shemer and built his capital there; his son Ahab raised temples and palaces adorned with the famous Samaria ivories. The city withstood sieges by Aram-Damascus but fell to the Neo-Assyrian Empire under Shalmaneser V and Sargon II in 722 BC, ending the northern kingdom; much of its population was deported and replaced by settlers from elsewhere in the empire — a policy that biblical tradition associates with the origins of the Samaritans, though Samaritan tradition claims descent from the tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh.

Under Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman rule the city was repeatedly rebuilt; Herod the Great refounded it as Sebaste in honour of Augustus. The region gave its name to the Samaritans, whose temple stood on Mount Gerizim, and it figures prominently in the New Testament, including the parable of the Good Samaritan and Jesus's encounter with the Samaritan woman at Jacob's Well.

Modern usage

The name survives in the designation "Judea and Samaria" used in Israel for the West Bank, and the ancient site of Samaria-Sebaste, near the Palestinian village of Sebastia, is an archaeological park with remains ranging from Israelite palace walls to a Roman forum and Byzantine church.
