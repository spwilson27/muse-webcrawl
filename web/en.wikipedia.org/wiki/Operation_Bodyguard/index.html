Operation Bodyguard

Date1943 – 1944
LocationEurope
Planned byLondon Controlling Section, Ops (B)
ObjectiveDeceive Germany about the time and place of the Allied invasion of north-west Europe

Operation Bodyguard was the code name for a World War II deception strategy employed by the Allied states before the 1944 invasion of north-west Europe. Bodyguard set out an overall stratagem to mislead the German high command as to the date and location of the invasion. The plan contained several operations, the most famous of which was Operation Fortitude, and succeeded in its objectives, keeping German forces in the Pas-de-Calais and elsewhere long after the Normandy landings of 6 June 1944.

The name was taken from a remark by Winston Churchill to Joseph Stalin at the Tehran Conference in 1943: "In wartime, truth is so precious that she should always be attended by a bodyguard of lies."

Background

Deception had already proved its worth in North Africa, notably before the Second Battle of El Alamein and in Operation Mincemeat, which misled Germany about the invasion of Sicily in 1943. With the cross-Channel invasion (Operation Overlord) planned for 1944, planners under the London Controlling Section developed Bodyguard to conceal the real target, Normandy, and to suggest landings elsewhere — in the Pas-de-Calais, Norway, the Balkans, southern France and elsewhere.

Component plans

Bodyguard comprised numerous sub-plans. Operation Fortitude North created a fictitious British Fourth Army in Scotland threatening Norway, while Fortitude South invented the First United States Army Group (FUSAG), supposedly commanded by General George S. Patton and poised to invade the Pas-de-Calais. Other elements included Operation Zeppelin (threats in the Balkans and Aegean), Operation Royal Flush (diplomatic pressure on neutral states) and Operation Gravedigger and Ironside. The deception was sustained by double agents of the Double Cross System, dummy aircraft and tanks, false radio traffic and controlled leaks.

Outcome

German intelligence largely accepted the deceptions. Even after the Normandy landings, Hitler kept the powerful Fifteenth Army in the Pas-de-Calais for weeks, believing the Normandy assault was a diversion. The delay gave the Allies time to build up their lodgement and is regarded as one of the most successful deception operations in military history.
