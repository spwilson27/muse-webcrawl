Economic calculation problem

The economic calculation problem is a criticism of using central planning as a substitute for market-based allocation of production factors. First proposed by Ludwig von Mises in 1920, it argues that without market prices for capital goods, planners cannot rationally calculate how to allocate resources efficiently.

Mises's argument

In his article "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" and book Socialism (1922), Mises argued rational calculation requires money prices formed by exchange of privately owned means of production. Friedrich Hayek extended the argument as a knowledge problem: prices aggregate dispersed, tacit knowledge no planner can collect.

Socialist calculation debate

Oskar Lange, Abba Lerner and others responded with market socialism models using trial-and-error prices set by a planning board. The debate continued through the 20th century and revived after the collapse of Soviet planning.

Criticism and responses

Critics argue computers, linear programming, and modern data could solve calculation, while Austrians reply the problem is not computation alone but the absence of entrepreneurial appraisal and genuine price signals.
