Aristotelian physics

[Image: Aristotelian physics — The four elements and the celestial spheres in a medieval diagram of the Aristotelian cosmos]

Aristotelian physics is the form of natural philosophy described in the works of the Greek philosopher Aristotle (384–322 BC). In his work Physics, Aristotle intended to establish general principles of change that all natural bodies — animate and inanimate, celestial and terrestrial — would follow. This systematised framework dominated scientific thought for nearly two thousand years, until it was displaced during the Scientific Revolution of the 16th and 17th centuries.

Core concepts

  • Four elements: earth, water, air and fire (with a fifth, aether, for the heavens), each with a natural place.
  • Natural motion: heavy bodies fall toward the centre of the universe; light bodies rise.
  • Four causes: material, formal, efficient and final causes explain any change; teleology is inherent in nature.
  • Unmoved mover: an eternal, immaterial first cause sustains the rotation of the outermost celestial sphere.
  • Two realms: the sublunary world of change and decay, and the perfect, unchanging superlunary heavens moving in circles.

Key claims and later criticism

Aristotle held that heavier bodies fall faster than lighter ones in proportion to their weight, that a vacuum is impossible, and that violent (forced) motion requires a continuous mover in contact with the moved body. Medieval commentators such as John Philoponus and John Buridan criticised these doctrines and developed the impetus theory; Galileo's experiments and thought experiments, and Newton's laws of motion, finally replaced the Aristotelian system with classical mechanics.

Legacy

Despite its empirical shortcomings, Aristotelian physics was a systematic, qualitative theory tightly integrated with Aristotle's logic, metaphysics and biology, and it structured natural philosophy throughout the ancient, Islamic and medieval European worlds.
