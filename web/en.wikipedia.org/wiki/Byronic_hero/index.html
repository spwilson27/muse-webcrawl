Byronic hero

The Byronic hero is a variant of the Romantic hero, a character type named after the English poet Lord Byron and exemplified by his works and his public persona. The Byronic hero is an antihero: brooding, flawed, and in conflict with society, yet possessing charisma, intelligence, and intense passion that make him compelling. The type became one of the most influential character archetypes in Western literature.

Characteristics

Typical traits of the Byronic hero include:

  • Arrogance, cynicism, and contempt for social norms and authority;
  • A dark, mysterious past, often involving a secret sin or crime;
  • Self-destructive tendencies, melancholy, and emotional turmoil;
  • High intelligence, perceptiveness, and sophistication;
  • Exile or self-exile — he is an outsider and a wanderer;
  • Passionate capacity for devotion to a beloved, alongside cruelty or coldness to others;
  • Physical attractiveness and magnetic charisma.

Origins

The type first appeared in Byron's semi-autobiographical epic poem Childe Harold's Pilgrimage (1812) and was developed in the protagonists of his verse tales — Conrad in The Corsair, Lara, and the defiant title character of Manfred (1817), who refuses all submission, human or supernatural. Byron's own scandalous reputation — Lady Caroline Lamb famously called him "mad, bad, and dangerous to know" — fused author and characters in the public mind. Precursors include Milton's Satan in Paradise Lost, the Gothic villain-heroes of the 1790s, and Goethe's Werther.

Influence

The Byronic hero shaped major figures of 19th-century literature: Heathcliff in Emily Brontë's Wuthering Heights, Mr Rochester in Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre, Pushkin's Onegin, Lermontov's Pechorin, Dumas's Count of Monte Cristo, and the vampire Lord Ruthven (modelled on Byron by his physician John Polidori), an ancestor of Dracula. The type persists in modern popular culture in brooding antiheroes of film, television, and romance and fantasy fiction.
