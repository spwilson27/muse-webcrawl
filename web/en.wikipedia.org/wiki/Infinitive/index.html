Infinitive

Infinitive forms in Latin, English, German and French

An infinitive is a linguistic term for the basic, uninflected form of a verb — the form typically used as the dictionary entry (lemma) — which in many languages is used as a non-finite verb form with particular syntactic functions. The word derives from the Latin infinitivus, "unlimited", because the infinitive is not limited by person, number or tense. In English, the infinitive is usually the base form of the verb preceded by the particle to ("to run"), though a bare infinitive without to appears after modal verbs and some other verbs ("I can run").

Forms across languages

In Latin the infinitive is a single word marked by endings such as -are, -ere, -ire (amare, "to love"); Romance languages retain single-word infinitives (French aimer, Spanish amar). German forms infinitives with -en or -n (laufen), and Scandinavian languages mark them with a particle cognate with English to (Norwegian å). Some languages, including modern Greek and most Balkan languages, have largely lost the infinitive and replace it with subjunctive-like finite clauses — a feature of the Balkan sprachbund.

Uses in English

The English infinitive can function as a noun phrase ("To err is human"), as a modifier ("a book to read"), in purpose clauses ("she left early to catch the train"), and as the complement of many verbs ("he wants to leave"). The so-called split infinitive, in which an adverb intervenes between to and the verb ("to boldly go"), was criticised by 19th-century prescriptivists but has always been used and is generally accepted today. English also has perfect ("to have gone"), progressive ("to be going"), passive ("to be seen") and negated infinitives, making the infinitive phrase a versatile non-finite construction.
