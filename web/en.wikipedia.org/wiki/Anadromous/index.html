Anadromous

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. This article is about fish migration; "anadromous" redirects here.

Anadromous fish are born in fresh water, spend most of their lives in the sea, and return to fresh water to spawn. The word comes from the Greek anadromos, "running upward." The best-known anadromous fish are the Pacific and Atlantic salmon, which hatch in streams, migrate to the ocean to grow, and navigate back — often to their exact natal stream — to reproduce and, in Pacific species, die.

Life cycle

Anadromy is the opposite of catadromy, exemplified by freshwater eels that spawn in the ocean. Fish that migrate between salt and fresh water must osmoregulate in both environments, reversing the function of their gills and kidneys as they transition. Other anadromous species include shad, alewives, sturgeon, striped bass, lampreys, and steelhead trout.

Ecological and human importance

Anadromous migrations transfer marine nutrients far inland, feeding bears, eagles, and forests, and support major commercial and recreational fisheries. Dams, overfishing, and habitat degradation have severely reduced many runs; fish ladders, dam removals, and hatcheries are used to restore them.
