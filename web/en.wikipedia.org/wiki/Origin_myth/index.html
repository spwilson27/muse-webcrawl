Origin myth

Creation accounts are among the most widespread origin myths.

An origin myth is a type of myth that describes the origins of a people, a custom, a ritual, an institution, a natural feature, or the world itself. Origin myths are a subtype of etiological myths — stories told to explain why something exists or how it came to be. Every known culture possesses origin myths, which are typically regarded not as literal history but as repositories of a society's deepest values, identity, and understanding of its place in the cosmos.

Types of origin myths

Cosmogonic myths

Creation myths (cosmogonies) describe the beginning of the universe and of humankind. They include creation ex nihilo, as in Genesis; creation from chaos, as in the Babylonian Enūma Eliš and Hesiod's Theogony; world-parent myths, in which the world is formed from the union or separation of primordial parents such as the Māori Rangi and Papa; and earth-diver myths, widespread in Native American and Siberian traditions, in which an animal dives into primordial waters to fetch mud from which the earth is formed.

Foundation and charter myths

Many origin myths legitimate a people's possession of a homeland or its political order. The Roman legend of Romulus and Remus explained the founding of Rome; the Japanese imperial house traced its descent from the sun goddess Amaterasu, as recorded in the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki; and the Israelites' Exodus narrative grounded their covenant and claim to the land of Canaan. Anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski called such narratives "charter myths" because they function as warrants for present-day institutions and social arrangements.

Etiological tales

Shorter origin stories explain particular customs, names, or natural phenomena: why the leopard has spots, how fire came to humanity (the Greek Prometheus myth), why a ritual is performed, or how death entered the world. Such stories occur in sacred texts, epics, and folktale collections worldwide.

Interpretation

Scholars have interpreted origin myths in many ways: as primitive science (attempts to explain nature), as allegory, as distorted memory of real events (euhemerism), and as symbolic expressions of psychological or social truths. Mircea Eliade emphasised that for traditional societies, reciting the origin myth re-actualises the sacred time of beginnings (in illo tempore) and renews the world.
