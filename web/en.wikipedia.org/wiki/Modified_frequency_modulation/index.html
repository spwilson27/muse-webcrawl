Modified frequency modulation (MFM) is a run-length limited (RLL) line code used to encode data on most floppy disks and some early hard disk drives. It was first introduced in disk drives in 1970 with the IBM 3740 and became the de facto standard for floppy disk formats through the 1970s and 1980s, doubling the storage capacity achievable with plain frequency modulation (FM) encoding.

Encoding rules

In MFM, data bits are encoded so that flux transitions occur only at bit-cell boundaries or centres, according to rules that guarantee a minimum and maximum spacing between transitions. A binary 1 always produces a transition in the middle of its bit cell; a 0 produces a transition at the cell boundary only when the preceding bit was also 0.

Because clock transitions are omitted when data transitions are present, the density of flux reversals is halved compared with FM, allowing roughly twice the data capacity on the same medium at the same maximum flux-reversal rate.

Applications

MFM encoding was used by the IBM PC and compatibles for both 360 KB and 1.2 MB 5.25-inch floppies and for 720 KB and 1.44 MB 3.5-inch floppies, always in what documentation described as 'MFM, double density' format.

Early hard disk drives such as the Seagate ST-506 also used MFM before RLL controllers offered a further 50 percent increase in capacity.
