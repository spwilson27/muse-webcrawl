Theobald Smith

Theobald Smith (July 31, 1859 – December 10, 1934) was a pioneering American microbiologist and pathologist, regarded as one of the founders of American bacteriology. Born in Albany, New York, he was educated at Cornell University and Albany Medical College.

Career and discoveries

Working at the federal Bureau of Animal Industry in Washington, D.C., Smith demonstrated in 1889–1893, with F. L. Kilborne, that Texas cattle fever is caused by a protozoan parasite (Babesia bigemina) transmitted by cattle ticks — the first demonstration that an arthropod could transmit an infectious disease, a discovery that opened the way to understanding malaria and yellow fever transmission. He also distinguished the bovine and human tubercle bacilli, described anaphylaxis-related phenomena in guinea pigs (the "Theobald Smith phenomenon"), and isolated the bacterium now named Salmonella (after his chief, Daniel Elmer Salmon).

Smith later worked at Harvard Medical School and directed comparative pathology at the Rockefeller Institute's animal pathology department in Princeton, New Jersey.

Legacy

Smith's combination of laboratory rigor and field epidemiology set the pattern for American microbiology; many contemporaries considered him America's greatest bacteriologist.
