Moral rights

Moral rights are rights of creators of copyrighted works that protect the personal and reputational connection between an author and their work, independently of the author's economic rights. The concept — droit moral — originates in French law and is recognised in Article 6bis of the Berne Convention. Core moral rights include the right of attribution (to be named as author, or to remain anonymous or pseudonymous) and the right of integrity (to object to distortion, mutilation or other derogatory treatment of the work prejudicial to the author's honour or reputation).

Scope of rights

Some systems recognise further rights: the right of disclosure (to decide when a work is complete and may be published), the right of withdrawal (to retract a work from circulation, compensating transferees), and protection against false attribution. Moral rights protect the work as an extension of the author's personality; they therefore typically cannot be sold or transferred, though they may pass to heirs and in many jurisdictions can be waived by consent.

International treatment

The Berne Convention obliges members to provide attribution and integrity rights lasting at least until the expiry of economic rights, but leaves the means of redress to national law. Civil-law countries such as France give moral rights their strongest form — in France they are perpetual, inalienable and imprescriptible, and have been invoked, for example, against colourisation of black-and-white films shown on French television. TRIPS expressly excludes moral rights from WTO obligations, reflecting common-law resistance to the doctrine.

Common-law jurisdictions

The United States recognises moral rights only narrowly: the Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990 (VARA) grants attribution and integrity rights to authors of a limited class of visual art — paintings, drawings, prints, sculpture and exhibition photographs — generally for the artist's life. Broader protection is pieced together from contract, defamation, unfair competition and trademark law, as in the Gilliam v. ABC litigation over edited Monty Python broadcasts. The United Kingdom introduced moral rights in the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, but requires some rights to be asserted and permits broad waiver, making them weaker in practice. Canada and Australia provide attribution and integrity rights of intermediate strength.

Duration and waiver

Duration varies from perpetual (France) to life of the author (VARA) to coextensive with copyright (UK, Canada, Australia). Because moral rights can complicate adaptation and commercialisation — a film studio altering a script, a building owner modifying an architect's building — waiver clauses are standard in creative-industry contracts in jurisdictions that allow them, a practice critics say hollows out the rights for the creators least able to resist.
