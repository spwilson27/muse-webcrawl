Modern liberalism in the United States

Franklin D. Roosevelt, whose New Deal defined modern American liberalism

Modern liberalism in the United States is a political philosophy that combines ideas of civil liberty and social equality with support for social justice, a mixed economy and an active federal government. It differs from classical liberalism in holding that government intervention is often necessary to make formal freedoms effective in practice — for example through welfare provision, economic regulation and protection of civil rights.

History

Modern American liberalism took shape in the Progressive Era and was consolidated by Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal in the 1930s, which expanded federal responsibility for employment, social security and financial regulation in response to the Great Depression. Harry Truman's Fair Deal, John F. Kennedy's New Frontier and Lyndon B. Johnson's Great Society extended the programme, adding Medicare and Medicaid and backing the civil rights legislation of the 1960s.

From the 1970s liberalism faced a conservative ascendancy, and the Democratic Party under Bill Clinton moved toward the centre with the "New Democrat" programme. In the 21st century, debates within liberalism cover healthcare reform, climate policy, income inequality and the role of identity and cultural issues, while its electoral base is concentrated in cities and among college-educated voters.

Relationship to the Democratic Party

Since the New Deal coalition, liberalism has been the dominant ideology of the Democratic Party, although the party also contains centrist and progressive currents that distinguish themselves from the liberal label.
