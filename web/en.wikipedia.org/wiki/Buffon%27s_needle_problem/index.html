Buffon's needle problem

Buffon's needle problem
Field: Probability
Also known as: Buffon's needle
Named after: Comte de Buffon

The Buffon's needle problem asks for the probability that a needle of length l, dropped onto a floor of parallel strips of width d (with l ≤ d), will land across a line separating two strips. It is one of the oldest and most famous problems in geometric probability.

Formulation

Let x be the distance from the centre of the needle to the nearest line, uniformly distributed on [0, d/2], and let θ be the acute angle between the needle and the lines, uniformly distributed on [0, π/2]. The needle crosses a line exactly when x ≤ (l/2) sin θ.

Calculation

Integrating the favourable region over the joint distribution gives the probability P = 2l / (πd). For a "short" needle this result holds directly; for a "long" needle (l > d) the formula is more complicated, involving inverse trigonometric terms, and the probability of at least one crossing increases, though the expected number of crossings remains 2l / (πd) in general.

Estimating π

Rearranging the short-needle formula lets experimenters estimate π by dropping needles repeatedly — an early example of a Monte Carlo method, predating the formal concept by well over a century.

Related concepts

The problem is related to Buffon's needle, Buffon's noodle, Bertrand's paradox, and the theory of integral geometry.
