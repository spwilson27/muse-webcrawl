Pump action

Pump action is a type of manually operated firearm action in which the shooter cycles the mechanism by sliding a movable fore-end (the "pump") backward and forward. The motion extracts and ejects the spent cartridge, cocks the hammer, and chambers a fresh round from a magazine, usually a tubular magazine beneath the barrel. Pump action is most strongly associated with shotguns but has also been used in rifles.

History

Early pump-action designs appeared in the late nineteenth century; the Winchester Model 1893 and its successor, the Model 1897 designed by John Browning, established the form, and the Winchester Model 1912 ("Model 12") refined it. Iconic later designs include the Remington Model 870 (introduced 1950) and the Mossberg 500 series, which became standard police, military, and sporting shotguns worldwide. Pump-action rifles such as the Winchester Model 1890 and Colt Lightning also enjoyed popularity.

Characteristics

Pump actions are mechanically simple, robust, and tolerant of varied ammunition, including low-power and specialty loads that may not cycle a semi-automatic action — one reason they remain common for law-enforcement use with less-lethal munitions. Their disadvantages relative to semi-automatics include slower follow-up shots and the possibility of "short-stroking," an incomplete cycle that causes a malfunction.
