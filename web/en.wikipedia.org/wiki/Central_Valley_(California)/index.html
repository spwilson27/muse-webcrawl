The Central Valley is a broad, flat valley that dominates inland California in the United States. It is about 450 miles (720 km) long, running roughly northwest to southeast between the Sierra Nevada to the east and the California Coast Ranges to the west. Covering some 18,000 square miles (47,000 km²), it is one of the most productive agricultural regions in the world.

Geography

The valley is divided into the Sacramento Valley in the north, drained by the Sacramento River, and the San Joaquin Valley in the south, drained by the San Joaquin River. The two river systems meet in the Sacramento–San Joaquin River Delta before flowing to San Francisco Bay. The southernmost San Joaquin Valley is an endorheic basin, historically draining into Tulare Lake.

Agriculture

Although it occupies less than 1% of United States farmland, the Central Valley produces about 8% of the nation's agricultural output by value, including a large share of its tomatoes, almonds, grapes, cotton, asparagus and lettuce. This productivity depends heavily on irrigation supplied by the federal Central Valley Project and the State Water Project, which move water from the wetter north to the arid south. Intensive groundwater pumping has caused significant land subsidence in parts of the valley, and the region is a focus of California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act.

Climate and environment

The valley has a Mediterranean climate in the north grading to semi-arid in the south. Winter tule fog is a notable hazard. Once covered by wetlands, oak savanna and grasslands supporting vast bird migrations, the valley's ecosystems have been extensively modified by agriculture and flood control.
