Rationalization (sociology)

In sociology, rationalization is the replacement of traditions, values and social actions motivated by emotion with actions motivated by reason and practicality. The concept was central to the work of Max Weber, who saw rationalization as the defining process of modernity and the rise of capitalism in the West.

Weber's account

Weber argued that modern societies increasingly organise life according to formal rationality: calculability, efficiency, predictability and control, embodied in bureaucracy, modern law, capitalist accounting and industrial production. He feared the result would be an "iron cage" (stahlhartes Gehäuse) of rational order in which meaning and freedom wither — a process of "disenchantment" (Entzauberung) of the world.

Ritzer's McDonaldization

George Ritzer extended the idea with his concept of "McDonaldization": the spread of fast-food principles — efficiency, calculability, predictability and control — throughout society. Critics note that rationalization can yield irrational outcomes, a point Ritzer calls the "irrationality of rationality".
