Prehensile tail

A prehensile tail is the tail of an animal that has adapted to grasp or hold objects. Fully prehensile tails can be used to hold and manipulate objects, and in particular to help arboreal creatures find and eat food in the trees. If the tail cannot be used for holding, it is considered only partially prehensile; such tails are often made of a fleshy pad used to grip tree branches. Many animals with prehensile tails are found in South and Central America.

Evolution

Prehensile tails evolved independently in several groups: New World monkeys (atelids such as spider monkeys and howler monkeys, and capuchins), kinkajous and binturongs among mammals, opossums, anteaters, harvest mice, and among reptiles such as chameleons, some geckos, and certain skinks; seahorses use their tails to grip coral and seaweed.

Anatomy

Fully prehensile tails, as in spider monkeys, often have a bare tactile pad on the underside near the tip, covered with friction skin similar to a fingerprint, giving a secure grip. The muscular and neural control is refined enough that some monkeys can pick up small objects with the tail alone. The tail can support the full body weight of the animal while it hangs.

Partially prehensile tails

Many animals, including porcupines and some rodents, have partially prehensile tails that assist in climbing but cannot manipulate objects. Seahorses and pipefish grip stationary objects to avoid being swept away by currents.
