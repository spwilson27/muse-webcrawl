Tabloid journalism

Tabloid journalism is a style of largely sensational journalism, as opposed to broadsheet journalism and other respectable forms, that takes its name from the compact tabloid newspaper format. The size became associated with sensationalist stories, and tabloid newspapers, also called rag newspapers, usually feature extensive coverage of celebrity news, gossip, crime and sport, with large photographs and bold headlines, though many also cover politics.

Characteristics

Tabloid journalism emphasises the emotive, the personal and the dramatic: crime stories, scandal, celebrity misbehaviour, and human-interest pieces take precedence over policy reporting. Headlines are often compressed, punning or shocking, and stories are typically shorter and more colloquially written than in broadsheets. In Britain the "red tops" — named for their red mastheads — such as The Sun and the Daily Mirror exemplify the style; in the United States, supermarket tabloids such as the National Enquirer are a related genre.

History

The tabloid format became popular in Britain at the beginning of the twentieth century with the Daily Mirror (founded 1903), and in the United States with the New York Daily News (founded 1919), whose picture-led coverage of sensation and sport made it the country's largest-selling newspaper. The style's heyday of aggressive practices — including door-stepping, long-lens photography and paying sources — led to recurring controversies over privacy and accuracy, most famously in Britain's phone-hacking scandal and the subsequent Leveson Inquiry of 2011–12.

Criticism

Critics charge tabloid journalism with trivialising public affairs, invading privacy, and sometimes fabricating or distorting stories, while defenders argue it brings news to readers ignored by elite papers and has exposed genuine wrongdoing through campaigning journalism.
