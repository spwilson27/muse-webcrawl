Hematite (also spelled haematite) is a common iron oxide compound with the formula Fe2O3 and is widely found in rocks and soils. Hematite crystals belong to the rhombohedral lattice system, which is designated the alpha polymorph of Fe2O3. It has the same crystal structure as corundum (Al2O3) and ilmenite. Hematite forms as a primary mineral and as an alteration product in igneous, metamorphic, and sedimentary rocks.

Hematite is one of the most abundant minerals on Earth's surface and in the shallow crust. It is mined as the main ore of iron. The name hematite is derived from the Greek word for blood, αἷμα (haima), due to the red coloration found in some varieties of hematite. Hematite's color can be black to steel or silver-gray, brown to reddish-brown, or red. Large deposits of hematite are found in banded iron formations.

Properties

Hematite is very heavy and relatively hard oxide mineral. Its streak, the color of the powder produced when it is dragged across an unglazed porcelain plate, is characteristically red to reddish-brown, which distinguishes it from other iron oxides such as magnetite (which has a black streak). Hematite is weakly magnetic; it is antiferromagnetic below its Morin transition at about 250 K and weakly ferromagnetic above it, a property connected to its use in ancient compasses and to the magnetism of Martian dust.

Varieties of hematite include kidney ore (kidney-shaped masses), martite (pseudomorphs after magnetite), iron rose (a rosette-like crystalline aggregate), and specularite (specular hematite, with a shiny micaceous habit). The mineral is opaque, with a metallic to submetallic luster in crystalline forms and a dull earthy luster in fine-grained varieties such as red ochre.

Occurrence and mining

Hematite deposits are found worldwide. Major producing regions include Brazil, Australia (particularly the Hamersley Range in Western Australia), China, India, Russia, Ukraine, and Venezuela. Gray hematite is typically found in places that have still, standing water or mineral hot springs, such as those in Yellowstone National Park in North America. The mineral can precipitate out of water and collect in layers at the bottom of a lake, spring, or other standing water.

Hematite also occurs without water, usually as the result of volcanic activity. Clay-sized hematite crystals can also occur as a secondary mineral formed by weathering processes in soil, and along with other iron oxides or oxyhydroxides such as goethite, is responsible for the red color of many tropical, ancient, or otherwise highly weathered soils.

The grey-black color of much of the mineral explains the ancient name "bloodstone" associations; in powder form, red ochre hematite has been used as a pigment since prehistoric times, notably in cave paintings, and in the modern era as rouge for polishing glass and jewellery.

Hematite on Mars

Spectral evidence for hematite was found at the surface of Mars by the Thermal Emission Spectrometer aboard NASA's Mars Global Surveyor and by the 2001 Mars Odyssey orbiter. The mineral was found in abundance at two sites on the planet, Terra Meridiani and Aram Chaos, leading to the selection of Meridiani Planum as the landing site of the Opportunity rover, which found hematite-rich concretions nicknamed "blueberries" that suggested the past presence of liquid water.

Uses

  • The principal ore of iron, used in the production of steel
  • A red pigment (red ochre) used in paints and cosmetics
  • Polishing powder (jeweller's rouge)
  • Radiation shielding and ballast, owing to its high density
  • Jewellery and ornamental carving in its polished, metallic form
