Smoke detector

A smoke detector is a device that senses smoke, typically as an indicator of fire. Commercial smoke detectors issue a signal to a fire alarm control panel as part of a fire alarm system. Household smoke detectors, also known as smoke alarms, generally issue an audible or visual alarm from the detector itself or several detectors if there are multiple devices interlinked.

Types

Ionization

Ionization detectors contain a tiny amount of the radioactive isotope americium-241, which ionizes air inside a sensing chamber. Smoke particles entering the chamber attach to ions and reduce the electrical current, triggering the alarm. They respond quickly to fast-flaming fires but are prone to nuisance alarms from cooking.

Photoelectric

Photoelectric detectors aim a beam of light into a sensing chamber. When smoke scatters the light onto a photocell, the alarm sounds. They respond more readily to slow, smoldering fires and are less prone to cooking-related false alarms. Many standards recommend photoelectric or dual-sensor alarms in homes.

History

The first automatic electric fire alarm was patented in 1890 by Francis Robbins Upton. The ionization smoke detector was invented in Switzerland in 1939 by Ernst Meili, and the first affordable home smoke detector was developed by Duane D. Pearsall in 1965. Widespread adoption followed fire-safety legislation in the 1970s, dramatically reducing residential fire deaths.

Regulation

Building codes in many countries require smoke alarms in dwellings, commonly on each level and in or near bedrooms. Detectors may be battery-powered or mains-powered with battery backup, and modern units often interconnect wirelessly so that all sound when one detects smoke.
