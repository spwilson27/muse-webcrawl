Scoring rule

In decision theory, a scoring rule is a function that measures the accuracy of a probabilistic forecast by assigning a numerical score based on the forecast distribution and the outcome that actually occurs. Scoring rules are used to evaluate and compare forecasters, and — when proper — to incentivise honest reporting of beliefs.

Proper scoring rules

A scoring rule is proper if a forecaster's expected score is maximised (or, in loss conventions, minimised) by reporting their true belief distribution; it is strictly proper if that optimum is unique. Proper scoring rules therefore make truthful forecasting the optimal strategy, a property central to prediction markets, weather forecast verification, and the elicitation of expert judgement.

Examples

  • Logarithmic score: the log probability assigned to the realised outcome. Strictly proper and local — it depends only on the probability of what happened — but unbounded, heavily penalising confident errors.
  • Brier score: the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and outcomes, introduced by Glenn Brier in 1950 for weather forecasts. Strictly proper and bounded.
  • Continuous ranked probability score (CRPS): generalises absolute error to full predictive distributions; widely used for ensemble weather and hydrological forecasts.
  • Spherical and quadratic scores: other strictly proper rules with different sensitivity profiles.

Applications

Scoring rules underpin forecast tournaments (such as the Good Judgment Project), meteorological verification, evaluation of machine-learning probability estimates, and scoring in prediction-market mechanisms such as Hanson's logarithmic market scoring rule (LMSR), which uses a proper scoring rule as an automated market maker.
