Bouba/kiki effect

The bouba/kiki effect is a non-arbitrary mapping between speech sounds and the visual shape of objects. When shown a rounded shape and a spiky shape and asked which is "bouba" and which is "kiki", people overwhelmingly assign "bouba" to the rounded shape and "kiki" to the spiky one — across cultures and languages.

Bouba/kiki effect
Field: Psychology of perception
Related to: Sound symbolism, synesthesia
Key researchers: Köhler; Ramachandran & Hubbard

History

The effect descends from Wolfgang Köhler's 1929 experiments with the words "baluba" and "takete". In 2001, V. S. Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard revived the paradigm with "bouba" and "kiki", reporting agreement rates above 95% among English speakers and Tamil speakers in India.

Interpretation

The effect is taken as evidence that the brain's mapping between sound and meaning is not entirely arbitrary: the articulation and acoustics of "bouba" involve rounded lips and low, smooth frequencies, echoing roundness, while the sharp consonants of "kiki" echo sharpness. It has been linked to sound symbolism in language evolution and to the cross-sensory correspondences seen in synesthesia.

Findings

The effect is present in young children and pre-literate infants, suggesting it precedes reading. It is somewhat reduced in some autistic populations and in congenitally blind subjects tested with touch, and it varies modestly with language and culture but remains robust worldwide.
