The Canadian Shield (French: Bouclier canadien), also called the Laurentian Plateau, is a geologic shield — a vast area of exposed Precambrian igneous and high-grade metamorphic rocks — that forms the ancient geological core of the North American continent. It extends from the Great Lakes to the Arctic Ocean, covering eastern, central, and northwestern Canada and the northern parts of several US states.

Geology

The Shield consists of some of the oldest rocks on Earth, including the Acasta Gneiss of the Northwest Territories (about 4.0 billion years old) and rocks associated with the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt in Quebec. Repeated continental collisions assembled its provinces between about 3.6 and 1.0 billion years ago. Pleistocene glaciers scraped away most soil, leaving thin soils, deranged drainage, and hundreds of thousands of lakes amid rolling rock surfaces mostly below 600 m elevation.

Ecology and resources

The Shield is covered largely by boreal forest in the south, grading to tundra in the north. Poor soils make it unsuitable for farming, but it is rich in minerals — nickel (Sudbury Basin, formed by an ancient meteorite impact), gold, copper, uranium, iron, and diamonds — and in hydroelectric potential, timber, and fur-bearing animals. Major cities on or near the Shield include Sudbury, Thunder Bay, and Yellowknife.
