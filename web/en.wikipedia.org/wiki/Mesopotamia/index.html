Mesopotamia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mesopotamia is a historical region of West Asia situated within the Tigris–Euphrates river system, in the northern part of the Fertile Crescent. In modern days it corresponds roughly to most of Iraq, Kuwait, and parts of Syria, Turkey and Iran. The name comes from Greek mesos potamos, "between the rivers". Mesopotamia is widely regarded as the cradle of civilisation: here, from about 3500 BC, the Sumerians built the world's first cities, invented writing (cuneiform), codified law, and developed the wheel, mathematics in base 60 (still used for time and angles), and organised irrigation agriculture.

History

Sumerian city-states such as Uruk, Ur and Lagash were succeeded by the Akkadian Empire of Sargon (c. 2334 BC), often called the first empire, then by Babylon — famous for Hammurabi's law code (c. 1754 BC) and, a millennium later, Nebuchadnezzar II — and by Assyria, whose empire dominated the Near East before falling in 612 BC. The region was successively ruled by Persians (from Cyrus, 539 BC), Alexander the Great, Parthians, Romans and Sasanians, and from the 7th century CE became the heartland of the Abbasid Caliphate, whose capital Baghdad (founded 762) was for centuries among the world's largest and most learned cities. Sites such as Babylon and Ur lie near the modern Euphrates and Tigris, the twin rivers that made irrigation farming possible in a dry land.

Legacy

From Mesopotamia came the earliest writing and literature (the Epic of Gilgamesh), the seven-day week and hour, early astronomy and mathematics transmitted via Greece and the Islamic world, and legal and urban models copied across the ancient Near East. Archaeology since the 19th century has recovered millions of clay tablets, allowing an unusually detailed reconstruction of daily life 4,000 years ago.
