Anthropometry

Anthropometry is the systematic measurement of the physical dimensions and proportions of the human body — including height, weight, limb lengths, breadths, circumferences, and skinfold thicknesses. It is a core method of physical anthropology and is widely applied in medicine, nutrition, ergonomics, and forensic science.

Standardised instruments such as the stadiometer and anthropometer are used for body measurements.

History

Interest in ideal bodily proportions goes back to ancient art and to Leonardo da Vinci's Vitruvian Man. In the 19th century Adolphe Quetelet applied statistics to human measurements and devised the body-mass index (the Quetelet index). Alphonse Bertillon developed a system of bodily measurements ("Bertillonage") for criminal identification, used by police forces before fingerprinting displaced it. In the 20th century, large surveys — for example of military recruits — produced anthropometric databases for equipment and clothing design, and historical anthropometry began using heights as an indicator of past living standards.

Applications

In medicine and public health, anthropometry assesses growth in children (height-for-age, weight-for-height), nutritional status, and obesity risk via body-mass index and waist circumference. Ergonomics uses population percentile data (e.g. 5th–95th percentile) to design workplaces, vehicles, clothing, and protective equipment. Forensic anthropologists use skeletal and body measurements to help identify remains. Modern methods extend the classical toolkit with 3-D body scanning and photogrammetry.

Variation

Body dimensions vary with genetics, sex, age, nutrition, disease, and socioeconomic conditions; secular trends in height over the past two centuries document improvements (and setbacks) in population health.
