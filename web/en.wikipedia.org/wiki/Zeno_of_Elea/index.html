Zeno of Elea

Zeno of Elea (c. 490 – c. 430 BC) was a pre-Socratic Greek philosopher of Magna Graecia and a member of the Eleatic school founded by Parmenides. He is best known for his paradoxes, arguments designed to show that motion and plurality lead to contradictions and thereby to defend Parmenides' doctrine that reality is one, unchanging and indivisible. Aristotle called him the inventor of the dialectic.

The paradoxes

None of Zeno's writings survive intact; the paradoxes are known mainly through Aristotle's Physics and later commentators. The most famous are:

  • Achilles and the tortoise: Achilles, racing a tortoise with a head start, must first reach the tortoise's starting point, by which time the tortoise has moved on — and so on infinitely, so he can never overtake it.
  • The dichotomy: to cross a distance, a traveller must first cross half of it, then half the remainder, and so on, requiring infinitely many tasks in finite time.
  • The arrow: at any instant, a flying arrow occupies a space equal to itself and is therefore at rest; since time is composed of instants, the arrow never moves.
  • The stadium: rows of bodies moving past each other in opposite directions generate contradictions about relative motion and the halving of time units.

Interpretation and legacy

Philosophers and mathematicians have debated the paradoxes for over two millennia. The development of the calculus and the modern theory of infinite series — in which an infinite sum of decreasing terms can have a finite total — provided a widely accepted resolution to the paradoxes of traversal, though philosophers continue to dispute whether they raise deeper questions about space, time and infinity. Zeno's method of reductio ad absurdum became a template for philosophical argument.
