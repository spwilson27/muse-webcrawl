Orthodox Judaism is the collective term for the traditionalist branches of contemporary Judaism. Theologically, it is chiefly defined by regarding the Torah, both Written and Oral, as revealed by God to Moses on Mount Sinai and faithfully transmitted ever since. Orthodox Judaism therefore, advocates a strict adherence to Jewish law, or halakha, which is to be interpreted and determined only according to traditional methods and adherence to the continuum of received precedent through generations.

It regards the entire halakhic system as ultimately grounded in revelation, and thus, in theory, immutable by a person's own or other's reasoning in contradiction to its instructions. However, Jewish law is also regarded as subject to contemporary interpretation and adaptation within its framework under current circumstances, as laid out by its decisors and decisor literature. Orthodox Judaism is not a centralized denomination, comprising many loosely connected sub-communities and movements.

History

Orthodoxy crystallised as a self-conscious movement in the 19th century in reaction to Reform Judaism and the challenges of emancipation and the Enlightenment (Haskalah) in Europe. Leaders such as Moses Sofer (the Hatam Sofer) in Hungary resisted change with the slogan "the new is forbidden by the Torah", while Samson Raphael Hirsch developed Neo-Orthodoxy in Frankfurt, combining strict observance with engagement in secular culture (Torah im Derech Eretz). In Eastern Europe, Hasidism, founded by the Baal Shem Tov in the 18th century, and its Lithuanian (Litvish) opponents, the Mitnagdim, both developed into pillars of Haredi Judaism.

Branches

Modern Orthodoxy seeks to synthesise traditional observance with participation in modern secular life; its institutions include Yeshiva University in New York. Haredi Judaism favours a more separatist stance, centred on full-time Torah study in yeshivas and kollels; it includes the many Hasidic dynasties (such as Satmar, Belz, Ger and Chabad-Lubavitch) and the non-Hasidic Litvish world centred in Bnei Brak and Jerusalem. Religious Zionism combines Orthodoxy with support for the State of Israel, maintaining its own yeshivas (hesder) and political parties. Orthodox Jews number roughly a fifth of world Jewry but form the fastest-growing sector because of high birth rates.

Beliefs and practices

Orthodox life is structured by the 613 commandments (mitzvot) as codified in the Shulchan Aruch: daily prayer three times a day, strict Sabbath observance, dietary laws (kashrut), family purity laws, and study of Torah as a supreme value. Maimonides' Thirteen Principles of Faith are widely (though not universally) treated as a summary of essential belief, including the coming of the Messiah and the resurrection of the dead.
