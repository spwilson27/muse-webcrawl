Politeness

Politeness is the practical application of good manners or etiquette so as not to offend others. It is a culturally defined phenomenon, and what is considered polite in one culture can sometimes be quite rude or simply strange in another. Linguists study politeness as a key part of pragmatics: how speakers manage social relationships and "face" — the public self-image a person wishes to maintain — through language.

Bowing is a conventional politeness gesture in many East Asian cultures.

Politeness theory

Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson's politeness theory (1978/1987), building on Erving Goffman's notion of face, distinguishes positive face (the desire to be approved of) from negative face (the desire not to be imposed upon). Speakers choose strategies — bald on-record, positive politeness, negative politeness, or off-record hints — depending on the social distance, power difference, and weight of the imposition involved in a "face-threatening act" such as a request or criticism.

Cultural variation

Politeness conventions range from honorific verb systems in Japanese and Korean, to elaborate greeting sequences in many African and Middle Eastern cultures, to expectations about directness that differ markedly between, for example, German and British conversational norms. Later scholars have criticised Brown and Levinson's model as too individualistic and Western, proposing instead approaches centred on social norms and moral order.
