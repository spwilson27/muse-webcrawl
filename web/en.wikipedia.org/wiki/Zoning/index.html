Zoning

Zoning is a method of land-use planning in which a municipality or other local government divides land into areas called zones, each of which has a set of regulations for new development that differs from other zones. Zones may be defined for a single use (e.g. residential, industrial), they may combine several compatible activities, or the regulations may focus on the density, height and bulk of buildings rather than their use.

History

Modern zoning emerged in the early twentieth century. New York City adopted the first comprehensive zoning resolution in 1916, prompted by towers such as the Equitable Building that cast shadows over whole streets. The US Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of zoning as an exercise of police power in Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (1926), giving its name to Euclidean zoning, the traditional single-use model. Similar systems developed elsewhere, though many European and Japanese systems rely more on national land-use categories and nuisance law.

Types and techniques

Beyond use-based zones, planners employ form-based codes regulating building shape and frontage, incentive zoning granting extra floor area in exchange for public amenities, overlay zones adding special rules for historic districts or floodplains, and transferable development rights. Individual projects may seek variances or conditional-use permits where strict application of the code would cause hardship.

Criticism

Critics argue that strict single-use zoning drives urban sprawl, car dependence and racial and economic segregation, and that minimum lot sizes, parking mandates and height limits restrict housing supply and raise prices. Reform movements such as New Urbanism advocate mixed-use, walkable development, and several jurisdictions have recently liberalised zoning to allow "missing middle" housing such as duplexes and small apartment buildings in formerly single-family areas.
