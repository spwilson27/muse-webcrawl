Mexican movement of 1968

The Mexican movement of 1968 was a social movement composed principally of students, but also involving professors, intellectuals, housewives, workers, and professionals in Mexico City and other cities, which demanded democratic change, civil liberties, and an end to political repression under the authoritarian government of President Gustavo Díaz Ordaz and the ruling Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI). The movement culminated in the Tlatelolco massacre of 2 October 1968, when government forces opened fire on a peaceful rally in the Plaza de las Tres Culturas, killing an unknown number of people — estimates range from dozens to several hundred — only ten days before the opening of the Mexico City Olympic Games.

Background

The movement arose amid the global wave of protests of 1968 and longstanding Mexican grievances: the PRI's one-party dominance, suppression of independent labor unions (as with the railroad workers' movement of 1958–59 and the doctors' movement of 1964–65), and the repression of rural guerrillas. Mexico's rapid economic growth, the so-called Mexican Miracle, had expanded universities and a middle class that increasingly demanded political opening.

The movement

The conflict began in late July 1968 with fights between rival student groups that were violently suppressed by the granaderos riot police. Police and army repression — including the bazooka attack on the door of the Escuela Nacional Preparatoria and the military occupation of the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM) in September — transformed a student dispute into a mass movement. The National Strike Council (CNH), formed by delegates from striking schools, issued a six-point petition demanding the release of political prisoners, abolition of the granaderos, repeal of repressive articles of the penal code, dismissal of police chiefs, indemnification of victims, and public dialogue with the government.

Hundreds of thousands marched in demonstrations through the summer, including the silent march of 13 September. Rector Javier Barros Sierra's defense of university autonomy made him a moral figure of the movement.

Tlatelolco massacre

On 2 October 1968, some 10,000 people gathered for a rally at Tlatelolco. As the meeting ended, army troops and the paramilitary Olimpia Battalion — whose members identified themselves with a white glove or handkerchief on the left hand — opened fire on the crowd. The official death toll at the time was a few dozen; later investigations and declassified documents suggest several hundred were killed. Hundreds more were arrested and imprisoned in Lecumberri Prison.

Legacy

The movement and massacre are widely regarded as a turning point in modern Mexican history, eroding the legitimacy of PRI rule and seeding later democratization. Writers such as Elena Poniatowska, in La noche de Tlatelolco (1971), preserved the testimonies of participants. In 1993 a memorial stele was erected in the plaza, and in 2018, on the 50th anniversary, the Mexican government formally recognized the massacre as a state crime. The slogan "¡2 de octubre no se olvida!" ("October 2nd is not forgotten!") remains a fixture of Mexican protest culture.
