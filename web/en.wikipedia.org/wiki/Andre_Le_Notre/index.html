André Le Nôtre (12 March 1613 – 15 September 1700) was a French landscape architect and the principal gardener of King Louis XIV of France. He is the supreme exponent of the French formal garden, the jardin à la française: vast, geometrically ordered landscapes of parterres, clipped hedges, long canals, fountains and radiating avenues that expressed the order and grandeur of the monarchy.

Career

Born into a family of royal gardeners — his father Jean was in charge of the Tuileries — Le Nôtre trained in painting and architecture before entering the royal gardening service. His breakthrough was the garden of Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661) for finance minister Nicolas Fouquet, created with architect Louis Le Vau and painter Charles Le Brun. After Fouquet's arrest, Louis XIV took the entire team to Versailles, where from the 1660s Le Nôtre spent four decades building the most famous gardens in Europe: the great east–west axis, the Grand Canal, the parterre d'eau, bosquets, fountains and the orangery.

Other works

Le Nôtre remodelled the Tuileries and extended its axis westward (the future Champs-Élysées), designed gardens at Saint-Germain, Saint-Cloud, Chantilly, Fontainebleau and Sceaux, advised on Greenwich Park in London, and influenced garden design across Europe. Despite his fame and wealth he was remembered for his modesty and genial wit in the king's company.

Legacy

His style defined formal landscape architecture until the rise of the English landscape garden in reaction against it, and his Versailles axis remains the archetype of monumental landscape planning.
