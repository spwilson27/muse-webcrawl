Small House Policy

Village houses in the New Territories built under the policy

The Small House Policy (Chinese: 小型屋宇政策), also known as the ding house policy (丁屋政策), is an administrative policy of the Hong Kong government introduced in 1972. It allows male indigenous inhabitants of the New Territories — male descendants in the male line of a person resident in a recognised village in 1898 — once in their lifetime to apply to build a small house for their own residence on village land, without paying the full land premium.

Background

The policy was introduced by the colonial government, under Governor Murray MacLehose, partly to improve rural housing and sanitation and partly in recognition of customary land rights acknowledged after Britain leased the New Territories in 1898. A "ding" (丁) house is limited to three storeys of up to 700 square feet each, a maximum of 2,100 square feet, and 8.23 metres in height, usually within the village environs zone of a recognised village (of which there are more than 600).

Controversy

The policy is one of Hong Kong's most contentious land issues. Critics call it discriminatory (it excludes women and non-indigenous residents), open to abuse through the sale of "ding rights" to developers who aggregate them into estates of village houses, and an unsustainable drain on scarce land. Supporters, notably the Heung Yee Kuk rural council, argue it protects lawful traditional rights. In 2019 the High Court ruled the policy fully lawful, including building on government land by free building licence, a position upheld on appeal in 2021; the Court of Final Appeal later confirmed the policy's constitutionality under Article 40 of the Basic Law, which protects the lawful traditional rights and interests of New Territories indigenous inhabitants.
