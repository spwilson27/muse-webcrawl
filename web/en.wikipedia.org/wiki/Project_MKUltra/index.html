Project MKUltra was a secret human experimentation program conducted by the United States Central Intelligence Agency between 1953 and 1973. The program sought methods of influencing and controlling human behaviour — for interrogation, incapacitation and manipulation — through the covert administration of drugs (especially LSD), hypnosis, sensory deprivation, isolation, and various forms of psychological and physical abuse. Experiments were frequently performed on unwitting subjects, including American and Canadian citizens.

History

MKUltra was authorised by CIA director Allen Dulles on 13 April 1953, growing out of earlier projects Bluebird and Artichoke, and reflecting Cold War fears that the Soviet Union, China and North Korea were using mind-control techniques on American prisoners of war. The program was run by the CIA's Office of Scientific Intelligence under chemist Sidney Gottlieb. It eventually encompassed some 149 subprojects carried out at more than 80 institutions — universities, hospitals, prisons and pharmaceutical companies — often with the institutions' staff unaware of the CIA funding source.

Experiments

Research ranged widely: the effects of LSD and other psychedelics, barbiturates and amphetamines; electroconvulsive therapy; hypnosis; and "psychic driving" — the attempt to erase and re-pattern personality through drug-induced sleep and repeated recorded messages. The latter was pursued by psychiatrist Ewen Cameron at the Allan Memorial Institute in Montreal under Subproject 68, leaving many patients with lasting damage. Under Operation Midnight Climax, CIA operatives in San Francisco and New York safehouses lured unsuspecting men to rooms where they were dosed with LSD and observed through one-way mirrors. Army scientist Frank Olson, who had been covertly dosed with LSD, died in 1953 after falling from a New York hotel window; his death became one of the program's most notorious episodes.

Exposure and aftermath

In 1973, CIA director Richard Helms ordered nearly all MKUltra files destroyed. The program nevertheless came to light in 1974 through reporting by Seymour Hersh in The New York Times on illegal CIA domestic activities. The Church Committee and the Rockefeller Commission investigated in 1975, and in 1977 a cache of some 20,000 surviving financial documents, discovered through a Freedom of Information Act request, led to further Senate hearings. The US government paid settlements to some victims, and President Gerald Ford issued an apology to Frank Olson's family. Canada settled with Cameron's patients in the 1990s.

In popular culture

MKUltra has become a byword for secret government experimentation and appears in numerous films, television series and conspiracy theories, including The Manchurian Candidate, Jacob's Ladder and Stranger Things.
