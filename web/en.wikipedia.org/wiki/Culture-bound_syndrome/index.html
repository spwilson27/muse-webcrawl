Culture-bound syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Placeholder image

In medicine and medical anthropology, a culture-bound syndrome, culture-specific syndrome or folk illness is a combination of psychiatric and somatic symptoms considered a recognizable disease only within a specific society or culture. The term was included in the fourth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV, 1994); the DSM-5 (2013) replaced it with the broader framework of "cultural concepts of distress".

Examples

  • Amok — a sudden dissociative outburst of violence, described in Malaysia.
  • Koro — panic that one's genitals are retracting, in South and East Asia.
  • Susto — "fright" illness in Latin America, attributed to a soul leaving the body.
  • Ataque de nervios — an acute anxiety/grief reaction in Latino cultures.
  • Taijin kyofusho — a Japanese fear of offending others by one's appearance or body odour.
  • Brain fag — fatigue and cognitive difficulty among West African students.
  • Dhat syndrome — anxiety about semen loss in South Asia.

Criticism

Scholars debate whether these syndromes are distinct entities, local expressions of universal disorders such as anxiety or depression, or social idioms of distress. Some argue that conditions regarded as universal in Western psychiatry — such as anorexia nervosa or PTSD — are themselves shaped by culture, challenging the asymmetry of the label.
