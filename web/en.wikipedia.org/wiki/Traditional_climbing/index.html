Traditional climbing

Traditional climbing (or trad climbing) is a type of rock climbing in which the lead climber places protection — such as nuts, hexes, and spring-loaded camming devices — into cracks and other natural features of the rock as they ascend, and the second climber removes the protection while following. Unlike sport climbing, which relies on permanent pre-drilled bolts, traditional climbing leaves no fixed hardware on the route and requires climbers to carry and place their own gear.

Equipment

Trad climbers carry a "rack" of removable protection: passive devices such as stoppers (nuts) and hexentrics, and active devices such as friends and other camming units, along with slings and quickdraws. The leader clips the rope into the gear to arrest a fall; the reliability of a placement depends on the quality of the rock and the climber's skill in selecting and seating gear.

Ethics and history

Traditional climbing grew out of the original style of rock climbing before the advent of expansion bolts and sport climbing in the 1980s. It is associated with a ground-up ethic and an emphasis on adventure, self-reliance, and minimal impact on the rock ("leave no trace"). Famous traditional climbing areas include Yosemite Valley, Joshua Tree, and the gritstone edges of England's Peak District. Because falls onto removable gear can be longer and less certain than falls onto bolts, trad climbing is often considered more psychologically demanding than sport climbing.
