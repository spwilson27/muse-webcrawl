Haemarthrosis

Haemarthrosis is bleeding into joint spaces. It is a common feature of haemophilia and other bleeding disorders, but it can also occur after trauma, in patients taking anticoagulants, and following joint surgery.

Signs and symptoms

The affected joint becomes swollen, warm, painful and stiff, with reduced range of motion. The knee is most commonly affected, followed by the elbow, ankle, hip and shoulder. Repeated episodes cause progressive joint destruction known as haemophilic arthropathy.

Causes

Causes include trauma such as ligament injury or fracture, bleeding disorders including haemophilia A and B and haemophilia C, anticoagulant therapy, tumours such as synovial haemangioma, and complications of arthroscopy or total knee replacement.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is suggested by history and examination and confirmed by aspiration of blood from the joint, ultrasound, MRI, and lipohaemarthrosis visible on X-ray in traumatic cases.

Treatment

Initial treatment includes rest, ice, compression and elevation, analgesia, and correction of the underlying coagulopathy, for example with factor replacement in haemophilia. Large or tense effusions may be aspirated. Repeated episodes may require synovectomy, physiotherapy, or joint replacement.
