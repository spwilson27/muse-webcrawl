Bok choy (American English, Canadian English and Australian English), pak choi (British English), or pok choi (Brassica rapa subsp. chinensis) is a type of Chinese cabbage, used as food. The Chinensis varieties do not form heads and have green leaf blades with lighter bulbous bottoms instead, forming a cluster reminiscent of mustard greens.

Bok choy was originally classified as a species of its own, Brassica chinensis, by Carl Linnaeus. Its name comes from the Cantonese baak choi (白菜), literally "white vegetable". It has been cultivated in China for centuries and is depicted in surviving Chinese literature from the 5th century AD.

Varieties

Two main size classes are recognised: ordinary bok choy, 30–60 cm tall with white petioles, and "Shanghai bok choy" (上海白菜), with light-green petioles and oval leaves. Dwarf "baby bok choy" is harvested young. The plant is grown year-round in mild climates and has become common in Western supermarkets since the late 20th century.

Culinary use and nutrition

Bok choy is used in stir-fries, soups, noodle dishes, dumpling fillings and salads. It cooks quickly—the stalks remain crisp while the leaves wilt—and is rich in vitamins A, C and K, folate and calcium, with very few calories. Raw bok choy contains glucosinolates, as do other brassicas, which are broken down during cooking.
