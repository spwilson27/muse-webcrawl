London sewerage system

The London sewerage system is part of the water infrastructure serving London, England. The modern system was developed during the Victorian era following cholera epidemics and the Great Stink of 1858, largely through the work of chief engineer Joseph Bazalgette for the Metropolitan Board of Works. London's sewer system is operated today by Thames Water.

Background

By the mid-19th century the Thames had become an open sewer: waste from a population of over two million was discharged into the river, along with effluent from slaughterhouses and factories. Cholera outbreaks in 1848–49 and 1853–54 killed tens of thousands, and in the summer of 1858 the "Great Stink" — the smell of untreated sewage in a hot summer — forced Parliament to act.

Bazalgette's system

Bazalgette's design (approved 1859, largely completed by 1875) intercepted sewage that had flowed into the Thames and carried it eastward by gravity and pumping to outfalls at Beckton (north) and Crossness (south). Its main elements include:

  • About 132 km (82 miles) of main intercepting sewers, and roughly 1,770 km of street sewers
  • The Northern Outfall Sewer and Southern Outfall Sewer
  • Pumping stations at Abbey Wood (Crossness) and elsewhere; the ornate Crossness Pumping Station (1865) is now a museum
  • The Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments, which narrowed and walled the river, housed the intercepting sewers and sped the river's flow

The system was designed for a population of about 4 million and for decades largely eliminated cholera from London.

Modern developments

Because London's sewers are mostly combined (carrying both sewage and rainwater), heavy rain can overwhelm the system and discharge untreated sewage into the Thames through combined sewer overflows. To address this, the Thames Tideway Tunnel ("super sewer"), a 25 km tunnel running under the river at depths of up to about 65 metres, was constructed from 2016 and brought into operation in the mid-2020s, greatly increasing storage and transfer capacity to the Lee Tunnel and Beckton treatment works.
