The Gouffre Berger is a cave in the Vercors massif in south-eastern France, on the plateau of the Sornin above the commune of Engins in the department of Isère. With a depth of 1,271 metres, it is among the deepest caves in France. It was the first cave in the world known to exceed 1,000 metres in depth.

Exploration history

The cave was discovered in 1953 by Joseph Berger during a prospecting walk. Expeditions in the following years pushed progressively deeper; in 1956 cavers reached the -1,000 metre mark for the first time in caving history, making the Gouffre Berger briefly the deepest known cave in the world. The cave system connects with neighbouring potholes and resurges at the Cuves de Sassenage.

Description

The entrance shaft descends into a series of pitches and meanders decorated with large chambers, including the vast Grand Eboulis. Below the "Cascades" the cave becomes an active streamway that can flood dangerously in wet weather, a hazard that has caused fatalities. In the lower sections the cave develops into the flooded passages of the resurgence system.
