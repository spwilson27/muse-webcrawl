Utopian socialism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Utopian socialism is the term for the early currents of modern socialist thought exemplified by the work of Henri de Saint-Simon, Charles Fourier, Étienne Cabet and Robert Owen. These thinkers presented visions of ideal societies based on cooperation and common ownership and sought to realise them by persuasion, example and the founding of model communities, rather than by class struggle or revolution.

Friedrich Engels's pamphlet Socialism: Utopian and Scientific (1880) fixed the label, contrasting the utopians — whose plans, he argued, sprang from reason alone and ignored the historical development of capitalism — with the “scientific” socialism he attributed to Marx. The pejorative edge of the term comes from that contrast, though later historians have treated the utopians more sympathetically as pioneers of feminism, communal living, town planning and the cooperative movement.

Thinkers and experiments

Saint-Simon proposed a society administered by industrialists and scientists; Fourier designed self-sufficient communities called phalanstères and championed women's emancipation (he is credited with early use of the word féminisme); Owen, a Welsh mill-owner, built the model village of New Lanark in Scotland and founded the short-lived community of New Harmony, Indiana (1825). Dozens of intentional communities inspired by these ideas were founded in Europe and North America in the nineteenth century; most dissolved within a few years, though their ideas fed into cooperatives, trade unions and later socialist parties.
