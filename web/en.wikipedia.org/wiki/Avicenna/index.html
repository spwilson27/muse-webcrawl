Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, 980–1037) was a Persian polymath — philosopher, physician, astronomer and logician — regarded as one of the most significant thinkers of the Islamic Golden Age. Largely self-educated, he mastered medicine as a teenager and wrote some 200 works, of which his two most famous are The Book of Healing (Kitāb al-Shifāʾ), a vast philosophical and scientific encyclopaedia, and The Canon of Medicine (Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb), which remained a standard medical textbook in Europe into the 17th century.

Philosophy

Avicenna's metaphysics distinguishes essence from existence and argues for a Necessary Existent — God — whose existence follows from the contingency of all other things; the argument influenced Aquinas and later scholasticism. His "Floating Man" thought experiment asks the reader to imagine a person created suspended in air, deprived of all sensation, and argues such a person would still be aware of their own existence — an anticipation, in some respects, of Descartes's cogito. His logic, theory of the soul, and modal metaphysics shaped both Islamic philosophy and Latin Scholasticism.

Life

Born near Bukhara, Avicenna served as physician and vizier to several rulers, spent periods in prison and flight amid the region's political turmoil, and died in Hamadan in 1037. His mausoleum there remains a landmark.
