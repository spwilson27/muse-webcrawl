Number needed to harm

The number needed to harm (NNH) is an epidemiological measure that indicates how many persons on average need to be exposed to a risk factor over a specific period to cause harm in an average of one person who would not otherwise have been harmed. It is the reciprocal of the absolute risk increase associated with the exposure, and is usually rounded up to the nearest whole number.

Definition and calculation

If the probability of an adverse outcome is pt in a treated (exposed) group and pc in a control group, then the absolute risk increase is pt − pc and NNH = 1 / (pt − pc). For example, if a drug raises the rate of a side effect from 1% to 5%, the absolute risk increase is 4% and the NNH is 25: on average, one extra patient is harmed for every 25 treated.

Interpretation

NNH is the counterpart of the number needed to treat (NNT). A low NNT (treatment is very effective) combined with a high NNH (harm is rare) indicates a favourable treatment. NNH varies with the baseline risk of the population, the outcome considered, and the duration of treatment, so it should always be reported with those contexts and with confidence intervals. Together with NNT it is used in evidence-based medicine and shared decision-making to weigh benefits against harms of an intervention.
