Shingle style

The Shingle style is an American architectural style that flourished between about 1880 and 1900, characterised by houses whose walls and roofs are uniformly covered in wooden shingles, producing a continuous, sculptural surface. It developed largely in the coastal resorts of New England and Long Island and is regarded as a distinctly American contribution to domestic architecture.

Characteristics

Shingle style houses typically feature a free-flowing, open interior plan; asymmetrical massing with gambrel or sweeping gable roofs; shingles that wrap around corners and over eyebrow dormers; stone foundations and chimneys; and a restrained use of ornament compared with the contemporary Queen Anne style. The monochromatic weathered-grey shingle surface emphasised the volume of the house rather than applied decoration.

Origins and architects

The style drew on English Queen Anne and Norman Shaw influences, colonial American precedents, and Japanese architecture displayed at the 1876 Philadelphia Centennial. Leading practitioners included McKim, Mead & White (the Isaac Bell House and the casino at Newport), William Ralph Emerson, Bruce Price, and the young Frank Lloyd Wright, whose early "shingle" houses influenced the Prairie School. Landmark examples include the William G. Low House by McKim, Mead & White (demolished) and H. H. Richardson's houses of the early 1880s.

Legacy

Although the style waned by 1900, it was revived in the later 20th century by architects such as Robert Venturi and continues to shape seaside house design in the northeastern United States.
