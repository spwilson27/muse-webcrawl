The French Revolutionary Wars were a series of conflicts fought between revolutionary France and shifting coalitions of European monarchies from 1792 to 1802. They are conventionally divided into the War of the First Coalition (1792–1797) and the War of the Second Coalition (1798–1802), and they merged into the Napoleonic Wars that followed.


The Battle of Valmy, 1792

War of the First Coalition

France declared war on Austria in April 1792; Prussia joined Austria, and an invading army's threat to Paris (the Brunswick Manifesto) helped radicalise the Revolution. The Prussian advance was checked at Valmy (20 September 1792), remembered as the first victory of the citizen army; the monarchy was abolished the next day. In 1793 Britain, Spain and the Dutch Republic joined the war, and the Republic, beset by invasion and civil war in the Vendée, responded with the levée en masse — mass conscription that created huge armies.

Victories at Fleurus (1794) and in the Netherlands, then Napoleon Bonaparte's dazzling Italian campaign of 1796–97 — Lodi, Arcole, Rivoli — forced Austria to sign the Treaty of Campo-Formio (1797).

War of the Second Coalition

France's expedition to Egypt (1798) provoked a new coalition of Britain, Austria, Russia and the Ottoman Empire. Nelson destroyed the French fleet at the Battle of the Nile; Russian general Suvorov campaigned in Italy and Switzerland. Bonaparte returned from Egypt, seized power in the coup of 18 Brumaire (1799), and defeated Austria at Marengo and (through Moreau) Hohenlinden, leading to the Treaty of Lunéville (1801). Britain made peace at Amiens in March 1802, briefly ending the wars.

Significance

The wars spread revolutionary reforms — legal equality, the metric system, the abolition of feudal privilege — across Europe through French "sister republics," transformed warfare through mass conscription and national mobilisation, and made the career of Napoleon.
