A credit score is a numerical expression based on a statistical analysis of a person's credit files, representing the person's creditworthiness — broadly, the predicted likelihood that the borrower will repay debts and not default. Lenders such as banks and credit-card companies use credit scores to evaluate applications, set interest rates and credit limits, and manage existing accounts. Scores are computed from data held by credit bureaus, including payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, types of credit used and recent credit enquiries.

Typical FICO score ranges, from poor (300–579) to exceptional (800–850).

FICO score

The most widely used credit score in the United States is the FICO Score, created by the Fair Isaac Corporation (now FICO) and introduced in 1989. The general-purpose score ranges from 300 to 850; higher is better. Published factor weights for the general population are approximately: payment history 35%, amounts owed (including credit utilisation) 30%, length of credit history 15%, new credit 10% and credit mix 10%. FICO also produces industry-specific scores (e.g. auto and bankcard) on a 250–900 scale, and newer versions such as FICO 10.

VantageScore and other models

VantageScore, launched in 2006 by the three national bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion), uses the same 300–850 range in its recent versions and similar (though differently weighted) factors. Because lenders use different models and bureau data, a consumer has many credit scores, not one. Outside the US, systems vary: the UK relies on bureau scores from Experian, Equifax and TransUnion with no single universal number, while countries such as Germany use Schufa scores; some countries historically used only negative (default) data rather than comprehensive positive reporting.

Uses and criticism

Beyond lending, credit scores may be consulted (in varying, jurisdiction-dependent ways) in tenant screening, insurance underwriting and some employment checks. Criticisms include the opacity of scoring models, errors in bureau data, the penalising of consumers with thin or no credit files, and disparate-impact concerns. Regulation such as the US Fair Credit Reporting Act gives consumers rights to see their reports, dispute errors and know when a score was used against them; the Equal Credit Opportunity Act prohibits discrimination in credit decisions.
