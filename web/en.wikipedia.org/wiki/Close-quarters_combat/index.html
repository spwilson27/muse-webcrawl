Close-quarters combat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Soldiers practise close-quarters battle during urban operations training.

Close-quarters combat (CQC) or close-quarters battle (CQB) is a type of fighting in which small units engage the enemy at very short range, typically within buildings, trenches, vehicles, or other confined spaces. It may involve firearms at point-blank range, hand-to-hand combat, knives, bayonets, and improvised weapons.

Characteristics

CQC is distinguished by its speed, violence of action, and the premium placed on surprise and overwhelming force at the point of entry. Engagements are usually short, chaotic, and decided in seconds. Because targets appear at very close range and often among non-combatants, CQC demands precise target discrimination and tight control of fields of fire.

Military use

Modern CQC doctrine developed through the world wars, from trench raiding in the First World War to the systematic room-clearing methods taught by figures such as W. E. Fairbairn and Rex Applegate in the Second. Urban warfare in places such as Stalingrad, Hue, and Fallujah made CQB a core infantry skill.

Typical techniques include:

  • Stacking — a team lines up at an entry point before breaching.
  • Breaching — explosive, mechanical, or ballistic entry through doors and walls.
  • Room clearing — entering and systematically securing corners and dead space ("slicing the pie" from the doorway).
  • Dynamic entry — rapid, aggressive movement intended to overwhelm occupants.

Law enforcement

Police SWAT and tactical units use CQB for hostage rescue, high-risk warrants, and counter-terrorism. Special forces units such as the British SAS developed hostage-rescue CQC to a high art, famously displayed in the 1980 Iranian Embassy siege in London.

Related disciplines

Hand-to-hand systems taught alongside CQC include Krav Maga, the US Marine Corps Martial Arts Program (MCMAP), and Russian Systema and Sambo derivatives. The term "close combat" in modern NATO usage also covers engagements under 100 metres in open terrain.
