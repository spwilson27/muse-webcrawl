Friedel–Crafts reaction

The Friedel–Crafts reactions are a set of reactions developed by Charles Friedel and James Crafts in 1877 to attach substituents to an aromatic ring. The two main types are Friedel–Crafts alkylation, in which an alkyl group is added using an alkyl halide and a strong Lewis acid catalyst such as aluminium chloride (AlCl3), and Friedel–Crafts acylation, in which an acyl group is added using an acyl chloride.

Mechanism and limitations

Both reactions proceed by electrophilic aromatic substitution: the Lewis acid generates a powerful electrophile (a carbocation or an acylium ion) which attacks the aromatic ring, and a base then restores aromaticity. Alkylation suffers from carbocation rearrangements and multiple substitution, because the alkylated product is more reactive than the starting material; acylation stops after a single substitution because the acyl group deactivates the ring, and the resulting ketone can be reduced (for example by Clemmensen reduction) to give mono-alkylated products that cannot be made cleanly by direct alkylation. Strongly deactivated rings, such as nitrobenzene, do not undergo Friedel–Crafts reactions at all.
