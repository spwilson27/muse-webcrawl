Void coefficient

In nuclear engineering, the void coefficient (more properly called the void coefficient of reactivity) is a number that can be used to estimate how much the reactivity of a nuclear reactor changes as voids (typically steam bubbles) form in the reactor moderator or coolant. Net reactivity in a reactor depends on several factors; the void coefficient relates specifically to the change in moderator density.

Steam bubbles ("voids") in the coolant change neutron moderation and absorption.

Explanation

In most water-moderated designs, boiling reduces the density of the moderator, so fewer neutrons are slowed to thermal energies and reactivity falls — a negative void coefficient, which is self-stabilising. In the Soviet RBMK design, however, the moderator is graphite and the water acts partly as a neutron absorber; boiling therefore removes absorber and reactivity rises — a positive void coefficient. This characteristic was a central contributing factor in the Chernobyl disaster of 26 April 1986.

Design implications

Most Western licensing regimes require a negative void coefficient under all operating conditions. Boiling water reactors exploit the negative coefficient for load following, while CANDU reactors have a small positive coolant void reactivity that is managed by fast-acting shutdown systems.
