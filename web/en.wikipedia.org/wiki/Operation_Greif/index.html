Operation Greif (German for "Griffin") was a special operation commanded by Waffen-SS commando Otto Skorzeny during the Battle of the Bulge in December 1944. German soldiers wearing American uniforms, speaking English, and using captured or disguised vehicles were to infiltrate behind American lines to seize bridges over the Meuse River and sow confusion by reversing signposts, spreading false orders, cutting communications, and misdirecting traffic. The operation was carried out by the specially formed Panzer Brigade 150.

Preparation

Hitler personally briefed Skorzeny on the plan in October 1944. A call went out for English-speaking volunteers, ideally familiar with American slang, but genuinely fluent speakers were scarce — of some 2,500 volunteers, only a small number could pass as Americans, and these formed commando teams (Einheit Stielau) in jeeps, while the rest of the brigade fought in conventional fashion with German vehicles, supplemented by a handful of Panther tanks crudely disguised with sheet metal to resemble American M10 tank destroyers, and captured Sherman tanks and trucks.

Execution and effects

When the Ardennes offensive began on 16 December 1944, most of the disguised armour was held up in traffic and never reached the Meuse bridges, and the small jeep teams were largely captured within days. Captured commandos, questioned by American intelligence, exaggerated — inadvertently or deliberately — the operation's scale, and one team claimed a squad was on its way to Paris to capture or kill General Eisenhower. This triggered a security panic far out of proportion to the operation itself: American military police set up checkpoints throughout the rear areas, quizzing soldiers on baseball, state capitals, and popular culture to unmask impostors; genuine American units were delayed, some soldiers were detained, and Eisenhower was confined under heavy guard for a period, much to his annoyance.

Legal aftermath

German commandos captured in American uniforms were in several cases tried by military courts and executed as spies. Skorzeny himself was tried at Dachau in 1947 on war-crimes charges relating to the use of enemy uniforms but was acquitted, the tribunal accepting testimony — including from the British agent F. F. E. Yeo-Thomas — that Allied commandos had used similar ruses without intending to fight while so disguised.
