Refuge (Buddhism)

In Buddhism, taking refuge is the first step in becoming a Buddhist and a central practice throughout the path. One takes refuge in the Three Jewels (Sanskrit: triratna; Pali: tiratana) — the Buddha, the Dharma (the teaching) and the Sangha (the community of practitioners) — expressing trust in them as guides to liberation from suffering. The traditional formula, recited three times, is:

Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi — I go to the Buddha for refuge.
Dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi — I go to the Dhamma for refuge.
Saṅghaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi — I go to the Saṅgha for refuge.

Meaning

The Buddha is refuge both as the historical teacher who discovered and proclaimed the path and, especially in Mahayana interpretations, as the embodiment of awakening. The Dharma is refuge as the teaching, the practice and its realisation. The Sangha is refuge as the noble community (ariya-sangha) of those who have realised stages of the path, and by extension the ordained community and one's fellow practitioners. Commentaries explain refuge in terms of fear of saṃsāra, faith born of seeing the qualities of the Three Jewels, and the resolve to entrust one's spiritual life to them.

Practice

Formally going for refuge is the act by which one becomes a lay Buddhist (upāsaka/upāsikā) and is a component of monastic ordination; it commonly precedes taking the Five Precepts and opens most Buddhist rituals and meditation sessions. In Vajrayana Buddhism, refuge is elaborated through the refuge tree visualisation and counted repetitions in the ngöndro preliminaries, and extra objects of refuge (lâm, yidam and ḍākinī, the "three roots") are added. Refuge vows are traditionally maintained by recalling the qualities of the Three Jewels, avoiding "mundane refuges" (reliance on worldly powers), and honouring images and texts.

In scripture

Texts such as the Khaggavisāṇa and Dhammapada praise refuge in the Triple Gem as secure and unsurpassed ("This refuge is supreme…"). The Mahayana Refuge is sometimes expanded into the "Four Refuges" including the guru in Tibetan traditions, and Pure Land and Zen schools express distinctive inflections of the same formula.
