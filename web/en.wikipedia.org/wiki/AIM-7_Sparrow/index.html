The AIM-7 Sparrow is an American medium-range semi-active radar homing air-to-air missile operated by the United States Air Force, United States Navy, and United States Marine Corps, as well as other air forces. It was the primary beyond-visual-range missile of Western air forces from the late 1950s until the 1990s, when it was progressively replaced by the active-radar AIM-120 AMRAAM.

Development

The Sparrow grew out of the Navy's Project Hotshot of 1947, led by the Sperry Gyroscope Company, and first flew successfully in the early 1950s. Early beam-riding AIM-7A and semi-active AIM-7C versions entered service in 1956–58 on aircraft such as the F3H Demon and F-4 Phantom II. Because the missile homes on radar energy reflected from the target, the launching aircraft must continuously illuminate the target until impact.

Combat use

In the Vietnam War, early Sparrows performed poorly, with a probability of kill under 10 percent. Improved models such as the AIM-7E-2 "Dogfight Sparrow" and the solid-state AIM-7F and AIM-7M performed markedly better; in the 1991 Gulf War, Sparrows achieved the majority of coalition air-to-air kills, including 24 Iraqi aircraft. Sparrow derivatives also armed ships as the RIM-7 Sea Sparrow point-defense missile, later developed into the Evolved Sea Sparrow Missile (ESSM).

Variants

  • AIM-7A/B/C — early beam-riding and first semi-active versions
  • AIM-7D/E — Vietnam-era production versions
  • AIM-7F — solid-state electronics, longer range
  • AIM-7M — monopulse seeker, improved ECCM
  • AIM-7P/R — later upgrades; RIM-7 naval derivatives
