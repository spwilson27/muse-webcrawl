The Battle of Fei River or Battle of Feishui (traditional Chinese: 淝水之戰; simplified Chinese: 淝水之战) took place in 383 AD in Anhui, China, between the Former Qin dynasty of the north and the Eastern Jin dynasty of the south. Despite being vastly outnumbered, the Jin won a crushing victory, preserving southern independence and precipitating the disintegration of Former Qin. The battle gave Chinese the idiom "fēng shēng hè lì, cǎo mù jiē bīng" (風聲鶴唳，草木皆兵), "every sound of the wind and cry of the cranes, every bush and tree an enemy soldier," describing a panicked army.

Background

Fu Jian, the Di ruler of Former Qin, had unified northern China by 381 and resolved to conquer the south. In 383 he mobilised an immense army — chronicles claim 870,000 men — and advanced on the Jin capital Jiankang. The Jin court, guided by Chancellor Xie An, entrusted the defence to the elite Beifu Army under Xie Shi and Xie Xuan, some 80,000 troops, who marched to meet the Qin advance guard at the Fei River.

The battle

The Jin challenged the Qin army, drawn up on the far bank, to withdraw slightly so the Jin could cross and give battle. Fu Jian agreed, intending to strike the Jin while they were mid-crossing. The withdrawal, however, degenerated into chaos: Jin agents planted in the Qin camp shouted "The Qin are defeated!", and the vast, loosely coordinated army panicked and stampeded. Fu Rong, Fu Jian's brother and field commander, was killed trying to stem the rout. The Jin pursued, and the retreating host suffered enormous losses from battle, exposure and starvation.

Aftermath

Fu Jian escaped wounded to the north, but his empire promptly disintegrated into warring successor states as subject peoples revolted; he was murdered in 385. The north-south division of China persisted for another two centuries, until the Sui dynasty reunified the country in 589.
