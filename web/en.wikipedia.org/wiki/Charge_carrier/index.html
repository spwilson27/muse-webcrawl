Charge carrier

In physics, a charge carrier is a particle or quasiparticle that is free to move, carrying an electric charge, especially the particles that carry electric currents in electrical conductors. Examples are electrons, ions and holes. In a conducting medium, an electric field can exert force on these free particles, causing a net drift through the medium; this is what constitutes an electric current.

In metals

In metallic conductors the charge carriers are electrons. In a metal, the outer (valence) electrons of each atom are not bound to any particular nucleus but form a delocalised "sea" that moves throughout the lattice. When a voltage is applied, these electrons drift slowly toward the positive terminal, with drift velocities of fractions of a millimetre per second, even though the electrical signal itself propagates near the speed of light.

In semiconductors

In semiconductors there are two types of charge carrier: free electrons and electron holes. A hole is the absence of an electron in the valence band and behaves as a mobile positive charge. Materials doped with electron donors are called n-type (electrons are the majority carriers), while those doped with acceptors are p-type (holes are the majority carriers). The balance and movement of these carriers underlies the operation of diodes, transistors and solar cells; the Hall effect can be used to determine carrier type, density and mobility.

In electrolytes and plasmas

In electrolytes and ionic solutions, the carriers are positive and negative ions (cations and anions) that migrate in opposite directions. In gases and plasmas, currents are carried by free electrons and positive ions, as in fluorescent lamps and lightning. In superconductors, the carriers are pairs of electrons known as Cooper pairs.
