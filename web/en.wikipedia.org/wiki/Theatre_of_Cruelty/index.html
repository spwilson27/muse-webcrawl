The Theatre of Cruelty (French: Théâtre de la cruauté) is a concept of theatre developed by French writer, actor, and director Antonin Artaud, set out in essays of the early 1930s and collected in The Theatre and Its Double (1938). Artaud sought to overturn the Western theatre of dialogue and psychology and replace it with a total, physical spectacle that would assault the audience's senses.

Concept

"Cruelty" for Artaud did not primarily mean bloodshed or sadism; he defined it as rigour, necessity, and implacable intention—an acknowledgement of the cruelty inherent in existence and creation. He rejected the primacy of the written text and the masterpiece, proposing instead a spatial language of gesture, movement, sound, lighting, and object: actors would function as "animated hieroglyphs", staging would envelop the spectators, and theatre would act on the audience like the plague—by releasing buried impulses and compelling a confrontation with truth.

Practice and influence

Artaud's own productions, including his 1935 staging of Shelley's The Cenci, were commercial failures, and he never fully realised his vision. After his death his ideas profoundly influenced experimental theatre: Jean-Louis Barrault, Jerzy Grotowski, the Living Theatre, and Peter Brook, whose 1964 Theatre of Cruelty season with the Royal Shakespeare Company led directly to his celebrated production of Marat/Sade.
