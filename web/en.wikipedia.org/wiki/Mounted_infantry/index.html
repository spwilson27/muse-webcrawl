Mounted infantry

Mounted infantry are infantry soldiers who move on horseback but dismount to fight on foot. Unlike cavalry, which historically fought from the saddle, and dragoons — who began as mounted infantry but evolved into conventional cavalry in most armies — mounted infantry used horses purely for operational mobility, riding to the battlefield and then fighting as ordinary foot soldiers.

History

The concept is ancient: various armies used horses, mules or camels to move foot soldiers rapidly. Mounted infantry became prominent in the 19th century, particularly in colonial warfare. In the American Civil War, many nominally cavalry units in fact fought largely as mounted infantry, especially late in the war when breech-loading and repeating carbines made dismounted firepower effective; Wilder's "Lightning Brigade" was a notable Union example.

During the Second Boer War (1899–1902), the British Army raised large numbers of mounted infantry after finding that Boer commandos — themselves essentially mounted infantry — could outmanoeuvre conventional forces. Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa also fielded mounted infantry and mounted rifles units, a tradition continued in the First World War by formations such as the Australian Light Horse, which combined mounted movement with dismounted action, most famously at Beersheba in 1917 (where it exceptionally charged mounted).

Decline

With the mechanisation of armies in the 20th century, horses were replaced by bicycles, motorcycles, trucks and armoured personnel carriers, and the mounted infantry concept survived in the modern form of motorised and mechanised infantry. A few armies retained ceremonial or niche mounted units for terrain where vehicles were impractical.
