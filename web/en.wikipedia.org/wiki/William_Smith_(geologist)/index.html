William Smith (23 March 1769 – 28 August 1839) was an English geologist, credited with creating the first nationwide geological map, and with discovering the principle that rock strata can be identified and ordered by the fossils they contain. He is known as the "Father of English Geology".

Early life

Smith was born in Churchill, Oxfordshire, the son of a blacksmith. With little formal education, he trained as a surveyor and worked on canal construction in the 1790s, notably the Somerset Coal Canal. Travelling the country for engineering work, he observed rock layers in canals, quarries and mines across England.

Faunal succession and the map

Smith realised that sedimentary strata always occur in the same relative order and that each stratum contains a distinctive assemblage of fossils — the principle of faunal succession. This allowed strata to be correlated across large distances. In 1815 he published A Delineation of the Strata of England and Wales with Part of Scotland, the first true geological map of a whole country, hand-coloured, at a scale of five miles to the inch.

Hardship and recognition

Smith's work was plagiarised by gentlemen geologists, and financial troubles landed him in debtors' prison in 1819. Only late in life was he honoured: the Geological Society awarded him its first Wollaston Medal in 1831, and he received a government pension. He died in Northampton on 28 August 1839.
