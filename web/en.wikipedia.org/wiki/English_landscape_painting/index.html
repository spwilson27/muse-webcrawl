English landscape painting

English landscape painting is the tradition of landscape art in England, which rose from a minor genre in the early eighteenth century to become, by around 1800, the country's most celebrated contribution to European art. Rooted in topographical watercolour drawing, the Dutch and classical landscape traditions, and the aesthetics of the Picturesque, the English school produced in John Constable and J. M. W. Turner two of the most influential landscape painters of the nineteenth century, whose work reshaped landscape painting across Europe.

Constable's Suffolk scenes epitomise the naturalistic strand of English landscape.

Origins: topography and the classical landscape

Early English landscape grew from topographical drawings of country houses and prospects, often in watercolour, and from emigrant and visiting artists working in the classical manner of Claude Lorrain and Gaspard Dughet. Richard Wilson (1714–1782), trained in Italy, is often called the father of English landscape painting for his classical compositions of British and Italian scenery. The young Thomas Gainsborough combined Dutch naturalism with feathery brushwork in Suffolk scenes, and a flourishing school of watercolourists — Paul Sandby, Thomas Girtin and the young Turner — elevated watercolour landscape to exhibition status by the 1790s.

The Picturesque and the great age

The theory of the Picturesque, debated by William Gilpin, Uvedale Price and Richard Payne Knight, taught tourists and artists to value roughness, variety and ruins in scenery like those of the Wye Valley and the Lake District. Between about 1800 and 1840 the genre reached its height. John Constable (1776–1837) painted the working countryside of the Stour valley with unprecedented freshness, making full-size oil sketches for large "six-footers" such as The Hay Wain (1821), which triumphed at the Paris Salon of 1824 and influenced the Barbizon school. J. M. W. Turner (1775–1851) moved from topographical watercolour through sublime mountain and marine subjects — The Fighting Temeraire (1839), Rain, Steam and Speed (1844) — to late, near-abstract essays in light and atmosphere.

Later developments

The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood applied microscopic naturalism to landscape backgrounds in the 1850s, while Samuel Palmer's visionary Shoreham pastorals were rediscovered in the twentieth century by neo-romantic painters. English landscape imagery — the "green and pleasant land" — became central to national identity, even as Constable and Turner were absorbed into the international story of modern art; Constable's cloud studies and oil sketches in particular were celebrated by later generations.
