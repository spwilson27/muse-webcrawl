Catalytic triad

A catalytic triad is a set of three amino acid residues found in the active sites of certain enzymes that work together to carry out catalysis. Triads are a classic example of convergent evolution: the same functional arrangement has evolved independently in many unrelated enzyme families, most famously the serine proteases.

The serine protease triad

In chymotrypsin, trypsin, and other serine proteases, the triad consists of serine, histidine, and aspartate. The aspartate polarises the histidine, which acts as a general base to deprotonate the serine hydroxyl, making it a powerful nucleophile that attacks the peptide bond of the substrate and forms a covalent acyl-enzyme intermediate; water then hydrolyses the intermediate to release the product. Remarkably, the unrelated bacterial protease subtilisin uses the identical triad in a completely different protein fold.

Variations

Other triads include the Ser–His–Glu of some esterases, the Cys–His–Asn of cysteine proteases such as papain, and the catalytic machinery of many lipases and amidases. In some enzymes only two residues are essential (a catalytic dyad). The triad's precise geometry, together with features such as the oxyanion hole, accounts for rate enhancements of many orders of magnitude.
