Asepsis

Asepsis
DefinitionThe state of being free from disease-causing microorganisms; also the practices used to achieve and maintain it
Key practicesHand hygiene; sterile gloves, gowns, and drapes; sterilised instruments; sterile fields
Related conceptsAntisepsis; sterilisation; disinfection
FieldsSurgery, nursing, dentistry, laboratory work, pharmaceutical manufacturing

Asepsis is the state of being free from pathogenic microorganisms, and, in clinical usage, the set of practices—called aseptic technique—used to prevent contamination by such organisms. Asepsis is distinguished from antisepsis: antiseptic methods kill or inhibit microbes already present on living tissue or surfaces, whereas aseptic methods aim to exclude microbes entirely, working with sterilised equipment, sterile fields, and barrier precautions.

History

The principles of asepsis developed in the later nineteenth century out of germ theory. Ignaz Semmelweis had shown in 1847 that handwashing with chlorinated lime solution drastically reduced deaths from puerperal fever in a Vienna maternity ward, though his findings were resisted. Joseph Lister's antiseptic surgery, introduced from 1867 with carbolic acid, transformed operative outcomes. In Germany, surgeons such as Ernst von Bergmann and Curt Schimmelbusch advanced the shift from antisepsis to asepsis—steam sterilisation of instruments and dressings (the autoclave), sterile gowns and caps, and, from the 1890s, rubber gloves popularised by William Stewart Halsted's team. Robert Koch's bacteriological work supplied the scientific foundation and standard methods for testing sterility.

Principles and practice

Aseptic technique is graded to the risk of the procedure. Surgical asepsis (sterile technique) is used in operating theatres and for invasive procedures: staff scrub and wear sterile gowns and gloves, instruments are sterilised by steam, dry heat, gas (ethylene oxide), or radiation, and a sterile field is maintained around the patient, with only sterile items permitted to touch it. Medical asepsis ("clean technique")—hand hygiene, gloves, and disinfection of surfaces and equipment—governs routine care such as wound dressing and catheter handling. Modern practice codifies these behaviours in bundles for specific risks, such as central-line insertion, that have sharply reduced hospital-acquired infections. Key maxims include that a sterile object remains sterile only when touched by another sterile object, and that anything of doubtful sterility is treated as contaminated.

Beyond the hospital

Aseptic processing is essential in pharmaceutical manufacturing, where sterile drugs and vaccines are filled in cleanrooms under rigorous environmental monitoring, and in microbiology laboratories, where aseptic technique protects both cultures from contamination and workers from cultures. Food and beverage industries apply related methods in aseptic packaging, in which sterilised products are sealed in sterile containers without preservatives or refrigeration.
