Intergroup contact theory

Contact under favourable conditions can reduce prejudice between groups.

Intergroup contact theory is a theory in social psychology proposing that, under appropriate conditions, interpersonal contact between members of different groups can be effective in reducing prejudice, stereotyping, and discrimination. It is sometimes called the contact hypothesis.

History

The theory is most closely associated with Gordon Allport, who set out the hypothesis in his 1954 book The Nature of Prejudice. Allport proposed that contact between groups could reduce prejudice when four conditions were present:

  1. Equal status between the groups in the situation;
  2. Common goals shared by the groups;
  3. Intergroup cooperation, rather than competition, in pursuit of those goals; and
  4. Support of authorities, law, or custom.

Allport also suggested that contact is most effective when it involves the possibility of friendship between members of the groups.

Research evidence

In 2006, Thomas Pettigrew and Linda Tropp published a meta-analysis of 515 studies conducted over several decades on the effects of intergroup contact. They found that contact was consistently associated with lower levels of prejudice, and that the effect held even in many settings where Allport's optimal conditions were not all present, although effects were stronger when the conditions were met. The effect generalized beyond the immediate outgroup members involved in the contact to the wider outgroup.

Extensions of the theory

Researchers have extended the contact hypothesis beyond direct face-to-face contact. Extended contact refers to the effect of knowing that a member of one's own group has a close relationship with an outgroup member. Imagined contact involves mentally simulating a positive interaction with an outgroup member, and parasocial contact describes exposure to outgroup members through media portrayals. Studies suggest these indirect forms of contact can also reduce prejudice.
