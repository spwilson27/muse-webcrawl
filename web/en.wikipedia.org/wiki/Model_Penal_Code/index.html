Model Penal Code

The Model Penal Code (MPC) is a model act designed to stimulate and assist US state legislatures to update and standardise the penal law of the United States. The code was produced by the American Law Institute (ALI) and completed in 1962; its chief reporter was Herbert Wechsler of Columbia Law School. It has been called the most influential attempt to rationalise American criminal law.

Contents

The Code is organised into general principles of liability, definitions of specific offences, excuses and justifications, and provisions on sentencing and corrections. It classifies crimes into felonies (of the first, second and third degree), misdemeanors, petty misdemeanors and non-criminal violations. Its most famous innovation is a systematic analysis of culpability into four mental states — acting purposely, knowingly, recklessly or negligently — which replaced the tangled common-law vocabulary of intent. The Code also systematised defences, attempt and complicity, de-criminalised private consensual adult sexual conduct, and proposed a structured sentencing framework with extended terms for dangerous offenders.

Influence

No state adopted the MPC wholesale, but more than two-thirds of the states enacted criminal codes substantially influenced by it during the codification wave of the 1960s and 1970s, and courts frequently cite it as persuasive authority representing the "modern" or majority approach. Its mens rea hierarchy and its treatments of mistake, provocation (recast as "extreme mental or emotional disturbance") and felony grades remain standard material in US law schools. The ALI has undertaken projects to revise parts of the Code, notably the sentencing provisions (approved in 2017) and the sexual assault articles.

Criticism

Critics have faulted the Code's treatment of rape and sexual offences as dated even at the time, its relatively lenient approach to some serious crime, and its original positions on capital punishment — the ALI withdrew its death penalty provision in 2009, citing the impossibility of guaranteeing a minimally adequate system.
