Elizabeth Petrovna (Russian: Елизаве́та Петро́вна; 29 December 1709 – 5 January 1762), also known as Yelisaveta, was the Empress of Russia from 1741 until her death in 1762. The second-eldest daughter of Peter the Great and Catherine I, she seized the throne in a coup against the infant Emperor Ivan VI and his regent Anna Leopoldovna, restoring the line of Peter the Great. Her reign of twenty years saw Russia take part in the War of the Austrian Succession and the Seven Years' War, and was marked by an efflorescence of court culture, architecture, and learning.

Rise to power

Elizabeth grew up a vivacious and popular princess, but after her parents' deaths she was pushed aside under the rulers Anna Ioannovna and the regency for Ivan VI. Fearing she would be confined to a convent or married off, on the night of 25 November (O.S.) 1741 she went to the barracks of the Preobrazhensky Guards, rallied them with a silver cross and a declaration, and arrested the regent's household without bloodshed. She was crowned in Moscow in April 1742.

Reign

Elizabeth abolished the Cabinet of Ministers and restored the Senate's authority as under her father. Her government, guided by favourites and ministers such as Alexey Bestuzhev-Ryumin, Mikhail Vorontsov, and the Shuvalov brothers, founded Moscow University (1755) and the Imperial Academy of Fine Arts, and commissioned the great Baroque and Rococo works of architect Bartolomeo Rastrelli, including the Winter Palace in St. Petersburg and the Catherine Palace at Tsarskoye Selo. She is also remembered for effectively abolishing capital punishment in practice, refusing to sign death sentences throughout her reign.

In foreign affairs she maintained the alliance with Austria against Prussia. Russian armies won major victories in the Seven Years' War, occupying East Prussia and briefly Berlin (1760) and defeating Frederick the Great at Kunersdorf (1759). Elizabeth's death in January 1762 brought her pro-Prussian nephew Peter III to the throne, who promptly withdrew Russia from the war — the reversal Frederick called the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg".

Legacy

Famous for her extravagance — she reportedly owned some 15,000 dresses and never wore the same gown twice — Elizabeth combined personal piety, devotion to her father's legacy, and court splendour with capable ministerial government. She named her nephew Peter of Holstein-Gottorp as heir and arranged his marriage to the future Catherine the Great.
