Notting Hill is an affluent district of West London, in the Royal Borough of Kensington and Chelsea. Known for its white stucco and pastel-coloured Victorian terraces, garden squares, the Portobello Road antiques market and the annual Notting Hill Carnival, it is one of London's most recognisable neighbourhoods.

History

The name derives from "Knottynghull" in medieval documents, of uncertain meaning. The area was rural until the 19th century, noted for pigs, potteries and gravel pits; the Ladbroke family developed it from the 1820s as a fashionable suburb (the Ladbroke Estate), with grand terraces around communal gardens. The suburb never fully prospered in the Victorian period and declined into multi-occupied slums in the early 20th century, becoming home to a large Afro-Caribbean community after the Second World War.

The area saw the Notting Hill race riots of 1958, in which white mobs attacked Caribbean residents — an event that helped spur the founding of the Notting Hill Carnival in 1964 by activist Rhaune Laslett, drawing on Claudia Jones's earlier indoor Caribbean carnival of 1959. Slum landlordism (notably Peter Rachman) made the district notorious in the 1960s.

Carnival and market

The Notting Hill Carnival, held each August bank holiday weekend, is Europe's largest street festival, drawing around a million or more visitors to its steel bands, sound systems, masquerade bands and food stalls. Portobello Road Market, established in the 19th century, is famous for antiques on Saturdays and for its fruit, vegetable and street-food stalls through the week.

Gentrification and popular culture

From the 1980s Notting Hill gentrified rapidly, becoming one of London's most expensive residential areas; the process was satirised and exported worldwide by the 1999 romantic comedy Notting Hill, starring Julia Roberts and Hugh Grant, set around a Portobello Road travel bookshop. Former Prime Minister David Cameron belonged to the "Notting Hill set" of Conservative politicians associated with the area.
