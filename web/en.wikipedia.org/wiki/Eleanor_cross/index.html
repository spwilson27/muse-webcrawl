Eleanor cross

[Hero image placeholder: Geddington Eleanor cross]

The Eleanor crosses were a series of twelve lavishly decorated stone monuments built by King Edward I of England in memory of his beloved wife, Eleanor of Castile, who died at Harby, Nottinghamshire, on 28 November 1290. The crosses marked the nightly resting places of her body on its funeral journey from Lincoln to Westminster Abbey in London, where she was buried. They were erected between 1291 and 1295 and are among the finest examples of early English Gothic (Decorated) architecture and sculpture.

The journey and the crosses

The funeral cortège travelled for twelve days in December 1290. At each overnight stop Edward ordered a cross to be raised. The crosses stood at:

Only three original crosses survive substantially intact: Geddington, Hardingstone, and Waltham Cross. Each cross rose in tiers decorated with statues of Eleanor, her heraldry (the arms of England, Castile and León, and Ponthieu), pinnacles, and a crucifix at the summit. The master mason John of Battle and sculptors including Alexander of Abingdon were among the craftsmen employed.

Legacy

The crosses established a template for English memorial crosses. The London cross at Charing gave its name to Charing Cross, the point from which distances to London are traditionally measured. The Victorian replica erected in 1865 in the forecourt of Charing Cross station was designed by Edward Middleton Barry.
