Fire apparatus

A fire apparatus (North American English) or fire appliance (UK and Commonwealth English) is any vehicle designed and equipped to assist in firefighting. Fire apparatus can take many forms depending on its role, from pumpers that deliver water to a fire, to aerial devices that provide elevated access, to specialised rescue and hazardous-materials vehicles.

Types

  • Fire engine (pumper) — carries a pump, water tank, hoses and a crew; the most common type of apparatus, used to supply water to extinguish fires.
  • Aerial apparatus — equipped with a turntable ladder or hydraulic platform (such as a tower ladder or snorkel) for rescue and elevated water streams.
  • Tiller truck — a tractor-drawn aerial with a separate steered rear axle, common in the United States.
  • Water tender (tanker) — carries a large volume of water to areas without hydrants.
  • Rescue vehicle — carries tools for vehicle extrication, rope rescue, trench rescue and other technical rescues.
  • Hazmat unit — equipped for hazardous materials incidents.
  • Wildland fire engine — smaller, often four-wheel-drive vehicles for brush and wildland fires.
  • Airport crash tender — specialised rapid-intervention vehicles for aircraft fires at airports.
  • Command vehicle — mobile incident command post.

Equipment

Depending on type, apparatus may carry fire hoses of various diameters, ground ladders, breathing apparatus, hydraulic rescue tools ("jaws of life"), thermal imaging cameras, foam systems, generators, lighting and medical equipment. Modern apparatus are usually built on custom or commercial truck chassis by manufacturers such as Pierce, E-One, Rosenbauer and Scania.

History

Early fire apparatus consisted of hand-pumped engines pulled by firefighters or horses. Steam pumpers appeared in the mid-19th century, and motorised apparatus replaced horse-drawn equipment in the early 20th century. Diesel engines, enclosed cabs, and roll-cage crew protection became standard in the later 20th century, driven in part by safety standards such as NFPA 1901 in North America.
