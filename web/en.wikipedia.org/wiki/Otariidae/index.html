The Otariidae or otariids, commonly known as eared seals, are a family of pinnipeds comprising the fur seals and sea lions — 16 species in seven genera. They differ from the earless or "true" seals (Phocidae) in possessing a visible external ear flap (pinna), long hairless foreflippers, and hind flippers that can rotate forward under the body, allowing them to walk, after a fashion, on land.

Anatomy and locomotion

Otariids propel themselves in water mainly with their large foreflippers, in a rowing or "flying" motion, steering with the hind flippers — the reverse emphasis of phocids, which swim with their hindquarters. On shore, eared seals are comparatively agile and can lift the body and gallop short distances. Sea lions are larger, smoother-coated animals, while fur seals bear a dense underfur beneath coarser guard hairs, the trait that made them targets of commercial sealing.

Sexual dimorphism is pronounced: males of many species are two to three times the mass of females and, in sea lions, develop thickened necks and manes — the South American sea lion's genus name, Otaria, and the male's lion-like crest reflect this. Well-known members include the California sea lion (Zalophus californianus), the Steller sea lion (Eumetopias jubatus), the northern fur seal (Callorhinus ursinus), and the Antarctic fur seal (Arctocephalus gazella).

Behaviour and distribution

Eared seals are found along temperate and subpolar coasts of the Pacific and the Southern Ocean, generally avoiding the high Arctic. Most are strongly polygynous: in the breeding season, males hold territories or harems on beaches and fast while defending them, and females alternate nursing their single pup with foraging trips to sea. Diets centre on fish, squid, and krill, varying by species.

Conservation

Fur seals were hunted nearly to extinction for their pelts in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; twentieth-century protection allowed spectacular recoveries, such as the Antarctic fur seal at South Georgia. Some species remain threatened, including the Australian and New Zealand sea lions, while the Japanese sea lion was declared extinct in the twentieth century.
