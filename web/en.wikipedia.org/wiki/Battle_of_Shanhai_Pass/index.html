Shanhai Pass, the gateway between Manchuria and China proper.

The Battle of Shanhai Pass, fought on 27 May 1644 at the eastern terminus of the Great Wall of China, was a decisive battle of the Ming–Qing transition. The Ming general Wu Sangui, commanding the frontier army at the pass, allied with the Manchu Qing forces under the prince regent Dorgon to defeat the rebel army of Li Zicheng, whose Shun regime had just taken Beijing and ended Ming rule. The Qing victory at the pass opened the way into China proper: within days Qing troops occupied Beijing, beginning more than 260 years of Qing rule over China.

Background

In April 1644, as Li Zicheng's rebels approached Beijing, the Chongzhen Emperor summoned Wu Sangui's elite frontier army from Ningyuan to defend the capital. Wu was still marching when Beijing fell on 25 April and the emperor hanged himself. Caught between Li Zicheng to the west and the Manchu Qing to the northeast, Wu initially moved to submit to Li, but reversed course — accounts cite Li's mistreatment of Wu's family in Beijing, including the seizure of his concubine Chen Yuanyuan — and seized Shanhai Pass, defeating the force Li sent against him. Li then marched east in person with the main rebel army, while Wu appealed to Dorgon for Qing assistance.

The battle

On 27 May, Li Zicheng deployed his army, perhaps 60,000 strong, on the plain west of the pass along the Sha River. Wu's troops bore the brunt of the initial fighting and were gradually driven back with heavy losses. As the battle hung in the balance, Dorgon committed the Qing cavalry, which had been held concealed on Wu's flank. Tradition holds that a sudden windstorm blew dust into the rebels' faces as the Manchu horsemen charged; Li's army broke and fled in disorder, suffering tens of thousands of casualties in the rout. Li Zicheng retreated to Beijing, briefly had himself proclaimed emperor, and then abandoned the capital, which Dorgon's forces entered on 6 June.

Consequences

Shanhai Pass is often treated as the moment the Qing conquest of China became possible: without Wu's defection and the victory at the pass, the Manchus might have remained beyond the wall. Wu Sangui was enfeoffed as a prince by the Qing, but three decades later he rebelled against his new masters, launching the Revolt of the Three Feudatories (1673–1681), the last great challenge to early Qing rule. In Chinese historical memory Wu has been persistently condemned as a traitor whose invitation to the Manchus — "borrowing" their army — cost China its independence, and the battle remains a staple of Ming–Qing historiography and popular culture.
