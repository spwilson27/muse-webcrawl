Shallot

The shallot is a cultivar group of the onion (Allium cepa), formerly classified as a separate species, Allium ascalonicum. Like garlic, shallots form a cluster of multiple bulbs (cloves) from a single planted bulb, and they are valued in cooking for a flavour that is milder and sweeter than the common onion, with a hint of garlic. Shallots are a staple of French cuisine and of many Southeast Asian cuisines.

Description and taxonomy

The shallot plant produces a cluster of elongated bulbs offset from a common base, covered in papery skins ranging from golden brown and copper to pinkish red and grey (the prized French grey shallot, or griselle, is often considered the "true" shallot and is propagated only vegetatively). Modern classification treats shallots as the Aggregatum group of Allium cepa, the same species as the common onion; the old name ascalonicum refers to Ashkelon, the ancient Levantine city from which the plant was once thought to have spread to Europe, possibly with returning Crusaders.

Cultivation

Shallots are grown from bulbs (sets) rather than seed in most traditional production: each planted bulb divides to yield a cluster of five to ten new bulbs. They prefer well-drained soil in full sun and are harvested when the foliage dies back, then cured for storage. Major producers include France (Brittany and the Loire valley), the Netherlands, and countries across Asia.

Culinary use

Finely minced shallot is a foundation of French sauces such as béarnaise and beurre blanc, and of vinaigrettes, where its mildness avoids the harshness of raw onion. In Indonesia, Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand, and the Philippines, fried shallots (bawang goreng) are a ubiquitous garnish, and raw shallots enter sambals, salads, and pickles. Shallots may be pickled whole in vinegar in Britain and elsewhere.
