Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck

Born20 March 1870, Saarlouis, Prussia
Died9 March 1964, Hamburg, West Germany
RankGeneralmajor (later General der Infanterie, honorary)
CommandSchutztruppe, German East Africa
Nickname"The Lion of Africa"

Paul Emil von Lettow-Vorbeck (20 March 1870 – 9 March 1964) was a German general who commanded the colonial forces of German East Africa during the First World War. For four years, with a small force of Europeans and African askari cut off from all resupply, he waged a mobile guerrilla campaign against much larger British, Belgian and Portuguese forces and surrendered only after the Armistice in Europe — the only German commander to invade British territory during the war and remain undefeated in the field.

Early career

Born at Saarlouis into a Pomeranian military family, Lettow-Vorbeck joined the Prussian cadet corps and served overseas in China during the Boxer Rebellion (1900–01) and in German South-West Africa during the Herero and Nama uprisings (1904–06), where he was wounded and came under the mentorship of Lothar von Trotha. He took command of the Schutztruppe in German East Africa in April 1914, a force of some 260 Europeans and 2,500 askari, with thousands more carriers essential to operations in roadless bush.

The East African campaign

Against the wishes of the colony's governor, who hoped for neutrality, Lettow-Vorbeck resolved to tie down as many Allied troops as possible. He repulsed a British amphibious landing at the Battle of Tanga (November 1914) — the "Battle of the Bees" — capturing badly needed rifles and equipment, and won again at Jassin (1915). From 1916, confronted by Jan Smuts's large South African-led offensive and superior numbers, he avoided decisive battle, striking railways and isolated columns and retreating through the colony. In November 1917 he crossed into Portuguese Mozambique, defeated a Portuguese force at Ngomano, and lived off captured supplies; in September 1918 he re-entered German East Africa and then raided into Northern Rhodesia. His force — by then about 1,300 askari and 150 Europeans — laid down its arms at Abercorn on 25 November 1918, two weeks after the European Armistice, having never been defeated.

Historians note the campaign's heavy cost to East African civilians and carriers, of whom tens of thousands died from disease, hunger and requisitioning by all sides — a toll that complicates Lettow-Vorbeck's heroic reputation, though his askari remained famously loyal to him.

Later life

Lettow-Vorbeck received a hero's welcome in Berlin in 1919, leading his askari in a victory parade through the Brandenburg Gate. He briefly entered politics, took part in the Kapp Putsch of 1920 (ending his army career), and sat in the Reichstag from 1928 to 1930 for the conservative DNVP. Though courted by the Nazis, he never joined the party; a (possibly apocryphal) story holds that he brusquely rejected Hitler's offer of an ambassadorship. After the Second World War he lived in reduced circumstances in Hamburg; British admirers, including former opponents, raised a fund for his support, and in 1953 the Federal Republic granted him a pension, paying arrears to his surviving askari after his final visit to Tanzania. He died in Hamburg in 1964, aged 93, and was buried in Pronstorf, Schleswig-Holstein.
