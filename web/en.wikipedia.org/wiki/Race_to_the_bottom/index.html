Race to the bottom

Race to the bottom is a socio-economic concept describing a situation in which states, territories or companies compete with one another by successively lowering standards — such as labour protections, environmental regulation, taxation or product quality — in order to attract or retain business. As competition intensifies, each participant feels compelled to match the lowest standards offered by rivals, producing a downward spiral toward the least protective regime.

Origin

The phrase is often traced to United States Supreme Court Justice Louis Brandeis, who in a 1933 dissent referred to states competing in the relaxation of corporate law as a race "not of diligence but of laxity". The concept is related to earlier descriptions of regulatory competition in American federalism, particularly in the market for corporate charters, in which states such as Delaware attracted incorporations with permissive rules.

Applications

The term is applied to international trade and globalisation, where critics argue that multinational companies shift production to countries with the cheapest labour and weakest protections; to tax competition, in which jurisdictions cut corporate taxes to attract investment ("tax havens"); and to environmental policy, where governments may hesitate to impose standards that could drive industry abroad. Related ideas include regulatory arbitrage, the "Delaware effect", and social dumping.

Criticism and counterarguments

Economists disagree about the strength of the effect. Some note that low standards are only one of many location factors, and that stability, infrastructure, skilled workforces and market access often outweigh regulatory savings; the opposite dynamic — the "California effect", in which firms adopt the strictest standard of any major market — has also been documented. Empirical studies find mixed evidence depending on the policy area examined.
