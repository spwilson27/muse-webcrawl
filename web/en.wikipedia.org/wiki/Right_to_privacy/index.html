Right to privacy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The right to privacy is a right, recognised in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 12) and the ICCPR (Article 17), to be free from arbitrary or unlawful interference with one's privacy, family, home and correspondence, and from attacks on honour and reputation. In many constitutions it is express; in others it is implied — most famously in the United States, where the Supreme Court located a constitutional right of privacy in the “penumbras” of the Bill of Rights in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965), grounding later decisions on contraception, abortion and intimacy, some since revisited (Dobbs, 2022).

Privacy law has two great branches: intrusion/defamation-adjacent torts (from Warren and Brandeis's 1890 article The Right to Privacy) and data protection — the regulation of personal information, pioneered in Europe (German census decision, 1983, on informational self-determination) and codified in the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (2016), with rights of access, correction, erasure and objection. Digital surveillance by states and platforms has made privacy one of the central legal battlegrounds of the twenty-first century.
