The London Stock Exchange (LSE) is a stock exchange in the City of London, one of the world's oldest and, by the value of its listed companies, among the largest. Its origins lie in the coffee houses of Change Alley in the late 17th century; it was formally constituted in 1801, and its motto is Dictum Meum Pactum — 'my word is my bond'. The exchange is now part of the London Stock Exchange Group, which also owns Refinitiv, Borsa Italiana and major clearing and index (FTSE Russell) businesses.

Markets and reform

Trading centres on the Main Market for large companies — its premium segment benchmarked by the FTSE 100 index — and AIM (the Alternative Investment Market, launched 1995) for smaller growth companies. The 'Big Bang' deregulation of 1986 abolished fixed commissions and the separation of brokers and jobbers, moving trading to screens and internationalising the City, one of the defining acts of Thatcher-era market reform. The LSE competes with the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq for international listings; in recent years debate has centred on London's relative loss of large IPOs to New York, a standing concern of UK economic policy.
