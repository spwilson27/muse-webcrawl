Florida land boom of the 1920s

Florida land boom
Periodc. 1920–1926
LocationFlorida, United States
Peak1925
Key developersGeorge E. Merrick, Carl G. Fisher, Addison Mizner

The Florida land boom of the 1920s was the first real estate bubble in Florida. The economic prosperity of the 1920s, Florida's pleasant climate, and aggressive promotion drew speculators from across the United States. Land prices rose rapidly, and lots were bought and sold repeatedly—often on small deposits of "binders"—without ever being developed. The boom collapsed in 1926, foreshadowing the Great Depression.

Background

Earlier developers such as Henry Flagler and Henry Plant had opened Florida's coasts with railroads and grand hotels in the late 19th century. After World War I, improved highways and the mass production of the automobile made Florida accessible to middle-class tourists and settlers. Miami's population grew rapidly, and developers promoted subdivisions across the state.

The boom

Developers such as George E. Merrick, who built Coral Gables, and Carl G. Fisher, who developed Miami Beach, sold a vision of tropical luxury. Addison Mizner transformed Palm Beach and Boca Raton with Mediterranean Revival architecture. At the peak in 1925, Miami had an estimated 75,000 real estate agents and salesmen, and property changed hands at extraordinary speed; some lots were resold a dozen times in a single day.

Collapse

By late 1925 the railroads, overwhelmed with building materials, embargoed non-essential freight to Florida, and northern newspapers began exposing speculative excesses and fraud. In January 1926 the schooner Prinz Valdemar sank in Miami harbor, blocking shipping for weeks and damaging the city's image. The devastating 1926 Miami hurricane of September 1926, followed by the Okeechobee hurricane of 1928, ended the boom entirely. Banks failed across Florida years before the nationwide banking crisis, and many buyers were left with worthless swampland.

Legacy

The boom left behind enduring communities and architecture, including Coral Gables and Miami's Mediterranean Revival districts, but also thousands of platted, undeveloped subdivisions. Economists cite it as a classic speculative bubble and a precursor of the 2000s housing bubble.
