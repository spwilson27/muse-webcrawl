Remortgage

A remortgage (known in the United States as refinancing) is the process of paying off one mortgage with the proceeds from a new mortgage using the same property as security. The new loan may be with the same lender or a different one. People remortgage for many reasons: to reduce their monthly repayments, to consolidate other debts, to avoid moving home, to reduce the term of the loan, or to raise capital for home improvements, business purposes or other major purchases.

Reasons for remortgaging

The most common trigger is the end of an introductory fixed or discounted rate period, after which a borrower would otherwise revert to the lender's higher standard variable rate. Borrowers may also remortgage because the value of their property has risen, giving access to a lower loan-to-value tier and cheaper rates, or because their circumstances have improved (for example, a better credit score) since taking out the original loan.

Process and costs

Remortgaging involves a new application, credit checks, a valuation of the property and legal work (conveyancing) to transfer the lender's charge — processes that some lenders cover with free legal and valuation packages. Costs can include arrangement fees, valuation fees, legal fees and, if the borrower leaves a deal early, an early repayment charge. Borrowers therefore compare the total cost, not just the headline rate, against the saving over the deal period.

Risks

Using a remortgage to consolidate unsecured debts converts them into debt secured on the home, so failure to pay can put the property at risk of repossession. Extending the term lowers monthly payments but usually increases the total interest paid.
