Eurozone crisis

The Eurozone crisis, or European sovereign debt crisis, was a multi-year crisis in the euro area beginning in late 2009, when the newly elected Greek government revealed that the country's budget deficit was far larger than previously reported, undermining confidence in Greek sovereign debt. Because eurozone states borrow in a currency they do not individually control, the loss of market confidence threatened a self-reinforcing spiral of rising interest rates and insolvency, spreading to Ireland, Portugal, Spain, Italy and Cyprus.

Course of the crisis

Ireland, whose banking system collapsed after a property bubble, required an EU–IMF bailout in 2010; Greece received its first rescue package the same year and a second in 2012, involving a restructuring of privately held debt, while Portugal was bailed out in 2011 and Cyprus in 2013. Spain obtained assistance to recapitalise its banks in 2012. Rescue lending was conditional on fiscal austerity, which deepened recessions in the affected countries; Greek unemployment exceeded 27% and youth unemployment passed 50%. Fears peaked in mid-2012 that the euro might break up, until European Central Bank president Mario Draghi pledged to do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro, backed by the Outright Monetary Transactions programme, after which spreads narrowed sharply.

Responses and legacy

The crisis produced lasting institutional changes: the European Stability Mechanism as a permanent rescue fund, stricter fiscal rules in the Fiscal Compact, the beginnings of a banking union with single supervision by the ECB, and quantitative easing from 2015. Greece exited its third bailout programme in 2018. The crisis reshaped politics across Europe, fuelling both Eurosceptic and protest parties and enduring debate about the design of monetary union.
