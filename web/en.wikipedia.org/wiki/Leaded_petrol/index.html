Leaded petrol, also called leaded gasoline, is petrol (gasoline) to which the organolead compound tetraethyllead (TEL) has been added as an anti-knock agent. Introduced commercially in the 1920s, leaded fuel enabled higher-compression engines but dispersed vast quantities of toxic lead into the environment, becoming one of the most damaging pollution sources of the twentieth century.

History

TEL was discovered as an anti-knock additive by Thomas Midgley Jr. and colleagues at General Motors in 1921 and marketed by the Ethyl Corporation. Although the toxicity of lead was well known — workers at TEL plants suffered severe poisoning, and some died, in 1924 — the additive was approved and spread worldwide. From the 1970s, evidence assembled by scientists such as Clair Patterson and Herbert Needleman linked environmental lead to elevated blood-lead levels and cognitive harm in children. The advent of catalytic converters, which lead poisons, also required unleaded fuel.

Phase-out

The United States phased down lead in petrol from the 1970s and banned it in road vehicles in 1996; the European Union banned leaded petrol in 2000, and China the same year. A UN Environment Programme partnership promoted the phase-out in developing countries, and in July 2021 Algeria became the last country to cease sales, ending the leaded-petrol era for road vehicles. Leaded aviation gasoline (avgas 100LL) remains in use for piston-engine aircraft.

Health and social effects

Population blood-lead levels fell dramatically following the bans, tracking the decline in petrol lead use. Some researchers have hypothesised a link between leaded-petrol exposure and later crime rates (the lead–crime hypothesis), though this remains debated. The episode is frequently cited as a landmark case of industry resistance to public-health regulation, and of successful global environmental policy.
