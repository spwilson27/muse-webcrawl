The Framingham Heart Study is a long-term, ongoing cardiovascular cohort study of residents of the town of Framingham, Massachusetts. It began in 1948 with 5,209 adult subjects and is now on its third generation of participants. The study is run by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI), in collaboration with Boston University since 1971.

History

Before the study, almost nothing was known about the natural history and causes of heart disease, then rising sharply as a cause of death in the United States. Under its first director, Thomas Royle Dawber, the cohort was examined every two years with detailed medical histories, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. A second-generation "Offspring Study" began in 1971, and a third-generation cohort in 2002.

Findings

Framingham is credited with popularising the concept of the cardiovascular "risk factor". A landmark 1961 paper identified high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and smoking as major risk factors for coronary heart disease. Later work added diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and psychosocial factors, and produced widely used risk-prediction tools such as the Framingham Risk Score for estimating a person's 10-year risk of cardiovascular disease. The study has generated thousands of peer-reviewed papers and informed prevention guidelines worldwide.

Criticism and legacy

The original cohort was overwhelmingly white and of European ancestry, limiting generalisability; later cohorts (Omni cohorts) broadened the sample. Framingham data have also been used for genetic and neurological research, including dementia studies.
