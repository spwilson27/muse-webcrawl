Liquid–liquid extraction

Liquid–liquid extraction, also known as solvent extraction and partitioning, is a method to separate compounds or metal complexes, based on their relative solubilities in two different immiscible liquids, usually water (polar) and an organic solvent (non-polar). There is a net transfer of one or more species from one liquid into another liquid phase, generally from aqueous to organic. The transfer is driven by a chemical potential.

A separatory funnel, the classic laboratory tool for liquid–liquid extraction.

Principles

The distribution of a solute between the two phases at equilibrium is described by its distribution (partition) coefficient, the ratio of its concentrations in the organic and aqueous phases. Extraction efficiency can be improved by repeated extraction with fresh solvent, by adjusting pH so the target compound is uncharged, or by adding complexing agents. In analytical chemistry the technique is performed batch-wise in a separatory funnel; industrially it runs continuously in mixer-settlers or extraction columns.

Applications

Liquid–liquid extraction is used on a huge scale in the refining of petroleum, the purification of pharmaceuticals and fragrances, the recovery of metals in hydrometallurgy (for example copper and rare earth elements), and in nuclear fuel reprocessing, where the PUREX process extracts uranium and plutonium from spent fuel using tributyl phosphate in kerosene.
