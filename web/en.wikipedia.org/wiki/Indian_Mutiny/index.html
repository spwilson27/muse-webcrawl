Indian Mutiny

The Indian Mutiny, also known as the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the Sepoy Mutiny or, in India, the First War of Independence, was a major uprising in India in 1857–1858 against the rule of the British East India Company.

Causes and course

The rebellion began on 10 May 1857 as a mutiny of sepoys (Indian soldiers) at Meerut, sparked in part by rumours that new rifle cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to Hindu and Muslim soldiers alike. Underlying causes included annexation policies such as the Doctrine of Lapse, heavy taxation, and social and religious grievances. The mutineers marched to Delhi and proclaimed the elderly Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah II their leader. Major centres of revolt included Delhi, Lucknow, Kanpur and Jhansi, where Rani Lakshmibai became a celebrated figure of resistance. The uprising featured brutal violence on both sides, including massacres at Kanpur and savage reprisals by British forces.

Aftermath

After the rebellion was suppressed, the East India Company was abolished; the Government of India Act 1858 transferred rule to the British Crown, beginning the period of the British Raj. Queen Victoria's proclamation promised religious tolerance and an end to annexations.
