Exotoxin

Image placeholder: Molecular structure of a protein exotoxin

An exotoxin is a toxin secreted by bacteria. An exotoxin can cause damage to the host by destroying cells or disrupting normal cellular metabolism. They are highly potent and can cause major damage to the host. Most exotoxins can be destroyed by heating. They may be secreted, or, similar to endotoxins, may be released during lysis of the bacterial cell.

Properties

Exotoxins are usually proteins produced by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. They are often encoded by genes carried on plasmids or bacteriophages rather than the bacterial chromosome. Because they are proteins, most are heat-labile (destroyed by boiling), are strongly antigenic, and can be converted into harmless toxoids — the basis of vaccines against diphtheria and tetanus. Minute quantities, in the nanogram per kilogram range, can be lethal.

Classification

Exotoxins are commonly grouped by their mechanism of action. Type I toxins, or superantigens, bind to the outside of target cells and provoke massive immune activation. Type II toxins damage host cell membranes, often by forming pores. Type III toxins are A–B toxins that enter cells: the B subunit binds the cell surface while the A subunit, an enzyme, is translocated inside and modifies a target molecule. Classic examples include diphtheria toxin, cholera toxin, botulinum toxin, tetanus toxin, and pertussis toxin.
