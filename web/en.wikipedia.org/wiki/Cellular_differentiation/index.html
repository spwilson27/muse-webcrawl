Cellular differentiation

Cellular differentiation is the process in which a stem cell changes from one type to a differentiated one. Usually, the cell changes to a more specialised type. Differentiation happens multiple times during the development of a multicellular organism as it changes from a simple zygote to a complex system of tissues and cell types, and continues in adulthood as tissue stem cells divide and create fully differentiated daughter cells during tissue repair and normal cell turnover.

Potency

Cells are classified by their developmental potential or potency. A totipotent cell such as the zygote can form an entire organism including extraembryonic tissues. Pluripotent embryonic stem cells can form all germ layers but not the placenta. Multipotent adult stem cells, such as haematopoietic stem cells, generate a limited family of cell types, while unipotent cells produce only one type. Differentiation generally proceeds with progressive loss of potency.

Mechanism

Differentiated cells differ not in their DNA sequence but in gene expression, controlled by combinations of transcription factors, epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation and histone modification, and signals from the cell's environment (morphogens, growth factors, neighbouring cells). A few "master regulator" transcription factors, like MyoD in muscle, can convert one cell type toward another, and forced expression of the Yamanaka factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc) can reprogram adult cells back to pluripotency as induced pluripotent stem cells.

Significance

Differentiation produces the hundreds of specialised cell types of the human body — neurons, hepatocytes, muscle fibres, blood cells — each expressing a characteristic subset of the genome. Failures of differentiation underlie developmental disorders, and cancer cells typically show blocked or abnormal differentiation, a feature exploited therapeutically, for example in differentiation therapy of acute promyelocytic leukaemia.
