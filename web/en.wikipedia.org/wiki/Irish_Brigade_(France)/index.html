Irish Brigade (France)

The Irish Brigade was a brigade of the French Royal Army composed of Irish exiles — the "Wild Geese" — who had left Ireland after the Treaty of Limerick (1691) ended the Williamite War. Formed in 1690–1692 from troops of the Jacobite army of James II, it served France with distinction for a century, until the aftermath of the French Revolution.

Origins

Under the military articles of the Treaty of Limerick, some 12,000–14,000 Irish soldiers sailed for France in 1691 in the episode remembered as the Flight of the Wild Geese. They were organised into regiments named for their colonels — among them Clare's, Dillon's, Berwick's, Roth's, and Lally's — and were notable for their red coats, which distinguished them from French blue and led British opponents to call them the "redcoats" before that term applied to the British Army itself.

Service

The Brigade fought in the Nine Years' War, the War of the Spanish Succession (including at Malplaquet and the celebrated stand at Cremona in 1702), and the War of the Austrian Succession, where at the Battle of Fontenoy (1745) its charge was credited with saving the day for France — a feat long commemorated in Irish nationalist memory. Irish regiments also served in India under the Comte de Lally and supported French campaigns in Germany and Flanders. The brigade's motto was Semper et ubique fidelis ("Always and everywhere faithful"), though its loyalty to the Jacobite cause inspired several abortive plans to restore the Stuarts in Ireland and Scotland.

Disbandment

The brigade was dissolved in the early 1790s during the Revolutionary reorganisation of the French army; some officers were executed in the Terror, while others, such as Arthur Dillon, had long embodied the brigade's integration into French society. Its traditions were later invoked by Irish nationalist units, including the Irish Brigade of the American Civil War and the Irish volunteers of the First World War.
