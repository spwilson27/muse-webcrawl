The House of York was a cadet branch of the English royal House of Plantagenet. Three of its members became kings of England in the late 15th century. The house gave its name and emblem, the White Rose, to one side of the dynastic struggle known as the Wars of the Roses, in which it contested the throne with the House of Lancaster.

Origins

The house was founded by Edmund of Langley, the fourth surviving son of Edward III, who was created Duke of York in 1385. His elder son Edward, 2nd Duke of York, was killed at the Battle of Agincourt in 1415. The younger son, Richard of Conisburgh, Earl of Cambridge, was executed for his part in the Southampton Plot against Henry V; through his wife Anne de Mortimer he transmitted to his descendants a claim to the throne derived from Lionel of Antwerp, Edward III's second son, which many considered senior to that of the Lancastrian kings, who descended from the third son, John of Gaunt.

Claim to the throne

Richard, 3rd Duke of York, became a leading magnate and served as Lord Protector during the mental illness of Henry VI. His rivalry with the Lancastrian court, especially Edmund Beaufort, Duke of Somerset, escalated into open war at the First Battle of St Albans in 1455. York was killed at the Battle of Wakefield in 1460, but his son Edward defeated the Lancastrians at Towton in 1461 and was crowned as Edward IV.

Yorkist kings

Edward IV reigned from 1461 to 1470 and again from 1471 until his death in 1483, interrupted by the brief restoration of Henry VI. His marriage to Elizabeth Woodville and the ambitions of his brother George, Duke of Clarence — executed in 1478 — divided the family. On Edward's death, his twelve-year-old son Edward V succeeded but was deposed by his uncle, Richard, Duke of Gloucester, who took the throne as Richard III after the young king and his brother (the "Princes in the Tower") were declared illegitimate and disappeared.

Fall

Richard III was killed at the Battle of Bosworth Field on 22 August 1485 by the forces of Henry Tudor, who became Henry VII and founded the Tudor dynasty. The male line of York became extinct with the execution of Edward Plantagenet, Earl of Warwick, in 1499. Yorkist pretenders such as Lambert Simnel and Perkin Warbeck troubled Henry VII's reign, and the de la Pole family carried the claim into the 16th century.
