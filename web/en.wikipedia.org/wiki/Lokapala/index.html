Lokapala (Sanskrit: लोकपाल, "guardian of the world") are the deities who act as guardians of the directions in Indian religions, principally Hinduism and Buddhism, and also Jainism. Traditionally eight in number (the Ashta-Dikpala, "guardians of eight directions"), each lokapala presides over one cardinal or intercardinal direction and is typically depicted mounted on his characteristic vehicle (vahana).

The eight guardians

The classic set of eight comprises: Indra, king of the gods, guardian of the east, riding the elephant Airavata; Agni, god of fire, south-east, on a ram or goat; Yama, god of death, south, on a buffalo; Nirrti, south-west, on a man or lion; Varuna, god of the waters, west, on a makara; Vayu, god of wind, north-west, on an antelope; Kubera, god of wealth, north, on a man; and Ishana (a form of Shiva), north-east, on a bull.

Expanded sets

Some traditions extend the group to ten (Dasadikpala) by adding Brahma for the zenith and Vishnu (or Shesha) for the nadir. The lokapalas appear prominently in temple architecture, where their images are placed on the walls or ceilings corresponding to their directions, and in ritual diagrams such as mandalas.

In Buddhism

In Buddhism the concept was adapted as the Four Heavenly Kings (Caturmaharaja), guardians of the four cardinal directions who protect the world and the dharma, dwelling on the slopes of Mount Sumeru.
