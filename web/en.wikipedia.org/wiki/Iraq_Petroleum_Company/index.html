The Iraq Petroleum Company (IPC) was an oil company that held a virtual monopoly on oil exploration and production in Iraq from 1925 until 1961, and continued operating until its nationalisation in 1972. Founded in 1912 as the Turkish Petroleum Company, it was a consortium of the major British, Dutch, French and American oil companies, organised by the Armenian businessman Calouste Gulbenkian, whose 5 percent share earned him the nickname "Mr Five Percent."

History

After the First World War, the consortium obtained a concession from the Kingdom of Iraq covering most of the country, formalised in 1925. Oil was struck in large quantities at Baba Gurgur near Kirkuk on 14 October 1927, one of the most famous gushers in oil history. Pipelines to the Mediterranean ports of Haifa and Tripoli opened in the 1930s. Under the 1928 "Red Line Agreement," the partners agreed not to compete independently within the former Ottoman territories, a self-denying arrangement that shaped Middle Eastern oil for decades. A fifty-fifty profit-sharing agreement with Iraq followed in 1952, but relations deteriorated after the 1958 revolution; Law No. 80 of 1961 stripped the IPC of 99.5 percent of its concession area, and the Ba'athist government nationalised its remaining operations in 1972.

Legacy

The IPC's operations were absorbed into the Iraq National Oil Company. Its Kirkuk fields and pipelines remain central to Iraq's oil industry, and the company is a classic case study of the concession-era oil business and the resource nationalism that succeeded it.
