Cartan criterion

Cartan’s criteria characterize solvable and semisimple Lie algebras through the Killing form.

In mathematics, Cartan’s criterion refers to results by Élie Cartan that characterize solvable and semisimple Lie algebras in terms of the Killing form. The Killing form of a Lie algebra g is the bilinear form B(x, y) = tr(ad x ∘ ad y), where ad is the adjoint representation.

Criterion for semisimplicity

Over a field of characteristic zero, a finite-dimensional Lie algebra is semisimple if and only if its Killing form is nondegenerate. This connects an algebraic property—the absence of a nonzero solvable radical—with the nondegeneracy of a canonical bilinear form.

Criterion for solvability

A related criterion states that a Lie algebra g is solvable if and only if B(x, y) = 0 for all x in g and y in the derived algebra [g, g]; equivalently, the Killing form vanishes on g × [g,g]. In particular, for a solvable algebra the Killing form is highly degenerate.

Context and use

Cartan’s criteria are foundational in the structure theory of Lie algebras. Together with the Levi decomposition and root system theory, they help reduce the classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras to the study of simple algebras and their Dynkin diagrams.
