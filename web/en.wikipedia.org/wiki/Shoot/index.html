Shoot (botany)

In botany, a shoot consists of any plant stem together with its appendages, leaves and lateral buds, flowering stems and flower buds. The new growth from seed germination that grows upward is a shoot, where leaves will develop. In the spring, perennial plant shoots are the new growth that grows from the ground in herbaceous plants or the new stem or flower growth that grows on woody plants.

Structure

A shoot is composed of a stem axis bearing nodes, where leaves and buds arise, and internodes, the segments between nodes. At the tip of the shoot is the apical meristem, a region of actively dividing cells that drives the elongation of the stem and produces new leaf primordia. Leaves are arranged along the shoot in patterns (phyllotaxis) characteristic of each species.

Two types of shoots are commonly distinguished in woody plants: long shoots, which extend the framework of branches, and short shoots (or spur shoots), with very short internodes, which often bear flowers and fruit, as in apple and many other fruit trees.

Development

Shoot growth is regulated by plant hormones, particularly auxins produced at the shoot apex, which also maintain apical dominance by suppressing the growth of lateral buds below. Light, gravity, and water availability direct shoot growth: shoots generally exhibit negative gravitropism and positive phototropism, growing away from the centre of the Earth and toward light, optimising the leaves' exposure for photosynthesis.

Culinary and horticultural use

Young shoots of many plants are eaten as vegetables — asparagus, bean sprouts, and bamboo shoots are familiar examples. In horticulture, shoots are propagated by cuttings, grafting and budding, and pruned to shape plants and encourage fruiting.
