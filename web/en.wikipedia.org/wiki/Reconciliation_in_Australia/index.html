Reconciliation in Australia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reconciliation in Australia refers to the ongoing process of building respectful relationships between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and non-Indigenous Australians, and of addressing the historical and continuing effects of colonisation, dispossession and discrimination. Since the early 1990s it has been pursued through formal government processes, community initiatives and national commemorations.

Background

British colonisation of Australia began in 1788. Indigenous peoples were dispossessed of their lands without treaty or compensation, and for much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries were subjected to protection and assimilation policies. Under assimilation policies, many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children were forcibly removed from their families, becoming known as the Stolen Generations.

The 1967 referendum amended the Australian Constitution to allow the Commonwealth to make laws for Aboriginal people and to count them in the census. The Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976 and the High Court's decision in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), which recognised native title, were further milestones.

Council for Aboriginal Reconciliation

In 1991 the Commonwealth Parliament unanimously established the Council for Aboriginal Reconciliation, chaired initially by Patrick Dodson, to lead a formal process of reconciliation over a ten-year period. The Council presented its final report and a proposed Australian Declaration Towards Reconciliation in 2000. At Corroboree 2000, held at the Sydney Opera House in May 2000, hundreds of thousands of Australians walked across the Sydney Harbour Bridge in support of reconciliation.

The Apology

The 1997 Bringing Them Home report of the Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission documented the removal of Indigenous children and recommended a national apology. On 13 February 2008, Prime Minister Kevin Rudd delivered the National Apology to Australia's Indigenous peoples, in particular the Stolen Generations, in the Parliament of Australia.

Closing the Gap

The Closing the Gap framework, launched in 2008, set targets for reducing disparities between Indigenous and non-Indigenous Australians in life expectancy, child mortality, education and employment. A new National Agreement on Closing the Gap was signed in 2020, developed in partnership with the Coalition of Peaks representing Indigenous organisations, and built around priority reforms including shared decision-making and strengthening the community-controlled sector.

Uluru Statement and the Voice referendum

In May 2017, Indigenous leaders issued the Uluru Statement from the Heart, calling for a constitutionally enshrined First Nations Voice to Parliament, a Makarrata Commission to supervise agreement-making, and truth-telling about Australian history. In October 2023, a referendum to alter the Constitution to recognise Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples through a Voice was held and was not carried, with a majority of voters in all states voting No.

National Reconciliation Week

National Reconciliation Week is held annually from 27 May to 3 June, dates commemorating the 1967 referendum and the High Court's Mabo decision. Reconciliation Australia, established in 2001 as the successor body to the Council, promotes reconciliation and supports organisations in developing Reconciliation Action Plans.
