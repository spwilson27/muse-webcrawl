The Confederate States of America (CSA), commonly referred to as the Confederate States, the Confederacy, or "the South", was an unrecognized breakaway republic in the Southern United States that existed from February 8, 1861, to May 9, 1865. It was formed by eleven states that seceded from the United States following the election of Abraham Lincoln, in a dispute centred on the institution of slavery and its expansion. The Confederacy was never diplomatically recognized as a country by any foreign government.

Formation

South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, and six more Deep South states followed before Lincoln's inauguration. Delegates met in Montgomery, Alabama, in February 1861, adopted a constitution modelled closely on the United States Constitution but explicitly protecting slavery, and elected Jefferson Davis as president. After the bombardment of Fort Sumter in April 1861, four more states joined. The capital moved to Richmond, Virginia.

Civil War

The American Civil War (1861–1865) pitted the Confederacy against the Union. Confederate forces under generals such as Robert E. Lee won notable victories in the East, but the Union's advantages in population, industry, rail transport and naval power proved decisive. The Union victory at Gettysburg and the capture of Vicksburg in July 1863 are generally seen as turning points. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865, and the remaining Confederate armies capitulated over the following weeks.

Government and society

The Confederate constitution created a confederation of sovereign states with a comparatively weak central government, complicating wartime mobilization; Davis nevertheless introduced conscription (1862), impressment of supplies, and suspension of habeas corpus. Of the Confederacy's population of about nine million, roughly 3.5 million were enslaved African Americans, whose labour underpinned the wartime economy. Inflation, shortages and the Union naval blockade eroded the home front.

Dissolution and legacy

With defeat, the Confederacy ceased to exist and the seceded states were readmitted to the Union during Reconstruction, following ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment abolishing slavery. The Confederacy remains a subject of historical debate, particularly regarding the causes of secession and the "Lost Cause" interpretation that emerged among white Southerners after the war.
