Cenomanian-Turonian boundary event

Organic-rich black shale marks the boundary interval in many sections.

The Cenomanian-Turonian boundary event, also known as Oceanic Anoxic Event 2 (OAE2), was a global episode of widespread oceanic anoxia about 93.9 million years ago, at the boundary between the Cenomanian and Turonian stages of the Late Cretaceous. It caused significant extinctions among marine organisms and is recorded worldwide by the deposition of organic-rich black shales and a pronounced positive carbon-isotope excursion.

Causes

The event is generally attributed to massive volcanism, likely from a large igneous province such as the Caribbean Plateau, which released carbon dioxide, warmed the climate, accelerated nutrient delivery to the oceans, and boosted primary productivity. Decomposition of the resulting organic matter consumed oxygen faster than it could be replenished, spreading oxygen-depleted waters through much of the ocean.

Effects

Among the groups severely affected were planktic foraminifera, ammonites, and marine reptiles; a substantial fraction of marine invertebrate genera disappeared. On land the effects were less severe. Sea levels were near their highest of the Phanerozoic during the event, and recovery of marine ecosystems took several hundred thousand years.
