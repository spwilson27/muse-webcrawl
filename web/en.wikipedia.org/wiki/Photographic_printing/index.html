Photographic printing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Prints in developing trays in a darkroom.

Photographic printing is the process of producing a final image on paper for viewing, using chemically sensitized paper. The paper is exposed to a photographic negative or a positive transparency (or slide) using an enlarger, or to digital image data using a laser or LED light source, and is then processed with photographic chemicals to produce a visible, stable print.

Black-and-white printing

In traditional black-and-white printing, an enlarger projects the negative onto silver-halide paper in a darkroom lit by a safelight. Exposure is controlled by time and aperture, and tonal adjustments are made by dodging (lightening) and burning in (darkening) selected areas. The exposed print is developed, stopped, fixed, and washed. Paper grades or variable-contrast filters control image contrast.

Colour printing

Colour prints are made on paper coated with three emulsion layers sensitive to red, green, and blue light, and processed in chromogenic chemistry (the RA-4 process for prints from colour negatives). Historically, dye-transfer and Cibachrome (Ilfochrome) processes were used for high-end work. Since the 1990s most commercial printing has been done by digital minilabs that expose conventional colour paper with lasers from digital files.

Alternative processes

Historic and alternative printing processes include salt prints, albumen prints, platinum and palladium printing, gum bichromate, and cyanotype, all still practised by fine-art photographers for their distinctive tonal qualities and permanence.
