The Screen Actors Guild (SAG) was an American labor union which represented over 100,000 film and television principal and background performers worldwide. Founded in 1933 during the Great Depression, it was established to combat the onerous contracts and working conditions imposed by the major Hollywood studios. On March 30, 2012, SAG merged with the American Federation of Television and Radio Artists (AFTRA) to form SAG-AFTRA.

History

Actors in the early studio system were bound by long-term option contracts with excessive hours and no residuals. A group of actors including Berton Churchill, Grant Mitchell and Ralph Morgan founded SAG in 1933; Eddie Cantor became its first president, and the Guild gained recognition from the studios in 1937 after a threatened strike. The Guild negotiated the first residuals for television reruns (1960 strike, led by president Ronald Reagan) and for films sold to television. SAG also enforced "Rule One" (members may not work for non-signatory producers) and administered franchise agreements with talent agents.

Functions

SAG negotiated collective bargaining agreements setting minimum pay ("scale"), working conditions, and residual payments for work in film and television; it policed member professional names (no two members could share a name, which is why many actors adopted stage names); and it presented the Screen Actors Guild Awards. Its health and pension plans were funded by employer contributions.

Merger

After earlier failed attempts, members of SAG and AFTRA voted in March 2012 to merge, creating SAG-AFTRA, which represents actors, broadcasters, recording artists and other media performers.
