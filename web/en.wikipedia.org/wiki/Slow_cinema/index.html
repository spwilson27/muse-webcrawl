Slow cinema

Slow cinema is a genre of art cinema characterised by a style of long takes, little or no narrative, and a pronounced emphasis on contemplation and observation. The term emerged in criticism in the early 2000s to describe a tendency in world cinema reacting against the accelerating editing pace of commercial filmmaking, itself sometimes measured against the falling average shot lengths documented by film scholar David Bordwell and statistician Barry Salt.

Characteristics

Slow films favour extremely long shots, minimal dialogue, static or slowly moving cameras, everyday subject matter and durations that dwell on landscape and duration itself. Narrative, if present, is attenuated; boredom and contemplation are part of the intended aesthetic experience, echoing André Bazin's defence of the long take and deep focus.

Filmmakers

Directors most associated with the movement include Béla Tarr (whose Sátántangó runs over seven hours), Tsai Ming-liang, Apichatpong Weerasethakul, Lav Diaz, Carlos Reygadas, Pedro Costa, Hou Hsiao-hsien and Lisandro Alonso. Precursors include Andrei Tarkovsky, Michelangelo Antonioni, Chantal Akerman (Jeanne Dielman, 1975), Theo Angelopoulos and Yasujirō Ozu.

Criticism

The movement has been debated fiercely: Nick James's 2010 Sight and Sound editorial criticising festival "slow" films as passive and self-indulgent provoked responses from critics including Jonathan Romney, touching off a wider argument about difficulty, attention and cinephilia in the digital age.
