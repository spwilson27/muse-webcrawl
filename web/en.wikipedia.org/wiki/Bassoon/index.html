Bassoon

Bassoon
ClassificationWoodwind, double reed, aerophone
RangeB♭1 to E5 (approx.)
Related instrumentsContrabassoon, oboe, dulcian
SystemsHeckel (German), Buffet (French)

The bassoon is a woodwind instrument in the double reed family, which plays in the tenor and bass ranges. It is known for its distinctive tone colour, wide range, versatility, and virtuosity. Appearing in its modern form in the early 18th century, the bassoon figures prominently in orchestral, concert band, and chamber music literature, and is occasionally heard in pop, rock, and jazz.

A modern bassoon (Heckel system).

Construction

The bassoon disassembles into six main pieces, including the very long double reed and a curved metal tube called the crook or bocal. Its tube is about 2.5 metres (8 feet) long, folded back on itself within a body usually made of maple, and it is played held diagonally across the body. Two fingering systems survive: the German (Heckel) system used by most of the world, and the French (Buffet) system, retained mainly in France and a few other countries, with a somewhat different timbre.

Repertoire

The bassoon features in famous orchestral solos: the opening of Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring, the grandfather in Prokofiev's Peter and the Wolf, and the waddling theme in Dukas's The Sorcerer's Apprentice. Major concertos include works by Vivaldi (who wrote 39), Mozart, Weber, Saint-Saëns and Gordon Jacob. The instrument's expressive bass voice has earned it the nickname "the clown of the orchestra" for its comic potential in staccato passages.

History

The bassoon descends from the dulcian, a one-piece Renaissance instrument. Baroque bassoons had only a few keys; the modern Heckel instrument, developed in 19th-century Germany by Johann Adam Heckel and Wilhelm Heckel, standardised the bore and keywork used today.
