Art rock

Art rock is a subgenre of rock music that generally reflects a challenging or avant-garde approach to rock, emphasising experimentation, conceptual ambitions, and influences from classical music, jazz, and the avant-garde. The term is often used interchangeably with progressive rock, though art rock is sometimes distinguished as more song-oriented and atmospheric, drawing on art-school culture and studio innovation rather than virtuoso display alone.

Art rock performances often emphasised costume, staging, and concept.

History

The mid-1960s work of the Beatles (Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band, 1967), the Beach Boys (Pet Sounds, 1966), and Frank Zappa helped redefine the album as an artistic statement. The Velvet Underground, managed by Andy Warhol, fused rock with avant-garde art practice, while British bands including Pink Floyd, Roxy Music, and the solo work of Brian Eno and David Bowie exemplified an art-school sensibility — music conceived alongside fashion, film, and performance. In the 1970s, acts such as Genesis, Yes, and King Crimson overlapped with progressive rock; Kate Bush and Peter Gabriel carried art-rock approaches into the pop charts. Punk reacted against art rock's perceived pretension, yet post-punk and later alternative acts (Talking Heads, Radiohead) revived its experimental ambitions.

Characteristics

Art rock commonly features concept albums, unusual time signatures or song structures, literary or philosophical lyrics, studio effects as composition tools, and visual presentation treated as part of the artwork.
