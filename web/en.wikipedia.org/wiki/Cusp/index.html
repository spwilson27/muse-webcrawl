Cusp

[Image: The curve y² = x³ with a cusp at the origin]

Cusp (Latin cuspis, a point) most commonly refers to a pointed end or sharp transition. This article describes the mathematical concept; the word is also used for the pointed tips of heart valves and teeth, for the horned points of the crescent moon, and in architecture and astrology.

Mathematics

In geometry, a cusp is a point on a curve where the curve changes direction abruptly and the tangent reverses, so that the two branches of the curve meet with a common tangent line. The standard example is the semicubical parabola y² = x³, which has a cusp at the origin. Cusps are classified in singularity theory; the cusp catastrophe is one of René Thom's elementary catastrophes and appears in optics as the bright point at the centre of a caustic curve, such as the light pattern in a coffee cup.

Differential geometry of surfaces

In the theory of surfaces, cusps occur on pseudospherical surfaces and in the geometry of hyperbolic 3-manifolds, where a "cusp" is an end of the manifold shaped like a torus cross-section expanding to infinite volume — a usage important in the study of knot complements.

Other uses

In anatomy, cusps are the pointed flaps of the tricuspid and mitral heart valves and the chewing points of molar teeth. In astrology, the cusp is the boundary between two signs or houses. Figuratively, to be "on the cusp" is to be at the point of transition between two states.
