Ball culture — also called the ballroom scene, house-ballroom culture or drag ball culture — is an underground LGBTQ subculture that originated in New York City, in which participants compete ("walk") in elaborate events called balls for trophies, prizes and prestige. Created primarily by working-class Black and Latino queer people excluded from the wider society and from white-dominated drag pageantry, ball culture developed its own kinship system of houses, its own competitive categories, and its own language — terms such as voguing, reading, shade, realness and fierce that have since entered mainstream culture.

History

Drag balls in Harlem date to the 19th century; by the 1920s–30s, events such as the Hamilton Lodge Ball drew thousands, including spectators from downtown Manhattan society. In the 1960s–70s, Black queens such as Crystal LaBeija, protesting racist judging in the downtown pageant circuit, founded the first houses — the House of LaBeija (c. 1972) — and staged their own balls. Through the 1980s the scene flourished in Harlem even as the HIV/AIDS epidemic devastated it, and it spread to cities across the United States and, later, worldwide.

Houses

Houses are alternative families, usually led by a "mother" and/or "father", who provide guidance, shelter and belonging to their "children", many of whom were rejected by their biological families. Famous houses include LaBeija, Xtravaganza, Ninja (founded by voguing pioneer Willi Ninja), Ebony and Mizrahi. Members take the house name as a surname and compete under its banner.

Balls and categories

Competitors walk in categories judged on dance, costume, appearance and attitude — for example Executive Realness, Femme Queen Runway, Old Way/New Way voguing, Face, and Butch Queen up in Drag. "Realness" categories reward the ability to pass within straight, cisgender society — at once a satire of and a survival skill for a hostile world. Judges score on a "tens across the board" scale; contestants can be "chopped" (eliminated). The dance style voguing — angular poses drawn from fashion-magazine models, mixed with acrobatics and dips — is ballroom's signature art form.

Influence and media

Jennie Livingston's documentary Paris Is Burning (1990) brought the scene to international attention, as did Madonna's 1990 hit "Vogue", which employed ballroom dancers and choreographers. The FX series Pose (2018–2021), set in the 1980s–90s ballroom world, won wide acclaim and featured the largest cast of transgender actors in series-regular roles on television. Ballroom slang and aesthetics pervade contemporary pop, fashion and internet culture, and competitions such as the kiki scene continue the tradition for younger generations.
