Ronnie Davis (born Ronald Davis, 1937) is an American abstract painter associated with the shaped-canvas and abstract illusionism movements of the 1960s and 1970s. He is known for geometric paintings executed in polyester resin and fibreglass that create optical effects of three-dimensional space on a flat picture plane, including illusory torqued planes, cubes and snapped perspectives.

Career

Davis was born in Cheyenne, Wyoming, and studied at the University of Wyoming and the San Francisco Art Institute before settling in Los Angeles in the early 1960s. In 1966 he exhibited at the Nicholas Wilder Gallery in Los Angeles and showed in the landmark exhibition American Sculpture of the Sixties at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art. His dodecagon and other polygonal resin paintings — poured in moulds to produce hard-edged, single-image fields with a distinctive illusion of depth — attracted national attention while he was still in his twenties.

Work and later career

In the 1970s Davis moved to New Mexico, living near Arroyo Hondo and Taos, and his painting evolved through successive series — including the Circles, Flowers, and later digitally assisted works — while remaining concerned with colour, geometry and spatial illusion. In the 1990s and 2000s he experimented with computer-generated imagery printed on canvas and with shaped supports fabricated from aluminium. Retrospective attention has situated his resin paintings within postwar California art, alongside Finish Fetish and Light and Space contemporaries, though his work differs from those movements in its reliance on illusionistic depiction rather than literal surface.

Collections

His paintings are held by institutions including the Museum of Modern Art, New York; the Whitney Museum of American Art; the Los Angeles County Museum of Art; the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art; and Tate.
