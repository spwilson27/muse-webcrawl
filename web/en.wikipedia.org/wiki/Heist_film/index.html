Heist film

A heist film or caper film is a subgenre of crime film in which a group of criminals plan, execute, and attempt to get away with an elaborate robbery. A characteristic structure follows the preparation and planning of the crime, the assembly of a team of specialists, the execution of the theft, and frequently a series of twists, betrayals or near-failures. Attention is often given to the cleverness of the plan and the professional skills of the gang.

Characteristics

Heist films typically devote substantial screen time to the logistics of the crime: surveillance, timing, equipment and the rehearsal of the plan. Team members usually have defined specialities — for example a planner, a safecracker, a driver, a demolitions expert and an inside contact. Many of the films hinge on the "one last job" of a veteran criminal, and tension often comes from internal conflicts, double-crosses, or the intervention of the police.

History

The genre crystallised in the 1950s with films influenced by film noir, including John Huston's The Asphalt Jungle (1950), Jules Dassin's Rififi (1955), and Stanley Kubrick's The Killing (1956). The slick ensemble caper became popular in the 1960s with Ocean's 11 (1960). Later notable examples include The Italian Job (1969), Dog Day Afternoon (1975), Reservoir Dogs (1992), Heat (1995), the Ocean's Eleven remake trilogy (2001–2007), Inside Man (2006), Inception (2010) and Baby Driver (2017).
