Sephardic Jews (also Sephardi Jews or Sephardim) are a Jewish diaspora population associated with the Iberian Peninsula (Spain and Portugal). The term derives from Sepharad, a biblical place name identified in Jewish tradition with Spain. Sephardic culture flourished in medieval Islamic and Christian Spain, producing figures such as the philosopher Maimonides (who, though born in Córdoba, is often claimed by the Sephardic tradition) and a rich body of poetry, philosophy, and halakhic literature.

Expulsion and diaspora

The Alhambra Decree of 1492 expelled practising Jews from Spain, and Portugal followed with forced conversions in 1497. Refugees dispersed across North Africa, Italy, the Netherlands, and above all the Ottoman Empire — where Sultan Bayezid II welcomed them — establishing major communities in Salonica, Istanbul, Safed, and Jerusalem. In exile they preserved Judaeo-Spanish (Ladino), printed books, and developed distinctive liturgy, music, and cuisine. Others, the conversos or "New Christians", remained in Iberia under suspicion of secret Judaism and later founded communities in Amsterdam, Hamburg, London, and the Americas.

Modern era

The Balkan and Greek Sephardic heartlands were devastated in the Holocaust — Salonica's ancient community was almost entirely murdered. Today most Sephardim live in Israel, where "Sephardi" is also used loosely for Jews from Middle Eastern and North African countries (Mizrahim), as well as in France, the United States, and Latin America. In 2015 Spain and Portugal enacted laws offering citizenship to descendants of the expelled Sephardim as a measure of historical redress.
