Kayaking

Kayaking is the use of a kayak for moving over water. It is distinguished from canoeing by the sitting position of the paddler and the number of blades on the paddle: a kayaker sits facing forward with legs in front, using a double-bladed paddle to pull and push alternately on each side. Most kayaks have closed decks, although sit-on-top and inflatable kayaks are growing in popularity.

History

Kayaks (Inuktitut: qajaq) were developed by the Indigenous peoples of the Arctic — Inuit, Yupik and Aleut hunters — who stretched sealskin or other hides over frames of wood and bone for hunting on inland lakes, rivers and the sea. Europeans encountered them in Greenland and brought examples home; in the 19th century the craft was adapted for sport, and John MacGregor's Rob Roy canoes popularized paddling travel. Folding kayaks appeared in the early 1900s, fibreglass boats after the Second World War, and rotationally moulded polyethylene transformed the sport from the 1970s.

Types

Kayaking spans many disciplines: recreational paddling on calm water; sea kayaking on coasts and open water, often with rudders or skegs and sealed bulkheads; whitewater kayaking on rapids graded I–VI, including playboating and creeking; kayak touring and expedition camping; surf kayaking; and competitive racing — sprint and slalom events have been Olympic sports (slalom from 1972, permanently from 1992), joined recently by kayak cross. Kayak fishing and packrafting are popular variants.

Technique and safety

Core skills include the forward stroke, sweep and draw strokes, bracing, edging, and the Eskimo roll for righting a capsized kayak without exiting. Whitewater and sea kayakers wear spray decks, helmets and buoyancy aids as appropriate; hazards include cold water, strainers and weirs (low-head dams), and offshore winds, so instruction, appropriate clothing and float plans are widely recommended.
