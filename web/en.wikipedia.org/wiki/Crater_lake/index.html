Crater lake

A crater lake is a lake that forms in a volcanic crater or caldera, filled by precipitation, snowmelt, groundwater, or — in active volcanic areas — hydrothermal fluids. Crater lakes are often strikingly coloured and, when they overlie active vents, highly acidic.

Crater Lake, Oregon, with Wizard Island.

Formation

After an eruption empties or collapses a volcano's summit, the resulting closed depression has no outlet; if inflow exceeds evaporation and seepage, a lake accumulates. Crater Lake in Oregon — the deepest lake in the United States at 594 m — formed in the caldera of Mount Mazama after its climactic eruption about 7,700 years ago and is fed entirely by snow and rain, giving it famously clear, intensely blue water.

Notable examples

  • Lake Toba, Indonesia — the world's largest volcanic lake, in the caldera of a supervolcano eruption ~74,000 years ago.
  • Lake Taupo, New Zealand — caldera of the Hatepe eruption (c. 232 AD).
  • Lakes Nyos and Monoun, Cameroon — sites of limnic eruptions (1986 and 1984) in which dissolved carbon dioxide suddenly erupted, suffocating over 1,700 people at Nyos.
  • Kawah Ijen, Indonesia — a turquoise, hyper-acidic crater lake beside sulphur mining.
  • Lake Batur, Mount Pinatubo's crater lake, and Crater Lake (Oregon) — popular scenic sites.

Hazards

Crater lakes on active volcanoes can be expelled during eruptions, generating destructive mudflows (lahars) — as at Mount Ruapehu, New Zealand — and their acidic waters are monitored as indicators of volcanic unrest. Degassing pipes have been installed at Lakes Nyos and Monoun to prevent a repeat of the limnic disasters.
