Stewart platform

A Stewart platform is a type of parallel manipulator that uses an octahedral assembly of linear actuators connected between a fixed base and a movable platform. It has six degrees of freedom — three translational (surge, sway, heave) and three rotational (roll, pitch, yaw) — and is prized for its high stiffness, load capacity, and positioning accuracy relative to serial-link robots.

History

The mechanism was described by D. Stewart in a landmark 1965 paper presented to the UK Institution of Mechanical Engineers, where he proposed it as a flight simulator. Independently, inventor Eric Gough had developed a similar octahedral hexapod in the 1950s at the Dunlop Rubber Company for testing aircraft tyres, so the device is sometimes called the Gough–Stewart platform. Klaus Cappel later patented related designs used in flight simulation.

Kinematics

Each of the six legs connects a base joint to a platform joint through a prismatic (extending) actuator, typically with universal or spherical joints at each end. The inverse kinematics problem — computing leg lengths for a desired platform pose — is straightforward and closed-form. The forward kinematics problem — computing the pose from leg lengths — is much harder, with up to 40 possible solutions, and generally requires numerical methods.

Applications

Stewart platforms are widely used in flight simulators and driving simulators, where the platform carries an aircraft cockpit replica and reproduces accelerations and attitudes. Other applications include machine-tool positioning (hexapod mills), telescope primary-mirror alignment, robotic surgery and precision manipulation, satellite docking simulation, offshore crane and wave compensation, and amusement rides. The AMiBA radio telescope and many motion-base simulators are notable large examples.

Advantages and limitations

Because the actuators work in parallel, loads are shared, errors do not accumulate as in serial chains, and the moving mass is low, giving excellent dynamic performance. The drawbacks are a restricted workspace, complex calibration, singularities within the workspace where stiffness is lost, and interference between legs at extreme poses.
