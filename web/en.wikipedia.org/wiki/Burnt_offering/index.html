Burnt offering

Hebrewʿolah (עֹלָה)
Greekholokauston (ὁλόκαυστον)
SourcesLeviticus 1; Numbers 28–29

A burnt offering wholly consumed by fire on the altar.

A burnt offering is a form of sacrifice in which the sacrificial victim is completely consumed by fire. In the Hebrew Bible the burnt offering is called the ʿolah, "that which goes up", referring to the smoke ascending to God. The Greek term holokauston, "wholly burnt", passed into later languages and is the origin of the word "holocaust" in its original sacrificial sense.

In ancient Israel

Leviticus 1 prescribes the rite: the offerer brought an unblemished male animal — cattle, sheep, goats, or, for the poor, birds — laid a hand on its head, and slaughtered it. Priests sprinkled the blood around the altar, flayed and cut up the carcass, and burned the whole of it on the altar, except for the hide, which went to the priests. A burnt offering was presented every morning and evening at the sanctuary (the tamid), with additional offerings on Sabbaths and festivals.

Other traditions

Burnt sacrifice was widespread in the ancient world. In Greek religion, Olympian gods received portions of animals burned on altars, the smoke carrying the savour to heaven, while the meat was shared by the worshippers. Whole-burnt offerings also appear in Mesopotamian, Phoenician, and early Roman practice, and in the offerings described in Homer and other early literature.
