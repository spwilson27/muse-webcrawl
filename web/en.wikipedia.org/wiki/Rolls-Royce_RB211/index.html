The Rolls-Royce RB211 is a family of high-bypass turbofan engines made by Rolls-Royce. Originally developed for the Lockheed L-1011 TriStar, it entered service in 1972 and was produced in versions generating between about 37,400 and 60,600 pounds of thrust. The RB211 was the first production three-spool turbofan, an architecture that became the basis of the later Rolls-Royce Trent family. Development difficulties — notably the failure of its original Hyfil carbon-fibre fan blades under bird-strike testing, requiring a costly switch to titanium — overran so badly that Rolls-Royce was driven into bankruptcy and nationalisation by the British government in 1971.

Development

Rolls-Royce began the RB211 in the mid-1960s for the new generation of wide-body airliners. Its three concentric shafts allow the fan, intermediate compressor and high-pressure compressor to each rotate at their optimum speeds, giving high efficiency and good handling. The composite fan blade problem, fixed costs of the TriStar contract and development overruns led to the company's collapse in February 1971; the aero-engine business was taken into state ownership as Rolls-Royce (1971) Ltd, and the engine was completed with government backing. The RB211-22 powered the TriStar from 1972.

Variants and applications

The more powerful RB211-524 powered versions of the Boeing 747, including the 747-400, as well as the Lockheed L-1011-250 and the Boeing 767-300. The smaller RB211-535, the launch engine for the Boeing 757, was notable as the first Rolls-Royce engine selected by Boeing for a new type and for its service with airlines worldwide; a variant also powered the Russian Tupolev Tu-204. The industrial and marine derivatives of the RB211 core are used for power generation and naval propulsion. Lessons from the RB211's three-spool layout were carried into the Trent 700, 800 and later engines.

Specifications (RB211-524G/H)

  • Type: three-spool, high-bypass turbofan
  • Thrust: about 60,600 lbf (269.6 kN) for the -524H
  • Fan diameter: 86.3 in (2.19 m)
  • Bypass ratio: about 4.4:1; overall pressure ratio about 33:1
