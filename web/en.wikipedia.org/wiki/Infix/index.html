Infix

An infix is an affix inserted inside an existing word or word root. It contrasts with prefixes (added before), suffixes (added after) and circumfixes (added around). Infixes are relatively rare in Indo-European languages but are common in Austronesian languages and in some Native American and Australian languages.

Examples

In Tagalog, the infix -um- marks actor focus in verbs: sulat "writing" becomes sumulat "wrote (actor focus)", and -in- marks object focus, as in sinulat "was written". In Khmer and other Austroasiatic languages, infixes derive nouns from verbs. In Latin, a nasal infix -n- appears in certain present-tense stems, as in vincō "I conquer" from the root vic- (compare vīcī "I conquered").

English expletive infixation

English lacks true grammatical infixes, but speakers productively insert expletives inside words for emphasis — abso-bloody-lutely, fan-freaking-tastic — a process linguists call expletive infixation. Its placement obeys prosodic rules: the expletive is inserted before the stressed syllable. Similar processes insert words like "-ma-" in Homer Simpson's "saxo-ma-phone" in popular culture.

In chemistry and computing

The term is also used outside linguistics: in chemistry for inserted elements in nomenclature, and in computer science, "infix notation" is the familiar placement of operators between operands, as in a + b.
