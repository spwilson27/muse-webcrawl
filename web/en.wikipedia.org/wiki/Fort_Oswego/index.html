Fort Oswego

Fort Oswego was an 18th-century British trading post and fortification on the shore of Lake Ontario at the mouth of the Oswego River, in present-day Oswego, New York. Established in 1727, it anchored the British fur trade with the Iroquois and became a strategic objective in the French and Indian War.

History

New York traders began operating at Oswego in the 1720s, exchanging goods with Native nations and undercutting French traders at Niagara. Governor William Burnet ordered a stone trading house, Fort Oswego, completed in 1727. During King George's War additional defenses were planned, and two further works — Fort George and Fort Ontario — were begun nearby in the 1750s.

In August 1756, during the French and Indian War, the Marquis de Montcalm led a French and Native force against the Oswego forts. After a short siege, the British commander Colonel James Mercer was killed and the garrison surrendered; the French destroyed the forts and withdrew. The Battle of Fort Oswego was an important early French victory in the war.

British forces later rebuilt Fort Ontario on the east bank, which remained the principal fortification at the site through the American Revolution and the War of 1812, and a U.S. garrison remained into the 20th century. Fort Ontario is now a New York State Historic Site.
