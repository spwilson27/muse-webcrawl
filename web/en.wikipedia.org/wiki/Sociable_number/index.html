Sociable number

Sociable numbers are numbers in number theory whose aliquot sums form a repeating cycle of length greater than two. They generalise the concepts of perfect numbers (cycles of length 1) and amicable numbers (cycles of length 2). For a positive integer n, the aliquot sum s(n) is the sum of the proper divisors of n. Iterating this function produces an aliquot sequence; if the sequence eventually returns to its starting value after k > 2 steps, the numbers in the cycle are called sociable numbers of order (or period) k.

Example

The best-known sociable cycle is the five-number chain discovered by Paul Poulet in 1918:

12496 → 14288 → 15472 → 14536 → 14264 → (back to 12496)

That is, the proper divisors of 12496 sum to 14288; the proper divisors of 14288 sum to 15472; and so on, until the proper divisors of 14264 sum to 12496 again.

A famous longer example is the 28-cycle beginning with 14316, also published by Poulet in 1918:

14316 → 19116 → 31704 → 47616 → 83328 → 177792 → 295488 → 629072 → 589786 → 294896 → 358336 → 418904 → 366556 → 274924 → 275444 → 243760 → 376736 → 381028 → 285778 → 152990 → 122410 → 97946 → 48976 → 45946 → 22976 → 22744 → 19916 → 17716 → (back to 14316).

Known cycles

Thousands of sociable cycles are now known, most of them of length 4. Cycles of many other lengths have been found, including lengths 5, 6, 8, 9 and 28. Notably, no cycle of length 3 (and none of length 7) is known, and whether any exists is an open problem. Searches for sociable cycles are related to the Catalan–Dickson conjecture, which asserts that every aliquot sequence either terminates at a prime and then 1, or enters a cycle (perfect, amicable or sociable). Conversely, Guy and Selfridge conjectured that many aliquot sequences grow without bound.
