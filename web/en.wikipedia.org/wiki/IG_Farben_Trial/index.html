IG Farben Trial

IG Farben Trial
Officially: United States v. Carl Krauch et al.
Dates: 27 Aug 1947 – 30 Jul 1948
Venue: Nuremberg, Palace of Justice
Defendants: 24 (23 tried)

The IG Farben Trial was the sixth of the twelve 'Subsequent Nuremberg trials' for war crimes held by the United States in occupied Germany after the Second World War. Twenty-three directors of IG Farben, the German chemical conglomerate, were charged with planning aggressive war, war crimes and crimes against humanity — including plunder in occupied countries and the use of slave labour from concentration camps.

Indictment and proceedings

Counts included preparing aggressive war in alliance with the Nazi regime; plundering occupied France, Poland and elsewhere; and mass murder and enslavement through the Buna plant at Monowitz (Auschwitz III), built beside the camp and worked by prisoners, thousands of whom died. Farben subsidiaries also supplied Zyklon B via Degesch. The defence argued duress, lack of knowledge and that rearmament was not itself criminal.

Judgment

On 30 July 1948, thirteen defendants were convicted, mostly on the plunder and slave-labour counts, with sentences from one-and-a-half to eight years; ten were acquitted, including on the aggressive-war count, the tribunal finding the executives were neither policymakers nor initiators of war. All convicted men were released by the early 1950s amid Cold War clemency, and several resumed prominent business careers. See Subsequent Nuremberg trials.
