Matrix (mathematics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A rectangular array of numbers in brackets, with rows and columns labelled]

In mathematics, a matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, symbols, or expressions arranged in rows and columns. Matrices are used to represent linear transformations and systems of linear equations, and matrix algebra — addition, multiplication, determinants, inverses, eigenvalues — is the computational core of linear algebra.

The term was coined by James Joseph Sylvester in 1850; Arthur Cayley's 1858 memoir founded matrix theory, though equivalent arrays were used earlier in China (the Nine Chapters) and by Gauss for elimination.

Operations and theory

Matrices of the same size add entry-wise; multiplication (defined when dimensions match) is associative but not commutative, and encodes composition of linear maps. A square matrix may have a determinant and (if the determinant is non-zero) an inverse; its eigenvalues and eigenvectors describe its action along special directions, powering applications from stability analysis to PageRank.

Special classes include symmetric, orthogonal, Hermitian, diagonal, and sparse matrices; decompositions (LU, QR, singular value decomposition) underpin numerical computation.

Applications

Graphics and robotics (transformations), quantum mechanics (operators), statistics (covariance matrices), economics (input–output models), and machine learning — where data, weights, and transformations are matrices and tensors — all run on matrix computation, increasingly on GPUs.
