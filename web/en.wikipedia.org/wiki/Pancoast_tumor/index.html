Pancoast tumor

A Pancoast tumor, also called a superior sulcus tumor, is a tumor of the apex of the lung that arises in the superior pulmonary sulcus. Most Pancoast tumors are non-small-cell lung cancers, typically squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma. Because of their location, they invade adjacent structures — the parietal pleura, first and second ribs, brachial plexus, sympathetic chain, and vertebrae — producing a characteristic set of symptoms known as Pancoast syndrome. The tumors are named for radiologist Henry Pancoast, who described them in the 1920s and 1930s.

Signs and symptoms

The classic presentation is shoulder pain radiating down the arm in the distribution of the ulnar nerve, caused by invasion of the lower brachial plexus. Other features include atrophy of hand muscles, destruction of the first and second ribs, and Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) from invasion of the cervical sympathetic chain and stellate ganglion. Unlike most lung cancers, cough and breathlessness may be absent early because the tumor is peripheral.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by chest imaging (CT and MRI, the latter best defining invasion of the plexus and spine), followed by biopsy, often via CT-guided needle sampling. Once considered incurable, Pancoast tumors are now treated with combined chemoradiation followed by surgical resection (trimodality therapy), which substantially improved survival in suitable patients. Surgery aims for complete en-bloc removal of the tumor with involved chest wall and structures.
