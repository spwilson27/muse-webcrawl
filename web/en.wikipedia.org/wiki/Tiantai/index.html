Tiantai (Chinese: 天台, "Heavenly Terrace") is a major school of Mahayana Buddhism that developed in 6th-century China, systematised by the monk Zhiyi (538–597) on Mount Tiantai in present-day Zhejiang. It is also known as the Lotus school because of its central devotion to the Lotus Sutra, which it regards as the supreme and complete teaching of the Buddha. Transmitted to Japan by Saichō, it became the Tendai school; in Korea it is known as Cheontae.

Doctrine

Tiantai is noted for its comprehensive classification of the Buddha's teachings (panjiao), which reconciles the many sutras as progressive expressions of a single truth, culminating in the Lotus Sutra's teaching that all beings can attain Buddhahood — the doctrine of the "One Vehicle". Zhiyi's metaphysics centres on the Threefold Truth: phenomena are empty, they exist provisionally, and they are simultaneously both (the middle). His celebrated formula of "three thousand realms in a single thought-moment" (ichinen sanzen) holds that each moment of experience contains the full range of possible states of existence.

Practice

Zhiyi's meditation manual, the Mohe Zhiguan ("Great Cessation and Insight"), systematised zhiguan practice — the paired cultivation of calming (śamatha) and insight (vipaśyanā) — which became one of the most influential meditation treatises in East Asian Buddhism.

History and influence

The school flourished under the Sui and Tang dynasties, declined amid the persecutions of the 9th century and competition from Chan and Pure Land, and was revived in the Song dynasty. In Japan, Tendai became the dominant institutional form of Buddhism for centuries and the matrix from which the Kamakura schools — Pure Land, Zen, and Nichiren — emerged, their founders having all trained on Mount Hiei.
