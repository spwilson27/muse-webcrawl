Labor Day is a federal holiday in the United States celebrated on the first Monday of September to honor and recognize the American labor movement and the works and contributions of laborers to the development and achievements of the United States. It is the Monday of the long weekend known as the Labor Day weekend, which traditionally marks the unofficial end of summer. Canada's Labour Day is also celebrated on the first Monday of September.

History

The first Labor Day parade took place in New York City on 5 September 1882, organised by the Central Labor Union, and involved some 10,000 workers marching for the eight-hour day and other labour demands. Credit for proposing the holiday is variously given to machinist Matthew Maguire and carpenter Peter J. McGuire. Oregon was the first state to make it an official public holiday in 1887. In 1894, in the aftermath of the Pullman Strike, President Grover Cleveland signed legislation making Labor Day a national holiday — a timing often interpreted as an effort to placate organised labour.

Observance

The holiday is marked by parades, cookouts, community events and retail sales. Historically, it also served as a fashion deadline — the origin of the rule against wearing white after Labor Day — and as the traditional start of the school year in many districts, although many schools now begin earlier. Most other countries celebrate workers on International Workers' Day, 1 May.
