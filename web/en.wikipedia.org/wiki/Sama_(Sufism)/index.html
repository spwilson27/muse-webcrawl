Sama (Arabic: سَمَاع, samāʿ, "listening") is a Sufi ceremony performed as a form of dhikr (remembrance of God), in which music, sung poetry, and ritual movement are used to induce spiritual concentration and ecstasy. The practice is most famously embodied in the whirling ceremony of the Mevlevi Order founded by the followers of the 13th-century Persian poet and mystic Jalal al-Din Rumi, whose members are known in the West as "whirling dervishes."

Practice

In the Mevlevi sema, dervishes in tall camel-hair hats (sikke) and flowing white robes whirl continuously with the right palm turned upward to receive divine blessing and the left palm downward to convey it to the earth, orbiting the hall and their sheikh as planets orbit the sun. The ceremony (mukabele) is structured in sections, accompanied by a musical ensemble of reed flute (ney), frame drums, and singers performing compositions that frame sung poems of Rumi and Sultan Veled. More broadly, sama in many orders denotes sessions of listening to sung devotional poetry without whirling, including in South Asian Sufi practice, where it is closely related to qawwali.

Debate and symbolism

The lawfulness of music and movement in worship has been debated within Islam for centuries. Defenders such as al-Ghazali argued in the Ihya Ulum al-Din that audition is permissible when the listener's state and the content are fitting, regarding sama as a means of kindling love of God; stricter jurists condemned it as an innovation and a distraction. Adherents interpret whirling as symbolising the rotation of the universe, the abandonment of ego, and the soul's journey toward union with the Divine.

In 1925 the Turkish Republic closed the Mevlevi lodges, though the ceremony was later permitted as a cultural performance; UNESCO proclaimed the Mevlevi Sema Ceremony a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005.
