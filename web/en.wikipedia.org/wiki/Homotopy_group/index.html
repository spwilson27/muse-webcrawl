In mathematics, homotopy groups are used in algebraic topology to classify topological spaces. The first and simplest homotopy group is the fundamental group, denoted π1, which records information about loops in a space. Intuitively, homotopy groups record information about the basic shape, or holes, of a topological space.

The n-th homotopy group πn(X) of a space X is defined as the set of homotopy classes of continuous maps from an n-dimensional sphere Sn to X that send a chosen basepoint to a basepoint in X, equipped with a group operation given by "pinching" the sphere along an equator. For n ≥ 2 these groups are always abelian.

Definition

Fix a basepoint x0 in X. Then πn(X, x0) is the set of homotopy classes rel basepoint of maps (Sn, s0) → (X, x0). The group operation glues two spheres together along an equator in one coordinate, generalising the concatenation of loops for n = 1. Continuous maps between spaces induce group homomorphisms between their homotopy groups, making πn a functor.

Examples and computation

π1 of the circle is ℤ, since loops are classified by winding number; πn of a point, or of any contractible space, is trivial for all n. The higher homotopy groups of spheres are famously difficult: π3(S2) = ℤ, generated by the Hopf fibration, and in general πn+k(Sn) stabilises for large n to the stable homotopy groups of spheres, computed in part by spectral sequences.

Long exact sequence of a fibration

For a fibration F → E → B there is a long exact sequence relating the homotopy groups of fibre, total space, and base, one of the main computational tools of the subject. The Hurewicz theorem connects the first non-trivial homotopy group of a simply connected space with its homology.

History

The fundamental group goes back to Poincaré's Analysis Situs (1895). Higher homotopy groups were defined by Witold Hurewicz in 1935–36, transforming algebraic topology; Heinz Hopf had meanwhile discovered that maps S3 → S2 are classified by an integer, the Hopf invariant.
