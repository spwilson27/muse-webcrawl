Monopoly on violence

The monopoly on violence or monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force is the concept, originating with the sociologist Max Weber, that the state alone has the right to use or authorize the use of physical force within a given territory. In his 1919 lecture "Politics as a Vocation," Weber defined the state as "a human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory."

Weber's theory

For Weber, the monopoly did not mean the state is the only actor that uses violence, but that it is the sole source of the legitimacy of its use: other actors — police-authorized arrests, self-defence, private security — may exercise force only as permitted by the state. The concept became a core definition of the state in political science and sociology.

Development and critiques

Historians and sociologists such as Norbert Elias, in his theory of the civilizing process, and Charles Tilly, who described state formation as resembling organized crime ("war making and state making as organized crime"), have traced how European states gradually monopolised violence between the Middle Ages and the modern era. Critics note that the monopoly is an ideal type: in practice, failed states, civil wars, private military companies, organized crime and non-state armed groups challenge or share the use of force, and philosophers have questioned whether state violence can be equated with legitimate violence.
