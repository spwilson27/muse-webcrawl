Morning Prayer (Anglican)

Morning Prayer, also known as Matins or Mattins, is a service of the Anglican tradition, forming part of the Daily Office together with Evening Prayer. In the Book of Common Prayer it is titled "The Order for Morning Prayer". Thomas Cranmer constructed the office by condensing the medieval monastic hours of Matins, Lauds and Prime into a single service for daily use by clergy and laity alike.

Structure

In the 1662 Book of Common Prayer, the service proceeds as follows:

  • One or more sentences of Scripture, followed by an exhortation, general confession, and absolution.
  • The Lord's Prayer, then the versicles and responses ("O Lord, open thou our lips").
  • The Venite (Psalm 95), followed by the psalms appointed for the day.
  • Two lessons: one from the Old Testament and one from the New, each followed by a canticle — the Te Deum (or Benedicite) after the first lesson and the Benedictus (or Jubilate) after the second.
  • The Apostles' Creed, followed by prayers: the Kyrie, the Lord's Prayer, the suffrages, and the collects of the day, for peace, and for grace; further prayers and, in choral foundations, an anthem.

History and use

Morning Prayer appeared in the first Book of Common Prayer of 1549 and was expanded in 1552. For centuries it was, with Evening Prayer and the Litany, the principal Sunday morning service in most Church of England parishes, Holy Communion being celebrated relatively infrequently; sung Mattins at cathedrals, set to music by generations of composers of Anglican church music, became a distinctive feature of English religious life. Since the mid-twentieth century the Eucharist has largely resumed the central place in Sunday worship, but Morning Prayer remains widely used on weekdays, in schools, and as a service of the word, and its canticles — Te Deum, Benedictus, Benedicite and Jubilate — remain central to the choral repertoire.

The psalmody of the office uses Miles Coverdale's translation of the Psalms, retained in the 1662 book, renowned for its cadence when sung to Anglican chant.
