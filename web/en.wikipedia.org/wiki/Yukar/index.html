Yukar

Yukar (Ainu: ユカル) are Ainu sagas — epic poems and heroic narratives that form the core of the oral literature of the Ainu people of northern Japan. Traditionally performed by both men and women, yukar were recited through the night beside the hearth, often to the accompaniment of a rhythmic striking of the hearth frame with a wooden stick.

Form and content

Yukar are composed in formulaic verse, often in the first person: the narrator speaks as the hero of the tale, whether a culture hero, a deity (kamuy yukar, "songs of the gods") or an animal spirit recounting its own story. This first-person divine narration, framed by a refrain (sakehe) unique to each narrator-spirit, is a distinctive feature of Ainu oral tradition. The heroic epics (yukar in the narrower sense) tell of the culture hero Okikurmi and of wars between the Ainu and their enemies, while shorter sacred chants relate the experiences of animal gods such as the bear and the owl.

Collection and preservation

Because Ainu was traditionally an unwritten language, yukar survived purely by oral transmission. In the early twentieth century the Ainu storyteller and transcriber Yukie Chiri (1903–1922) recorded and translated a collection of kamuy yukar into Japanese, published as Ainu Shin'yōshū ("Collection of Ainu Chants of Spiritual Beings", 1923). The linguist Kyōsuke Kindaichi collected many further texts, and the Ainu narrator Matsu Kannari's performances, transcribed by her son's generation of scholars, preserved dozens of epics. Ainu was recognised by UNESCO as a critically endangered language, and recordings of yukar are central to revitalisation efforts.
