Hyaline cartilage

Hyaline cartilage is the most common type of cartilage in the human body. Named from the Greek hyalos ("glass") for its translucent, bluish-white, glassy appearance, it provides smooth surfaces for joint movement, structural support, and flexibility. It is found covering the ends of bones at synovial joints (where it is called articular cartilage), in the nose, larynx, trachea, and bronchi, in the costal cartilages connecting the ribs to the sternum, and as the embryonic precursor of most of the skeleton.

Structure

Hyaline cartilage consists of chondrocytes embedded in an extracellular matrix of type II collagen fibrils, proteoglycans (chiefly aggrecan), and water. The collagen fibrils are small and have a similar refractive index to the surrounding ground substance, so the matrix appears homogeneous under a light microscope. Chondrocytes sit in spaces called lacunae and often occur in isogenous groups formed by recent cell division. Most hyaline cartilage is covered by a fibrous membrane, the perichondrium; articular cartilage is an exception, lacking a perichondrium and being nourished instead by synovial fluid. Cartilage is avascular and aneural, relying on diffusion for nutrition, which limits its thickness and its capacity for repair.

Development and growth

During endochondral ossification, a hyaline cartilage model of each future bone is progressively replaced by bone tissue, while the epiphyseal (growth) plates of hyaline cartilage allow long bones to lengthen until maturity. Cartilage also grows interstitially from within and appositionally from the perichondrium.

Clinical significance

Damage to articular hyaline cartilage — through trauma or degenerative disease such as osteoarthritis — heals poorly because of the tissue's lack of blood supply, and the repair tissue formed is usually inferior fibrocartilage. Disorders of hyaline cartilage growth plates cause forms of dwarfism such as achondroplasia. Costochondritis is a common, benign inflammation of the costal cartilages.
