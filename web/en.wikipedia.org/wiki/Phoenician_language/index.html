Phoenician (śpt knʿn "language of Canaan", or śpt ʾadm "language of man") is an extinct Canaanite language of the Northwest Semitic family. It was originally spoken in the coastal region of the Levant known in Greek and Latin as Phoenicia, roughly corresponding to modern Lebanon, the coastal parts of Syria, and northern Palestine.

Phoenician spread around the Mediterranean through Phoenician trade and colonization, most famously to Carthage in North Africa, where a distinctive local variety, Punic, continued to be spoken into late antiquity. Phoenician is known from thousands of inscriptions, mostly short and formulaic, found across the Mediterranean world.

History

The Phoenicians were the first state-level society to make extensive use of an alphabet. The Phoenician alphabet, written right-to-left with 22 consonantal letters, is the ancestor of many modern alphabets, including the Greek alphabet (and through it the Latin and Cyrillic alphabets), the Aramaic alphabet, and the Hebrew alphabet.

The language is closely related to Hebrew, Moabite, and other Canaanite languages, and was mutually intelligible with them to a significant degree. Phoenician inscriptions are conventionally divided into homeland inscriptions (from Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, and elsewhere) and colonial inscriptions, including those in Punic from Carthage and its territories.

Phonology and grammar

Phoenician distinguished fewer consonantal phonemes than some related languages, having merged several Proto-Semitic consonants. Like other Semitic languages, it built words on consonantal roots, typically of three consonants. Its grammar closely resembled that of Biblical Hebrew: nouns had masculine and feminine gender, singular, dual, and plural number, and the verb system was based on perfect and imperfect conjugations.

Corpus

The Phoenician corpus consists largely of royal dedications, funerary inscriptions, and votive texts. Notable examples include the Ahiram sarcophagus inscription from Byblos (c. 1000 BC), the Karatepe bilingual (Phoenician and Hieroglyphic Luwian), which aided the decipherment of the Luwian script, and the Eshmunazar sarcophagus inscription from Sidon. Neo-Punic texts were written in a cursive script and later also in Latin letters, famously in a passage of Plautus's comedy Poenulus.
