Soil classification

World distribution of major soil orders

Soil classification deals with the systematic categorization of soils according to distinguishing characteristics and criteria that dictate choices in use. Classifications group soils by the properties of their profiles — horizons, texture, chemistry, mineralogy, moisture and temperature regimes — in order to predict their behavior for agriculture, engineering, and land management.

Major systems

Two international systems dominate. The USDA soil taxonomy, developed by the United States Department of Agriculture (first published in 1975), arranges soils into twelve orders — including Alfisols, Aridisols, Entisols, Histosols, Inceptisols, Mollisols, Oxisols, Spodosols, Ultisols, Vertisols, Andisols, and Gelisols — subdivided into suborders, great groups, subgroups, families, and series, using precisely defined diagnostic horizons and properties.

The FAO/UNESCO World Reference Base for Soil Resources (WRB), successor to the FAO soil classification of 1974 and the Legend of the Soil Map of the World, recognizes 32 reference soil groups such as Chernozems, Podzols, Ferralsols, Luvisols, and Anthrosols. Many countries also keep national systems — the French référentiel pédologique, the Russian system descended from Vasily Dokuchaev's genetic classification, the Australian Soil Classification, and engineering systems such as the Unified Soil Classification System, which classifies soils by grain size and plasticity for construction purposes.

Criteria

Classifications variously rely on measurable properties (particle-size distribution, pH, organic carbon, cation-exchange capacity), on diagnostic horizons (such as the mollic epipedon or argillic horizon), and on formative soil-forming factors — climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time — in the tradition of Dokuchaev and Hans Jenny.
