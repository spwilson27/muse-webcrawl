Social model of disability

The social model of disability identifies systemic barriers, derogatory attitudes and social exclusion as the main contributors to disabling people. It distinguishes impairment — an individual's physical, sensory or psychological difference — from disability, which is the disadvantage imposed on people with impairments by a society that fails to accommodate them. The model emerged from British disability activism in the 1970s, above all the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS), whose 1976 Fundamental Principles of Disability reframed disability as a form of social oppression, and was named and developed academically by Mike Oliver in the 1980s.

Principles

Under the social model, a wheelchair user is disabled not by paraplegia but by buildings with only stairs; a Deaf person is disabled by the absence of sign-language interpretation. The remedy is therefore social and political — removing barriers, changing attitudes and enforcing rights — rather than "fixing" the individual, as in the medical model. The model underpinned campaigns for anti-discrimination legislation such as the UK Disability Discrimination Act 1995 and influenced the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006).

Criticism and developments

Critics, including some disabled scholars such as Tom Shakespeare, argue that the strong British social model neglects the real limiting effects of impairment itself — pain, fatigue and illness — and that impairment and social barriers interact. Alternatives and refinements include the Nordic relational model, the biopsychosocial model embodied in the WHO's International Classification of Functioning (ICF), and critical disability studies approaches that treat the impairment/disability distinction itself as less clean than the original model supposed.
