Painted Grey Ware

The Painted Grey Ware culture (PGW) is an Iron Age Indian culture of the western Gangetic plain and the Ghaggar-Hakra valley in the Indian subcontinent, conventionally dated from roughly 1200 to 600–500 BCE. It is characterised by a fine, smooth, grey pottery painted with geometric patterns in black.

Characteristics

PGW pottery is made of well-levigated clay, wheel-thrown, and fired under reducing conditions to produce its distinctive ash-grey colour. Common forms are bowls and dishes, often on stands, decorated with simple painted motifs — lines, circles, dots, spirals and sigma patterns — in black pigment. The ware appears at more than a thousand recorded sites, including Hastinapura, Ahichchhatra, Mathura, Kurukshetra, Kaushambi, and Bhagwanpura.

Society

The culture is associated with the first large-scale use of iron in northern India, the reintroduction of rice cultivation alongside wheat and barley, horse remains, and village settlements with wattle-and-daub and some mud-brick houses. Its geographical distribution corresponds closely to the Kuru-Panchala realm described in later Vedic literature.

Chronology and successors

The PGW culture was succeeded across the Gangetic plain by the Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) culture from about 700–600 BCE, associated with the rise of the mahajanapadas and early historic cities. Archaeologists debate the relationship between PGW and the late Vedic period; many associate the culture with the painters of the Vedic Kuru kingdom traditions, though such identifications remain contested.
