Saul (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, Šāʾūl, "asked for"; c. 11th century BC) was, according to the Hebrew Bible, the first monarch of the United Kingdom of Israel. His reign, traditionally dated to the late 11th century BC, marked Israel's transition from a tribal confederacy led by judges to a monarchy.

Biblical account

According to the books of Samuel, Saul, a tall and handsome Benjamite, was anointed king by the prophet Samuel after the Israelites demanded a king "like all the nations". He won early victories over the Ammonites, Philistines and Amalekites, but twice disobeyed God's commands — offering sacrifice before battle and sparing King Agag and the Amalekite livestock — after which Samuel announced that God had rejected him and secretly anointed the shepherd David as his successor.

Rivalry with David

David entered Saul's court as a musician and armour-bearer and won fame by killing Goliath. Tormented by an "evil spirit", Saul grew jealous and repeatedly tried to kill David, who fled and became an outlaw. Saul's son Jonathan befriended David, against his father's wishes.

Death

On the eve of his final battle, Saul consulted the Witch of Endor, who summoned Samuel's spirit to foretell his doom. At the Battle of Mount Gilboa the Philistines routed Israel; Jonathan and two other sons were killed, and Saul fell on his own sword rather than be captured. David lamented them in the "Song of the Bow". Saul's surviving son Ish-bosheth briefly ruled Israel before David succeeded to the whole kingdom.

Historicity

No contemporary extra-biblical evidence of Saul has been found; scholars debate how much of the Samuel narrative reflects a historical highland chieftain of the early Iron Age and how much is later literary construction.
