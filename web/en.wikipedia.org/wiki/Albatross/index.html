Albatrosses are large seabirds of the family Diomedeidae, related to petrels and shearwaters in the order Procellariiformes. They range widely across the Southern Ocean and the North Pacific, and include among the largest of all flying birds: the wandering albatross has the greatest wingspan of any living bird, reaching about 3.5 metres.

Flight

Albatrosses are masters of soaring flight, using dynamic soaring and slope soaring to travel thousands of kilometres over the open ocean with scarcely a wingbeat. A shoulder-lock tendon mechanism holds the wing outstretched at little muscular cost, and satellite tracking has shown individual birds circumnavigating the Southern Ocean in a matter of weeks.

Breeding and diet

Albatrosses form long-term, usually lifelong, pair bonds and breed in colonies on remote islands, laying a single egg per attempt; the largest species breed only every second year. They feed mainly on squid, fish and krill taken at the surface, and may live for more than 60 years — the Laysan albatross known as Wisdom, banded in 1956, has bred into her seventies.

Conservation

Most albatross species are threatened, principally by longline fisheries, in which birds are hooked and drowned when scavenging baited lines, and by introduced predators at breeding colonies. The international Agreement on the Conservation of Albatrosses and Petrels (ACAP) coordinates protection. In maritime folklore an albatross was a sign of good luck — a belief immortalised in Coleridge's The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (1798).
