United Nations Special Commission

The United Nations Special Commission (UNSCOM) was a United Nations inspection regime created by Security Council Resolution 687 in April 1991, following the Gulf War, to oversee the elimination of Iraq's weapons of mass destruction and ballistic missiles with a range greater than 150 kilometres, and to establish monitoring to prevent their reconstitution.

Operations

Led first by Rolf Ekéus (1991–1997) and then by Richard Butler (1997–1999), UNSCOM conducted hundreds of inspections across Iraq. Its teams destroyed large quantities of chemical weapons, agent and precursor chemicals, the Al Hakam biological weapons facility, missile launchers and production equipment, and uncovered much of Iraq's clandestine nuclear programme in cooperation with the IAEA. Inspectors faced systematic Iraqi obstruction, concealment and harassment, including standoffs over access to "presidential sites" in 1997–98.

Crises and end

Iraq suspended cooperation in August 1998 and ended it entirely that October. UNSCOM inspectors were withdrawn in December 1998 immediately before the US–UK Operation Desert Fox bombing campaign. Subsequent reporting alleged that some inspection information had been used by national intelligence services, damaging the commission's perceived impartiality. UNSCOM never returned to Iraq; in December 1999 the Security Council replaced it with UNMOVIC under Resolution 1284. After the 2003 invasion, the Iraq Survey Group confirmed that Iraq's major WMD stockpiles had in fact been destroyed or abandoned in the 1990s, though unresolved questions about documentation remained.

UNSCOM is widely regarded as a landmark in intrusive international verification, demonstrating both the feasibility of on-site disarmament under UN authority and the political difficulties of sustaining it against determined resistance.
