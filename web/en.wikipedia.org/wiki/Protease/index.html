Protease

Protease
EC number3.4
Other namesPeptidase, proteinase, proteolytic enzyme
ReactionHydrolysis of peptide bonds
ClassesSerine, cysteine, aspartic, metallo-, threonine, glutamic

A protease (also called a peptidase, proteinase, or proteolytic enzyme) is an enzyme that catalyzes proteolysis, breaking down proteins into smaller polypeptides or single amino acids by hydrolyzing the peptide bonds that link amino acids together. Proteases occur in all organisms, from prokaryotes to eukaryotes and viruses, and are involved in a multitude of physiological reactions ranging from simple digestion of food proteins to highly regulated cascades such as blood clotting, apoptosis, and the complement system.

Classification

Proteases are classified in EC group 3.4 and are sub-divided according to the residue or cofactor essential for catalysis at the active site:

  • Serine proteases — use a serine residue in the catalytic triad (e.g. trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, thrombin, subtilisin).
  • Cysteine proteases — use a cysteine thiol (e.g. papain, cathepsins, caspases).
  • Aspartic proteases — use two aspartate residues and an activated water molecule (e.g. pepsin, renin, HIV-1 protease).
  • Metalloproteases — require a divalent metal ion, usually zinc (e.g. thermolysin, matrix metalloproteinases, angiotensin-converting enzyme).
  • Threonine proteases — use an N-terminal threonine, as in the proteasome.
  • Glutamic proteases — a rarer class first described in fungi.

Proteases are also described as endopeptidases, which cleave internal peptide bonds, or exopeptidases, which remove amino acids from the ends of a chain (aminopeptidases and carboxypeptidases).

Mechanism

Proteases catalyze the hydrolysis of a peptide bond by activating a water molecule or a nucleophilic amino-acid side chain that attacks the carbonyl carbon of the scissile bond. In serine and cysteine proteases a covalent acyl-enzyme intermediate is formed and then hydrolyzed; in aspartic and metalloproteases a water molecule activated by the enzyme attacks the bond directly. Many proteases are synthesized as inactive precursors (zymogens) and activated by proteolytic cleavage, providing tight regulation.

Biological functions

Proteases carry out protein digestion in the stomach and small intestine, activate hormones and growth factors, remodel the extracellular matrix, present antigens, and execute programmed cell death through the caspase family. The ubiquitin–proteasome system uses proteolysis to control the cell cycle and protein quality. Pathogens also exploit proteases: viral proteases such as the HIV-1 protease are essential for maturation of infectious virus particles.

Inhibitors and medicine

Proteins called protease inhibitors regulate protease activity in vivo; examples include serpins such as alpha-1 antitrypsin and the cystatins. Synthetic protease inhibitors are major drugs: HIV protease inhibitors transformed AIDS therapy, ACE inhibitors treat hypertension, and direct thrombin and factor Xa inhibitors are used as anticoagulants.

Uses in industry and research

Proteases are used in laundry detergents, leather processing, food production (cheese making, meat tenderizing), and molecular biology, where sequence-specific proteases such as TEV protease and thrombin are used to remove purification tags.
