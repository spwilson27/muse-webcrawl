Bird anatomy

Bird anatomy, or the physiological structure of birds' bodies, shows many unique adaptations, mostly aiding flight, but birds also show adaptations for their terrestrial and aquatic lifestyles. Birds are warm-blooded (endothermic) vertebrates with feathers, toothless beaked jaws, a high metabolic rate, a four-chambered heart, and a strong yet lightweight skeleton.

Skeletal system

The bird skeleton is highly adapted for flight. Many bones are hollow (pneumatised) with internal struts, and are reinforced by connections to the respiratory system's air sacs. The sternum bears a large keel (carina) anchoring powerful flight muscles. Several bones are fused — including the pygostyle at the tail, the synsacrum in the pelvis, and the carpometacarpus in the wing — providing rigidity. Flightless birds, such as ratites, lack a keeled sternum.

Respiratory system

Birds possess one of the most efficient respiratory systems of any animal. Air flows unidirectionally through rigid lungs via a system of air sacs that act as bellows, so that gas exchange occurs during both inhalation and exhalation. This cross-current exchange supplies the high oxygen demands of flight and endothermy.

Digestive system

Lacking teeth, birds use a beak adapted to their diet. Many species have a crop for food storage and a two-part stomach: the glandular proventriculus and the muscular gizzard, which grinds food, often with swallowed grit. Birds have a cloaca through which digestive, urinary, and reproductive products pass.

Feathers and flight

Feathers are unique to birds and serve for flight, insulation, waterproofing, and display. Flight feathers (remiges on the wings, rectrices on the tail) are asymmetric in flying species. Birds maintain feathers by preening, applying oil from the uropygial gland.

Nervous system and senses

Birds generally have excellent vision, with large eyes and, in many species, tetrachromatic colour vision; some can perceive ultraviolet light and the Earth's magnetic field (magnetoreception), aiding navigation during migration. Hearing is acute in nocturnal hunters such as owls, while smell is well developed in some groups such as kiwis and vultures.
