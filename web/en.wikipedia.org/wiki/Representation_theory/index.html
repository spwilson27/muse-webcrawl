Representation theory

Representation theory is a branch of mathematics that studies abstract algebraic structures by representing their elements as linear transformations of vector spaces, and studies modules over these abstract algebraic structures. In essence, a representation makes an abstract algebraic object more concrete by describing its elements by matrices and their algebraic operations (for example, matrix addition, matrix multiplication). The theory of matrices and linear operators is well-understood, so representations of more abstract objects in terms of familiar linear algebra objects can help to study their properties and sometimes simplify computations within a more abstract theory.

Definitions and concepts

Let V be a vector space over a field F. A representation of an algebraic structure A on V is an assignment of an endomorphism of V to each element of A, satisfying the algebraic relations of A. For a group G, a representation is a group homomorphism from G to the general linear group GL(V) of invertible linear transformations of V. Similar definitions apply to Lie algebras, associative algebras and other structures.

A central theme is the decomposition of representations into simpler pieces: a representation is called irreducible (or simple) if it has no proper invariant subspaces, and a representation is completely reducible if it decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible ones. Maschke's theorem guarantees that finite-dimensional representations of a finite group over a field whose characteristic does not divide the order of the group are completely reducible.

Branches

Representation theory is notable for the number of branches it has and the diversity of approaches to studying representations of groups and algebras. The main branches include:

  • Representation theory of finite groups — includes character theory, developed by Frobenius, Burnside and Schur, in which the trace of each representing matrix yields powerful numerical invariants.
  • Representation theory of Lie groups and Lie algebras — connected to the study of symmetries in differential equations and physics; the representation theory of compact groups and the Peter–Weyl theorem are central results.
  • Modular representation theory — representations over fields of positive characteristic.
  • Representation theory of algebras — including quivers and Auslander–Reiten theory.

Applications

Representation theory has important applications throughout mathematics and in theoretical physics. In number theory it underlies the Langlands program. In physics, symmetry groups acting on the states of a quantum system are described by representations; for example, representations of the Lorentz group and Poincaré group classify particles by mass and spin, and unitary representations of SU(3) led to the quark model of hadrons.

History

The subject grew out of work by Ferdinand Georg Frobenius in the 1890s on group characters and group determinants, William Burnside's theory of groups of finite order, and Issai Schur's work on polynomial representations. Emmy Noether recast the theory in terms of modules and ideals, giving the subject its modern abstract form.
