Goal setting


Recording specific goals is a central element of goal-setting practice.

Goal setting involves the development of an action plan designed in order to motivate and guide a person or group toward a goal. It is a major component of personal development, management and sports psychology literatures, and is supported by one of the most robust bodies of empirical research in organisational psychology.

Goal-setting theory

Goal-setting theory, developed principally by Edwin A. Locke and Gary P. Latham from the late 1960s onward, proposes that specific and challenging goals, when accepted and accompanied by feedback, lead to higher performance than easy goals, vague exhortations such as "do your best", or no goals at all. Reviews of hundreds of studies found this effect held in roughly 90 per cent of cases. Goals affect performance by directing attention and effort, increasing persistence, and prompting the discovery of task-relevant strategies. The theory specifies moderators: goal commitment, self-efficacy, feedback, task complexity and situational constraints all influence whether difficulty translates into performance. Goals that are too difficult, or that are tied to crude rewards, can backfire — encouraging unethical behaviour, narrow focus or excessive risk-taking.

SMART criteria and practice

In management practice, goals are frequently formulated using the SMART criteria — Specific, Measurable, Assignable (or Achievable), Realistic and Time-related — a formulation popularised by George T. Doran in 1981. Related frameworks include Objectives and Key Results (OKRs), management by objectives, and the distinction between performance goals and learning goals: on complex novel tasks, specific learning goals ("discover three strategies") can outperform specific outcome goals. Research further distinguishes proximal subgoals, which sustain motivation through visible progress, from distal goals, which provide direction; the two are most effective in combination.
