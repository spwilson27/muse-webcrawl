Manobo

The Manobo (also spelled Manuvu, Manobo, or Minuvu) are an indigenous peoples group of the Philippines, one of the largest of the country's ethnolinguistic groups. They live principally in the interior and eastern regions of Mindanao, in the provinces of Agusan, Bukidnon, Cotabato, Davao, and Surigao, as part of the broader Lumad (indigenous, non-Muslim, non-Christian) peoples of Mindanao.

Languages and subgroups

"Manobo" is an umbrella term covering many related groups and languages of the Manobo branch of the Philippine languages, including Western Bukidnon Manobo, Cotabato Manobo, Agusan Manobo, Ata Manobo, Matigsalug, and others; some of these languages are mutually unintelligible. Subgroups include the Bagobo (often treated separately), Higaonon neighbours, and the Tasaday of South Cotabato, who were presented (controversially) in the 1970s as an isolated "Stone Age" community.

Culture and livelihood

Traditional Manobo communities practiced swidden (shifting) agriculture, growing upland rice, corn, root crops, and abacá, supplemented by hunting, fishing, and gathering. Society was led by chieftains (datu) and shamans (baylan). Manobo peoples possess rich oral literatures — epics such as the Tuwaang cycle recorded from the Bagobo-Manobo — and are known for beadwork, weaving, and brass work. Today most Manobo are Christian, though traditional beliefs persist and are sometimes combined with Christian practice.

Contemporary issues

Manobo communities have been affected by logging, mining, plantation agriculture, land disputes over ancestral domains, and armed conflict in Mindanao, including mass displacements (evacuations of Lumad communities, with schools — bakwit — becoming a symbol of protest in the 2010s).
