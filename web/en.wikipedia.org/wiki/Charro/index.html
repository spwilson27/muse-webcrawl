A charro is a traditional horseman from Mexico, originating in the states of Jalisco, Michoacán, and Guanajuato. The charro tradition developed from the equestrian culture of the Spanish colonial haciendas, blending Indigenous, Spanish, and mestizo horsemanship into a distinctive national symbol. Practitioners compete in charrería, Mexico's national sport, a competitive rodeo tradition inscribed in 2016 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

History

Horses arrived in New Spain with the conquistadors in the 16th century, and colonial restrictions initially forbade Indigenous people from riding. As the great cattle haciendas expanded, Indigenous and mestizo vaqueros developed the roping, riding, and cattle-handling skills that became the core of charro culture. By the 19th century the charro had become a romantic national figure, celebrated in song and literature; charros fought in Mexico's wars of independence and revolution, and "chinaco" cavalrymen of the Reform War were their direct counterparts.

Charrería

A charreada consists of nine scored events (suertes) performed in a ring called a lienzo charro, including the cala de caballo (reining display), piales (roping the hind legs of a mare), bull riding, and the paso de la muerte (pass of death), in which a rider leaps from a galloping horse onto a bareback bronc. Women's teams, the escaramuzas, perform synchronized precision riding sidesaddle. The sport is governed in Mexico by the Federación Mexicana de Charrería and in the United States by regional charro associations.

Attire

The formal charro suit (traje de charro) is heavily codified: a short, fitted jacket and trousers of wool or suede decorated with silver embroidery and buttons, a wide-brimmed sombrero, a silk bow tie, and a sash. The dress of mariachi musicians derives directly from charro attire, which is why the charro image is inseparable from Mexico's musical identity.
