Risk analysis

Risk analysis is the process of identifying, characterising, and evaluating risk—the potential for loss, harm, or other adverse outcomes arising from uncertainty. It forms the analytical core of risk management and risk assessment, and is applied in fields as diverse as public health, engineering safety, finance, environmental protection, information security, and project management.

In broad terms, a risk analysis asks three questions: What can go wrong? How likely is it to go wrong? And if it does go wrong, what are the consequences? The answers may be expressed qualitatively (for example, on ranked scales of likelihood and severity), quantitatively (as numerical probabilities and expected losses), or in a combination of the two.

Components

The United States National Research Council's influential 1983 "Red Book" framework for health and environmental risk assessment divides the work into:

  • Hazard identification — determining whether an agent or situation can cause harm.
  • Dose–response (or probability) assessment — characterising the relationship between exposure or fault conditions and the likelihood or magnitude of effects.
  • Exposure assessment — estimating who or what is exposed, how often, and how much.
  • Risk characterisation — combining the above into an overall statement of risk, including its uncertainties.

Methods

Qualitative methods

Qualitative analysis uses expert judgement, checklists, and structured techniques such as hazard and operability studies (HAZOP), failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), and risk matrices that plot likelihood against consequence to prioritise hazards. These methods are fast and useful for screening, but their rankings can be coarse and sometimes misleading.

Quantitative methods

Quantitative risk analysis (QRA) assigns numerical values using techniques such as fault tree analysis, event tree analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, Bayesian networks, and probabilistic safety assessment—methods developed extensively in the nuclear, chemical process, and aerospace industries, notably following the 1975 Reactor Safety Study (WASH-1400). In finance, quantitative analysis uses measures such as value at risk; in project management, schedule and cost risk analysis uses simulation to estimate confidence intervals for completion dates and budgets.

Uncertainty

Risk analysis distinguishes variability (inherent randomness in the world) from epistemic uncertainty (lack of knowledge). Sensitivity analysis identifies which assumptions drive the results; uncertainty analysis propagates ranges of inputs through the model so decision-makers can see the confidence attached to a conclusion. A recurring criticism of risk analyses is that they can create an impression of precision that the underlying data do not support, and that framing choices—what counts as a harm, whose risks are counted—embed value judgements in apparently technical work.

Risk analysis versus risk management

Risk analysis is conventionally separated from risk management: analysis characterises the risk, while management decides what to do about it, weighing benefits, costs, laws, and public values. In practice the two interact, and frameworks such as ISO 31000 treat risk assessment (identification, analysis, evaluation) as one component of an overall risk-management process, iterated as new information arrives.
