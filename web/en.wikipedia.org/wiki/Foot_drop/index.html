Foot drop

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Foot drop (or drop foot) is a difficulty in lifting the front part of the foot, caused by weakness or paralysis of the muscles that dorsiflex the ankle. The toes drag or catch while walking, and patients compensate with a high, slapping "steppage gait", swinging the leg or hiking the hip to clear the ground. It is a sign, not a disease in itself.

Causes

The most common cause is injury to the common peroneal (fibular) nerve where it winds around the neck of the fibula below the knee — vulnerable to compression from leg-crossing, squatting, casts, or direct trauma. Other causes include L5 radiculopathy from a herniated lumbar disc, sciatic nerve injury, peripheral neuropathies such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, and central disorders including stroke and multiple sclerosis. Muscle diseases can occasionally cause it directly.

Diagnosis

Examination shows weak dorsiflexion and toe extension, sometimes with sensory loss over the top of the foot. Nerve conduction studies and electromyography can localise the lesion, and MRI is used where a disc protrusion or other structural cause is suspected. Distinguishing a peroneal palsy from an L5 root lesion matters because treatment differs; inversion of the foot is preserved in peroneal lesions but weak in L5 lesions.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the cause where possible: removing compression, physiotherapy, or surgery for disc or nerve entrapment. Many patients are fitted with an ankle–foot orthosis (AFO), a brace that holds the foot at a right angle; functional electrical stimulation of the peroneal nerve is an alternative. Prognosis depends on the cause — compressive neuropathies often recover over weeks to months as the nerve remyelinates or regrows.
