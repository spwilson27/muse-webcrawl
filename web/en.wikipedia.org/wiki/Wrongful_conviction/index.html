A wrongful conviction is a conviction of a person for a crime they did not commit, or for an act that was not a crime at all. Such miscarriages of justice come to light when new evidence—most dramatically, DNA testing—establishes innocence, leading to exoneration, the official clearing of the convicted person. The Innocence Project and similar organizations have documented thousands of exonerations in the United States since 1989, involving people who collectively spent tens of thousands of years in prison.

Causes

Studies of exonerations identify recurring factors: mistaken eyewitness identification, the single largest contributor in DNA exoneration cases; false confessions, especially by juveniles and people with intellectual disabilities; misapplied or invalid forensic science, such as overstated hair and bite-mark comparisons; use of incentivized jailhouse informants; inadequate defense lawyering; and official misconduct in concealing exculpatory evidence. A significant share of exonerees had pleaded guilty—sometimes through Alford pleas or ordinary plea bargains—despite being innocent, to avoid the risk of a far longer sentence after trial, a phenomenon that complicates the line between wrongful convictions after trial and wrongful guilty pleas.

Consequences and compensation

Exonerees frequently leave prison with no resources, damaged health, and a criminal record that statutes in most states—but not all—allow to be cleared and compensated. Compensation amounts and eligibility vary widely by jurisdiction, and some exonerees have been re-imprisoned on technicalities or waited years for payment. The real perpetrators in some cases committed additional crimes while the wrong person was incarcerated.

Reforms

Wrongful conviction research has driven reforms including double-blind, sequential eyewitness lineups; electronic recording of interrogations; preservation of biological evidence; accreditation of crime laboratories; and conviction integrity units within prosecutors' offices to re-examine questionable convictions. Procedural safeguards at trial—from voir dire to the rules governing evidence—are also debated in light of the exoneration record.
