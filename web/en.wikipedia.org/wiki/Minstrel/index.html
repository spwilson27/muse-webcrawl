Minstrel

A minstrel was an entertainer in medieval Europe, originally an attendant at court, later a professional musician, singer, poet, storyteller, juggler or acrobat who performed songs and tales for noble households and in public places. The term derives from the Latin ministralis, "servant". Minstrels flourished from the 12th to the 17th centuries.

History

In early medieval courts, minstrels were household servants who provided entertainment; by the High Middle Ages, gifted performers enjoyed high status and patronage. Many were itinerant, travelling between courts, fairs and festivals, carrying news as well as music. They overlapped with, and are often hard to distinguish from, troubadours and trouvères — who composed courtly lyric poetry — and from jongleurs, who performed the works of others along with tricks and tumbling.

Role and decline

Minstrels performed epic poems (chansons de geste), romances, ballads, dances and devotional songs, accompanying themselves on instruments such as the vielle, harp, lute, psaltery and pipe and tabor. In England and France many joined guilds; the Parisian Confrérie de Saint-Julien-des-Ménétriers was founded in 1321. The minstrel tradition declined with the spread of printed music, the rise of professional theatre, and Puritan hostility to secular entertainment in the 17th century.

Legacy

The romanticized figure of the minstrel was revived by poets such as Walter Scott (The Lay of the Last Minstrel, 1805). In 19th-century America the word acquired a second meaning through the unrelated minstrel show, in which performers in blackface presented caricatures of African American music and dance — a tradition now widely regarded as racist.
