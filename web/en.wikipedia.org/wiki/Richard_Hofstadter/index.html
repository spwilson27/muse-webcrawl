Richard Hofstadter

Richard Hofstadter (August 6, 1916 – October 24, 1970) was an American historian and public intellectual widely regarded as one of the leading historians of the twentieth century. He was the DeWitt Clinton Professor of History at Columbia University.

Hofstadter won the Pulitzer Prize for History twice, for The Age of Reform (1955) and Anti-intellectualism in American Life (1963). His 1964 essay "The Paranoid Style in American Politics" became a classic analysis of conspiratorial and extremist movements.

Life and career

Born in Buffalo, New York, to a Jewish father and a Lutheran mother, Hofstadter studied at the University at Buffalo and earned his PhD at Columbia in 1942. His dissertation became Social Darwinism in American Thought (1944). He taught at Brooklyn College and Johns Hopkins University before returning to Columbia in 1952.

Major works

The American Political Tradition and the Men Who Made It (1948) offered revisionist portraits of presidents from Jefferson to Franklin D. Roosevelt, emphasizing shared liberal-capitalist consensus. The Age of Reform reinterpreted Populism and Progressivism, stressing status anxiety. Anti-intellectualism in American Life traced suspicion of intellect in religion, politics, business, and education.

Influence

Hofstadter helped define consensus history, though later historians criticized its downplaying of conflict over race, class, and gender. He died of leukemia at age 54.
