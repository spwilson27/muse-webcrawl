Mohs surgery

Mohs surgery, developed in 1938 by general surgeon Frederic E. Mohs, is a microscopically controlled surgery used to treat both common and rare types of skin cancer. During the surgery, after each removal of tissue and while the patient waits, the removed tissue is examined for cancer cells under the microscope. If cancer cells remain, more tissue is removed at precisely the spot identified by the microscopic map. The process repeats until no cancer cells remain.

Staged excision with complete margin examination.

Technique

The surgeon removes a thin layer of tissue, which is colour-coded, mapped, frozen, sectioned horizontally, and read by the surgeon-pathologist. Because the horizontal sections display essentially 100% of the peripheral and deep margins — unlike standard vertical "bread-loaf" sections, which sample a small fraction — Mohs surgery achieves the highest cure rates of any skin cancer treatment while removing the least healthy tissue.

Indications and outcomes

Mohs is preferred for basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma that are recurrent, large, poorly defined, located on the face, ears, hands or genitals, or arising in immunosuppressed patients. Reported cure rates reach about 99% for primary basal cell carcinoma and 97% for recurrent tumours. The technique is also used for dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, microcystic adnexal carcinoma, and, increasingly, selected melanomas with specialized staining.
