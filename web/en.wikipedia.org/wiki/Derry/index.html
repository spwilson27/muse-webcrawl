Derry

Derry, officially Londonderry, is the second-largest city in Northern Ireland and the fifth-largest city in the island of Ireland. It lies on the River Foyle in the north-west of Ireland, near the border with County Donegal in the Republic of Ireland. The old walled city on the west bank of the Foyle is famous for its complete circuit of 17th-century city walls, among the finest in Europe.

Name

The name Derry comes from the Irish Doire, meaning "oak grove", after the early monastic settlement associated with St Columba. In 1613 the city was granted a royal charter and the prefix "London" was added in recognition of the investment of the livery companies of London in its rebuilding. The name remains politically contested: nationalists generally use "Derry", unionists "Londonderry", and the local council has been Derry City Council since 1984.

History

Planted during the Plantation of Ulster, the walled city withstood the Siege of Derry in 1689, when Jacobite forces failed to starve out its Williamite defenders — an event still commemorated by the Apprentice Boys. In the 20th century Derry was a flashpoint at the start of the Troubles: the Battle of the Bogside in 1969 and Bloody Sunday in 1972, when British paratroopers shot dead 14 civil rights marchers, occurred there. The city has since reinvented itself, serving as the first UK City of Culture in 2013.
