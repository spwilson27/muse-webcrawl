An electric arc (or arc discharge) is an electrical breakdown of a gas that produces a prolonged electrical discharge. Current flows through normally non-conductive media such as air and produces a plasma, which may emit visible light. Arc discharges are characterised by very high current density and temperature, and by a lower voltage than a glow discharge.

Formation and properties

An arc can be struck by bringing two electrodes in contact and separating them, or by ionising the gap with a high-voltage spark. Once formed, the arc sustains itself: the hot cathode emits electrons copiously (thermionic and field emission), keeping the plasma ionised. The arc has negative dynamic resistance — as current increases, the voltage across the arc decreases — so circuits must include a ballast or other current limiting to prevent runaway. Arc temperatures range from a few thousand kelvin in lamp arcs to tens of thousands of kelvin in high-current arcs.

History

The arc was first reported by Vasily V. Petrov in 1802 and demonstrated by Humphry Davy around 1808 using a large battery and charcoal electrodes, the arc bending upward into an arch shape — hence the name. Arc lamps (such as the Yablochkov candle) provided the first practical electric lighting in the late 19th century before incandescent lamps displaced them for general use.

Applications

Controlled arcs are used in arc welding, electric arc furnaces for steelmaking, plasma cutting and spraying, carbon-arc and xenon-arc lamps (including film projectors and searchlights), fluorescent and discharge lighting, and arc-jet thrusters. A Jacob's ladder demonstrates the rising arc driven by heated air.

Undesired arcs

Uncontrolled arcs are hazards: arcing faults in switchgear can cause explosive arc flashes; series arcs in damaged wiring are a fire risk addressed by arc-fault circuit interrupters (AFCIs); and arcs erode electrical contacts. Engineers suppress arcs with magnetic blowouts, vacuum or SF₆ interrupters, and quenching chambers.
