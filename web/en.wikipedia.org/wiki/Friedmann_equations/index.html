Friedmann equations

Friedmann equations
FieldPhysical cosmology, general relativity
Derived byAlexander Friedmann (1922, 1924)
Used inFriedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker model; Lambda-CDM

The Friedmann equations, also known as the Friedmann–Lemaître equations, are a set of equations in physical cosmology that govern cosmic expansion in homogeneous and isotropic models of the universe within the context of general relativity. They were first derived by Alexander Friedmann in 1922 from Einstein's field equations for a universe filled with a fluid of constant density and pressure.

The equations

The first Friedmann equation relates the expansion rate — expressed by the Hubble parameter H = ȧ/a, where a is the scale factor — to the total energy density ρ, spatial curvature k, and the cosmological constant Λ:

H² = (8πG/3) ρ − kc²/a² + Λc²/3

The second (acceleration) equation shows that the acceleration of the expansion ä/a depends on density and pressure: ä/a = −(4πG/3)(ρ + 3p/c²) + Λc²/3. Positive pressure therefore slows expansion, while a positive cosmological constant accelerates it.

Assumptions and models

The equations follow from the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric, which assumes the cosmological principle: the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. Combined with equations of state for matter, radiation, and dark energy, they yield the standard Lambda-CDM model, in which a flat universe expands forever at an accelerating rate.

Density parameter

The ratio of the actual density to the critical density, Ω, determines spatial geometry: Ω > 1 implies a closed universe, Ω < 1 an open one, and Ω = 1 flatness — the case favoured by observations of the cosmic microwave background.
