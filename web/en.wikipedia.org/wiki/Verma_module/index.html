Verma module

In mathematics, a Verma module is a fundamental object in the representation theory of Lie algebras and related algebraic structures. Named after the Indian mathematician Daya-Nand Verma, who introduced them in his 1966 Yale PhD thesis, Verma modules are the universal highest-weight modules: every module generated by a highest-weight vector of a given weight is a quotient of the corresponding Verma module.

Construction

Let 𝔤 be a (typically semisimple) Lie algebra with Cartan subalgebra and a choice of positive roots, so that 𝔤 decomposes into negative, Cartan and positive parts. For a weight λ, the Verma module M(λ) is constructed by inducing a one-dimensional representation of the Borel subalgebra (on which the positive root spaces act by zero and the Cartan acts by λ) up to the universal enveloping algebra of 𝔤. As a vector space it is isomorphic to the enveloping algebra of the negative part, so a basis is given by monomials in lowering operators applied to a highest-weight vector vλ.

Structure

Each Verma module has a unique maximal proper submodule, and the quotient by it is the irreducible highest-weight module L(λ). Verma modules are the projective and standard objects of the Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand category O. Whether M(μ) embeds into M(λ) is governed by the Bruhat order (the Verma–BGG theorem), and their characters and multiplicities are given by the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjectures, proved via the work of Beilinson–Bernstein and Brylinski–Kashiwara. Analogues exist for quantum groups, Kac–Moody algebras, the Virasoro algebra in conformal field theory, and superalgebras.
