Bicubic interpolation

Bicubic interpolation is an extension of cubic interpolation for interpolating data points on a two-dimensional regular grid. The interpolated surface is smoother than surfaces obtained by bilinear interpolation or nearest-neighbour interpolation. Bicubic interpolation is widely used in image processing for resampling images, where it generally produces sharper results than bilinear interpolation at greater computational cost.

Bicubic interpolation uses the 16 nearest neighbours of a point.

Computation

To estimate the value at a point within a unit square, bicubic interpolation fits a bicubic surface of the form of a sum over terms x^i y^j for i, j from 0 to 3, giving sixteen coefficients. These are determined from the function values at the four corners of the square and from estimates of the derivatives (in x, in y, and the cross-derivative) at those corners, which are usually computed from the surrounding 4×4 block of samples using finite differences.

Bicubic convolution

An alternative formulation, the bicubic convolution algorithm described by Keys (1981), uses a separable cubic convolution kernel. The common Catmull–Rom spline is a special case frequently used in graphics because it passes through the sample points and is computationally convenient.

Comparison

Compared with bilinear interpolation, bicubic interpolation better preserves fine detail and produces fewer blocky artefacts, though it can overshoot near sharp edges, sometimes producing values outside the range of the samples. Lanczos resampling may give still better quality at higher cost.
