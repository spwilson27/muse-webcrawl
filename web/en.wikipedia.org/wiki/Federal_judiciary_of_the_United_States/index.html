Federal judiciary of the United States

The federal judiciary of the United States is one of the three branches of the federal government of the United States organized under the United States Constitution and laws of the federal government.

Federal judiciary

Established: Article III, U.S. Constitution (1789)
Highest court: Supreme Court of the United States
Lower courts: 13 Courts of Appeals, 94 District Courts

Structure

Article III courts include the Supreme Court, the courts of appeals for 13 circuits (including the Federal Circuit), and 94 district courts. Article I tribunals include bankruptcy courts, the Tax Court, and the Court of Federal Claims. Judges of Article III courts are nominated by the President, confirmed by the Senate, and serve for life during good behaviour.

Jurisdiction

Federal courts hear cases involving federal law, the Constitution, disputes between states, diversity of citizenship, admiralty, and bankruptcy. Judicial review, established in Marbury v. Madison (1803), allows courts to strike down laws violating the Constitution.

Administration

The Judicial Conference and the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts manage policy and administration; the Federal Judicial Center handles research and education.
