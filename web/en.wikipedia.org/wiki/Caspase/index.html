Caspases (cysteine-aspartic proteases, cysteine aspartases or cysteine-dependent aspartate-directed proteases) are a family of protease enzymes playing essential roles in programmed cell death. They are named caspases because of their specific cysteine protease activity – a cysteine in their active site nucleophilically attacks and cleaves a target protein only after an aspartic acid residue. The first caspase was identified in 1993.

Caspases are crucial mediators of apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death in which cells dismantle themselves in an orderly, controlled manner. Some caspases are also required in the immune system, for the maturation of cytokines such as interleukin-1β and interleukin-18, and in the inflammatory cell-death pathway known as pyroptosis.

Types of caspase

As of 2009, there are 12 confirmed caspases in humans and 10 in mice, categorised into three groups by function:

  • Apoptotic initiator caspases: caspase-2, caspase-8, caspase-9, caspase-10
  • Apoptotic effector (executioner) caspases: caspase-3, caspase-6, caspase-7
  • Inflammatory caspases: caspase-1, caspase-4, caspase-5, caspase-11, caspase-12

Initiator caspases are activated by specific signalling complexes — the apoptosome activates caspase-9 in the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway, while death-inducing signalling complexes (DISC) activate caspase-8 and caspase-10 in the extrinsic (death receptor) pathway. Once active, initiators cleave and activate the effector caspases, which then dismantle the cell by cleaving hundreds of structural and regulatory proteins.

Structure and activation

Caspases are synthesised as inactive precursors called procaspases (zymogens). Apoptotic initiator procaspases contain a long N-terminal prodomain carrying a caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD) or death effector domain (DED), which mediates their recruitment into activating complexes. Effector procaspases have short prodomains and exist as constitutive dimers. Activation typically involves dimerisation and proteolytic cleavage between the large (p20) and small (p10) subunits that make up the mature active site.

Role in disease

Defects in apoptosis regulation involving caspases contribute to cancer, neurodegenerative diseases and autoimmune disorders. Insufficient apoptosis allows damaged cells to survive and proliferate, whereas excessive apoptosis contributes to tissue loss in conditions such as Alzheimer's disease and stroke. Caspase inhibitors and activators have therefore been investigated as therapeutic agents.

Discovery

The importance of caspases in apoptosis was first established in 1993, when the ced-3 gene of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans — essential for developmental cell death — was found to encode a protein homologous to the mammalian interleukin-1β-converting enzyme (ICE), now known as caspase-1. Horwich and Yuan's work rapidly led to the identification of the wider caspase family.
