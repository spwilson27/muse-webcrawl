Lissencephaly

SpecialtyNeurology, medical genetics
SymptomsSmooth brain surface, seizures, severe intellectual disability, failure to thrive
CausesDefective neuronal migration, usually genetic
TreatmentSupportive care

Lissencephaly (from Greek λissós "smooth" and enképhalos "brain") is a set of rare brain disorders in which the whole or parts of the surface of the brain appear smooth rather than folded into the usual gyri and sulci. It is caused by defective neuronal migration during the 12th to 24th weeks of gestation, resulting in a lack of development of brain folds and grooves.

MRI scan showing the smooth cerebral surface characteristic of lissencephaly.

Classification and genetics

The spectrum ranges from complete absence of folds (agyria) through reduced folding (pachygyria) to milder forms such as subcortical band heterotopia. Mutations in a number of genes are implicated, including PAFAH1B1 (encoding LIS1) on chromosome 17, in which larger deletions also cause Miller–Dieker syndrome; DCX (doublecortin) on the X chromosome; and tubulin genes such as TUBA1A, as well as ARX, RELN, VLDLR and LAMB1.

Signs and symptoms

Affected children commonly show severe developmental delay and intellectual disability, epilepsy that is often difficult to control, low muscle tone progressing to spasticity, feeding difficulties and a shortened life expectancy in the most severe forms, although prognosis varies with the underlying cause and degree of malformation.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by brain imaging (MRI) showing the smooth cortex, increasingly supplemented by genetic testing, and the condition can sometimes be detected prenatally. There is no cure; treatment is supportive and includes anti-seizure medication, feeding support and physical, occupational and speech therapy.
