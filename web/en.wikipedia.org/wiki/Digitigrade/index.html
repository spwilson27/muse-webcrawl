Digitigrade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digitigrade posture

The hind leg of a dog, a digitigrade animal

In terrestrial vertebrates, digitigrade locomotion is walking or running on the toes (digits), with the ankle and heel raised off the ground. Digitigrade animals include most running mammals — dogs, cats, and many other carnivorans, as well as birds and theropod dinosaurs. The posture effectively lengthens the leg by adding the foot bones to its length, the animal standing on its metapodials and toes, which increases stride length and speed.

Comparison with other postures

Digitigrade posture contrasts with plantigrade locomotion, in which the whole sole of the foot, including the heel, touches the ground — as in humans, bears and raccoons — and with unguligrade locomotion, in which only the tips of the toes, often encased in hooves, touch the ground, as in horses and deer. Digitigrades tend to be adapted for running and tend to move more quietly than plantigrades, an advantage for predators and prey alike.

Anatomy

What appears to be a digitigrade animal's backward-bending "ankle" is actually its true ankle joint; the long segment below it is the metapodial bones of the foot. In dogs, for example, the weight is borne on the third and fourth digits, with a paw pad cushioning the metapodials. Many digitigrades have reduced or lost the first digit (the dewclaw in dogs).
