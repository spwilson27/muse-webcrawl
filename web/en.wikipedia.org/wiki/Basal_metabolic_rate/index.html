Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the rate of energy expenditure per unit time by endothermic animals at rest. It is the amount of energy needed to sustain the body's essential functions — circulation, breathing, cell production, nutrient processing, protein synthesis and ion transport — while awake, in a thermoneutral environment, in a post-absorptive state, and at complete physical rest. In humans it is usually expressed in kilocalories per day and accounts for the largest share of total daily energy expenditure.

Measurement and estimation

BMR is measured by indirect calorimetry, which determines oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production under strictly standardised conditions: after an overnight fast and a night's sleep, lying still in a darkened, temperature-controlled room. In practice it is usually estimated from predictive equations. The Harris–Benedict equations (1919, revised 1984) estimate BMR from weight, height, age and sex; the Mifflin–St Jeor equation (1990) is now generally considered the most accurate for healthy adults. For an average adult man BMR is roughly 1,600–1,800 kcal/day and for a woman about 1,400–1,500 kcal/day.

Factors affecting BMR

Fat-free mass is the strongest determinant of BMR: muscle and organs consume far more energy at rest than adipose tissue. BMR declines by roughly 1–2 percent per decade after early adulthood, is higher in men than women of the same weight, and is raised by fever, hyperthyroidism, growth, pregnancy and lactation, and lowered by hypothyroidism, fasting and energy restriction. Across species, metabolic rate scales approximately with body mass to the three-quarter power, a regularity known as Kleiber's law.

Related concepts

Resting metabolic rate (RMR) is measured under less stringent conditions and is typically slightly higher than BMR; the two terms are often used interchangeably in nutrition practice. Total energy expenditure adds to BMR the thermic effect of food and the energy cost of physical activity.
