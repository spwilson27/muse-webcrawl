The North-West Rebellion (French: Rébellion du Nord-Ouest), also known in modern Canadian usage as the North-West Resistance, was an armed uprising in 1885 by the Métis people under Louis Riel and by Cree and Assiniboine bands against the Government of Canada, in what was then the District of Saskatchewan in the North-West Territories.

The Métis, a people of mixed Indigenous and European ancestry concentrated along the Red and Saskatchewan rivers, feared for their land rights, language, and way of life as Canadian surveyors and settlers moved west and the buffalo herds on which their economy depended disappeared. After petitions to Ottawa went unanswered, Riel — who had led the Red River Resistance of 1869–70 — returned from exile in Montana and proclaimed a provisional government at Batoche on 19 March 1885.

Background

Following the Manitoba Act of 1870, many Métis migrated west to the Saskatchewan valley. By the 1880s they faced collapsing buffalo hunts, disputed scrip and land grants, and indifference from the federal government of Prime Minister John A. Macdonald, which was preoccupied with completing the Canadian Pacific Railway. Indigenous peoples of the plains, including the Cree chiefs Poundmaker and Big Bear, were simultaneously suffering hunger after signing treaties and being confined to reserves.

Course of the conflict

Fighting began on 26 March 1885 at Duck Lake, where a Métis and Cree force under Gabriel Dumont defeated a North-West Mounted Police column. The government rushed thousands of militia west by the nearly completed railway — the conflict became the first in which Canada deployed troops largely by train.

Major engagements included:

  • Duck Lake (26 March) — rebel victory.
  • Frog Lake Massacre (2 April) — warriors of Big Bear's band killed nine settlers and officials; Big Bear himself opposed the killings.
  • Battle of Fish Creek (24 April) — Dumont's ambush checked General Frederick Middleton's advance.
  • Battle of Batoche (9–12 May) — Middleton captured Riel's capital; Riel surrendered on 15 May.
  • Battle of Frenchman's Butte and Loon Lake (late May – 3 June) — final engagements against Big Bear's band.

Poundmaker surrendered on 26 May after the Battle of Cut Knife (2 May), where his warriors had repelled a government column. Big Bear surrendered on 2 July, ending the resistance.

Aftermath

Riel was tried for high treason in Regina by a six-man, English-speaking Protestant jury, convicted, and hanged on 16 November 1885 despite widespread protest in Quebec — an execution that permanently reshaped Canadian politics and French–English relations. Eight Indigenous men were hanged at Battleford on 27 November 1885 in the largest mass hanging in Canadian history. Poundmaker and Big Bear received prison sentences; Dumont escaped to the United States and later returned under amnesty.

The completion of the Canadian Pacific Railway, hastened by the rebellion's demand for troop transport, was assured, and the government tightened control over the West through the pass system restricting Indigenous movement off reserves.

Terminology

Older histories called the event a "rebellion," but many historians and the Government of Canada now prefer "resistance," reflecting that the Métis had petitioned for rights guaranteed in spirit by earlier agreements, and that Riel's provisional government claimed to negotiate rather than overthrow the Crown. Both terms remain in use; the related article North-West Resistance uses the newer name.
