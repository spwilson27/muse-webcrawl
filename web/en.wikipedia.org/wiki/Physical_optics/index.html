Physical optics

Diffraction, a phenomenon explained by physical optics but not by geometric optics.

In physics, physical optics, or wave optics, is the branch of optics that studies interference, diffraction, polarization and other phenomena for which the ray approximation of geometrical optics is not valid. The name is also used for a high-frequency approximation method commonly used in electrical engineering and applied physics, intermediate between geometrical optics and full wave electromagnetism.

Wave description of light

Physical optics treats light as an electromagnetic wave, described by Maxwell's equations. From this basis it explains phenomena such as:

  • Interference — the superposition of coherent waves, as in thin-film colours and the double-slit experiment.
  • Diffraction — the bending and spreading of waves around obstacles and through apertures.
  • Polarization — the orientation of the electric field oscillation.
  • Dispersion — the dependence of refractive index on wavelength.

The physical optics approximation

In the approximation used in engineering, the field on a scattering surface is approximated by the field that would exist if the surface were an infinite tangent plane, and the scattered field is then computed from these surface currents by integration. This approximation is widely used for antenna design and radar cross-section calculations, and is accurate when the object is large compared with the wavelength and radii of curvature are large.

History

The wave theory developed from the work of Christiaan Huygens in the 17th century and was firmly established by Thomas Young's interference experiments and Augustin-Jean Fresnel's diffraction theory in the early 19th century, displacing Newton's corpuscular theory until the photon concept of quantum mechanics reinterpreted the picture in the 20th century.
