The Vega Expedition of 1878–1880, under the leadership of the Finland-Swedish scientist and explorer Adolf Erik Nordenskiöld, was the first voyage to navigate the Northeast Passage, the sea route along the Arctic coast of Siberia from the Atlantic to the Pacific.

Background

For three centuries, European seafarers had sought a northern sea route to Asia, but ice had blocked all attempts east of the Kara Sea. Nordenskiöld, who had already led several Arctic expeditions and reached the mouth of the Yenisei River in 1875, believed a well-equipped steamer could hug the Siberian coast and pass through. The expedition was financed by King Oscar II of Sweden, the Gothenburg merchant Oscar Dickson, and the Russian industrialist Alexander Sibiryakov.

Voyage

The steam barque Vega, a former whaler strengthened for ice, left Gothenburg on 4 July 1878 with an international scientific staff. Sailing via Tromsø and the Kara Sea, she rounded Cape Chelyuskin — the northernmost point of Eurasia — in August 1878. Progress slowed in the ice of the East Siberian Sea, and on 28 September 1878, only about 200 kilometres short of the Bering Strait, the Vega was frozen in near the Chukchi settlement of Pitlekaj, where the expedition wintered.

The ship was released on 18 July 1879 and passed through the Bering Strait two days later, completing the first transit of the Northeast Passage. The expedition returned home via Yokohama, Hong Kong, the Suez Canal, and the Mediterranean, arriving in Stockholm on 24 April 1880 to a hero's welcome.

Significance

The voyage demonstrated that the passage was navigable with great care, and the scientific collections in zoology, botany, geology, and ethnography — including observations of the Chukchi people — were extensive. It paved the way for later development of the Northern Sea Route.
