The Hatfield–McCoy feud was a long-running conflict between two families of the Tug Fork valley along the West Virginia–Kentucky border in Appalachia. Involving the Hatfields of West Virginia, led by William Anderson "Devil Anse" Hatfield, and the McCoys of Kentucky, led by Randolph "Ole Ran'l" McCoy, the feud became the most famous family vendetta in American history and a byword for bitter interfamily violence.

Origins

Tensions had roots in the Civil War: Devil Anse Hatfield was a Confederate sympathiser and guerrilla, while Asa Harmon McCoy, a Union soldier and Randolph's brother, was murdered in 1865, allegedly by Hatfield's associates. The feud ignited in earnest in 1878, when Floyd Hatfield and Randolph McCoy disputed ownership of a razorback hog; a court ruled for the Hatfields, and the McCoy-allied witness Bill Staton was later killed by two McCoy nephews. In 1880 a romance between Johnse Hatfield and Roseanna McCoy — abandoned, pregnant, by Johnse — deepened the hatred. In 1882 three McCoy brothers stabbed and shot Ellison Hatfield; Devil Anse's men captured them and executed them in revenge.

Escalation

The feud's bloodiest episode came on New Year's night 1888, when a Hatfield party attacked the McCoy cabin, killing Alifair and Calvin McCoy and badly beating their mother Sarah. Bounty hunters and posses crossed state lines; Kentucky raided into West Virginia, and the governors of both states mobilised militias, bringing the dispute to national attention and nearly to the U.S. Supreme Court in Mahon v. Justice. In 1889 nine Hatfield men were tried in Kentucky; seven received life sentences and Ellison "Cottontop" Mounts was hanged in 1890. Violence largely ceased after 1891.

Legacy

The feud entered American folklore through books, films, and television, including the 2012 History channel miniseries Hatfields & McCoys. Descendants of both families symbolically signed a truce in 2003, and the feud region now hosts trails, museums, and annual festivals.
