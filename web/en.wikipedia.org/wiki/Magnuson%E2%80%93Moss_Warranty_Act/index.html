Magnuson–Moss Warranty Act

Magnuson–Moss Warranty Act
EnactedJanuary 4, 1975
Codified15 U.S.C. §§ 2301–2312
Named forSen. Warren Magnuson and Rep. John Moss
AgencyFederal Trade Commission

The Magnuson–Moss Warranty Act is a United States federal law enacted in 1975 that governs warranties on consumer products. Sponsored by Senator Warren G. Magnuson of Washington and Representative John E. Moss of California, the Act is administered by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). Its purposes are to improve the adequacy of information available to consumers, prevent deception, and improve competition in the marketing of consumer products. The Act does not require manufacturers or sellers to give a warranty, but if they do, the warranty must comply with the Act's standards.


President Gerald Ford signed the Act into law in January 1975.

Provisions

Disclosure

Warrantors must disclose, in simple and readily understood language, the terms and conditions of written warranties. The FTC's Pre-Sale Availability Rule requires sellers to make warranty terms available to consumers before purchase, in stores, in catalogues, and online.

Full and limited warranties

A warranty designated as "full" must meet federal minimum standards: the warrantor must remedy defects without charge within a reasonable time, without imposing unreasonable conditions, and, if the product cannot be repaired or is not repaired after a reasonable number of attempts, must replace it or refund the purchase price. Any warranty not meeting all of these is a "limited" warranty and must be conspicuously designated as such.

Implied warranties

The Act prohibits a warrantor who gives a written warranty from disclaiming or modifying implied warranties (such as the implied warranty of merchantability) arising under state law, and limits how long implied warranties may be restricted when a limited written warranty is offered.

Tie-in sales and maintenance

Generally, a warrantor may not condition warranty coverage on the consumer's use of a particular brand of part or service — such as requiring factory parts or authorized service — unless the part or service is provided free of charge or the warrantor obtains an FTC waiver. This provision is frequently cited in right-to-repair debates and means consumers may generally use independent repair shops or aftermarket parts without voiding a warranty, though damage caused by such parts or service need not be covered.

Enforcement and remedies

Before suing, a consumer must generally give the warrantor a reasonable opportunity to cure — that is, to repair the product. The Act permits consumers to bring individual actions and class actions in state or federal court, and a prevailing consumer may recover attorney's fees. The Act also encourages informal dispute settlement mechanisms, such as arbitration programmes meeting FTC standards (Rule 703), and allows the FTC and state attorneys general to enforce its terms.

Relationship to lemon laws

Because the Act itself sets no uniform number of repair attempts, most states have enacted "lemon laws," especially for automobiles, that work alongside it and provide presumptions for refunds or replacements after a specified number of failed repairs or days out of service.
