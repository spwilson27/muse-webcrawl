Premature obituary

Premature obituary
TypeJournalistic error
Famous exampleAlfred Nobel (1888)

A premature obituary is an obituary published before the person it describes has actually died. Such errors occur for several reasons: hoaxes and false reports, confusion between people with similar names, the accidental release of pre-written obituaries prepared in advance by news organisations, and simple miscommunication. News outlets routinely prepare obituaries of prominent people ahead of time — a practice that occasionally results in the draft being published or leaked while its subject is still alive.

Notable examples

Alfred Nobel

The most famous case is that of Alfred Nobel. In 1888, when his brother Ludvig died, a French newspaper erroneously published an obituary of Alfred instead, condemning him as a "merchant of death" (le marchand de la mort est mort) who had grown rich by finding ways to "kill more people faster than ever before". The episode is often credited with prompting Nobel to establish the Nobel Prizes in order to improve his legacy — although historians debate how direct the connection was.

Mark Twain

In 1897, reports circulated that Mark Twain was dying. The confusion arose from the serious illness of his cousin James Ross Clemens. Twain responded with the remark usually quoted as "The reports of my death are greatly exaggerated"; his actual note to a journalist read, "The report of my death was an exaggeration."

Other cases

  • In 2003, CNN's pre-written obituaries for several public figures (including Fidel Castro, Nelson Mandela, Ronald Reagan and Pope John Paul II) were accidentally made publicly accessible online. Some contained placeholder text copied between templates.
  • In 2008 Bloomberg News accidentally published a pre-written obituary of Apple co-founder Steve Jobs, which included blank spaces for his age and cause of death; Jobs later joked about it on stage.
  • In 2012 the French broadcaster Radio France Internationale published premature obituaries of numerous public figures after a technical error pushed drafts live; similar incidents have affected many outlets in the social-media era, when death hoaxes spread rapidly.

Causes

Premature obituaries most often result from mistaken identity, reliance on a single unreliable source, pranks or hoax phone calls, and production errors involving advance material stored in content-management systems. In the 24-hour news cycle and on social media, unverified reports of a celebrity's death can be amplified worldwide within minutes, and corrections rarely travel as far as the original error.
