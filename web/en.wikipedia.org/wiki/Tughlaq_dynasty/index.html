The Tughlaq dynasty was a Muslim dynasty of Turkic origin that ruled the Delhi Sultanate in India from 1320 to 1413. Under its second sultan, Muhammad bin Tughluq, the sultanate reached its greatest territorial extent, covering most of the Indian subcontinent, before overextension, rebellion and famine broke it apart. The dynasty is remembered both for ambitious — and often disastrous — experiments in policy and for major works of architecture in Delhi.

Rulers and reigns

Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq (r. 1320–1325), a former frontier governor, founded the dynasty after overthrowing the Khaljis and built the fortress-city of Tughlaqabad. His son Muhammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325–1351) was famed for learning and for bold schemes that failed: shifting the capital to Daulatabad in the Deccan and forcing Delhi's population to move; issuing token brass and copper currency that was widely counterfeited; and raising taxes in the Doab during famine. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta served as a judge at his court and left a vivid account of the reign.

Firuz Shah Tughlaq (r. 1351–1388) pursued public works — canals, towns such as Firozabad and Hisar, hospitals and the transport of Ashokan pillars to Delhi — but the empire continued to fragment, with Bengal, the Deccan (Bahmani Sultanate) and other provinces breaking away. After his death, civil war among rival Tughlaq princes weakened the sultanate fatally; in 1398 Timur (Tamerlane) invaded and sacked Delhi. The last Tughlaq sultan died in 1413, and the Sayyid dynasty succeeded.
