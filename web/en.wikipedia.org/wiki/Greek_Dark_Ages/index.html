The Greek Dark Ages (c. 1100 BC – c. 800 BC) were the period of Greek history between the collapse of the Bronze Age Mycenaean palace civilisation and the rise of the Archaic Greek city-states. The era is "dark" in large part because writing disappeared: the Linear B script used for palace administration vanished with the palaces, and Greece remained illiterate for roughly three centuries until the Greek alphabet was adapted from the Phoenician script around the 8th century BC.

Collapse and conditions

Around 1200–1100 BC the Mycenaean palaces at Mycenae, Pylos, Tiryns and elsewhere were destroyed or abandoned amid the wider Late Bronze Age collapse. Population fell sharply, settlements shrank to small farming villages, long-distance trade contracted, and crafts such as ivory carving and monumental building ceased. Burial practices changed, and iron — rather than increasingly scarce bronze — became the main metal for tools and weapons. The period's pottery styles, Submycenaean, Protogeometric and Geometric, provide the main archaeological framework.

Recovery

From about 900 BC trade and population began recovering; by the 8th century BC Greece entered a renaissance marked by the spread of the alphabet, the composition or writing-down of the Homeric epics (the Iliad and Odyssey, set in the remembered Mycenaean world), the founding of colonies around the Mediterranean, the Olympic Games (traditionally 776 BC) and the emergence of the polis.
