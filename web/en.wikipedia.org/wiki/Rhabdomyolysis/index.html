Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis
Specialty: Nephrology, critical care
Symptoms: Muscle pain and weakness, dark (tea-coloured) urine
Causes: Crush injury, extreme exertion, drugs, infection, electrolyte disorders
Key test: Elevated creatine kinase

Rhabdomyolysis is a condition in which damaged skeletal muscle breaks down rapidly, releasing its contents — including the protein myoglobin, potassium, phosphate and the enzyme creatine kinase — into the bloodstream. These substances can harm the kidneys, and rhabdomyolysis is a recognised cause of acute kidney injury. The name derives from Greek words for "rod", "muscle" and "loosening".

Causes

Causes divide broadly into traumatic and non-traumatic. Traumatic causes include crush injuries (historically described after the London Blitz), prolonged immobilisation, burns and electrocution. Non-traumatic causes include extreme or unaccustomed exercise, seizures, alcohol and drug toxicity (including statins and cocaine), infections, heat stroke, malignant hyperthermia, and metabolic or electrolyte disturbances.

Signs and diagnosis

The classic triad is muscle pain, weakness and dark, tea- or cola-coloured urine, though all three occur in a minority of patients. The hallmark laboratory finding is a markedly elevated serum creatine kinase, often more than five times the upper limit of normal and sometimes in the hundreds of thousands. Myoglobin in urine may give a positive dipstick "blood" reading despite an absence of red cells under the microscope.

Treatment

The mainstay is early, aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation to maintain urine output and protect the kidneys. Electrolyte abnormalities (especially high potassium) must be corrected; compartment syndrome may require fasciotomy, and severe kidney failure may need dialysis. With prompt treatment most patients recover renal function.
