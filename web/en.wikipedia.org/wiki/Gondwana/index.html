Gondwana

Gondwana was a large landmass, sometimes referred to as a supercontinent, that formed during the late Neoproterozoic (about 550 million years ago) and began to break up during the Jurassic period (about 180 million years ago). It incorporated the present-day landmasses of South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia, Zealandia, Arabia, and the Indian subcontinent.

Formation and Pangaea

Gondwana assembled from collisions of continental blocks as the supercontinent Rodinia broke apart. During the Carboniferous it merged with Laurussia and other landmasses to form the supercontinent Pangaea, making Gondwana the largest continental region of the combined mass.

Breakup

Rifting began in the Jurassic: Africa and South America separated from the eastern block (Antarctica, India, Australia) as the South Atlantic and Indian Oceans opened. India rafted north across the Tethys Ocean to collide with Asia, raising the Himalayas; Australia separated from Antarctica about 85–45 million years ago; South America finally detached from Antarctica around 34 million years ago, allowing the Antarctic Circumpolar Current and the glaciation of Antarctica.

Evidence

The geologist Eduard Suess recognised striking similarities — the Glossopteris flora and glacial deposits — among the southern continents and named the former landmass after the Gondwana sedimentary region of central India. Fossil distributions of Glossopteris, Mesosaurus, Lystrosaurus, and Cynognathus across now-distant continents provided key early evidence for continental drift and plate tectonics.
