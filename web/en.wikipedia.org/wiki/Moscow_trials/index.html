The Moscow trials were a series of three show trials held by the Soviet Union between 1936 and 1938, at the height of the Great Purge. Senior figures of the Communist Party and the Soviet state were accused of espionage, sabotage ("wrecking") and conspiring with foreign powers and the exiled Leon Trotsky to overthrow the government of Joseph Stalin. The trials are widely regarded by historians as staged proceedings in which the verdicts were predetermined and the confessions extracted by coercion.

The three trials

The first trial, of the so-called "Trotskyite-Zinovievite Terrorist Centre", was held in August 1936. Its principal defendants were Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev, former allies and then rivals of Stalin. All sixteen defendants confessed and were sentenced to death; they were shot the day after the verdict.

The second trial, in January 1937, targeted the "Parallel Anti-Soviet Trotskyist Centre", including Karl Radek and Georgy Pyatakov. Of seventeen defendants, thirteen were executed and the rest sent to labour camps, where most soon died.

The third and largest trial, in March 1938, was that of the "Bloc of Rightists and Trotskyites", featuring Nikolai Bukharin, Alexei Rykov and former secret police chief Genrikh Yagoda. The accused were charged with an implausible array of crimes, including plotting assassinations, wrecking industry and conspiring with Germany and Japan. Twenty-one defendants were sentenced to death.

Confessions and evidence

The prosecution, led by state prosecutor Andrey Vyshinsky, rested almost entirely on the defendants' confessions; virtually no documentary evidence was produced. Later research and the testimony of survivors indicate that confessions were obtained through sleep deprivation, threats against defendants' families and false promises that their lives would be spared. The Dewey Commission, an independent inquiry convened in 1937 under philosopher John Dewey, examined the charges against Trotsky and concluded that the trials were frame-ups.

Significance

The Moscow trials eliminated the remaining "Old Bolshevik" leadership that had made the October Revolution, consolidating Stalin's absolute rule. They also provided the public justification for the mass terror of 1937–38, in which hundreds of thousands of people were executed. During Nikita Khrushchev's de-Stalinization, and again under glasnost in the 1980s, many of the defendants were officially rehabilitated.
