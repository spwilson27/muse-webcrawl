Below a critical temperature, a macroscopic fraction of bosons accumulates in the lowest-energy quantum state — Bose–Einstein condensation.

A Bose gas is a quantum-mechanical phase of matter formed by a gas of bosons — particles with integer spin that obey Bose–Einstein statistics. Unlike fermions, any number of bosons may occupy the same single-particle quantum state, and the mean occupation of a state of energy ε is given by the Bose–Einstein distribution:

⟨n(ε)⟩ = 1 / ( e(ε − μ)/kBT − 1 )

where μ is the chemical potential, kB is Boltzmann's constant, and T is the temperature. The statistics were introduced by Satyendra Nath Bose in 1924 for photons and generalised by Albert Einstein to massive particles in 1924–25. The ideal Bose gas is the quantum counterpart of a classical ideal gas and provides the standard theoretical model for understanding superfluidity and Bose–Einstein condensation.

Bose–Einstein condensation

Einstein noted a striking consequence: as an ideal Bose gas is cooled, the chemical potential rises toward the ground-state energy, and below a critical temperature Tc the excited states can no longer accommodate all the particles. A finite fraction of the particles then "condenses" into the single-particle ground state, forming a Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC). For a uniform gas of particles of mass m and number density n, the critical temperature scales as Tc ∝ n2/3/m. The condensate fraction grows as N0/N = 1 − (T/Tc)3/2 below Tc.

Bose–Einstein condensation was achieved experimentally in dilute atomic vapours in 1995, when groups led by Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman (rubidium-87, at about 170 nanokelvin) and Wolfgang Ketterle (sodium-23) cooled gases using laser cooling and evaporative cooling — work recognised by the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Thermodynamics of the ideal Bose gas

The statistical mechanics of the ideal Bose gas is developed using the grand canonical ensemble. Above Tc the gas behaves much like a classical gas but with quantum corrections that depend on the thermal de Broglie wavelength; its pressure and internal energy can be expressed through Bose functions gν(z), where z is the fugacity. At Tc the heat capacity has a cusp — continuous but with a discontinuous derivative — and below Tc it varies as T3/2. Because condensed particles carry no entropy and exert no pressure, the thermodynamics below Tc is determined entirely by the remaining excited particles.

Einstein's theory also underlies the description of blackbody radiation: a photon gas is a Bose gas with μ = 0 whose energy distribution is Planck's law.

Examples

  • Photons — blackbody radiation is a Bose gas of massless particles with zero chemical potential.
  • Phonons — quantised lattice vibrations in solids behave as a Bose gas and explain the low-temperature T3 heat capacity of insulators (Debye model).
  • Helium-4 — liquid 4He becomes a superfluid below the lambda point (2.17 K); Fritz London proposed that superfluidity is related to Bose–Einstein condensation of the helium atoms, though strong interactions mean only a fraction are in the condensate.
  • Dilute atomic gases — ultracold vapours of rubidium, sodium, lithium, and other species form nearly ideal Bose gases used to study condensation, vortices, and atom optics.
  • Exciton-polaritons and magnons — quasiparticle systems in which Bose-gas behaviour and condensation-like phenomena are also observed.

History

In June 1924, Bose, a physicist at the University of Dhaka, sent Einstein a paper deriving Planck's radiation law from a new counting of photon states. Einstein translated and championed the paper, then extended the method to material gases in two papers of 1924–25, predicting the condensation phenomenon. The prediction attracted limited attention until London's work on superfluid helium in 1938, and the ideal-gas transition was fully realised in the laboratory only with the atomic-gas experiments of 1995.
