Food waste

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Surplus food being sorted for redistribution at a food recovery warehouse]

Food waste (or food loss and waste) is food that is discarded, lost or uneaten along the supply chain, from farm to fork. It is a major economic, environmental and ethical problem: the food produced but never eaten consumes land, water and energy, and its decomposition in landfill generates methane, a potent greenhouse gas. Reducing food waste is a target of Sustainable Development Goal 12.3, which aims to halve retail and consumer food waste by 2030.

Scale

Estimates differ by method. The FAO has estimated that about 14% of the world's food is lost between harvest and retail, while the UN Environment Programme's Food Waste Index estimated that around 19% of food available to consumers was wasted at retail, food service and household level in 2022 — roughly a billion tonnes. Households account for the majority of consumer-level waste in most countries.

Causes

Losses before retail stem from harvesting limits, cosmetic standards rejecting "ugly" produce, poor storage and cold chains, and market gluts. At consumer level, causes include oversized portions and packaging, confusion over "best before" versus "use by" dates, poor meal planning and storage, and cultural norms of abundance. In lower-income countries losses concentrate early in the chain (storage and transport); in higher-income countries, late (retail and homes).

Responses

The food-recovery hierarchy prioritises prevention, then redistribution to people (food banks and apps selling surplus cheaply), animal feed, industrial uses such as anaerobic digestion, composting, and finally landfill. France in 2016 became the first country to require large supermarkets to donate unsold edible food rather than destroy it. Date-label reform, smaller pack sizes and public campaigns are among the common policy tools; the issue connects directly to food security, since wasted food is food someone else could have eaten.
