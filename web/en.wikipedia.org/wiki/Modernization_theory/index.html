Modernization theory

Modernization theory is a theory used to explain the process of modernisation within societies. It holds that "traditional" societies will develop as they adopt "modern" practices — industrialisation, urbanisation, mass education, market economies and democratic institutions — broadly following the path taken earlier by Western Europe and North America. The theory flourished among American social scientists in the 1950s and 1960s and shaped US foreign aid and development policy during the Cold War.

Key ideas

Walt W. Rostow's The Stages of Economic Growth (1960) proposed five stages, from "traditional society" through "take-off" to the "age of high mass consumption." Talcott Parsons supplied a sociological framework contrasting traditional and modern "pattern variables"; Daniel Lerner's The Passing of Traditional Society linked modernisation to media exposure and empathy; Seymour Martin Lipset argued that economic development fosters democracy. Underlying these works was a dichotomy between tradition and modernity, and an assumption that development is linear, progressive and convergent.

Criticism

From the late 1960s the theory was attacked from several directions. Dependency theorists such as Andre Gunder Frank and Raúl Prebisch argued that underdevelopment was not an original condition but the product of the same global capitalism that enriched the core countries, so following the Western path was impossible for the periphery. World-systems theory (Immanuel Wallerstein) developed this critique. Others attacked modernization theory as ethnocentric, as a rationalisation of Cold War intervention, and as empirically wrong: countries modernised selectively, combined "modern" economies with authoritarian politics, and produced "multiple modernities" (Shmuel Eisenstadt) rather than a single endpoint. The theory's influence waned in the 1970s but revived in modified forms after the Cold War in debates about globalisation, democratisation and the "end of history."
