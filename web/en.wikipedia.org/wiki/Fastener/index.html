Fastener

A fastener (US English) or fastening (UK English) is a hardware device that mechanically joins or affixes two or more objects together. In general, fasteners are used to create non-permanent joints — that is, joints that can be removed or dismantled without damaging the joining components — in contrast to welding and other permanent joining processes. Fasteners are among the most fundamental components of machines, structures, vehicles, furniture, and clothing.

Common mechanical fasteners: screws, bolts, nuts, washers, rivets and nails.

Types

  • Threaded fasteners: screws, bolts, and nuts, which engage helical threads. A bolt passes through unthreaded holes and is secured with a nut; a screw forms or mates with a thread in the material itself.
  • Nails: unthreaded pins driven into material, holding by friction and bending.
  • Rivets: permanent fasteners deformed after insertion to form a second head.
  • Pins and clips: dowel pins, cotter pins, circlips, retaining rings.
  • Anchors: devices that fasten objects to concrete, masonry, or drywall.
  • Clothing fasteners: buttons, zippers, hook-and-loop fasteners (Velcro), buckles, snaps, and toggles.
  • Other: clamps, staples, ties, latches, and cable ties.

Materials and standards

Fasteners are made from steel (the most common, in grades distinguished by strength classes such as ISO property classes 8.8 and 10.9 for bolts), stainless steel, brass, aluminium, titanium, and plastics. Standardisation of thread systems — principally the ISO metric thread and the Unified Thread Standard used in the United States — allows interchangeability between manufacturers. The choice of fastener depends on the load (tension, shear, vibration), environment (corrosion, temperature), and the materials being joined; washers spread loads and protect surfaces, while thread-locking compounds and lock nuts resist loosening under vibration.

History

Simple fastenings such as pegs, lashings, and brooches are prehistoric. The screw thread was described by Archimedes and used in Roman-era presses, but cheap mass-produced threaded fasteners arrived only with industrialisation; Joseph Whitworth's standardised thread of 1841 was an early milestone. The modern fastener industry produces billions of units annually, and fastener failure — from the de Havilland Comet disasters (attributed in part to stress concentration at fastener holes) to bridge collapses — has repeatedly driven improvements in design codes and inspection.
