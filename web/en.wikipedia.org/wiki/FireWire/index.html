FireWire

FireWire is Apple's brand name for the IEEE 1394 interface, a high-speed serial bus standard for connecting peripheral devices to a computer. Sony's implementation is branded i.LINK, and Texas Instruments calls it Lynx. FireWire was widely used for digital video cameras, external hard drives, and professional audio interfaces during the late 1990s and 2000s.

History

FireWire was developed by Apple beginning in the late 1980s, led by Michael Johansen Teener, and was standardised by the IEEE in 1995 as IEEE 1394. Apple included it on Macintosh computers from 1999 onward. A licensing dispute over royalties slowed early adoption relative to the cheaper USB standard.

Technical features

FireWire 400 (IEEE 1394a) transfers data at up to 400 Mbit/s, and FireWire 800 (IEEE 1394b) at up to 800 Mbit/s, in full-duplex. It supports isochronous data transfer — guaranteed bandwidth streams suited to video — peer-to-peer device communication without a host computer, daisy-chaining of up to 63 devices per bus, and bus power. Connectors include 4-pin (unpowered), 6-pin, and 9-pin variants.

Decline

Although technically competitive, FireWire lost ground to USB 2.0 and later USB 3.0 and Thunderbolt, which were cheaper to implement and more broadly supported. Apple removed FireWire ports from its laptops starting in 2012, and the standard is now considered legacy, though it persists in some professional audio and industrial equipment.
