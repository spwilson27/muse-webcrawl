The Zhuang people (Zhuang: Bouxcuengh; Chinese: 壮族; pinyin: Zhuàngzú) are a Tai-speaking ethnic group who mostly live in the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region in southern China. With a population of about 19.6 million, they are the largest of China's 56 officially recognized ethnic minorities. The Zhuang languages belong to the Tai branch of the Kra–Dai language family and are related to Thai and Lao.

History

The Zhuang descend from the ancient Baiyue peoples who inhabited southern China before Han Chinese expansion. During the Song dynasty, the Zhuang leader Nong Zhigao led a major rebellion and briefly established his own kingdom. Over the centuries, Zhuang lands were progressively integrated into the Chinese imperial system, often under hereditary local chieftains (tusi). In 1958, the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region was established.

Language and script

Zhuang comprises a cluster of related Tai varieties divided broadly into northern and southern dialect groups. Traditionally, Zhuang was written with Sawndip, a script of Chinese-derived characters used for folk songs and stories. In the 1950s a Latin-based alphabet was created for Standard Zhuang, based on the dialect of Wuming, and it is used in education and official contexts in Guangxi.

Culture

Traditional Zhuang culture centres on rice cultivation in the river valleys of Guangxi. The Zhuang are known for their song festivals, in which young people court through antiphonal singing; the Sanyuesan festival, held on the third day of the third lunar month, is the most important. Zhuang brocade, bronze drums, and the indigenous Mo religion — officiated by mo priests — are distinctive cultural features. The legendary figure Buluotuo is venerated as the creator ancestor of the Zhuang.
