Flemish Movement

The Flemish lion, symbol of the Flemish Movement.

The Flemish Movement (Dutch: Vlaamse Beweging) is an umbrella term which encompasses various political groups in the Belgian region of Flanders and French Flanders which advocate increased autonomy, the promotion of Dutch culture and language, and in some cases the secession of Flanders from Belgium or its unification with the Netherlands. It has been one of the most influential political movements in Belgium.

Origins

The movement arose in the 19th century in reaction to the privileged position of French in the Belgian state founded in 1830. Although Dutch speakers formed a majority of Belgium's population, Dutch was excluded from government, the courts, secondary and higher education, and the army's officer corps. Early Flemish activists, such as the writer Hendrik Conscience, whose novel The Lion of Flanders (1838) celebrated medieval Flemish heroism, worked mainly through cultural and linguistic campaigns, petitioning for the recognition of Dutch.

Language legislation

From the 1870s onward, a series of language laws gradually extended the use of Dutch: in criminal courts (1873), in public administration in Flanders (1878), and in state secondary education (1883). The Equality Law of 1898 formally placed Dutch on an equal footing with French as an official language. Nevertheless, French retained social prestige, and the francisation of Brussels continued.

The World Wars

During the First World War, grievances among Flemish soldiers at the Yser front, commanded largely by French-speaking officers, gave rise to the radical Front Movement. The German occupation authorities pursued a Flamenpolitik encouraging Flemish separatism, and a minority of activists collaborated, a legacy that tainted the movement after the war. A similar pattern of partial collaboration recurred under the German occupation of 1940–44, when some Flemish nationalists hoped for concessions, while others joined the resistance.

Post-war developments

After 1945 the movement regrouped around democratic parties, notably the Volksunie (founded 1954). Demands shifted from language rights to federalism. A fixed language border was established in 1962–63, and the university of Leuven was split into Dutch- and French-speaking institutions in 1968 after Flemish protests. Successive state reforms (1970, 1980, 1988–89, 1993, 2001, 2011) transformed Belgium into a federal state with autonomous Flemish and French communities and regions.

Contemporary movement

Modern Flemish nationalism is represented chiefly by the Nieuw-Vlaamse Alliantie (N-VA), a conservative party advocating confederalism, and by Vlaams Belang, a right-wing party favouring Flemish independence. Cultural organisations continue to promote Dutch language and Flemish heritage. The movement's demands range from further devolution within Belgium to outright independence, and its political claims remain central to Belgium's recurring government-formation crises.
