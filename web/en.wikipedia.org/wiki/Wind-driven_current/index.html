A wind-driven current is an ocean current driven primarily by the stress exerted by wind on the sea surface. Winds transfer momentum to the ocean through friction, setting the surface layer in motion; the rotation of the Earth, acting through the Coriolis effect, deflects this motion and produces a characteristic vertical structure known as the Ekman spiral. Wind-driven circulation dominates the upper few hundred metres of the ocean and organises the great subtropical gyres.

Ekman theory

The Norwegian oceanographer Vagn Walfrid Ekman, building on observations by Fridtjof Nansen during the Fram expedition, showed in 1902 that surface water moves at about 45° to the right of the wind direction in the Northern Hemisphere (to the left in the Southern Hemisphere), with the direction rotating and the speed decaying with depth. The net transport of the wind-driven layer — Ekman transport — is at 90° to the wind, to the right in the Northern Hemisphere.

Gyres and upwelling

Because the trade winds and westerlies blow in opposite directions at different latitudes, Ekman transport converges water into the centres of ocean basins, piling it up into broad mounds. Geostrophic flow around these mounds forms the subtropical gyres, such as the North Atlantic Gyre, bounded by currents including the Gulf Stream and the Kuroshio. Where Ekman transport carries surface water away from a coast or the equator, deeper nutrient-rich water rises to replace it — a process called upwelling that supports some of the world's most productive fisheries.

Sverdrup balance

Harald Sverdrup showed in 1947 that the depth-integrated wind-driven circulation is governed by the curl of the wind stress: the meridional transport of the ocean interior is proportional to the wind stress curl divided by the planetary vorticity gradient. This "Sverdrup balance" explains the broad, slow equatorward drift of the gyre interiors and the need for intense western boundary currents to return the flow.
