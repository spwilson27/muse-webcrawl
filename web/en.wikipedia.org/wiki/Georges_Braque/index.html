Georges Braque (13 May 1882 – 31 August 1963) was a major French painter, collagist, draughtsman, printmaker and sculptor. Together with Pablo Picasso he was the co-founder of Cubism, one of the most influential art movements of the twentieth century, and he played a key role in the development of collage.

Early work and Fauvism

Braque grew up in Le Havre, where his father ran a house-painting and decorating business, and trained initially as a decorator. Moving to Paris, he fell under the influence of the Fauves and exhibited brightly coloured landscapes with the group in 1906–07. A turning point came in 1907, when he saw Picasso's Les Demoiselles d'Avignon and the retrospective of Cézanne's work.

Cubism

In the summer of 1908 Braque painted landscapes at L'Estaque in the south of France, reducing houses and trees to geometric solids; the critic Louis Vauxcelles described them as composed of "little cubes", giving Cubism its name. From 1909 to 1914 Braque and Picasso worked in intense collaboration — Picasso compared them to "two mountaineers roped together" — first developing the fragmented, near-monochrome style of Analytic Cubism (Violin and Candlestick, 1910; The Portuguese, 1911) and then, from 1912, Synthetic Cubism. Braque invented the papier collé technique, pasting printed paper into drawings, and introduced stencilled lettering, sand and imitation wood grain into painting. He was also an early member of the Section d'Or circle and designed sets and costumes for Diaghilev's Ballets Russes.

Later career

Braque was seriously wounded in the head while serving in the First World War in 1915. After his recovery his work became more personal and contemplative: still lifes, interiors and, in the 1940s and 1950s, the celebrated Atelier (Studio) series and paintings of birds in flight. He received the Grand Prize for Painting at the 1948 Venice Biennale and in 1961 became the first living artist to have his work exhibited at the Louvre. He died in Paris in 1963 and was given a state funeral.
