Purkinje cells are large neurons found in the cerebellar cortex of the brain. They are among the largest and most elaborately branched neurons in the mammalian nervous system, and they form the sole output channel of the cerebellar cortex: every computation performed by the cortex must leave through a Purkinje cell. Named after the Czech anatomist Jan Evangelista Purkyně, who described them in 1837, Purkinje cells release the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA onto neurons of the deep cerebellar and vestibular nuclei.

Anatomy

Purkinje cell bodies are arranged in a single sheet — the Purkinje layer — between the molecular and granule cell layers of the cerebellar cortex. Each cell body sends out a spectacular dendritic tree that branches almost entirely within a single plane, like a fan or a pressed tree, oriented perpendicular to the long axis of the cerebellar fold (folium). A human Purkinje cell's dendrites may receive on the order of 150,000–200,000 synaptic contacts, more than almost any other neuron type.

Inputs

Two excitatory systems converge on the Purkinje cell. Parallel fibres, the axons of granule cells, run along the folium and make weak synapses on the spiny dendritic branchlets — each Purkinje cell samples input from a vast number of granule cells. A single powerful climbing fibre from the inferior olivary nucleus wraps around the dendritic tree and makes hundreds of synapses, reliably triggering a distinctive "complex spike". Purkinje cells fire simple spikes (driven by parallel fibres) at high rates and complex spikes at roughly 1 Hz.

Role in motor learning

Long-term depression (LTD) at parallel-fibre–Purkinje-cell synapses, induced when parallel fibre activity coincides with a climbing-fibre signal, is a leading cellular model of cerebellar motor learning (the Marr–Albus–Ito framework). By this mechanism the cerebellum fine-tunes movement, posture, balance and conditioned responses such as eyeblink conditioning.

Clinical significance

Purkinje cells are selectively vulnerable in several disorders. Their loss or dysfunction produces cerebellar ataxia — uncoordinated movement — in spinocerebellar ataxias, chronic alcoholism, paraneoplastic syndromes (anti-Yo antibodies target Purkinje cells), and certain toxic and metabolic conditions. They are also implicated in research on autism and essential tremor.
