Constructive empiricism

Constructive empiricism is a form of empiricism in the philosophy of science developed by Bas van Fraassen, most fully in The Scientific Image (1980). It holds that the aim of science is to produce theories that are empirically adequate — true about what is observable — while remaining agnostic about the truth of claims concerning unobservable entities.

Core thesis

Science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate; and acceptance of a theory involves as belief only that it is empirically adequate.

Accepting a theory, for van Fraassen, means believing it gets the observable phenomena right, without committing to the existence of the unobservable entities (electrons, genes as molecules, etc.) the theory postulates. This is a middle way between scientific realism and instrumentalism.

Observable versus unobservable

Van Fraassen draws the observable/unobservable line with the unaided human senses (extended to what could in principle be observed by humans). He accepts no sharp boundary problems as fatal: borderline cases like distant galaxies are observable in principle, while subatomic particles are not.

Criticism

Critics argue the distinction is arbitrary (why privilege human senses?), that it makes science's success miraculous (the no-miracles argument), and that observability itself is theory-laden. Van Fraassen has responded in later work including Laws and Symmetry (1989) and The Empirical Stance (2002).
