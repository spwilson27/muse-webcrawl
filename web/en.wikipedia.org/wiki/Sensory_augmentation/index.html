Sensory augmentation
A tactile compass belt, an example of sensory augmentation
FieldHuman–computer interaction, neuroscience, assistive technology
Related conceptsSensory substitution, neuroplasticity, extended cognition

Sensory augmentation is the use of technology to extend or enhance the human senses beyond their natural limits, by converting information that is normally imperceptible — such as magnetic fields, ultraviolet light, or distant events — into stimuli that an existing sense can register, typically touch, hearing, or vision. It is distinguished from sensory substitution, which replaces the input of an impaired sense, although the two fields share much of their technology and theory.

History

Research into artificial senses dates to the 1960s, when Paul Bach-y-Rita demonstrated that visual information delivered through tactile displays could be perceived in a quasi-visual manner by blind subjects, an early finding on brain plasticity. In the 2000s and 2010s, inexpensive sensors and microcontrollers enabled a wave of wearable augmentation experiments, including magnetic implants, compass belts, and devices developed by researchers and "biohacker" communities.

Examples

The feelSpace belt, developed at the University of Osnabrück, vibrates in the direction of magnetic north; after several weeks of wear, users reported changes in spatial perception and navigation, suggesting the new signal had been integrated into their perceptual system. North Sense and similar body-worn devices indicate orientation through vibration. The eyeborg, worn by colour-blind artist Neil Harbisson, translates colour into sound frequencies through an antenna-like sensor. Seismic senses, echolocation aids and ultraviolet cameras have also been explored experimentally.

Mechanisms

Augmentation is generally held to work through neuroplasticity: with training, the brain learns to interpret a novel but consistent stream of stimuli as a new perceptual channel rather than as mere touch or sound. Philosophers and cognitive scientists debate whether long-term use produces a genuinely new sense (qualia) or an unusually fluent form of inference, a question connected to theories of extended and embodied cognition.
