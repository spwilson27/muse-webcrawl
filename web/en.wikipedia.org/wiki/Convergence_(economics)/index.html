Convergence (economics)

The catch-up effect: growth rates tend to decline as income rises

In economics, convergence refers to the hypothesis that poorer economies' per capita incomes will tend to grow at faster rates than richer economies, so that in the long run all economies should converge in terms of per capita income. The idea is also known as the catch-up effect. In the Solow–Swan growth model, poorer countries have lower capital per worker and therefore a higher marginal return to investment, which propels faster growth until they approach their steady state.

Types of convergence

Absolute convergence predicts that poor economies will catch up regardless of their characteristics; empirical support for it is weak across the whole world. Conditional convergence holds that countries converge toward their own steady states, determined by savings rates, population growth, human capital, and institutions, so poor countries with sound fundamentals can catch up while others stagnate. Researchers also distinguish beta-convergence (faster growth of the initially poorer) from sigma-convergence (falling dispersion of incomes).

Evidence

Convergence is well documented within groups of similar economies, such as the US states, Japanese prefectures, and the original members of the European Union, typically at a rate of about 2 percent per year. The rapid growth of East Asian economies after 1960 is a prominent example of catch-up, while the persistent divergence of much of sub-Saharan Africa illustrates its limits.
