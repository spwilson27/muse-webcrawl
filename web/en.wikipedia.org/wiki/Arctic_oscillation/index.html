Arctic oscillation

The Arctic oscillation (AO), also known as the Northern Annular Mode (NAM), is a dominant pattern of non-seasonal sea-level pressure variability north of 20°N latitude. It describes the seesaw of atmospheric mass between the Arctic and the mid-latitudes and strongly influences winter weather across North America, Europe, and northern Asia.

Definition

The AO is defined statistically as the leading empirical orthogonal function of monthly sea-level pressure anomalies over the Northern Hemisphere. Its index is positive when surface pressure is anomalously low over the Arctic and high over the mid-latitudes, and negative when the pattern reverses. The concept was introduced by David Thompson and John Wallace in 1998. The closely related North Atlantic Oscillation can be regarded as a regional expression of the same annular mode.

Effects

In the positive phase, a strong polar vortex keeps frigid air bottled up near the pole, the jet stream flows in a relatively straight west-to-east path, and northern Europe and much of the northern United States and Asia tend to be warmer and (in Europe) wetter than average, while Greenland and the Mediterranean are colder and drier. In the negative phase, the vortex weakens and wavers, pressure rises over the Arctic, and cold air spills southward in outbreaks — bringing severe winter weather to the mid-latitudes, as during the strongly negative winter of 2009–10.

Variability and trends

The AO varies on timescales from days to decades with no regular period. It trended positive during the 1980s and early 1990s, contributing to Arctic warming, and researchers continue to debate proposed links between Arctic sea-ice loss, a wavering jet stream, and mid-latitude weather extremes. The pattern has a counterpart in the Southern Hemisphere, the Antarctic oscillation or Southern Annular Mode.
