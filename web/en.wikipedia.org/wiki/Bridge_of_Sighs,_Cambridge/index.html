The Bridge of Sighs in Cambridge, England, is a covered bridge built in 1831 across the River Cam within the grounds of St John's College, University of Cambridge. It connects the college's Third Court, on the east bank of the river, with the New Court on the west bank. Designed by the architect Henry Hutchinson, it is one of Cambridge's best-known landmarks and a Grade I listed building.

History and name

The bridge takes its name from the Bridge of Sighs in Venice, which it was said to resemble, although in fact the two bridges have little in common beyond being enclosed. A persistent student legend holds that the Cambridge bridge was named for the sighs of students crossing it on their way to examinations; Queen Victoria is said to have declared it, along with King's College Chapel, one of the sights not to be missed in the city. Before the bridge was built, the college's courts on either side of the Cam were linked by a wooden bridge near the college kitchens.

Architecture

The bridge is a single-span stone crossing with an enclosed, neo-Gothic covered walkway. Its decorative traceried openings — a row of tall, pointed-arch windows along each side — and its crenellated parapet give it a picturesque collegiate character. Because the western landing meets the river bank at a lower point, the bridge incorporates a ramp within the covered passage. The bridge is reserved to members of the college and their guests and is not open to the general public, though it is a favourite subject of photographs taken from the river by passing punts.

Pranks

The bridge has featured in famous student pranks. On two occasions in 1963, members of the college succeeded in suspending an Austin Seven car beneath the bridge: in June a group lowered a derelict car from the bridge by ropes, and a week later another group repeated the feat, the car this time being cut down into the river and later recovered.
