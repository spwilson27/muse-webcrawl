Hartmann's operation

Hartmann's operation, also called Hartmann's procedure or proctosigmoidectomy, is the surgical removal of the rectum and sigmoid colon with the creation of an end colostomy and closure of the rectal stump. It is named after the French surgeon Henri Albert Hartmann, who described it in 1921.

Indications

The procedure is performed when a primary anastomosis would be unsafe — most commonly as emergency surgery for complicated diverticulitis (perforation or obstruction), obstructing or perforated colorectal cancer, ischaemic colitis, or trauma. By removing the diseased segment and diverting the faecal stream, it avoids the risk of anastomotic leakage in an unstable or septic patient.

Procedure

Through a laparotomy (or laparoscopically), the diseased sigmoid colon and upper rectum are resected. The proximal colon is brought out through the abdominal wall as an end colostomy, and the distal rectal stump is stapled or sutured closed and left in the pelvis. The anatomy may later be restored by Hartmann's reversal, in which the colostomy is taken down and joined to the rectal stump, though a substantial proportion of patients never undergo reversal.

Outcomes

Because it is often performed on elderly, acutely ill patients, Hartmann's operation carries appreciable morbidity and mortality. Reversal is a major operation in its own right, and modern practice increasingly considers primary anastomosis or laparoscopic lavage in selected cases of perforated diverticulitis.
