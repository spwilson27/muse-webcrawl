Irish Hobby

Country of originIreland
StatusExtinct
UseRiding, light cavalry, warfare

The Irish Hobby was a breed of horse developed in Ireland prior to the 13th century, now extinct. It was a small, sturdy and swift horse, noted for its comfortable ambling gait, and was widely sought after throughout medieval Europe for riding and for use by light cavalry.

History

The breed is thought to descend in part from horses brought to Ireland by early settlers, possibly including stock related to the Asturcón of northern Spain and the Garrano of Portugal. Irish Hobbies were exported in large numbers to England and Scotland, where mounted troops known as hobelars — light cavalrymen riding hobbies — became a feature of warfare in the 13th and 14th centuries, notably during the Scottish Wars of Independence under Edward I and Edward III.

The horse's natural pacing gait made it comfortable for long journeys, and the word "hobby" in English came to be associated with a small horse or pony, and later with a pastime pursued for pleasure. The breed declined as heavier horses were favoured for warfare and as systematic crossing altered Irish horse stock; the modern Connemara pony and Irish Draught are sometimes said to retain hobby ancestry.

Characteristics

Irish Hobbies stood roughly 13 to 14 hands high. They were hardy, sure-footed and fast over rough ground, qualities that suited the boggy and broken terrain of Ireland and the raiding style of warfare common there.
