Combinatorial geometry

Combinatorial geometry is a branch of mathematics concerned with the combinatorial properties of geometric objects — for example, how many regions an arrangement of lines divides the plane into, or the maximum number of unit distances determined by a set of points. In modern usage the term overlaps heavily with discrete geometry, and some authors use the two interchangeably; others reserve "combinatorial geometry" for the study of finite incidence structures and extremal problems.

Typical problems

  • Unit distances: What is the maximum number of pairs of points at distance exactly 1 among n points in the plane? (posed by Paul Erdős)
  • Sylvester–Gallai theorem: Any finite set of points not all on one line determines a line passing through exactly two of them.
  • Distinct distances: The Erdős distinct distances problem, largely resolved by Guth and Katz in 2010.
  • Incidence bounds: The Szemerédi–Trotter theorem bounds the number of incidences between points and lines.
  • Happy ending problem: Any sufficiently large set of points in general position contains a convex polygon of a given size.

Methods and connections

Combinatorial geometry draws on graph theory, the polynomial method, and probabilistic arguments. Its results inform computational geometry, coding theory, and incidence geometry. Erdős was the field's most prolific problem-poser in the 20th century, and many of its central conjectures carry his name.
