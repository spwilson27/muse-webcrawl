The Doomsday Clock is a symbol that represents the estimated likelihood of a human-made global catastrophe, maintained since 1947 by the members of the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists. The clock is a metaphor, not a prediction, with midnight representing global disaster. Originally focused on nuclear weapons, its assessment now also includes climate change, disruptive technologies, and other threats.

History

The Clock first appeared on the cover of the Bulletin in June 1947, designed by artist Martyl Langsdorf and set at seven minutes to midnight, largely for visual balance. In 1949, after the Soviet Union's first atomic test, it was moved to three minutes. The farthest setting from midnight, 17 minutes, came in 1991 at the end of the Cold War and the signing of the Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty.

Recent settings

In January 2020 the Clock was set to 100 seconds to midnight, the closest ever at that point, citing nuclear risks and climate change. In January 2023 it advanced to 90 seconds, largely because of the war in Ukraine. In January 2025 it moved to 89 seconds to midnight — the closest setting in its history — citing nuclear escalation risks, climate disruption, biological threats, and the spread of misinformation amplified by artificial intelligence.

How it is set

The setting is decided annually by the Bulletin's Science and Security Board in consultation with its Board of Sponsors, which includes Nobel laureates. The decision is announced at a press event each January.
