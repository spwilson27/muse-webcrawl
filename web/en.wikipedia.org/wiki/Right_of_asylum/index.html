The right of asylum is an ancient legal concept under which a person persecuted by their own country may be protected by another sovereign authority. In modern international law it is anchored in Article 14 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948): "Everyone has the right to seek and to enjoy in other countries asylum from persecution" (with exceptions for prosecutions genuinely arising from non-political crimes or acts contrary to the purposes of the United Nations). Scholars debate the extent to which this confers an individual right to be granted asylum, as opposed to the right of states to grant it; in practice, protection is principally delivered through refugee status and the prohibition of refoulement.

Refugee protection

The 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol define a refugee as a person outside their country of nationality who is unable or unwilling to return owing to a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion. Article 33 prohibits states from expelling or returning ("refouler") a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened on those grounds — the principle of non-refoulement, regarded as customary international law and echoed in torture and human rights treaties. Complementary protection is given in many systems to persons facing torture, inhuman treatment, or indiscriminate violence even if they fall outside the refugee definition.

History

Asylum has roots in the sacred refuges of the ancient world — Greek temples, Hebrew cities of refuge, and the sanctuary offered by churches in medieval Europe — and in early modern rules on the extradition of political offenders. The mass displacements of the twentieth century produced the first international refugee instruments under the League of Nations (including the Nansen passport) and, after the Second World War, the 1951 Convention and the office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR).

Contemporary practice

Asylum systems determine claims individually, usually through administrative procedures with rights of appeal. Regional systems — the Common European Asylum System in the EU, and broader definitions in the African Union's 1969 Refugee Convention and the 1984 Cartagena Declaration in Latin America — extend protection beyond the 1951 definition. Burden-sharing, safe-third-country arrangements, and deterrence policies remain politically contested, while UNHCR reports global forced displacement exceeding 100 million people in the 2020s.
