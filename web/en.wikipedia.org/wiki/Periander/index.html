Periander

Periander (Greek: Περίανδρος)
Reign: c. 627 – c. 585 BC
Title: Tyrant of Corinth
Predecessor: Cypselus
Notable: Counted among the Seven Sages of Greece (in some lists)

Bust of Periander, tyrant of Corinth

Periander (died c. 585 BC) was the second tyrant of Corinth, succeeding his father Cypselus around 627 BC and ruling for some forty years. Under him Corinth reached the height of its archaic prosperity and power, though ancient sources also remember Periander as one of the archetypally cruel tyrants.

Rule

Periander continued his father's policies of overseas expansion, controlling colonies and trade routes including Corcyra and Potidaea, and maintaining influence over the Isthmus of Corinth, where he is said to have attempted (or at least conceived) a canal and instead built the Diolkos, a paved trackway across which ships were hauled between the Corinthian and Saronic gulfs. He patronised trade, industry, and the arts — the poet Arion was attached to his court — and maintained alliances with Thrasybulus of Miletus and ties to Egypt and Lydia. Herodotus relates the famous anecdote in which Thrasybulus silently advised Periander, by lopping the tallest ears of grain, to eliminate the most prominent citizens.

Reputation

Later in his reign Periander was remembered for harshness: Herodotus tells of his killing his wife Melissa, his war with his father-in-law Procles of Epidaurus, the revolt of Corcyra, and the death of his son Lycophron. Yet other traditions counted Periander among the Seven Sages of Greece, crediting him with maxims such as "practice makes perfect" and "forethought in all things". Aristotle regarded him as the inventor of certain tyrannical devices, such as occupying the population with great public works to prevent sedition. On his death the tyranny passed to his nephew Psammetichus, whose overthrow three years later ended Cypselid rule.
