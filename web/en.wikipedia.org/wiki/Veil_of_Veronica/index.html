Veil of Veronica

The Veil of Veronica, or Sudarium (Latin for "sweat cloth"), also known as the Vernicle and often called simply the Veronica, is a Christian relic consisting of a piece of cloth said to bear an image of the Holy Face of Jesus produced by other than human means (an acheiropoieton, "made without hand"). Various existing images have been claimed to be the authentic imprint, receiving worship by many Christians, especially Roman Catholics.

The legend

According to tradition, Saint Veronica, a pious woman of Jerusalem, encountered Jesus on the Via Dolorosa as he carried the Cross to Calvary. Moved with pity, she offered him her veil so that he could wipe his face; when he returned it, the image of his face was miraculously imprinted upon the cloth. The episode is commemorated in the sixth Station of the Cross, though Veronica is not named in the canonical Gospels.

The name "Veronica" itself is sometimes explained as a compound of the Latin vera ("true") and Greek eikon ("image") — the "true image" of Christ.

The relic

A cloth identified as the Veil has been kept in St Peter's Basilica in Rome since the Middle Ages; it was already a major object of pilgrimage by the 13th century and was shown on particular feasts, though it is rarely displayed today and survives in a damaged state behind glass. Other cloths in Genoa, Jaén, Alicante, and Manoppello have also been venerated as the Veil or as copies of it, and the Manoppello image has attracted renewed interest in recent decades.

In art

The Veil is a frequent subject in Western art: Veronica is depicted holding up the cloth bearing Christ's face, often shown frontally and crowned with thorns. Memling, El Greco, and many others treated the theme, and the image became one of the most reproduced devotional images of late medieval Europe.
