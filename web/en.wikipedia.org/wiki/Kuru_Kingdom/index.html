The Kuru Kingdom was a Vedic Indo-Aryan state of ancient India, attested in the period roughly 1200–500 BCE in the area of modern Haryana, Delhi, and the western Ganges–Yamuna doab. Formed by the union of the Bharata and Puru tribes, it was the first attested state-level polity of the Indian subcontinent and the centre in which much of the Vedic corpus was composed and codified.

History

The Bharata tribe, celebrated in the Rigveda for King Sudās's victory in the Battle of the Ten Kings, settled in the Sarasvatī–Dṛṣadvatī valley. In the early first millennium BCE the Bharatas merged with the Purus to form the Kuru people, whose realm — Kurukṣetra, the "field of the Kurus" — became the sacred heartland of Brahmanical culture. Under kings such as Parikṣit and Janamejaya, remembered in the later Vedic texts, the Kuru court patronised the elaboration of the śrauta ritual, the redaction of the Vedas, and the composition of the Brāhmaṇas and early Upaniṣads.

Archaeologically, the Kuru realm is associated with the Painted Grey Ware culture. Its capital is placed first at Āsandīvat, and in later tradition at Hastinapura on the Ganges. A flood at Hastinapura is said to have driven King Nicakṣu to move the capital to Kauśāmbī. By about 500 BCE the kingdom had declined and been absorbed into the rising mahājanapada order, with Panchala eclipsing it to the east.

In the Mahābhārata

The Sanskrit epic Mahābhārata is set in the Kuru royal house: the dynastic struggle between the Kaurava and Pāṇḍava cousins culminates in the Kurukṣetra War on the ancient sacred field of the kingdom. While the epic's events are legendary in scale, historians regard its setting as a distant memory of the real Kuru realm of the early Iron Age.

Legacy

Kuru's codification of Vedic religion shaped classical Hinduism: the region is praised in the texts as Brahmāvarta and Brahmarṣideśa, the land where dharma was perfected, and the Bhagavad Gītā opens on "the field of dharma, the field of the Kurus."
