Symmetry breaking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Illustration of a ball resting in the brim of a Mexican-hat potential, having rolled to one side]
Spontaneous symmetry breaking: the ground state chooses one of many equivalent minima.

Symmetry breaking in physics describes a system whose equations are symmetric under some transformation, but whose actual state is not. In spontaneous symmetry breaking, the laws and the Hamiltonian are symmetric while the lowest-energy state (the vacuum) is not — a ball balanced atop a circular ridge must roll down to one side, though every side is equivalent.

The concept runs through modern physics: ferromagnetism (the magnetisation picks a direction), Superconductivity (gauge symmetry breaking in BCS theory, where the condensate of Cooper pairs chooses a phase), the Higgs mechanism (electroweak symmetry broken by the Higgs field's vacuum value), and chiral symmetry breaking in Quantum chromodynamics. Its field-theoretic formulation — by Nambu, Goldstone, Higgs, Brout, Englert and others in the 1960s — explains the appearance of massless Goldstone bosons when continuous symmetries break, and of massive gauge bosons when the broken symmetry is gauged.

Explicit vs spontaneous

Explicit breaking puts asymmetry into the laws themselves (the weak interaction's built-in handedness); spontaneous breaking hides it in the state. The Higgs case is the gauged one: the would-be Goldstone modes are 'eaten' to give the W and Z bosons their longitudinal polarisations and masses — the mechanism that makes the Electroweak interaction consistent. In cosmology, symmetry-breaking phase transitions in the early universe are invoked to explain the separation of the fundamental forces and possibly the origin of cosmic structure (inflation).
