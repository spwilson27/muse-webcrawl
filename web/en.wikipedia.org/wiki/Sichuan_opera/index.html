Sichuan opera

Sichuan opera (Chinese: 川剧; pinyin: Chuānjù) is a form of Chinese opera originating in China's Sichuan province, with a history of some three hundred years. It is performed in Chengdu, Chongqing, and other parts of southwest China, and is distinguished by its witty libretti, its relaxed and humorous style, and above all by bian lian (变脸), the secret art of face-changing, in which performers switch painted silk masks in a fraction of a second.

A Sichuan opera performer demonstrating bian lian (face-changing).

History

Sichuan opera took shape during the Qing dynasty, when several regional singing styles brought into Sichuan by migrant troupes — including Kunqu, Huqin, Tanxi, and Dengxi traditions — fused with local folk arts. Five vocal styles are traditionally recognized within the form. Unlike the highly stylized Peking opera, Sichuan opera developed a reputation for vivid, earthy libretti drawn from folk tales, historical romance, and ghost stories.

Performance

Performances combine singing, acrobatics, comedy, and special stunts. Signature techniques include face-changing, fire-spitting, beard manipulation, and "third eye" tricks in which a performer's forehead ornament appears to move. Face-changing is a closely guarded secret passed within families and troupes; the performer, with a breath, a blink, or a movement of a fan, swaps one mask for another of a different colour and expression, revealing the character's changing emotions. Male performers have traditionally dominated the art, though women also perform it today.

Repertoire and music

The singing is typically in the Sichuan dialect, accompanied by a small ensemble led by percussion and the piercing gaohu-family fiddles and clappers; a chorus of helpers (known as bangqiang) comments on the action from the orchestra, a distinctive feature of the form. Well-known plays include adaptations of The Legend of the White Snake and stories from the classical novels.
