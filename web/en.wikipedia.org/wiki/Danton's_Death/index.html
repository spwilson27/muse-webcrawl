Danton's Death

Danton's Death (German: Dantons Tod) is a play by Georg Büchner, written in 1835 and first published the same year; it was first performed only in 1902, decades after the author's death. Büchner's first and longest play, it dramatises the last weeks of the French revolutionary Georges Danton, his conflict with Maximilien Robespierre, and his execution during the Reign of Terror in 1794.

The play is considered a landmark of German drama, notable for its documentary use of historical speeches, its fragmented scene structure, and its bleak, philosophical meditation on history, revolution and determinism.

Background

Büchner wrote the play in about five weeks in early 1835, partly to finance his flight from arrest for his revolutionary pamphlet The Hessian Courier (1834). Drawing extensively on historical sources — roughly a sixth of the text consists of adapted documentary material, including speeches by Danton and Robespierre — Büchner portrayed revolution as a devouring force beyond individual control.

Plot

Paris, spring 1794. Danton, weary of bloodshed, wishes to end the Terror, but his former ally Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety see his "indulgence" as treason. Danton refuses to flee, remarking that a man cannot take his country away on the soles of his shoes. He and his supporters, including Camille Desmoulins, are arrested, tried before the Revolutionary Tribunal in a rigged proceeding, and guillotined on 5 April 1794. The play ends with Lucile Desmoulins, mad with grief, crying "Long live the King!" so that she too will be executed and follow her husband.

Themes

The play explores historical fatalism ("the Revolution is like Saturn, it devours its own children"), the conflict between hedonism and virtue, and the terror of a world without meaning — Danton's speeches contain some of the earliest expressions of modern existential pessimism and nihilism in German literature.

Reception

Unperformed in Büchner's lifetime, Danton's Death was rediscovered in the early 20th century and became a cornerstone of the modern repertoire, inspiring Alban Berg's operatic contemporary Wozzeck in spirit; Gottfried von Einem composed an opera Dantons Tod (1947). It is now regarded as one of the greatest German plays.
