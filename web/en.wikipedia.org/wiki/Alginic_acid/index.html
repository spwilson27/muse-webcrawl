Alginic acid, also called algin, is a naturally occurring, edible polysaccharide found in brown algae. It is hydrophilic and forms a viscous gum when hydrated. Its salts, the alginates — including sodium alginate (E401), potassium alginate (E402) and calcium alginate (E404) — are widely used in the food, pharmaceutical and textile industries.

Structure

Alginic acid is a linear copolymer of two uronic acids: β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G), linked by (1→4) glycosidic bonds and arranged in homopolymeric blocks (MM and GG) and alternating sequences. The proportion and arrangement of the blocks vary with the algal species and determine the gelling properties: sequences rich in guluronate form strong, brittle gels with divalent cations such as calcium in the so-called "egg-box" model, while mannuronate-rich alginates give softer, more elastic gels.

Sources and production

Commercial alginates are extracted from giant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera), Laminaria species, Ascophyllum nodosum and other brown seaweeds harvested off the coasts of Norway, Scotland, France, California and South Africa. British chemist Edward C. C. Stanford first isolated the substance in 1881. Certain bacteria, including Azotobacter and Pseudomonas species, also produce alginate-like polymers.

Uses

Sodium alginate is used as a thickener and emulsifier in ice cream, salad dressings and sauces; calcium alginate fibres make absorbent wound dressings and dental impression material; alginate gels are used in molecular gastronomy for "spherification", in textile printing pastes, in controlled-release drug formulations, and in cell encapsulation for biotechnology research.
