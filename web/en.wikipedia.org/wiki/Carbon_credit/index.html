A carbon credit is a generic term for any tradable certificate or permit representing the right to emit a set amount of carbon dioxide or an equivalent amount of another greenhouse gas. One credit conventionally represents one metric tonne of CO₂ equivalent (tCO₂e). Carbon credits are a central component of national and international emissions trading schemes designed to mitigate growth in greenhouse gas concentrations.

Types

Allowances are issued by regulators under cap-and-trade systems such as the EU Emissions Trading System: a cap sets the total permitted emissions, and firms that emit less can sell surplus allowances to those that emit more. Carbon offsets are credits generated by projects that reduce, avoid, or remove emissions elsewhere — renewable energy, methane capture, reforestation, and direct air capture among them — and may be sold in compliance programmes or in the voluntary carbon market, where companies buy them to support net-zero claims.

History

Tradable carbon units were institutionalised by the 1997 Kyoto Protocol through the Clean Development Mechanism and Joint Implementation, which generated Certified Emission Reductions and Emission Reduction Units. The EU ETS, launched in 2005, became the world's largest compliance market, and voluntary standards such as Verra's Verified Carbon Standard and the Gold Standard came to certify most voluntary offsets.

Criticism

Carbon credits have been criticised over additionality (whether a project would have happened anyway), permanence (for example, credited forests later burning), leakage, and double counting. Investigations into avoided-deforestation (REDD+) credits in the 2020s intensified scrutiny and prompted reforms to standards and claims codes. Supporters argue that, rigorously verified, credits channel finance to mitigation that would not otherwise occur.
