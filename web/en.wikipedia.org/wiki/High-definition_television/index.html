High-definition television

High-definition television (HDTV) describes a television system which provides a substantially higher image resolution than the previous generation of technologies, standard-definition television (SDTV). Common HDTV formats are 720p, 1080i and 1080p, in a 16:9 widescreen aspect ratio, compared with the 4:3, 480-line systems of the NTSC era.

History

Japan's NHK began research into high-definition systems in the 1960s, leading to the analogue MUSE/Hi-Vision system broadcast from the 1990s. In the United States, the Grand Alliance of electronics companies developed digital standards adopted by the FCC in 1996 as ATSC, while Europe developed the DVB family of standards. Digital compression (MPEG-2, later H.264) made practical terrestrial, satellite and cable HDTV broadcasting possible, and during the 2000s HDTV sets and services became the norm worldwide.

Successors

HDTV has been succeeded in the premium market by ultra-high-definition television (UHDTV), offering 4K (2160p) and 8K resolutions, wide colour gamut and high dynamic range.
