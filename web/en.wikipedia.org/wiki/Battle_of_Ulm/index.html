Battle of Ulm

The Battle of Ulm was a series of manoeuvres and combats fought in September and October 1805, during the War of the Third Coalition, in which Napoleon Bonaparte's Grande Armée encircled and destroyed the Austrian army of General Karl Mack von Leiberich in and around the city of Ulm in Württemberg. Strictly a campaign rather than a single battle, Ulm is celebrated as one of the greatest strategic envelopments in military history: Napoleon, marching from the Channel coast, crossed the Danube behind the Austrians, severed their communications with Vienna, and compelled Mack to surrender on 20 October 1805 with the bulk of his army — at trivial cost to the French.

Background

In August 1805 Austria joined the Third Coalition, and Mack — the effective, if not nominal, commander of the Austrian army in Germany — advanced into Bavaria and occupied a position around Ulm on the Danube, there to await the Russian army marching from the east. Mack expected the French to come through the Black Forest, directly from the west, and was slow to grasp that Napoleon intended something else entirely.

Napoleon had abandoned the planned invasion of England, renamed his invasion force the Grande Armée, and hurled it eastward in seven corps along parallel roads — a march of some 600 km (370 mi) completed in a matter of weeks. The French crossed the Rhine on 25 September and wheeled south-east, passing north of Mack and crossing the Danube at Donauwörth, Ingolstadt and Neuburg on 6–7 October. The Grande Armée now stood across the Austrian line of retreat to Vienna.

The encirclement

A series of sharp actions tightened the noose. At Wertingen (8 October) and Günzburg (9 October) French corps struck Austrian detachments falling back on Ulm. At Haslach-Jungingen (11 October) a single French division under Dupont repelled a much larger Austrian force and convinced Mack the French were weaker and nearer than they were. At Elchingen (14 October) Ney stormed the abbey heights north of the Danube, completing the investment of the city; Murat's cavalry meanwhile harried the Austrian corps trying to escape eastward, and Werneck's corps surrendered at Trochtelfingen on 19 October. Archduke Ferdinand cut his way out with some cavalry and reached Bohemia, but Mack, his army starving, outmanoeuvred and deceived by French agents and false reports, lost his nerve for a breakout.

Surrender and aftermath

On 20 October 1805 Mack laid down his arms at Ulm. Some 25,000–30,000 Austrian soldiers marched out and surrendered their muskets and colours — Napoleon, it was said, had destroyed an army with his legs rather than his bayonets, remarking that he had beaten the Austrians "simply by marches". The road to Vienna lay open; the capital fell on 13 November. With Mack's army gone, the Russian forces under Kutuzov fell back into Moravia, where Napoleon followed and destroyed the Coalition at Austerlitz on 2 December. On the same day Mack capitulated at Ulm, the Royal Navy annihilated the combined Franco-Spanish fleet at Trafalgar — the twin victories, on land and sea, that defined the war of 1805.
