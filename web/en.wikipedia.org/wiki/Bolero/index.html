Bolero

Bolero is a genre of song and dance originating in Spain in the late eighteenth century, performed in triple time and moderate tempo, and later the name of a distinct Latin American romantic song tradition that spread through Cuba, Mexico and the wider Spanish-speaking world.

Spanish bolero

The Spanish bolero emerged around 1780 from the seguidilla, combining dance, song and guitar accompaniment. It is danced by a solo performer or couple in 3/4 time, with characteristic heel-work, turns and sudden statuesque pauses, the dancer often accompanying the steps with castanets. It became a staple of the Spanish lyric theatre and was taken up by classical composers; the most famous example is Maurice Ravel's orchestral Boléro (1928), built on a single repeated theme over an insistent snare-drum rhythm, originally commissioned as a ballet by Ida Rubinstein.

Latin American bolero

In Cuba in the 1880s, the bolero absorbed influences from the Spanish form, the danzón and Afro-Cuban rhythm, slowing into a sentimental ballad in 2/4 or 4/4 time with lyrics of romantic longing. Composers such as Manuel Corona and later Agustín Lara in Mexico made the genre the dominant form of Latin romantic song for much of the twentieth century, performed by trios of guitarists and by great interpreters including Los Panchos. In 2021 the Cuban and Mexican bolero traditions were inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Related uses

The name is also given to a short jacket worn open over a dress, styled after the jackets of Spanish bolero dancers, and to a rhythm used in ballroom dancing competitions under the American Rhythm category.
