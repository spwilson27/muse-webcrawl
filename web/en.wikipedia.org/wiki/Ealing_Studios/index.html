Ealing Studios

TypeFilm and television studio
Founded1902 (as a production base); studios built 1931
LocationEaling Green, Ealing, London, England
Known forThe Ealing comedies

Ealing Studios is a television and film production company and facilities provider at Ealing Green in west London. It is the oldest continuously working film studio in the world, with stages in use since 1912. The studio is best remembered for the celebrated cycle of comedy films — the Ealing comedies — produced in the years after the Second World War under studio head Michael Balcon.

History

The site was first used for film production by Will Barker from 1902, and purpose-built studios opened in 1912. In 1929 the site was acquired by Associated Talking Pictures under Basil Dean, and the distinctive white art-deco-style studio building fronting Ealing Green was constructed in 1931. Michael Balcon took over production in 1938 and released films under the Ealing Studios name.

During the war years Ealing made semi-documentary features such as Went the Day Well? (1942). After the war came the comedies for which the studio is famous, among them Hue and Cry (1947), Passport to Pimlico (1949), Whisky Galore! (1949), Kind Hearts and Coronets (1949), The Lavender Hill Mob (1951), The Man in the White Suit (1951) and The Ladykillers (1955). Many starred Alec Guinness and were directed by filmmakers including Alexander Mackendrick, Charles Crichton, Robert Hamer and Henry Cornelius.

In 1955 the studios were sold to the BBC, which used them for television production including drama filmed on location inserts. In the 1990s the site passed to the National Film and Television School and then to a consortium that relaunched Ealing Studios as a commercial facility. Films made there in the 21st century include Notting Hill, St Trinian's, The Theory of Everything and The Imitation Game, and the site houses production companies, a film school (Met Film School) and post-production facilities.

The Ealing comedies

The Ealing comedies are noted for their gentle satire of British institutions and post-war society, often celebrating small communities resisting authority or bureaucracy. They remain among the best-loved films in British cinema.
