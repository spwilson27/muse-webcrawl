Investment Company Act of 1940

Long titleAn Act to provide for the registration and regulation of investment companies and investment advisers, and for other purposes
Enacted by76th United States Congress
EffectiveNovember 1, 1940
Codification15 U.S.C. §§ 80a-1–80a-64

The Investment Company Act of 1940 (commonly referred to as the '40 Act) is an act of Congress which regulates the organization of investment companies and the activities in which they and their affiliated persons engage. It forms part of the federal securities laws of the United States, alongside the Securities Act of 1933, the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and the Investment Advisers Act of 1940, which was enacted at the same time.

Background

The Act was passed in the wake of the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the ensuing Great Depression. A multi-year study of investment trusts and investment companies by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) documented widespread abuses, including opaque structures, excessive leverage, self-dealing and inadequate disclosure to investors. Congress responded with a comprehensive regulatory framework for pooled investment vehicles.

Scope and registration

The Act generally requires companies that issue and are primarily engaged in investing, reinvesting or trading in securities — including mutual funds, closed-end funds, unit investment trusts and face-amount certificate companies — to register with the SEC. Section 3(a)(1) supplies the core definition of an "investment company", while various provisions exclude entities such as banks, insurance companies and certain private funds from the definition or from registration.

Key provisions

  • Governance: requirements for boards of directors, including a proportion of directors who are not "interested persons" of the fund.
  • Capital structure: restrictions on leverage and on the issuance of senior securities, notably in Section 18.
  • Affiliated transactions: Section 17 prohibits or conditions transactions between a fund and its affiliates without SEC approval.
  • Custody: Section 17(f) requires fund assets to be held with qualified custodians.
  • Disclosure: registration statements, periodic reports and proxy rules modelled on the other securities laws.
  • Rule 12b-1: permits funds, under conditions, to use fund assets to pay for distribution.

Exemptions

Sections 3(c)(1) and 3(c)(7) exclude privately offered funds limited to accredited or qualified purchasers, which is the basis on which most hedge funds and private equity funds operate outside full registration. The SEC also has broad exemptive authority under Section 6(c), which it has used, among other things, to permit exchange-traded funds to operate.
