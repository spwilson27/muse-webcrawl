The Basilica of the Holy Cross in Jerusalem (Latin: Basilica Sanctae Crucis in Hierusalem, Italian: Basilica di Santa Croce in Gerusalemme) is a Catholic minor basilica and titular church in Rome, Italy, built around a chapel created by the Roman emperor Constantine's mother, Saint Helena, in about 325 to house the relics of the Passion of Jesus Christ brought by her from the Holy Land. It is one of the Seven Pilgrim Churches of Rome.

History

The basilica was built on the site of the Sessorian Palace, the residence of Helena. A hall of the palace was converted into a chapel whose floor was covered, tradition holds, with earth from Jerusalem — hence the dedication "in Jerusalem". The church was restored repeatedly over the centuries; its present Baroque facade and oval vestibule, designed by Pietro Passalacqua and Domenico Gregorini, date to 1743–1744 under Pope Benedict XIV.

Relics

The Chapel of the Relics, reached by a stairway, houses the basilica's famous Passion relics: fragments traditionally identified as pieces of the True Cross, a nail from the Crucifixion, thorns from the Crown of Thorns, part of the Titulus Crucis (the inscription placed above Christ), and the finger of Saint Thomas. The authenticity of these relics is a matter of faith and tradition rather than historical verification, but they have drawn pilgrims for over a millennium and a half. Below the basilica is the Chapel of Saint Helena, with a mosaic-decorated vault.
