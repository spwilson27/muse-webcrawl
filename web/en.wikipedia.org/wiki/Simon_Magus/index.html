Simon Magus

Simon Magus (Greek: Σίμων ὁ μάγος), also known as Simon the Sorcerer or Simon the Magician, was a religious figure who appears in the New Testament book of the Acts of the Apostles (chapter 8) and in numerous later Christian writings. In Acts, Simon is a Samaritan magician who, after being baptized by Philip the Evangelist, offers the apostles Peter and John money in exchange for the power to confer the Holy Spirit — an act condemned by Peter. From his name derives the term simony, the sin of buying or selling spiritual gifts and ecclesiastical offices.

In the New Testament

According to Acts 8:9–24, Simon had amazed the people of Samaria with his sorcery and was called "the Great Power of God". After believing and being baptized, he offered money for the ability to lay hands on others so they might receive the Holy Spirit. Peter rebuked him sharply — "May your silver perish with you" — and Simon asked for prayer on his behalf. The narrative is the scriptural basis for the concept of simony.

Later traditions

Church Fathers such as Justin Martyr (himself a Samaritan), Irenaeus, and Hippolytus expanded Simon into the archetypal heresiarch — the "father of all heresies" — and credited him with founding a Gnostic sect, the Simonians, and with proclaiming himself divine. Hippolytus's Refutation of All Heresies quotes a work attributed to Simon, the Apophasis Megale (Great Declaration), presenting a system of a boundless primal power expressed through paired syzygies. The apocryphal Acts of Peter and the Pseudo-Clementine literature depict a prolonged contest between Simon and Peter in Rome, ending in legends that Simon attempted to fly (or levitate) before Nero and fell to his death when Peter prayed — a story widely depicted in medieval art.

Historical assessment

Scholars debate how much of the later Simon tradition reflects the historical magician of Acts; some regard him as a genuine 1st-century Samaritan religious teacher, possibly connected with Samaritan and proto-Gnostic currents, while others see the heresiological portrait as largely polemical construction. Simon Magus has remained a durable figure in literature and occult tradition, often paired with a companion named Helen, whom later accounts said he presented as the "first thought" (Ennoia) of God.
