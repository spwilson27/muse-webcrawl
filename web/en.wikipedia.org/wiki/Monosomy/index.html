Monosomy

Monosomy is a form of aneuploidy in which an organism has only one copy of a chromosome instead of the usual two in its cells. It is the opposite of trisomy, in which there are three copies. Monosomy usually arises from nondisjunction during meiosis, which produces a gamete lacking a chromosome. Complete monosomy of an autosome is generally lethal in humans; the only survivable full monosomy is monosomy X.

Human monosomies

  • Turner syndrome (monosomy X, 45,X): the affected individual has a single X chromosome and no second sex chromosome. It occurs in females and is characterised by short stature, webbed neck, infertility and certain heart and kidney anomalies; intelligence is usually normal.
  • Partial monosomies result from deletions of part of a chromosome. Examples include cri-du-chat syndrome (deletion of part of chromosome 5p) and 1p36 deletion syndrome.
  • Mosaic monosomy, in which only some cells are monosomic, may produce milder effects.

Causes and consequences

Nondisjunction or anaphase lag can cause a gamete to miss a chromosome; fertilisation then produces a zygote with 45 chromosomes instead of 46 in humans. Because genes are dosage-sensitive, missing a whole autosome disrupts development so severely that complete autosomal monosomies are almost always lost as early miscarriages; even in Turner syndrome, most 45,X conceptions do not survive to birth.

In other organisms

Monosomics are used as genetic tools in plants such as wheat, where series of lines each missing one chromosome help locate genes. In some insects, males are naturally effectively monosomic for sex-determining elements under haplodiploid systems.
