Henge

A henge is a type of prehistoric earthwork, typically consisting of a roughly circular or oval bank with an internal ditch, enclosing an area of at least 20 metres in diameter. Henges were built in the British Isles during the Neolithic period, roughly between 3000 and 2000 BC, and often contain or are associated with stone circles or timber circles. The word derives from Stonehenge, although Stonehenge itself is atypical in having its ditch outside its bank.

Classification and purpose

Archaeologists class henges by the number of entrances through the bank and ditch (Class I having one entrance, Class II having two, and Class III having four) and distinguish very large examples sometimes called henge enclosures or "superhenges", such as Avebury, Durrington Walls, and Marden Henge. Because the bank lies outside the ditch, henges would have been poor defensive structures; they are generally interpreted as ceremonial or ritual monuments, possibly used for gatherings, feasting, and ceremonies connected with the seasons and the dead. Many are linked to other monuments by avenues and form parts of larger ritual landscapes.

Distribution

Around a hundred henges are known, concentrated in southern and eastern England but extending into Scotland, Wales, and Ireland. Famous examples include Avebury in Wiltshire (the largest), the Ring of Brodgar in Orkney, Arbor Low in Derbyshire, and Thornborough Henges in Yorkshire.
