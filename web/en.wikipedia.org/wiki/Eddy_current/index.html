Eddy current

Eddy currents (also called Foucault currents) are loops of electrical current induced within conductors by a changing magnetic field in the conductor, due to Faraday's law of induction. Eddy currents flow in closed loops within the plane of the conductor, in planes perpendicular to the magnetic field. They can be induced within nearby stationary conductors by a time-varying magnetic field from an electromagnet or transformer, or by the relative motion between a magnet and a nearby conductor.

History

The effect was first observed in 1824 by François Arago, who noticed that a rotating copper disk could drag a magnetic needle — "Arago's rotations." Michael Faraday explained the phenomenon with his law of induction in 1831, and the currents were studied in detail by Léon Foucault in 1855. The first practical use was electromagnetic separation and testing in the late 19th century.

Properties

By Lenz's law, eddy currents create their own magnetic field that opposes the change that produced them. This produces drag forces (used in braking) and repulsive effects. Because conductors have resistance, eddy currents dissipate energy as heat — a loss mechanism in transformer and motor cores, but a useful effect in induction heating. To reduce unwanted eddy currents, magnetic cores are built from thin laminated sheets insulated from one another, or from ferrites and powdered iron of high resistivity. At high frequencies the currents concentrate near the surface of the conductor — the skin effect — which limits their penetration depth.

Applications

  • Eddy current brakes slow trains, roller coasters and exercise equipment without friction or wear.
  • Induction heating and cooking: rapidly alternating fields heat metal cookware and industrial workpieces.
  • Non-destructive testing: eddy current probes detect cracks and measure conductivity and coating thickness in metals, widely used in aircraft inspection.
  • Metal detectors and sorting machines, induction motors, electricity meters and electromagnetic damping in balances and galvanometers also exploit the effect.
