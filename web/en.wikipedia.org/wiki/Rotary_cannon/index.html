Rotary cannon

A rotary cannon, rotary autocannon, rotary gun, or simply Gatling gun, is any large-calibre, multiple-barrel automatic weapon that uses an externally powered rotating barrel assembly to fire. Based on the principle of Richard Gatling's 1861 hand-cranked gun, the modern rotary cannon spreads the cycle of loading, firing, and extracting across several barrels, so that each barrel fires once per revolution and has the rest of the revolution to cool.

Principle and advantages

Because the firing cycle is divided among multiple barrels, a rotary cannon achieves rates of fire far beyond those of a single-barrelled automatic cannon, whose cycle time and barrel heating impose hard limits. A stalled or misfired round is simply extracted and ejected by the continuing rotation, making the type exceptionally reliable; and because power comes from an electric motor, hydraulic drive, or (in some designs) the firing gases, the gun does not depend on the energy of the cartridge to function. The disadvantages are weight, bulk, and the brief "spin-up" time needed to reach full rate of fire.

Development

Gatling's original gun was used through the end of the 19th century but was then displaced by recoil- and gas-operated machine guns. The concept was revived by the United States Air Force in Project Vulcan after the Second World War, when experience suggested that single cannon could not deliver enough hits in fleeting jet-age engagements. The result was the 20 mm M61 Vulcan (1959), capable of 6,000 rounds per minute, which became the standard gun armament of American fighters.

Examples

  • M61 Vulcan — 20 mm, six barrels, standard NATO aircraft cannon.
  • M134 Minigun — 7.62 mm rifle-calibre derivative, named for its small size relative to the Vulcan.
  • GAU-8 Avenger — 30 mm, seven barrels, built around the A-10 Thunderbolt II for the anti-tank role.
  • GAU-12 Equalizer and GAU-19 — five-barrel 25 mm and three-barrel 12.7 mm designs.
  • Gryazev-Shipunov GSh-6-23 and GSh-6-30 — Soviet/Russian gas-operated rotary cannon with extremely high rates of fire.

Rotary cannon also serve as the core of close-in weapon systems such as the Phalanx CIWS on warships. Their distinctive buzzing roar and immense short-burst firepower have made them prominent in both aviation and popular culture.
