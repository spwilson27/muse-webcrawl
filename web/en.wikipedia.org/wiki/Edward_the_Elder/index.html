Edward the Elder (c. 874 – 17 July 924) was King of the Anglo-Saxons from 899 until his death. He was the son of Alfred the Great and Ealhswith. Edward inherited the throne of Wessex at a time when much of northern and eastern England was under Scandinavian control (the Danelaw). Together with his sister Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians, he conquered the southern Danelaw in a series of campaigns in the 910s, laying the foundations for the unification of England completed by his son Æthelstan.

Early life and accession

Edward was Alfred's second child and eldest son. As a young man he fought alongside his father against the Vikings, winning a notable victory over the Danes of East Anglia at the Battle of Farnham in 893. He succeeded Alfred on 26 October 899, though his cousin Æthelwald contested the succession and allied with the Danes before being killed at the Battle of the Holme in 902.

Conquest of the Danelaw

From 909 Edward and Æthelflæd pursued a systematic strategy of building fortified burhs and campaigning into Danish territory. Victories at Tettenhall (910) destroyed the Northumbrian Danish army. By 918 all the Danish kingdoms south of the Humber had submitted, and after Æthelflæd's death that year Edward took direct control of Mercia, deposing her daughter Ælfwynn. In 920 the kings of Scotland and Strathclyde and the ruler of Bamburgh acknowledged his overlordship at Bakewell.

Death and legacy

Edward died at Farndon in Cheshire on 17 July 924, shortly after suppressing a Mercian and Welsh revolt at Chester, and was buried at the New Minster, Winchester. Though sometimes overshadowed by his father and son, historians regard Edward as one of the most effective of the Anglo-Saxon kings: he more than doubled the territory under West Saxon rule and created the administrative framework of the unified English kingdom.
