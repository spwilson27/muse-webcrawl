Stare decisis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stare decisis is the legal principle by which judges are obliged to respect the precedent established by prior decisions. The words originate from the Latin maxim stare decisis et non quieta movere — to stand by decisions and not disturb settled matters. It is the operating core of common law: the ratio decidendi (reason for the decision) of a higher court binds lower courts, and usually the same court or its successors, producing consistency, predictability and equality of treatment.

The doctrine is stronger vertically (lower courts bound by higher) than horizontally. The United States Supreme Court and the UK Supreme Court (formerly the House of Lords, which freed itself from absolute self-binding by the Practice Statement of 1966) may overrule their own precedents, weighing reliance, workability, factual change and the quality of the original reasoning — factors debated in cases overruling famous precedents. Courts may also distinguish precedents on their facts. Civil-law systems give precedent persuasive but not formally binding force, though in practice their courts follow settled case lines (jurisprudence constante) closely.
