Trouton's rule

Trouton's rule states that the (molar) entropy of vaporization is almost the same value, about 85–88 J/(K·mol), for most liquids at their normal boiling points. It is named after Frederick Trouton, who proposed it in 1884.

Explanation

The entropy of vaporization is the latent heat of vaporization divided by the boiling temperature. The rule holds because, at their boiling points, different liquids pass into vapours of roughly comparable molar volume and disorder, so the increase in entropy on vaporization is similar from one liquid to another. It provides a convenient estimate of the enthalpy of vaporization when only the boiling point is known.

Exceptions

Liquids with strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding, such as water, ethanol, and formic acid, have notably higher entropies of vaporization (water is about 109 J/(K·mol)), because their liquid phase is unusually ordered. Conversely, liquids whose vapours are structured or which are already disordered, and very light liquids such as hydrogen and helium, fall below the rule's value.
