Governor of New York

The governor of New York is the head of government of the U.S. state of New York, the head of the executive branch of New York's state government and the commander-in-chief of the state's military forces. The governor has a duty to enforce state laws, and the power to either approve or veto bills passed by the New York State Legislature, to convene the legislature, and to grant pardons, except in cases of treason and impeachment. The office is widely considered one of the most powerful governorships in the United States, in part because of the governor's central role in preparing the state executive budget. The incumbent is Kathy Hochul, a Democrat, who succeeded Andrew Cuomo upon his resignation on August 24, 2021, becoming the first woman to hold the office; she was elected to a full term in 2022.

History

The office dates to the New York Constitution of 1777, adopted during the American Revolution; George Clinton, the first governor, served seven terms (1777–1795 and 1801–1804). Successive constitutions (1821, 1846, 1894, 1938) reshaped the office: the term was extended to four years in the 20th century, and budgetary power was concentrated in the governor by amendments in the 1920s, making New York's governor the initiator of the state's executive budget. Four governors of New York have gone on to become president of the United States: Martin Van Buren, Grover Cleveland, Theodore Roosevelt and Franklin D. Roosevelt (and others, such as DeWitt Clinton and Al Smith, were major national candidates).

Powers and duties

Under Article IV of the state constitution, the governor:

  • Serves as chief executive and commander-in-chief of the state militia (the New York National Guard when not in federal service)
  • Prepares and submits the executive budget, whose appropriations the legislature may reduce or strike but generally may not increase without resubmission procedures — a system upheld by the Court of Appeals
  • Signs or vetoes legislation, including an item veto over appropriations; vetoes may be overridden by two-thirds of both houses
  • Grants pardons, commutations and reprieves
  • Appoints heads of most executive departments (subject to Senate confirmation), judges of the Court of Appeals, and other officials
  • Delivers the annual State of the State address and may call special legislative sessions
  • Exercises broad emergency powers under the Executive Law

Election and succession

The governor is elected statewide to a four-year term with no term limit, in midterm election years, on a joint ticket with the lieutenant governor (though the two are nominated separately in primaries). To be eligible, a candidate must be a United States citizen, at least 30 years old, and a resident of the state for five years. In case of vacancy, the lieutenant governor succeeds to the office — as occurred in 2008 (David Paterson succeeding Eliot Spitzer) and 2021 (Kathy Hochul succeeding Andrew Cuomo). The official residence is the New York State Executive Mansion in Albany, used since 1875.

List of governors (selected)

From George Clinton (1777) the office has been held by more than fifty individuals, including DeWitt Clinton, Martin Van Buren, Horace Greeley's rival William H. Seward, Samuel J. Tilden, Grover Cleveland, Theodore Roosevelt, Charles Evans Hughes, Al Smith, Franklin D. Roosevelt, Thomas E. Dewey, Nelson Rockefeller, Mario Cuomo, George Pataki, Eliot Spitzer, David Paterson, Andrew Cuomo and Kathy Hochul. Nelson Rockefeller and George Clinton were among the longest-serving.
