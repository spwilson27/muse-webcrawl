The American Medical Association (AMA) is a professional association and lobbying group of physicians and medical students in the United States. Founded in 1847, it is the largest association of physicians in the country and publishes the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA), one of the world's most widely circulated medical journals.

History

The AMA was founded at the Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia in response to concerns about the quality of medical education and practice, championed by physician Nathan Smith Davis. In its early decades it established a code of medical ethics and pushed for standardised medical education — efforts that contributed to the reforms following the Flexner Report of 1910. Through the 20th century the AMA became a powerful political force, notably campaigning against proposals for national health insurance from the Truman era through the debates over Medicare, which it opposed before its enactment in 1965.

Activities

The association maintains the CPT (Current Procedural Terminology) coding system used for billing medical services in the United States, a significant source of revenue and influence. It accredits, with partners, medical schools and residency programmes, and its House of Delegates sets policy on issues from public health to scope of practice. Its journal JAMA and the JAMA Network of specialty journals are leading medical publications.

Criticism

Critics have accused the AMA of acting to restrict the supply of physicians and protect doctors' incomes, of historical discrimination — Black physicians were long excluded, leading to the formation of the National Medical Association in 1895 — and of political positions misaligned with public health. The AMA apologised in 2008 for its history of racial inequality. Its membership represents a minority of practising US physicians, though it remains influential in health policy.
