Division ring

In algebra, a division ring, also called a skew field, is a nontrivial ring in which division by nonzero elements is possible. Specifically, it is a nontrivial ring R such that every nonzero element a has a multiplicative inverse. In other words, a ring is a division ring if and only if the group of units equals the set of all nonzero elements.

Division rings differ from fields only in that their multiplication is not required to be commutative. A division ring that has commutative multiplication is a field. Every field is a division ring, and the most famous noncommutative example of a division ring is the ring of quaternions, introduced by William Rowan Hamilton in 1843.

Properties and theorems

Every finite division ring is a field — this is Wedderburn's little theorem, proved by Joseph Wedderburn in 1905. Division rings are simple rings with trivial two-sided ideals, and by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, semisimple rings are products of matrix rings over division rings, making division rings fundamental building blocks in ring theory. The center of a division ring is a field, and a division ring is a vector space over its center; noncommutative division rings finite-dimensional over their centers (such as quaternion algebras) are described by the Brauer group of the center field. Frobenius's theorem states that the only finite-dimensional associative division algebras over the real numbers are the reals, the complex numbers, and the quaternions.

Examples

  • The rational, real, and complex numbers (commutative — hence fields);
  • The quaternions ℍ, a noncommutative division ring;
  • Rings of twisted Laurent series over a field with a nontrivial automorphism;
  • The Weyl algebra's quotient division ring (the rational function field in noncommuting variables arising in the theory of D-modules).
