Covenant Chain


Wampum belt

The Covenant Chain was a series of alliances and treaties developed during the seventeenth century between the Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee) and the English colonies of North America, beginning with agreements between the Iroquois and the colony of New York. Anchored in the metaphor of a chain — first of rope, then of iron, and finally of silver, which could be polished to keep it bright — the relationship was periodically renewed at treaty councils through the eighteenth century.

Origins

The Chain grew out of earlier Dutch–Mohawk trading relations at Fort Orange in the 1640s. After the English takeover of New Netherland in 1664, Governor Edmund Andros formalised the alliance at Albany in 1677, making Albany the principal site of Anglo-Iroquois diplomacy. Through the Chain, the Iroquois and the English colonies coordinated trade and military policy, particularly against New France and its Indigenous allies.

Diplomatic practice

Councils followed Indigenous protocol: speeches were accompanied by exchanges of wampum belts that recorded agreements, condolence ceremonies "cleared the eyes and throat" of grieving parties, and the parties spoke of "polishing the chain" to repair frictions. The most famous episode was the Treaty of Lancaster (1744), negotiations at which inspired Benjamin Franklin's reflections on colonial union.

Decline

The Chain effectively ended with the American Revolution, when the Iroquois Confederacy split between British and American allegiances, and Britain made peace without protecting its Indigenous allies in the Treaty of Paris (1783).
