Cleavage (geology)

Calcite showing rhombohedral cleavage.

In geology and mineralogy, cleavage is the tendency of a crystalline mineral to break along flat planes of structural weakness, producing smooth, planar surfaces. Cleavage planes correspond to directions of weaker atomic bonding in the crystal lattice. The quality of cleavage is described as perfect, good, distinct, or imperfect, and the number of directions and the angles between them are diagnostic properties used in mineral identification.

Types

Minerals may show one direction of cleavage (basal, as in the micas, which split into thin sheets), two directions (as in feldspars and pyroxenes), three (cubic in halite; rhombohedral in calcite), four (octahedral in fluorite), or six (dodecahedral in sphalerite). Diamond has perfect octahedral cleavage, which gem cutters exploit—and must avoid accidentally triggering—when shaping stones.

Cleavage versus fracture and parting

Cleavage differs from fracture, in which a mineral breaks along surfaces unrelated to any lattice weakness (for example, the conchoidal, shell-like fracture of quartz, which has no cleavage). Parting resembles cleavage but occurs only along planes of structural weakness caused by twinning or exsolution, and is not present in every specimen of a mineral.

Rock cleavage

The term is also applied to rocks: slaty cleavage is a penetrative planar fabric in fine-grained metamorphic rocks, produced by the alignment of platy minerals under directed pressure, allowing slate to be split into thin sheets. Axial-planar cleavage forms parallel to the axial planes of folds in deformed sedimentary sequences.
