Golden parachute

A golden parachute is an agreement between a company and an employee, usually a senior executive, specifying that the employee will receive significant benefits — such as severance pay, cash bonuses, stock options or continued benefits — if their employment is terminated, typically following a merger or takeover (a "change of control"). The term is generally used pejoratively, as the payments can be very large and are not linked to performance.

History

The first known use of the term is credited to creditors of Trans World Airlines (TWA), after financier Howard Hughes relinquished control of the airline in 1961 and executives were protected by lucrative contracts. Golden parachutes proliferated during the hostile-takeover wave of the 1980s, when they were adopted both as a retention device for management and, critics argued, as a mild anti-takeover defence that raised the cost of acquiring a company.

Regulation

In the United States, payments contingent on a change of control that exceed three times an executive's average annual compensation are classified as "excess parachute payments" under Internal Revenue Code sections 280G and 4999, subjecting the recipient to a 20% excise tax and denying the company a deduction. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 required public companies to give shareholders a non-binding "say on golden parachutes" vote disclosing such arrangements in merger proxies.

Criticism and defence

Critics contend that golden parachutes reward failure, insulate executives from the consequences of takeovers and transfer shareholder value to management — large payouts accompanying job losses for ordinary employees attract particular controversy. Defenders argue that the clauses encourage executives to consider value-creating mergers impartially, without fearing for their own jobs, and help attract and retain senior talent.
