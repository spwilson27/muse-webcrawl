Overview

The Norman conquest of southern Italy unfolded over the 11th and 12th centuries as Norman adventurers — initially pilgrims and mercenaries in Lombard and Byzantine service — carved out lordships across the Mezzogiorno and Sicily. The Hauteville family rose highest: Robert Guiscard became Duke of Apulia and Calabria (1059), his brother Roger I conquered Muslim Sicily (1061–91), and Roger II united the mainland and island possessions into the Kingdom of Sicily (crowned 1130).

Character

The conquest was piecemeal, opportunistic and as much Byzantine-Arab-Lombard diplomacy as war: Normans defeated a pope at Civitate (1053) and then sought papal investiture; they besieged Bari (to 1071, ending Byzantine Italy), took Palermo (1072) and Salerno, and pushed into the Abruzzi and against Benevento. Roger II's kingdom — governed in Latin, Greek and Arabic, famed for the Cappella Palatina and Monreale — became one of Europe's richest and most sophisticated states. The kingdom passed by marriage to the Hohenstaufen (1194) and then to Angevin and Aragonese rule, dividing Naples from Sicily.
