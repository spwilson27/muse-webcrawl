Purgatory (Latin: purgatorium) is, in the teaching of the Catholic Church, an intermediate state after physical death in which those who die in God's grace — assured of heaven but still imperfectly purified — undergo purification so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter heaven. Only those who die in a state of grace but with remaining venial sins or temporal punishment due to sin enter purgatory; the damned go to hell, and the perfectly purified go directly to heaven.

Doctrine and practice

The Church teaches that the living can assist the souls in purgatory by prayers, almsgiving, works of penance and especially the sacrifice of the Mass, and through indulgences. Scriptural support is traditionally drawn from passages such as 2 Maccabees 12:43–46 (prayers for the dead), 1 Corinthians 3:15 ("saved, but only as through fire"), and Matthew 12:32.

History

Belief in a post-mortem purification developed in the early Church through prayer for the dead. The doctrine took systematic form in medieval theology and was famously depicted in the second part of Dante's Divine Comedy, where Purgatory is a seven-terraced mountain. The doctrine was affirmed at the Councils of Lyons II, Florence and Trent. At the Reformation, Protestant churches rejected purgatory as unscriptural, a rejection tied to disputes over indulgences; the Eastern Orthodox Church accepts prayer for the dead and an intermediate state but not the Latin doctrine of purgatorial fire and satisfaction.
