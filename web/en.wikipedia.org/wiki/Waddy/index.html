Waddy

Type: Club / hunting stick

Origin: Aboriginal Australia

Materials: Hardwood, sometimes with a hardened point or stone head

A waddy (also spelled wadi, waddie or whaddie) is a heavy wooden club traditionally used by Aboriginal Australian peoples as a weapon for hunting and fighting, and also as a digging and fire-making tool. The word entered English from the language of the Dharawal people of the Sydney region, recorded by the colonists of the First Fleet in 1788.

Description and uses

Waddies were typically carved from a single piece of dense hardwood, such as mulga or other acacia, often with a bulbous or knobbed head and a shaft of roughly club length. Fire was sometimes used to harden the wood. In hunting, a thrown or swung waddy could kill or stun game such as birds and small mammals at close quarters; the related hooked throwing stick sometimes called a "swan neck" waddy was used to strike around cover. In conflict the waddy was a close-combat weapon used together with a shield.

Terminology

Because the word was used generically by early settlers, "waddy" came to be applied to a range of Aboriginal club types across the continent, including the nulla-nulla, a term from the Sydney region also widely used for similar weapons. Surviving historical examples are held in museum collections in Australia and abroad, and waddies remain a recognised item of Aboriginal material culture.
