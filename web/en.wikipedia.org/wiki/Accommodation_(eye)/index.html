Accommodation (eye)

The lens becomes more convex for near vision and flatter for distance.

Accommodation is the process by which the vertebrate eye changes optical power to maintain a clear image, or focus, on an object as its distance varies. In humans it is achieved mainly by changing the shape of the crystalline lens.

Mechanism

According to the Helmholtz theory, when the ciliary muscle contracts, tension on the zonular fibres (zonules of Zinn) suspending the lens is released. The elastic lens capsule then rounds the lens into a more convex shape, increasing its refractive power for near objects. When the ciliary muscle relaxes, zonular tension increases and the lens flattens for distant vision. Accommodation is part of the near reflex triad, together with convergence of the eyes and constriction of the pupils.

Amplitude and ageing

The range of accommodation, measured in dioptres, is greatest in childhood (around 14 dioptres) and declines steadily with age as the lens hardens. The resulting loss of near focus, presbyopia, typically becomes noticeable in the mid-forties.

Disorders

Disorders of accommodation include accommodative insufficiency, accommodative spasm, and paralysis of accommodation from disease or drugs affecting the ciliary muscle or its parasympathetic innervation via the oculomotor nerve.
