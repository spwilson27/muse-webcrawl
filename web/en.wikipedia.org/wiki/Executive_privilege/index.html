Executive privilege

Executive privilege
CountryUnited States
BasisSeparation of powers; Article II
Key caseUnited States v. Nixon (1974)

Executive privilege is the right of the president of the United States and other members of the executive branch to maintain confidential communications within the branch and to resist some subpoenas and other oversight by the legislative and judicial branches of government, in pursuit of the separation of powers. The right is implicit in the constitution, derived from the separation of powers.

History

George Washington first withheld documents from Congress in 1796 over the Jay Treaty. The phrase itself was coined in the Eisenhower administration. The Supreme Court first recognised the privilege in United States v. Nixon (1974), arising from the Watergate scandal: the Court held that a constitutional privilege for confidential presidential communications exists, but that it is qualified and must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a criminal trial. President Nixon resigned shortly after complying with the order to release the tapes.

Scope and disputes

Presidents of both parties have invoked the privilege against congressional investigations — over matters from the Iran–Contra affair to Fast and Furious and congressional subpoenas of presidential advisers and records. Courts distinguish the presidential communications privilege from the weaker deliberative process privilege, and disputes are often settled by negotiation ('accommodation') rather than litigation.
