Henri Poincaré

Jules Henri Poincaré (29 April 1854 – 17 July 1912) was a French mathematician, theoretical physicist, engineer and philosopher of science. Often described as a polymath and the last universalist of mathematics, he made fundamental contributions to topology, celestial mechanics, dynamical systems, mathematical physics and the philosophy of science, publishing some 500 papers and 30 books.

Life

Poincaré was born in Nancy into an influential family — his cousin Raymond Poincaré became President of France. Educated at the Lycée in Nancy and the École Polytechnique, he trained as a mining engineer while completing a doctorate in mathematics (1879) under Charles Hermite. He taught at Caen and then Paris, holding chairs at the Sorbonne and the École Polytechnique, and was elected to the Académie des Sciences in 1887 and the Académie française in 1908. During the Dreyfus affair he lent his authority to the Dreyfusard cause, testifying on the statistical errors in the evidence against Alfred Dreyfus. He died in Paris in 1912 following surgery.

Work

For King Oscar II's prize on the stability of the solar system, Poincaré's memoir on the three-body problem (1889–1890) revealed homoclinic tangles and sensitive dependence on initial conditions — the first mathematical glimpse of deterministic chaos. He founded algebraic topology with his paper Analysis Situs (1895), introducing the fundamental group and homology, and in 1904 posed the Poincaré conjecture, that every simply connected closed 3-manifold is a sphere; it stood until Grigori Perelman proved it in 2002–2003. His work on automorphic functions, differential equations, and the recurrence theorem bears his name across mathematics.

In physics, Poincaré developed Lorentz transformations, discussed the relativity principle and the synchronisation of clocks, and in 1905–1906 published papers on the dynamics of the electron closely paralleling Einstein's special relativity. His philosophical works — Science and Hypothesis (1902), The Value of Science (1905) and Science and Method (1908) — defended conventionalism in geometry and remain classics of the philosophy of science.
