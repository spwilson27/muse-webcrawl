Intergenerational equity

Intergenerational equity concerns fairness between people of different generations.

Intergenerational equity in economics, psychology, and sociology is the concept of fairness or justice between generations. The concept can be applied to fairness in dynamics between children, youth, adults, and seniors, in terms of treatment and interactions. It can also be applied to fairness between generations currently living and future generations.

Discussions about intergenerational equity occur across several fields. It is often discussed in public economics, especially regarding transition economics, social policy, and government budget-making. Many cite the growing US national debt, environmental degradation, and climate change as examples of failures of intergenerational equity, because benefits are enjoyed in the present while burdens are shifted to future generations.

Sustainable development

The most widely quoted definition tied to the concept comes from the 1987 Brundtland Commission report, Our Common Future, which defined sustainable development as development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs". Intergenerational equity is the principle that underlies this definition: each generation holds the Earth in common with past and future generations, and should pass it on in no worse condition than it received it.

Economic context

In fiscal policy, intergenerational equity arguments hold that government borrowing should finance investments whose benefits accrue to the future taxpayers who will repay the debt, rather than current consumption. If debt is used to pay for current consumption, future generations inherit the repayment burden without receiving corresponding benefits. The pay-as-you-go structure of public pension and health programs such as Social Security raises similar questions when demographic change alters the ratio of workers to beneficiaries.

Environmental context

Environmental applications of the principle hold that the present generation may use natural resources but must not exhaust or irreversibly degrade them. Edith Brown Weiss developed a theory of intergenerational equity for international environmental law, proposing that each generation is both a trustee of the planet for future generations and a beneficiary of the legacy of past generations. Climate policy is frequently framed in these terms, since greenhouse gas emissions today produce consequences spread over centuries.
