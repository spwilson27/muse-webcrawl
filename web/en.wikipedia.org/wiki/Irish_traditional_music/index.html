Irish traditional music

Irish traditional music (also known as Irish trad, or in Irish ceol traidisiúnta) is a genre of folk music that has developed in Ireland over centuries, encompassing instrumental dance music, songs in Irish and English, and slow airs. It is played worldwide, having travelled with the Irish diaspora, and remains a living tradition centred on informal pub sessions, céilí dances, and competitions.

Instruments and forms

Core instruments include the fiddle, wooden flute, tin whistle, uilleann pipes, concertina, button accordion, tenor banjo, harp, bodhrán (frame drum), guitar and bouzouki. Dance tunes are played in sets and are chiefly reels (4/4), jigs (6/8), hornpipes, polkas and slides, often ornamented with rolls, crans and triplets. Sean-nós ("old style") singing in Irish is a highly ornamented unaccompanied tradition. Regional styles are recognised — for example the driving rhythm of Donegal fiddling and the lyrical style of East Clare.

History

The tradition absorbed influences from Scotland, England and continental Europe, and was transformed in the early 20th century by 78 rpm recordings made in America by emigrant musicians such as Michael Coleman, which were re-imported to Ireland. The foundation of Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann in 1951 and its Fleadh Cheoil competitions helped revive and standardise the music; the folk revival of the 1960s–70s, through groups such as The Chieftains, Planxty and The Bothy Band, brought it to international audiences, and later acts fused it with rock and world music.

Sessions

The session — a gathering of musicians playing tunes in common, usually in a pub — is the social heart of the tradition; etiquette values shared repertoire, listening, and restraint from accompanists. Thousands of tunes form the common stock, documented in collections from Captain Francis O'Neill's Music of Ireland (1903) onward.
