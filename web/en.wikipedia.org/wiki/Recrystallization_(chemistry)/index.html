Recrystallization (chemistry)

In chemistry, recrystallization is a procedure for purifying compounds. The most common method involves dissolving an impure crystalline solid in a hot solvent, then slowly cooling the solution so that pure crystals of the compound form while impurities remain dissolved in the mother liquor. The crystals are then collected by filtration and dried.

Principle

Recrystallization exploits differences in the temperature dependence of solubility. An ideal solvent dissolves the desired compound poorly at low temperature but well at high temperature, while impurities are either insoluble in the hot solvent (and can be filtered out) or remain soluble in the cold solvent (and stay in solution when the purified crystals precipitate).

Procedure

The typical steps are: choose a suitable solvent; dissolve the crude solid in the minimum volume of near-boiling solvent; optionally treat with activated charcoal to remove colored impurities and hot-filter; cool slowly, sometimes seeding, to grow crystals; collect the crystals by vacuum filtration (for example with a Büchner funnel); wash with a small amount of ice-cold solvent; and dry. Slow crystal growth tends to exclude impurities from the crystal lattice, yielding material of much higher purity.

Applications

Recrystallization is a standard technique in organic synthesis and pharmaceutical manufacture and is also used at the laboratory scale for purifying solids such as benzoic acid and acetanilide.
