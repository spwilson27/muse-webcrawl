Social constructionism

Social constructionism is a theory of knowledge in sociology and communication that examines the development of jointly constructed understandings of the world. It assumes that many features of what people take to be objective reality — categories, concepts, identities and institutions — are in fact created and maintained through social interaction, language and shared practices, rather than being simply given by nature. The approach is closely associated with Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann's The Social Construction of Reality (1966) and with the later work of Kenneth Gergen in psychology.

Core ideas

Berger and Luckmann argued that society is created by human activity through a threefold process: externalisation (people produce a social world), objectivation (that world comes to be experienced as an objective reality standing over them), and internalisation (individuals absorb it through socialisation). Institutions, roles and identities are thus "constructed" yet entirely real in their consequences. Later constructionists emphasised language and discourse: the categories available in a culture shape what can be thought, said and done, a theme developed in gender studies, science studies and the sociology of mental illness and deviance.

Constructionism vs constructivism

Social constructionism is often distinguished from social constructivism: constructivism (following Piaget and Vygotsky) focuses on how individuals construct knowledge in learning, whereas constructionism focuses on collectively produced meanings and artefacts of culture. Strong versions of constructionism claim that even scientific categories are socially negotiated; weaker, "contextual" versions accept a material world while stressing that its description and significance are social.

Criticism

Critics, including realist philosophers and participants in the "science wars" of the 1990s, have charged some constructionist writing with relativism — with implying that because knowledge is socially produced it is arbitrary or unconstrained by reality. Constructionists generally reply that "socially constructed" does not mean "unreal": money, marriage and national borders are constructed and nonetheless powerfully real.
