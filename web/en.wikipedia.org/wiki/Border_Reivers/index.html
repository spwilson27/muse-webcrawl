The Border Reivers were raiders along the Anglo-Scottish border from the late 13th century to the beginning of the 17th century. They included both Scottish and English people, and they raided the entire border country without distinction of nationality, largely in family groups known as "surnames" or graynes. Their heyday was the Tudor period, when the great riding families — Armstrongs, Elliots, Nixons, and Charltons on the English side; Scotts, Kerrs, and Maxwells among the Scots — dominated a lawless frontier.

Background

Centuries of war between England and Scotland devastated the borderlands, ruining conventional farming. Raiding — "reiving" (an archaic word for robbing) — became a way of life and a seasonal trade: reivers stole cattle, sheep, horses, and household goods, and extorted "black mail" (protection money; the origin of the word blackmail) from farmers. Their allegiance was to family before king, and inter-family feuds could rage for generations, fuelling the Border ballads.

Life and warfare

Reivers rode sure-footed ponies ("hobblers," the source of hobby horse), wore light armour such as the steel bonnet and jack, and carried lance, sword, and later pistols. Raids took place on moonlit autumn and winter nights. The landscape is still marked by their fortified houses: peel towers and bastle houses. Border law, administered by Wardens of the Marches, had its own customs, including the "hot trod" — legal cross-border pursuit within six days, with horn and hound, giving us the phrase "hue and cry."

Suppression

When James VI of Scotland inherited the English throne in 1603, the frontier became the interior "Middle Shires" of a united kingdom, and raiding lost its shelter. A joint commission pacified the region with summary executions (some under the notorious "Jeddart justice" — hang first, try afterwards), mass deportations of the unruly Graynes to Ireland, and disarmament, ending the reiving era within a generation.

Legacy

The reivers left a rich legacy of ballads, surnames still common in the Borders and among the Scots-Irish diaspora, and words in English including blackmail and bereave. Annual "common ridings" in Border towns commemorate the riding traditions of the period.
