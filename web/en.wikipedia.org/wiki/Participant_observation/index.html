Participant observation is one type of data collection method by practitioner-scholars typically used in qualitative research and ethnography. This type of methodology is employed in many disciplines, particularly anthropology (including cultural anthropology and ethnology), sociology, cultural studies, political science, and other social sciences. It aims to gain a close and intimate familiarity with a given group of individuals (such as a religious, occupational, youth group, or a particular community) and their practices through an intensive involvement with people in their cultural environment, usually over an extended period of time.

History

The method originated in the field research linked to European and American explorers, but became a formal hallmark of anthropology through the work of Franz Boas in North America and especially Bronisław Malinowski, whose fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands during the First World War — published as Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) — set the template: long-term residence, learning the local language, and systematic daily observation recorded in field notes.

Method

The researcher takes part in the daily activities, rituals, interactions, and events of the people being studied, while observing and recording notes. Degrees of participation vary along a spectrum from complete observer to complete participant; the role may be overt (the group knows the researcher is studying them) or covert. Data typically consist of field notes, which are later analysed and written up as an ethnography.

Criticism

Participant observation has been criticised for lack of replicability, potential observer bias and "going native", and ethical problems — especially in covert research, where subjects cannot give informed consent. Reflexive approaches in modern ethnography attempt to acknowledge the researcher's influence on the field situation.
