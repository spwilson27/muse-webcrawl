Papain

Papain
EC number: 3.4.22.2
Type: Cysteine protease
Source: Papaya (Carica papaya) latex
[Image: Milky white latex dripping from a scored unripe green papaya on the tree]

Papain, also known as papaya proteinase I, is a cysteine protease enzyme present in papaya (Carica papaya) and mountain papaya. It is obtained commercially by drying the milky latex that oozes from cuts in the unripe fruit. Papain breaks down peptide bonds, especially after basic amino acids, and is a member of the same enzyme family as bromelain (from pineapple) and ficin (from figs).

Uses

Papain's ability to digest protein makes it the active ingredient in powdered meat tenderisers and a classic marinade component; it is also used to clarify beer (preventing chill haze), in the production of chewing gum, cosmetics and tooth-whitening products, in wound care for digesting dead tissue, and in laboratories for cell dissociation and antibody fragmentation — papain cleavage of immunoglobulin G into Fab and Fc fragments was historically important in immunology. In the kitchen, raw papaya's papain features in salads such as som tam and tenderises meat in many tropical cuisines.

Properties and safety

The enzyme is stable at relatively high temperatures and works across a broad pH range. Concentrated papain can irritate skin and mucous membranes and is a recognised allergen, cross-reacting in some people with latex allergy; its use in some over-the-counter digestive products has been restricted by regulators in some countries.
