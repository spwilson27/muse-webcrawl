Karl Barry Sharpless (born 28 April 1941) is an American chemist and two-time Nobel laureate. He is known for his work on stereoselective oxidation reactions — above all the Sharpless epoxidation — and for originating the concept of "click chemistry". He is one of only a handful of people to have won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry twice.

Career

Sharpless studied at Dartmouth College and earned his PhD at Stanford University in 1968 under Eugene van Tamelen. After postdoctoral work at Stanford and Harvard, he joined the faculty of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1970, later moving to Stanford and then, in 1990, to the Scripps Research Institute in La Jolla, California, where he holds the W. M. Keck Professorship.

Research

Around 1980 Sharpless developed the titanium-catalysed asymmetric epoxidation of allylic alcohols (the Sharpless epoxidation), a reaction of great practical value in the synthesis of pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals, together with methods for asymmetric dihydroxylation and aminohydroxylation. In the 1990s his group discovered that simple chiral ligands dramatically accelerated osmium-catalysed dihydroxylation ("ligand-accelerated catalysis"). In 2001, with Hartmuth Kolb and M. G. Finn, he introduced click chemistry: a philosophy of building molecules from reliable, high-yielding, modular reactions — exemplified by the copper-catalysed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC). Later he championed sulfur(VI) fluoride exchange (SuFEx) as a second generation of click reactions.

Nobel Prizes

Sharpless shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with William S. Knowles and Ryōji Noyori "for his work on chirally catalysed oxidation reactions". In 2022 he shared the prize again, with Carolyn Bertozzi and Morten Meldal, "for the development of click chemistry and bioorthogonal chemistry" — making him only the fifth person to win two Nobel Prizes in the same discipline, and the first in chemistry since Frederick Sanger.
