Token money

Token money derives its value from declaration or convention rather than from its material.

Token money is money whose face value exceeds its intrinsic value — the cost of the material from which it is made. It is a type of money accepted as a medium of exchange not because of its commodity value but because of an act of declaration, law or custom that gives it a fixed value. Modern coins and banknotes are token money: a banknote's paper is worth far less than its denomination.

Characteristics

Token money is contrasted with commodity money, whose value comes from the material itself (such as gold or silver coins minted at full weight), and with representative money, which is a claim on a commodity held in reserve. Token money circulates at face value because the issuing authority limits its supply, guarantees its acceptability (for example for tax payments), and enforces it as legal tender.

History

During periods of shortage of official small change — notably in 17th- and 18th-century England and in the United States during the Civil War — merchants and municipalities issued their own trade tokens redeemable at the issuing shop. These private tokens were eventually suppressed as state mints supplied adequate low-denomination coinage.

Economics

The difference between face value and production cost of token coinage is known as seigniorage, traditionally a source of state revenue. Because token money depends on public confidence and supply control, over-issue risks inflation and loss of acceptability.
