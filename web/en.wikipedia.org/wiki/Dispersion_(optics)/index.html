Dispersion (optics)

In optics, dispersion is the phenomenon in which the phase velocity of a wave depends on its frequency, or equivalently in which the refractive index of a material varies with wavelength. Its most familiar consequence is the separation of white light into a spectrum of colours by a prism, as demonstrated by Isaac Newton in the 1660s: blue light is refracted more strongly than red in most transparent materials, a regime called normal dispersion.

Description

Dispersion is quantified by the relation between refractive index and wavelength, often modelled by the Cauchy or Sellmeier equations. The Abbe number of an optical glass measures its dispersion: low Abbe numbers mean strong dispersion (as in flint glass) and high values mean weak dispersion (as in crown glass). Near an absorption resonance, the index can decrease with frequency — anomalous dispersion.

Consequences and applications

Dispersion causes chromatic aberration in lenses, corrected by combining crown and flint elements in achromatic doublets. It is exploited in prism spectrometers and in the rainbows produced by water droplets. In optical fibres, material and waveguide dispersion spread pulses in time, limiting data rates; dispersion-shifted and dispersion-compensating fibres, and chirped-pulse amplification in lasers, are engineered around it. Group-velocity dispersion governs the behaviour of ultrashort pulses and solitons.
