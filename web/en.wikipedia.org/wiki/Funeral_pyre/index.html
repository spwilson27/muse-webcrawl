Funeral pyre

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An open-air cremation pyre on a ghat along the Ganges at Varanasi.

A funeral pyre (also called a pyre, from Greek pyra, "fire") is a structure, usually made of wood, for burning a body as part of a funeral rite or execution. Placing the deceased on a pyre and then setting it alight is an open-air form of cremation, one of humanity's oldest funerary practices, documented in prehistoric burials and still in daily use in parts of Asia.

Historical practice

Pyre cremation features in the epics of ancient Greece — the pyres of Patroclus and Hector in the Iliad — and was the standard Roman method of disposing of the dead for much of the Republic and Empire. In Germanic and Norse tradition, ship burials and pyres figure in accounts such as the funeral of Baldr. Viking-age pyres were also used for the infamous sati-like sacrifices described by the traveller Ibn Fadlan.

Modern practice

Open-air pyres remain central to Hindu and Sikh funerals, in which the eldest son or a male relative traditionally lights the pyre; holy cities such as Varanasi host continuous cremations on the ghats of the Ganges. Balinese ngaben ceremonies burn ornate, tower-like pyres. In most Western countries, open-air pyres are restricted by environmental and public-health law; a landmark 2010 ruling in the United Kingdom (the Davender Ghai case) confirmed that open-air funeral pyres are lawful under certain conditions.

Pyres in punishment and protest

Burning at the stake, for heresy and other offences, used a pyre as a method of execution. In the 20th century, self-immolation by fire — such as that of the Buddhist monk Thích Quảng Đức in 1963 — used pyre-like burning as political protest.

Environmental concerns

Open pyres consume several hundred kilograms of wood per body and release smoke and particulates; electric and gas cremation is promoted in India partly to reduce deforestation and river pollution, though traditional practice persists.
