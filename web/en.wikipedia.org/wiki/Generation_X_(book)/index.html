Generation X: Tales for an Accelerated Culture

Generation X: Tales for an Accelerated Culture is a 1991 novel by Canadian author Douglas Coupland. It popularized the term "Generation X" for the generation born from the early 1960s to the early 1980s, and helped define the cultural vocabulary of that cohort through its narrator and his friends, who have retreated to the California desert and tell each other stories while working low-paying "McJobs".

Content

The novel follows Andy Palmer, a 29-year-old from Oregon, and his friends Claire and Dag, who live in bungalows in Palm Springs, California, working menial service jobs while exchanging stories, lists, and coined terms — "McJob", "bleeding ponytail", "poverty jet set" — printed in the book's wide margins as sidenotes. The narrative is episodic and collage-like, interweaving the characters' tales with drawings by Coupland and faux-statistical asides. The title derives from Paul Fussell's Class and from Billy Idol's former band Generation X; Coupland had earlier written a magazine article of the same name.

Reception and influence

Initially a modest seller, the novel became a word-of-mouth success and a marketing phenomenon, with "Generation X" adopted by media and advertisers as the label for post-baby-boom youth. Critics were divided: some praised its capture of slacker disaffection and consumer-culture irony, while others found the characters self-indulgent. Coupland has both embraced and distanced himself from the "spokesman for a generation" role. The book influenced a wave of 1990s fiction and film about young adults, including the slacker genre and works by Bret Easton Ellis's contemporaries.
