Working Time Directive

The Working Time Directive 2003/88/EC is a directive of the European Union laying down minimum safety and health requirements for the organisation of working time. It is a key instrument of EU labour law, intended to protect workers from the health and safety risks of excessive hours and inadequate rest.

Main provisions

  • A maximum average working week of 48 hours, including overtime, usually averaged over a reference period (commonly four months).
  • A minimum daily rest period of 11 consecutive hours per 24-hour period.
  • A rest break where the working day exceeds six hours.
  • A minimum weekly rest of 24 uninterrupted hours in addition to the daily rest.
  • A minimum of four weeks' paid annual leave.
  • Special limits on night work: an average of eight hours in any 24 hours, with health assessments for night workers.

The individual opt-out

The directive permits member states to allow workers to agree individually, in writing, to work more than 48 hours per week — the so-called "opt-out", used notably in the United Kingdom before Brexit. Attempts to phase out the opt-out repeatedly stalled in negotiations between the European Parliament and Council.

Case law

The Court of Justice of the European Union has interpreted the directive expansively, holding in cases such as SIMAP (2000) and Jaeger (2003) that on-call time spent at the workplace counts fully as working time, and in later rulings that employers must set up systems to measure daily working time (the 2019 CCOO judgment) and that standby time heavily constraining a worker's free time also counts.

Implementation

Member states transpose the directive into national law; in the UK this was done through the Working Time Regulations 1998, which were retained in modified form after Brexit.
