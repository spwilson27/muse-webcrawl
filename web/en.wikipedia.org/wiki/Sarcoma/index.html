Sarcoma

A sarcoma is a malignant tumour arising from connective tissues — bone, cartilage, fat, muscle, blood vessels, tendons and ligaments — as distinct from a carcinoma, which arises from epithelial tissue. Sarcomas are relatively rare, accounting for about 1% of adult cancers but a substantially larger share (around 15%) of childhood cancers, and comprise more than 70 recognised subtypes.

Classification

Sarcomas are broadly divided into bone sarcomas (such as osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma) and soft-tissue sarcomas (such as liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, angiosarcoma, synovial sarcoma and gastrointestinal stromal tumours, or GIST). Many subtypes are defined by characteristic genetic translocations, such as the EWSR1–FLI1 fusion in Ewing sarcoma.

Signs and symptoms

Soft-tissue sarcomas often present as a painless, enlarging lump; bone sarcomas more often cause pain, sometimes worse at night, and swelling. Because they are rare and often benign-mimicking, diagnosis may be delayed.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on imaging (MRI for soft tissue, X-ray and CT for bone), core-needle biopsy interpreted by specialist pathologists, and staging scans. Treatment is usually centred on wide surgical excision at specialist centres, often combined with radiotherapy; chemotherapy is important for chemosensitive types such as Ewing and rhabdomyosarcoma, and targeted drugs — imatinib for GIST being the landmark example — have transformed some subtypes.

Epidemiology

Risk factors for particular sarcomas include prior radiotherapy, chronic lymphoedema (Stewart–Treves syndrome), certain inherited syndromes (Li–Fraumeni, neurofibromatosis type 1, hereditary retinoblastoma) and exposures such as vinyl chloride for hepatic angiosarcoma. Kaposi sarcoma, driven by HHV-8, became prominent in the context of HIV/AIDS.
