Hazael

Hazael (Hebrew: חֲזָאֵל, Ḥăzāʾēl; Aramaic: Ḥazaʾel) was a king of Aram-Damascus in the 9th century BCE, reigning approximately from 842 BCE until about 800 BCE. Originally a court official of King Ben-Hadad II, he seized the throne and founded a dynasty that made Damascus the dominant power in the southern Levant.

Biblical account

According to the Hebrew Bible (2 Kings 8), the prophet Elisha visited Damascus, where the ailing Ben-Hadad sent Hazael to ask whether he would recover. Elisha wept, foreseeing the cruelties Hazael would inflict on Israel, and told him the king would recover — yet that he would surely die. The next day Hazael suffocated Ben-Hadad with a wet cloth and took the throne.

Hazael waged repeated wars against the kings of Israel and Judah, defeating Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah at Ramoth-Gilead, overrunning Israelite territory east of the Jordan, and marching on Jerusalem, which King Jehoash bought off with tribute from the Temple treasury. The biblical text presents him as an instrument of divine judgement who reduced Israel's military strength drastically.

Historical record

Hazael is also known from Assyrian inscriptions of Shalmaneser III, who campaigned against Damascus in 841 and 838 BCE but failed to take the city. Assyrian texts call Damascus "the land of Hazael" and refer to him, pointedly, as a "son of nobody" — a usurper without royal ancestry.

Archaeological finds associated with Hazael include a decorated bronze horse blinker and forehead plaque from his reign, booty later dedicated in Greek sanctuaries at Eretria and Samos, bearing an inscription referring to his crossing of the Orontes. The Tel Dan Stele, an Aramaic victory inscription found in northern Israel, is widely attributed to Hazael and appears to boast of killing kings of Israel and of the "House of David".

Succession

Hazael was succeeded by his son Ben-Hadad III. Under his successors, Damascus weakened under renewed Assyrian pressure, allowing Israel under Jehoash and Jeroboam II to recover lost territory.
