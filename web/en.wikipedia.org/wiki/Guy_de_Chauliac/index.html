Guy de Chauliac (c. 1300 – 23 July 1368), also called Guido de Caulhiaco, was a French surgeon and physician, widely regarded as one of the most influential medical writers of the later Middle Ages. He served as physician to three popes at Avignon — Clement VI, Innocent VI and Urban V — and his great textbook of surgery, the Chirurgia Magna (1363), remained a standard authority for more than two centuries.

Life

Guy was born around 1300 in Chaulhac, a village in the Margeride region of France. He studied medicine at Montpellier, then the leading medical school in Christian Europe, and also spent time at Paris and Bologna, where he learned anatomy and surgery. After practising in Lyon, he settled in Avignon, the seat of the papacy during its exile from Rome, and rose to become physician and surgeon to Pope Clement VI.

During the Black Death of 1348, Guy remained in Avignon and treated plague victims, despite the near certainty of catching the disease himself. He fell ill with a continuous fever and an aposteme (abscess) in the groin, but recovered after the abscess was lanced — a remarkable survival that he himself recorded. His first-hand account of the plague in Avignon, distinguishing the pneumonic and bubonic forms of the disease, is one of the most important medical records of the epidemic.

Guy also lanced Pope Clement VI and advised him to sit between two great fires to purify the air — advice that, by driving away the rats and their fleas, may genuinely have protected the pope, who survived the plague. Guy died in Avignon in 1368.

Chirurgia Magna

Guy's masterpiece, the Inventarium sive chirurgia magna ("Inventory, or Great Surgery"), was completed in 1363. It is a comprehensive treatise in seven treatises (books), covering anatomy, apostemes and tumours, wounds, ulcers, fractures and dislocations, special diseases, and an antidotary of remedies. The work synthesised the Greek, Arabic and Latin surgical traditions — Galen, Avicenna, Albucasis, Roger Frugard, Bruno da Longobucco and Guy's own teacher Henri de Mondeville — into a single systematic textbook.

The Chirurgia Magna was translated into French, English, Dutch, German, Italian, Provençal and Hebrew, and printed in dozens of editions after the invention of printing. It was still a prescribed text at some universities in the eighteenth century, earning Guy the reputation, in later medical historiography, of the "father of surgery" in the medieval Latin tradition.

Contributions and views

Guy advocated a return to the wound-management of Galen and the cleanly cut, primary healing approach for some wounds, though he also perpetuated the doctrine of "laudable pus". He described the use of soporific sponges (inhalation anaesthesia using opium and other drugs), traction for fractures, and the surgical treatment of hernias and cataracts, including a warning against charlatan "couchers" of cataracts. He insisted that the surgeon should be learned — literate, trained in anatomy, and versed in both theory and practice — helping to raise the professional status of surgery within learned medicine.

Legacy

Guy de Chauliac's writings shaped surgical education for over 200 years. His plague narrative remains a key primary source for historians of the Black Death, and his name is attached to a number of instruments and procedures in older surgical literature.
