Decompression sickness

Decompression sickness (DCS; also called divers' disease, the bends, or caisson disease) is a medical condition caused by dissolved gases emerging from solution as bubbles inside body tissues during decompression. DCS most commonly occurs during or after ascent from a dive, when a diver breathes gas at high pressure underwater and dissolves extra nitrogen in the blood and tissues; if the pressure falls too quickly, the nitrogen forms bubbles instead of being exhaled gradually.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms range from mild joint pain, fatigue, and skin itching or rash ("skin bends") to serious neurological injury, paralysis, and death. The most common presentation is pain in or around the joints — hence "the bends", from the stooped posture of affected caisson workers. Type I DCS is generally limited to pain and skin or lymphatic symptoms; Type II involves the nervous system, inner ear ("the staggers"), or cardiopulmonary system ("the chokes"). Symptoms usually appear within hours of surfacing, and most within the first day.

Causes and history

The condition was first widely observed in the 19th century among workers in pressurized caissons building bridge foundations, such as those of the Brooklyn Bridge, whose chief engineer Washington Roebling was himself disabled by it. Physiologist Paul Bert demonstrated in 1878 that the bubbles were nitrogen, and John Scott Haldane later developed staged decompression tables for divers. DCS can also occur in aviators and astronauts exposed to rapid pressure reduction at altitude.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention relies on limiting depth and bottom time, ascending slowly, and making decompression stops according to dive tables or a dive computer. Breathing nitrox reduces nitrogen uptake. Treatment is recompression in a hyperbaric chamber with 100% oxygen, which shrinks bubbles and drives nitrogen out of the tissues; prompt first aid with oxygen improves outcomes.
