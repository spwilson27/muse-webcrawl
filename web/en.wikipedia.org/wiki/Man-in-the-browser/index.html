Man-in-the-browser

A man-in-the-browser (MITB) attack is a form of Internet threat related to the man-in-the-middle attack, in which a trojan horse infects a web browser and modifies pages, transactions or communications in real time, inside the victim's authenticated session. Because the attack operates within the browser, the malicious changes are invisible to both the user and the server, and they pass over encrypted (HTTPS) connections unimpeded, since the trojan acts after decryption.

How it works

The trojan typically installs as a malicious browser extension or browser helper object, or by hooking browser API functions. During an online banking session it can:

  • Modify a transaction the user submits — changing the destination account and amount — while displaying the user's original values back to them.
  • Inject extra form fields into genuine pages to harvest credentials or card numbers ("webinjects").
  • Alter displayed balances and statements to conceal fraudulent transfers.

Notable malware

The Zeus trojan (Zbot) and its variants, along with SpyEye, Carberp and Torpig, made MITB attacks a major threat to online banking from roughly 2007 onward. Zeus configurations targeted banks in many countries and enabled fraud on a large scale before its author's arrest and several botnet takedowns.

Countermeasures

Defences include out-of-band transaction verification (confirming details on a separate device), transaction signing, browser hardening, and behavioural fraud-detection systems at the bank that flag anomalous payees or amounts. Ordinary TLS encryption alone does not protect against MITB, because the malware operates on the trusted side of the encryption.
