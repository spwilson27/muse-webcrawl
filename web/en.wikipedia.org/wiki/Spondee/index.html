Spondee

A spondee is a metrical foot consisting of two long syllables in classical Greek and Latin verse, or of two stressed syllables in accentual-syllabic English verse. It is conventionally notated — —. The word derives from the Greek spondē, a libation, because the slow, solemn rhythm accompanied the pouring of offerings in religious ritual.

In classical verse

In Greek and Latin dactylic hexameter, the spondee can substitute for a dactyl (— ⏑ ⏑) in the first four feet, lending weight and gravity to the line; the fifth foot is nearly always a dactyl, and the sixth is a spondee or trochee. Lines heavy with spondees move slowly and are often used for solemn, laborious, or ponderous subject matter.

In English verse

English, with its strong stress contrasts, produces true spondees of two equally stressed syllables in words and phrases such as "heartbreak," "deadline," "ice cold," and "shortbread." Poets use spondaic substitutions within iambic lines to slow the rhythm and add emphasis, as in the opening of John Milton's Paradise Lost. Gerard Manley Hopkins employed spondees extensively, and some English words — "football," "bookmark" — are themselves naturally spondaic (though many speakers reduce the second stress, a matter of scholarly debate).

Spondee words in audiology

In speech audiometry, "spondee words" are bisyllabic words with equal stress on both syllables — examples include "baseball," "hotdog," "sidewalk," and "toothbrush" — used to establish a patient's speech reception threshold.
