Jeffersonian democracy, named after its advocate Thomas Jefferson, was one of two dominant political outlooks in the United States from the 1790s to the 1820s. The Jeffersonians were deeply committed to American republicanism, political equality, and expansion of the franchise. They stood for a strict construction of the Constitution, states' rights, a weak and frugal federal government, and an agrarian ideal centred on the independent yeoman farmer.

Core values

Jeffersonian principles included:

  • Strict interpretation of the Constitution and suspicion of broad federal powers
  • States' rights and local self-government
  • Low taxes, a small military, and repayment of the national debt
  • The yeoman farmer as the ideal citizen; suspicion of banks, cities, and financial speculation
  • Freedom of speech and the press, and separation of church and state
  • Expansion westward and support for France rather than Britain in foreign affairs

History

Jefferson founded the Democratic-Republican Party in opposition to the Federalist programme of Alexander Hamilton. His election in 1800 — the "Revolution of 1800" — brought the Jeffersonians to power for a generation: Jefferson served 1801–1809, followed by James Madison and James Monroe. Major events included the Louisiana Purchase (1803), the Embargo Act (1807) and the War of 1812.

Jeffersonian ideals left a lasting mark on American politics through the later Jacksonian democracy and in enduring American suspicion of concentrated government and financial power. Critics note the contradiction between Jeffersonian rhetoric of equality and the system's reliance on slavery and exclusion of women and Native Americans from the franchise.
