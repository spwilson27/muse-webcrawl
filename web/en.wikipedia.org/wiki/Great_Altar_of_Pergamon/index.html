The Pergamon Altar (German: Pergamonaltar) is a monumental construction built during the reign of the Ancient Greek King Eumenes II in the first half of the 2nd century BC on one of the terraces of the acropolis of the ancient Greek city of Pergamon in Asia Minor. The structure is 35.64 metres (116.9 ft) wide and 33.4 metres (110 ft) deep; the front stairway alone is almost 20 metres (66 ft) wide. The base is decorated with a frieze in high relief showing the battle between the Giants and the Olympian gods known as the Gigantomachy. There is a second, smaller, and less well-preserved high-relief frieze on the inner court walls surrounding the actual fire altar on the upper surface of the structure at the top of the stairs. In a set of steps running up the middle of the west front, there is an inset depicting the life of Telephus, son of Heracles and legendary founder of Pergamon.

Excavation and removal

The altar's remains were excavated by German engineer and archaeologist Carl Humann beginning in 1878, under an agreement with the Ottoman Empire. The frieze slabs had been reused in Byzantine and later fortifications on the acropolis. Under the terms of the agreement, the finds were transferred to Berlin, where the altar was reconstructed as the centrepiece of the Pergamon Museum, opened in 1930 on Museum Island. The transfer remains a prominent case in debates over the repatriation of cultural property; Turkey has periodically called for the altar's return.

The Gigantomachy frieze

The great frieze, about 113 metres long and 2.3 metres high, is considered a masterpiece of Hellenistic "baroque" sculpture. Gods and giants writhe in dramatic, deeply undercut high relief; individual figures can be identified by inscriptions and attributes, including Zeus fighting Porphyrion and Athena dragging the giant Alcyoneus by the hair while Gaia rises from the earth to plead for her sons. The programme celebrated the Attalid dynasty's victories by analogy with the Olympians' cosmic triumph over disorder.

Legacy

The altar is sometimes identified with the "throne of Satan" mentioned in the Book of Revelation (2:13), a reading that has given the monument an outsized place in popular imagination. In the 20th century its reconstructed staircase influenced monumental architecture, including Albert Speer's Zeppelinfeld in Nuremberg — an association frequently noted in scholarship.
