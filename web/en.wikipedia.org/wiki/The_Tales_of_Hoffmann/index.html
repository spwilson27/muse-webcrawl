The Tales of Hoffmann (French: Les contes d'Hoffmann) is an opéra fantastique by Jacques Offenbach, with a French libretto by Jules Barbier based on three short stories by E. T. A. Hoffmann, who is the opera's protagonist — a fictionalised version of the author himself. Offenbach died before completing the work; it was finished and orchestrated by Ernest Guiraud and premiered at the Opéra-Comique, Paris, in 1881.

Synopsis

In the prologue, the poet Hoffmann, waiting in a tavern for his beloved Stella, entertains students with tales of his three lost loves, each foiled by a villainous incarnation of his rival Lindorf. In the first tale he loves Olympia, a mechanical doll created by the inventor Spalanzani and the sinister Coppélius. In the second, he loves the singer Antonia, whose singing — urged on by the quack Dr Miracle — kills her. In the third, in Venice, the courtesan Giulietta, acting for the magician Dapertutto, steals Hoffmann's reflection. In the epilogue Hoffmann, drunk, renounces love; his Muse reveals that suffering has made him a poet.

Music and performance history

The opera contains some of Offenbach's best-known music, including the "Barcarolle" ("Belle nuit, ô nuit d'amour") from the Venice act and Olympia's coloratura "Doll Song". Because Offenbach left the score incomplete, numerous editions exist with differing orderings of the acts and restored numbers; the discovery of autograph material in the 20th century reshaped modern productions. The work remains a staple of the international operatic repertoire, and was adapted into the celebrated 1951 film by Powell and Pressburger.
