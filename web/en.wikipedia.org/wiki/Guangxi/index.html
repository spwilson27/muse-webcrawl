Guangxi, officially the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, is an autonomous region of the People's Republic of China, located in South China and bordering Vietnam (Hà Giang, Cao Bằng, Lạng Sơn, and Quảng Ninh provinces) and the Gulf of Tonkin. Formerly a province, Guangxi became an autonomous region in 1958. Its capital is Nanning.

Guangxi's location, in mountainous terrain on the far southern frontier of China, has placed it on the frontier of Chinese civilisation throughout much of history. The region is known for its dramatic karst topography, exemplified by the Li River scenery around Guilin and Yangshuo, and for its ethnic diversity: the Zhuang, China's largest ethnic minority, are concentrated here.

Geography

Guangxi borders Yunnan to the west, Guizhou to the north, Hunan to the northeast, and Guangdong to the east and southeast. The region is largely hilly and mountainous, with limestone karst formations in the north. The Xi River system drains most of the region, and the south coast fronts the Gulf of Tonkin, where Beihai is the principal port.

History

The area was first incorporated into China under the Qin dynasty in 214 BC. Guangxi was the scene of major fighting during the Taiping Rebellion — its leader, Hong Xiuquan, began the Jintian Uprising in the region in 1851 — and during the Second Sino-Japanese War. In the early twentieth century the Guangxi Clique of warlords exercised considerable influence in Chinese politics.

Demographics

Han Chinese form the largest group, speaking mainly Southwestern Mandarin and Cantonese varieties. The Zhuang, numbering over 14 million in the region, speak Tai languages. Other recognised groups include the Yao, Miao, Dong, and Gelao.

Economy

Guangxi is a major producer of sugarcane, rice, and nonferrous metals, and its position bordering Vietnam makes it a key gateway for trade between China and ASEAN. Tourism around Guilin is a longstanding pillar of the regional economy.
