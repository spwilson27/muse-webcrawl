Coat of arms of Iceland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The coat of arms of Iceland (Icelandic: Skjaldarmerki Íslands) displays a silver cross on a blue shield, with an inner red cross, supported by the four traditional protector spirits of Iceland — the landvættir: a bull, a giant, a dragon and an eagle (or griffin). The shield rests on a base of columnar basalt.

The landvættir

The four supporters come from a story in Heimskringla, in which King Harald Bluetooth of Denmark sent a sorcerer in whale form to scout Iceland for an invasion. At each quarter of the island the sorcerer was driven off by a guardian spirit: a dragon in the east, an eagle in the north, a bull in the west and a mountain giant in the south. These beings became Iceland's traditional protectors.

History

The present arms were adopted on 1 July 1944, when the Republic of Iceland was founded, replacing the arms of the Kingdom of Iceland (1918–1944), which had featured a crowned shield with a falcon or the cross design. Earlier Icelandic arms under Danish rule included a crowned stockfish (dried cod), used for centuries as the island's emblem. The 1944 design, drawn by Tryggvi Magnússon, kept the cross shield from the flag and added the four landvættir as supporters standing on a slab of basalt, a typical Icelandic rock formation.

Use

The coat of arms is used by the President of Iceland, the government, and state institutions. Its use is regulated by law, and it may not be used commercially without permission.
