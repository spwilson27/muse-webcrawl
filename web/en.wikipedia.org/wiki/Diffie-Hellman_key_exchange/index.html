Diffie–Hellman (DH) key exchange is a mathematical method of securely exchanging cryptographic keys over a public channel. It was one of the first public-key protocols, published by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976 in their paper "New Directions in Cryptography", and was the first practical method for establishing a shared secret over an unprotected communications channel. Ralph Merkle's earlier work on public-key distribution influenced the protocol, and it is sometimes called Diffie–Hellman–Merkle key exchange.

Protocol

Two parties publicly agree a large prime p and a generator g. Each chooses a secret integer (a and b) and sends the other ga mod p and gb mod p respectively. Each can then compute the shared secret gab mod p, while an eavesdropper who sees only the exchanged values faces the discrete logarithm problem, believed computationally infeasible for suitably large parameters.

Security

Basic Diffie–Hellman provides no authentication, so by itself it is vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks; deployed protocols therefore authenticate the exchange with digital signatures or pre-shared keys. Modern deployments prefer ephemeral Diffie–Hellman (DHE, or ECDHE over elliptic curves), which generates fresh keys per session and thereby provides forward secrecy. Weak parameter choices have caused real-world failures, notably the Logjam attack (2015) against 512-bit and widely reused 1024-bit groups.

History

It later emerged that an equivalent system had been developed in secret at the UK Government Communications Headquarters (GCHQ) by James Ellis, Clifford Cocks and Malcolm Williamson in the early 1970s; this was declassified in 1997.
