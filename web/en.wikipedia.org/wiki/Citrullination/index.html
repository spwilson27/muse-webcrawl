Citrullination

Citrullination, also called deimination, is a post-translational modification in which the amino acid arginine in a protein is converted into the amino acid citrulline. The reaction is catalysed by a family of calcium-dependent enzymes called peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs). Conversion replaces a positively charged imino group with a neutral ketone group, changing the protein's charge, structure, and interactions.

Deimination of a peptidyl-arginine residue to peptidyl-citrulline.

Physiological roles

Citrullination occurs normally in the skin, where PAD enzymes modify filaggrin during cornification; in the hair follicle, where trichohyalin is deiminated; in the brain, where myelin basic protein is citrullinated; and in neutrophils, where citrullination of histones by PAD4 is required for the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), part of the innate immune response.

Role in disease

Excess or misplaced citrullination can create neo-epitopes that provoke autoimmunity. Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies are a highly specific diagnostic marker for rheumatoid arthritis, and citrullinated proteins accumulate in inflamed synovium. Abnormal citrullination has also been implicated in multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer's disease, and some cancers; PAD inhibitors are under investigation as therapeutics.
