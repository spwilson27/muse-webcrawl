Stokes' law is an expression for the frictional force — also called drag force — exerted on a small spherical object moving through a viscous fluid. Derived by George Gabriel Stokes in 1851 from the Navier–Stokes equations in the limit of very low Reynolds number (creeping flow), it states:

Fd = 6πηrv

where Fd is the drag force, η is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid, r is the radius of the sphere, and v is its velocity relative to the fluid. The drag is therefore proportional to velocity (rather than to its square, as in high-speed turbulent drag).

Terminal velocity

Balancing drag, gravity and buoyancy gives the terminal settling velocity of a sphere of density ρp in a fluid of density ρf:

vs = (2/9) · (ρp − ρf) g r² / η

The dependence on r² means fine particles settle extremely slowly — the physical basis for sedimentation, for the persistence of aerosols, and for centrifuges, which multiply g to speed separation.

Applications and validity

Stokes' law is used to measure viscosity in falling-sphere viscometers, to predict the settling of sediment, silt and plankton in water, the descent of cloud droplets and dust in air, and in particle-sizing instruments. Robert Millikan's oil-drop experiment used the law (with the Cunningham correction for droplets comparable to the mean free path of air) to determine the elementary charge. The law requires laminar flow around the sphere, Reynolds number Re ≪ 1, a smooth rigid sphere, and a fluid of effectively infinite extent.
