Geometric abstraction

Geometric composition in primary colours, in the spirit of De Stijl.

Geometric abstraction is a form of abstract art based on the use of geometric forms sometimes, though not always, placed in non-illusionistic space and combined into non-objective (non-representational) compositions. Emerging in the early twentieth century, it rejected the imitation of nature in favour of arrangements of lines, planes, circles, rectangles, and other elementary shapes, often with a restricted palette.

Origins

Pioneers included Wassily Kandinsky, whose early abstractions retained lyrical and organic qualities, and Kazimir Malevich, whose Suprematism—announced with the Black Square (1915)—reduced painting to fundamental geometric forms. In the Netherlands, Piet Mondrian and Theo van Doesburg developed De Stijl (neoplasticism), limiting compositions to straight lines, right angles, and primary colours plus black, white, and grey. Russian Constructivism and the Bauhaus transmitted geometric ideas into design, typography, and architecture.

Development

The 1930s groups Cercle et Carré and Abstraction-Création spread the style internationally. After the Second World War, geometric abstraction continued in concrete art, in the hard-edge painting and Op art of the 1950s and 1960s (Bridget Riley, Victor Vasarely), in Josef Albers's systematic Homage to the Square series, and in Latin American movements such as Argentine Arte Concreto-Invención and Brazilian Neo-Concretism. Its emphasis on clarity, order, and rational structure influenced minimalism and graphic design.

Characteristics and criticism

Proponents often advanced utopian or spiritual claims—that pure geometry expressed universal harmony—while formalist critics later praised its self-referential purity. Others have contrasted it with the organic, gestural forms of lyrical abstraction and Abstract Expressionism, a polarity that structured much mid-century art debate.
