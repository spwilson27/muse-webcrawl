Frying pan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A cast-iron frying pan with a fried egg in it]

A frying pan, also called a skillet, is a flat-bottomed pan with low, flaring sides and a long handle, used for frying, searing and browning foods. Typical diameters are 20–30 cm. The wide, open shape lets moisture escape so food browns rather than steams, and the low sides make it easy to turn food with a spatula. Frying pans are among the most-used pans in Western kitchens, employed for eggs, pancakes, cutlets, stir-fried vegetables and pan sauces.

Materials

Cast iron is heavy, durable, retains heat superbly and can be seasoned to near-non-stick; it has been used for centuries. Carbon steel is the professional standard in France for sautéing and crêpes. Stainless steel (often clad with aluminium or copper cores) resists sticking less but excels at fond development for sauces. Non-stick coatings (such as PTFE) simplify egg and fish cookery but limit maximum heat and lifespan; ceramic coatings are an alternative.

Related pans

The sauté pan has straight sides and a lid; the crêpe pan is very shallow; the grill pan is ridged; the wok is the East Asian counterpart with deep sloping sides. Two handled, shallow pans such as the Spanish paella pan and the Balkan sač are regional relatives.

History

Frying pans are ancient — copper pans were used in Mesopotamia, and the Romans had the patella. The English word "skillet" historically described a metal pot with feet and a long handle set over a fire. The modern handled frying pan became standard with the enclosed kitchen range in the 19th century.
