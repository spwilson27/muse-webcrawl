Brake

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type: Mechanical device
Function: Slowing or stopping motion by converting kinetic energy

A brake is a mechanical device that inhibits motion by absorbing energy from a moving system. It is used for slowing or stopping a moving vehicle, wheel, axle, or to prevent its motion, most often accomplished by means of friction.

Most brakes commonly use friction between two surfaces pressed together to convert the kinetic energy of the moving object into heat, though other methods of energy conversion may be employed. For example, regenerative braking converts much of the energy to electrical energy, which may be stored for later use. Other methods convert kinetic energy into potential energy in such stored forms as pressurized air or pressurized oil.

History

Early brakes were simple wooden blocks pressed against the rims of carriage wheels. The development of the steam locomotive and later the automobile drove rapid innovation: drum brakes appeared in the early 1900s, followed by hydraulic actuation, and disc brakes—which had been patented as early as 1902—became widespread on production cars in the 1950s and 1960s, popularized by racing applications such as Jaguar's victory at the 1953 24 Hours of Le Mans.

Types

Friction brakes

Friction brakes are the most common type. They include disc brakes, in which pads squeeze a rotating disc or rotor, and drum brakes, in which curved shoes press outward against a rotating drum. Disc brakes dissipate heat more effectively and resist fade, while drum brakes remain common on rear axles and in parking-brake applications.

Other types

  • Regenerative brakes — used in electric and hybrid vehicles to recover energy.
  • Dynamic (rheostatic) brakes — electric motors act as generators, dissipating energy as heat in resistors.
  • Engine braking — using the resistance of a closed-throttle engine or an exhaust brake.
  • Aerodynamic brakes — air brakes and spoilers that increase drag, used on aircraft and some trains.
  • Eddy current brakes — electromagnetic induction creates opposing currents that resist motion without contact.

Characteristics

Brake performance is described by measures such as stopping distance, pedal feel, and resistance to brake fade—the temporary reduction in stopping power caused by overheating. Modern vehicles supplement friction brakes with anti-lock braking systems (ABS), electronic brakeforce distribution, and brake assist.
