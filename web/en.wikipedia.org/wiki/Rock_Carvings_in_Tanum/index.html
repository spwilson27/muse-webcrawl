The Rock Carvings in Tanum (Tanums hällristningsområde) are a collection of Bronze Age petroglyphs near Tanumshede in Bohuslän, western Sweden. In 1994 the area, covering some 45 hectares and including around 600 known carving sites, was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List for the outstanding artistic and documentary value of its rock art.

Description

The carvings were made between roughly 1800 and 500 BC, during the Nordic Bronze Age, on smooth glacier-polished granite outcrops that then lay near the shoreline of a fjord (the sea level was about 15 metres higher than today). Some 1,500–3,000 individual motifs are known. Many panels are painted red in modern times to make the shallow pecked images visible.

Common motifs include ships — hundreds of them, some carrying large crews — human figures (warriors with axes and spears, archers, acrobats, and processions), animals such as deer, bulls, horses and snakes, cup marks, sun-discs, footprints and weapons. The great Vitlycke panel shows, among much else, a famous "bridal couple" embracing, a man with a large axe, and a "grieving" figure.

Interpretation

Archaeologists interpret the carvings as expressions of Bronze Age religion and cosmology: the ship is often read as the vessel that carries the sun across the sky and through the underworld, and many scenes may depict rituals, myths or seasonal ceremonies connected with fertility, seafaring and warfare. The area around Tanum was a religious and communicative centre set in a landscape of burial cairns and settlements.

Visitor sites and museum

The best-known panels are accessible by marked trails: Vitlycke (with the Vitlycke Museum, an information and research centre), Aspeberget, Fossum — notable for hunters, animals and a skier-like figure — and Litsleby, which features a large spear-bearing god figure. The carvings are vulnerable to weathering and air pollution, and research and documentation programmes continue to record and protect them.
