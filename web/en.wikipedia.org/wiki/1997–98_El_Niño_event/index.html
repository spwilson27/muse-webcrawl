The 1997–98 El Niño event was one of the most powerful El Niño events in recorded history, rivalling the event of 1982–83. It caused widespread weather disruption across the globe, including droughts in Indonesia, Australia and parts of South America, flooding in Peru, Ecuador, Kenya and California, and an estimated tens of thousands of weather-related deaths and tens of billions of dollars in damage worldwide.

Development

Sea surface temperatures in the eastern equatorial Pacific began rising rapidly in early 1997, aided by a series of westerly wind bursts. By the boreal autumn of 1997, anomalies exceeded +4 °C in parts of the eastern Pacific, and atmospheric convection shifted eastward in the classic El Niño pattern. Forecasters, aided by the TAO/TRITON buoy array, predicted the event's development months in advance — a landmark for seasonal climate forecasting.

Impacts

In Indonesia and Papua New Guinea, severe drought contributed to massive forest fires whose smoke blanketed Southeast Asia. Peru and Ecuador suffered destructive floods and landslides, while northern Chile and Bolivia also flooded; conversely, parts of southern South America and East Africa — notably Kenya and Somalia — received torrential rains. In the United States, California experienced a very wet winter with floods and mudslides, while the northern states had an unusually mild winter. Fisheries off Peru collapsed as warm water displaced the nutrient-rich Humboldt Current.

Legacy

The event ended in mid-1998 and transitioned into a multi-year La Niña. It is widely credited with demonstrating the value of ocean observation systems and seasonal forecasts, and it temporarily boosted global average temperatures, making 1998 one of the warmest years then on record.
