Devolution in the United Kingdom is the process by which the Parliament of the United Kingdom has granted varying degrees of self-government to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland through their own legislatures and executives, while England — by far the largest part of the Union — continues to be governed directly by the UK Parliament and Government. Devolved powers include health, education, transport, environment and much of justice and taxation; defence, foreign policy, immigration and most macro-economic policy remain "reserved" to Westminster.

Unlike a federal constitution, devolution is asymmetric and revocable in law: the UK Parliament retains sovereignty (the Scotland Act expressly preserves its power to legislate for Scotland), though political convention — the Sewel convention — holds that Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without the devolved legislature's consent.

History

Demands for "home rule" began with Ireland in the nineteenth century; Northern Ireland had its own parliament from 1921 until direct rule was imposed in 1972 during the Troubles. Referendums on devolution for Scotland and Wales failed in 1979 (Scotland voted yes but fell short of a 40%-of-electorate threshold). In September 1997 fresh referendums approved a Scottish Parliament with tax-varying powers and a Welsh Assembly; the 1998 Good Friday Agreement underpinned a Northern Ireland Assembly with mandatory power-sharing. All three institutions first sat in 1999.

Scotland held a referendum on independence in 2014, voting 55% to 45% to remain in the UK. Wales has progressively gained powers, including partial income tax. London has had an elected Mayor and Assembly since 2000, and combined-authority mayors now govern many English city-regions, though these are administrative devolution rather than legislative.

Government and finance

Devolved administrations are funded mainly by block grants calculated through the Barnett formula, which allocates population-based shares of changes in comparable English spending. The Scottish Government now controls rates and bands of income tax on most earnings and several smaller taxes; Wales has partial income-tax powers; Northern Ireland may set its own corporation tax rate (not yet used). Intergovernmental relations were restructured in 2022 around a Council of the Nations and Regions after Brexit strained the older Joint Ministerial Committee system.

England and the West Lothian question

England has no parliament of its own, so MPs from Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland can vote on English matters while English MPs cannot vote on devolved equivalents — the "West Lothian question", named by Tam Dalyell in 1977. "English votes for English laws" procedures operated at Westminster from 2015 to 2021. Proposals for an English parliament or regional assemblies have repeatedly foundered, notably when the North East rejected an assembly in 2004 by 78%.
