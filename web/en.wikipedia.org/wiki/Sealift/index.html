Sealift

Sealift is the transport of military personnel, equipment and supplies by sea, and by extension the ships and organisation that make it possible. Because ships can move vastly more tonnage than aircraft, sealift carries the great majority — typically around 90 per cent — of the cargo in any major overseas military deployment, while airlift moves personnel and the most urgent items.

Organisation

In the United States, strategic sealift is provided by the Military Sealift Command (MSC), which operates government-owned cargo ships, fast sealift ships and large medium-speed roll-on/roll-off (LMSR) vessels, supplemented by the Maritime Administration's Ready Reserve Force and chartered commercial shipping. Prepositioning ships stationed near potential theatres carry equipment ready for use. Britain maintains a smaller strategic sealift capability, including its Point-class roll-on/roll-off ships.

History

Sealift was decisive in both world wars and in Korea, and in 1982 the United Kingdom famously requisitioned civilian vessels — "ships taken up from trade" (STUFT) — to support the Falklands campaign. The huge sealift of the 1990–91 Gulf War moved millions of tons of equipment to Saudi Arabia.

Capacity concerns

Analysts periodically warn that ageing reserve fleets and shortages of merchant mariners could limit surge sealift capacity in a future conflict.
