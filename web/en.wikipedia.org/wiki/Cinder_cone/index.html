Cinder cone

A cinder cone in a volcanic field.

A cinder cone, or scoria cone, is a steep conical hill of loose pyroclastic material (tephra) — volcanic cinders, scoria, and bombs — that accumulates around a volcanic vent during Strombolian eruptions. Cinder cones are the simplest and among the most common types of volcano, typically rising 30 to 300 metres above their surroundings with straight sides sloping at about 30–35 degrees (the angle of repose of the fragments) and a bowl-shaped crater at the summit.

Formation

When gas-rich basaltic magma erupts, expanding gases fragment it into clots and blobs of lava that cool and solidify in flight before falling back around the vent as cinders and scoria. The fragments are too coarse to travel far, so they pile up close to the vent, building the cone over days to years. Many cinder cones are monogenetic — formed in a single eruptive episode — and lava often breaches the base of the cone or issues from side vents, since the loose pile cannot support a lava column to its top. A famous example is Parícutin in Mexico, which grew from a cornfield beginning in 1943 and reached over 400 metres before activity ceased in 1952.

Occurrence

Cinder cones occur in volcanic fields on all continents and frequently on the flanks of larger shield volcanoes and stratovolcanoes. Notable fields include the San Francisco volcanic field in Arizona (home to Sunset Crater), the Craters of the Moon in Idaho, and clusters around Mount Etna and in Iceland. Their dark, oxidised scoria is mined for landscaping and construction aggregate.
