Tay–Sachs disease

Tay–Sachs disease is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder in which a deficiency of the enzyme hexosaminidase A causes the fatty substance GM2 ganglioside to accumulate in nerve cells, progressively destroying the nervous system. It is named after the British ophthalmologist Warren Tay, who in 1881 described the characteristic cherry-red spot on the retina, and the American neurologist Bernard Sachs, who described the disease's cellular changes and its prevalence among Ashkenazi Jews.

Forms and symptoms

In the classic infantile form, babies develop normally for the first few months, then regress: muscle weakness, loss of motor skills and vision, an exaggerated startle response, seizures and the retinal cherry-red spot appear; death usually occurs by age four or five. Juvenile and late-onset forms (the latter sometimes called LOTS) progress more slowly; late-onset disease may present in adulthood with weakness, ataxia, speech problems and psychiatric symptoms. The cause is mutation of the HEXA gene on chromosome 15, which encodes the alpha subunit of hexosaminidase A.

Prevalence and screening

Carrier frequency is about 1 in 27–30 among Ashkenazi Jews (compared with roughly 1 in 250–300 in the general population), and is also elevated among French Canadians of eastern Quebec, Louisiana Cajuns and some Irish populations. Tay–Sachs became a landmark of medical genetics: community carrier-screening programmes begun in the 1970s — including premarital testing in Orthodox Jewish communities through Dor Yeshorim — reduced births of affected children in screened populations by more than 90%, a model later applied to other recessive disorders.

Treatment

There is no cure; care is supportive. Research has explored enzyme replacement, substrate reduction, pharmacological chaperones and gene therapy, with early clinical trials of AAV-based gene therapy in the 2020s.
