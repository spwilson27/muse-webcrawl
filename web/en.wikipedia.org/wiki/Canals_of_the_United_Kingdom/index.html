Canals of the United Kingdom

Total navigable: ~2,000 miles (England & Wales CRT network)
Peak era: 1790s–1840s "canal mania"
Authority: Canal & River Trust

The canals of the United Kingdom are a network of largely artificial waterways built primarily during the Industrial Revolution to carry bulk goods — coal, pottery, grain and manufactures — before and alongside the railways.

History

Though Roman and medieval works existed, the modern era began with the Bridgewater Canal (1761), which halved the price of coal in Manchester. "Canal mania" in the 1790s produced thousands of miles of canals, narrow (7 ft locks) in the Midlands and broad elsewhere. Railways from the 1840s took traffic, commercial carrying dwindled into the 20th century, and many canals closed.

Revival

From the 1960s leisure boating, restoration campaigns and heritage value revived the network. Since 2012 most canals in England and Wales have been managed by the Canal & River Trust, with Scottish Canals responsible in Scotland. Notable routes include the Grand Union, Kennet and Avon, Llangollen (with the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct, a World Heritage Site), and the Caledonian Canal. See Narrowboat.
