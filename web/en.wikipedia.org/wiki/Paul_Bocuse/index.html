OccupationChef, restaurateur

Paul Bocuse (11 February 1926 – 20 January 2018) was a French chef based in Lyon, widely regarded as one of the most influential cooks of the 20th century. The leading ambassador of nouvelle cuisine and a tireless promoter of French gastronomy abroad, he held three Michelin stars at his restaurant L'Auberge du Pont de Collonges for more than five decades and founded the Bocuse d'Or, the world's most prestigious culinary competition.

Early life and training

Bocuse was born into a family of cooks at Collonges-au-Mont-d'Or, just north of Lyon, in the inn his ancestors had run since the 18th century. After wartime service in the Free French forces — during which he was wounded at the Battle of the Vosges — he apprenticed in Lyon, notably with the celebrated mère Eugénie Brazier, and then in Paris, before joining the kitchen of Fernand Point at La Pyramide in Vienne, the great mentor of modern French cooking. In 1956 he took over the family inn.

Nouvelle cuisine and fame

Bocuse won his first Michelin star in 1958, the second in 1960 and the third in 1965 — a rating the restaurant retained until 2020, an unmatched span. Championed by critics Henri Gault and Christian Millau, he became the emblem of nouvelle cuisine: lighter sauces, emphasis on fresh market produce, shorter cooking times and artful presentation, codified in his bestseller La Cuisine du marché (1976). Signature dishes included black-truffle soup VGE, created for President Valéry Giscard d'Estaing at the Élysée in 1975, and sea bass in a pastry crust.

Unusually for a French chef of his generation, Bocuse travelled constantly — Japan, the United States, Brazil — preaching the gospel of French cuisine and building a business empire of brasseries in Lyon, restaurants abroad and a culinary school at Écully. He was named "Chef of the Century" by Gault et Millau and by the Culinary Institute of America.

Bocuse d'Or

In 1987 Bocuse founded the Bocuse d'Or, a biennial world chef championship held in Lyon during the SIRHA hospitality fair, in which national teams cook elaborate dishes before a live audience and jury. Modelled on a sporting event, it became the most coveted prize in professional cooking.

Death and legacy

Bocuse died in January 2018 in the same inn where he was born, aged 91, having suffered from Parkinson's disease. President Emmanuel Macron led national tributes, calling him "the incarnation of French cuisine". The covered market of Lyon, Les Halles de Lyon, bears his name. In 2020, two years after his death, his restaurant lost its third Michelin star, ending a 55-year run; the decision was widely mourned in France as the closing of an era.
