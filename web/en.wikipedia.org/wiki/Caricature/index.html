Caricature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A caricature is a rendered image showing the features of its subject in a simplified or exaggerated way through sketching, pencils or other artistic drawings. Caricatures can be either insulting or complimentary and can serve a political purpose or be drawn solely for entertainment. The word derives from the Italian caricare — to charge or load — in the sense of a "loaded portrait".

History

Exaggerated portraits appear in Leonardo da Vinci's grotesque heads, and the Carracci family in Bologna is credited with establishing caricature as a genre in the late sixteenth century. The art flourished in Georgian Britain with satirists such as James Gillray and Thomas Rowlandson, whose prints savaged politicians and royalty; in nineteenth-century France, Honoré Daumier produced thousands of lithographs for journals such as La Caricature and Le Charivari — his depiction of King Louis-Philippe as a pear (poire) became an enduring symbol. Magazines such as Punch (founded 1841) made the political caricature a fixture of press culture, a tradition continued by modern editorial cartoonists.

Technique and use

The caricaturist identifies a subject's most distinctive features — a prominent nose, a hairstyle, a habitual expression — and exaggerates them while reducing others, ideally increasing likeness rather than losing it: a good caricature is often said to be "more like" the person than a straight portrait. Today caricatures are produced as editorial cartoons, as magazine illustration (for example the cover art of Mad), and as live entertainment at fairs and events, increasingly with digital tools.

Controversies

Because caricature trades in stereotype, it can shade into ethnic or religious insult, and depictions of religious figures or minority groups have repeatedly provoked disputes over the limits of satire and free expression.
