Pains and Penalties Bill 1820

The Pains and Penalties Bill 1820 was a bill introduced to the British Parliament in 1820 by the government of the Earl of Liverpool at the demand of King George IV. Its purpose was to dissolve the King's marriage to Caroline of Brunswick and to deprive her of the title of queen, on the ground of her alleged adultery with an Italian servant, Bartolomeo Bergami. The bill's passage through the House of Lords took the form of a quasi-trial of the Queen, which scandalised and captivated the public; the government abandoned the bill in November 1820 after its majority dwindled to single figures.

Background

George, Prince of Wales, had married Caroline of Brunswick in 1795; the couple separated almost immediately after the birth of their daughter, Princess Charlotte. Caroline lived thereafter mostly abroad, chiefly in Italy. When George III died in January 1820, Caroline returned to England to claim her rights as queen, to the King's fury. George pressed his ministers to introduce the bill, based on the evidence of the so-called "Delicate Investigation" and a later inquiry conducted in Milan.

The trial

From August to October 1820 the Lords heard witnesses — many of them Italian servants of the Queen's household, whose testimony was widely mocked in the press — and speeches by counsel, notably Henry Brougham and Thomas Denman for the Queen. Caroline became an unlikely popular heroine; crowds and petitions supported her, seeing the proceedings as the persecution of a wronged woman by a selfish king.

Abandonment and aftermath

The bill passed its second reading by 28 votes but its third reading (6 November 1820) by only nine, 108 to 99. Concluding it could not survive the Commons, Liverpool withdrew it on 10 November. Caroline was nonetheless refused entry to the King's coronation in July 1821 and died three weeks later, on 7 August 1821.
