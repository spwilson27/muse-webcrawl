RAFT (chemistry)

Full name:Reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer polymerization

Reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) polymerization is a technique of reversible-deactivation radical polymerization that makes use of a thiocarbonylthio compound — a RAFT agent, such as a dithioester, trithiocarbonate, xanthate, or dithiocarbamate — to mediate the polymerization via a reversible chain-transfer process.

RAFT was reported in 1998 by the CSIRO group of Ezio Rizzardo, Graeme Moad, and San Thang, and is closely related to the MADIX process developed by Rhodia. It has become one of the most versatile methods for producing polymers of predetermined molecular weight, narrow molecular-weight distribution, and complex architectures.

Mechanism

A conventional free-radical initiator generates radicals that add monomer and begin growing chains. The growing radical adds to the C=S bond of the RAFT agent, forming an intermediate radical that fragments, releasing a new radical and leaving the chain capped with the thiocarbonylthio group. Repeated addition–fragmentation equilibrium shuttles chains between active (radical) and dormant (capped) states, so that all chains grow intermittently and reach similar lengths — the hallmark of controlled or "living" behaviour.

Applications

Because RAFT works with a very wide range of monomers and tolerates water and functional groups, it is used to make block copolymers, star and graft polymers, polymer brushes, and materials for drug delivery, coatings, and nanotechnology. The thiocarbonylthio end-group can subsequently be removed or transformed (e.g., by aminolysis or thermolysis) to give colourless, odour-reduced products where needed.
