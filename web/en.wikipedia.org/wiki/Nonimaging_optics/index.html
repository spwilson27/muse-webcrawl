Nonimaging optics

Nonimaging optics (also called anidolic optics) is the branch of optics concerned with the optimal transfer of light radiation between a source and a target, without attempting to form an image. Whereas imaging optics is judged by how faithfully it reproduces an image, nonimaging design maximises the amount of energy transferred and controls its distribution, subject to the limits imposed by the conservation of étendue.

Principles and designs

The field emerged in the 1960s–1970s with the compound parabolic concentrator (CPC), developed for solar energy collection and for Cherenkov detectors by Roland Winston and others. The edge-ray principle states that if rays from the extreme edges of the source are redirected to the extreme edges of the target, all intermediate rays are transferred as well. Later methods include simultaneous multiple surface (SMS) design, in which several optical surfaces are calculated together, and flow-line and tailored-edge-ray approaches. The fundamental concentration limit follows from étendue conservation: for a three-dimensional concentrator the maximum concentration is the square of the ratio of the sines of acceptance and output angles (scaled by refractive index).

Applications

Nonimaging optics is used in solar concentrators and daylighting, LED illumination (collimators, street lights, headlamps), light pipes and light guides, infrared detectors, and photovoltaic concentrator systems. It has also entered architecture through anidolic daylighting systems that redirect daylight deep into buildings.
