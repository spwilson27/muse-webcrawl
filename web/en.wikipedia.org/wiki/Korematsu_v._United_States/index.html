Korematsu v. United States, 323 U.S. 214 (1944), was a landmark decision by the Supreme Court of the United States that upheld Executive Order 9066, which ordered Japanese Americans into incarceration camps during the Second World War regardless of their citizenship.

Background

After the attack on Pearl Harbor, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 in February 1942, authorising the military to exclude persons of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast. Fred Korematsu, a 23-year-old Japanese American citizen, refused to leave his home in San Leandro, California, and was arrested and convicted of violating the exclusion order.

Decision

In a 6–3 decision written by Justice Hugo Black, the Court held that the need to protect the country against espionage outweighed Korematsu's individual rights, and that the exclusion was a military necessity. The Court applied strict scrutiny but found the measure justified. Justices Murphy, Roberts, and Jackson dissented; Murphy called the decision the "legalisation of racism."

Later developments

In 1983, a federal court vacated Korematsu's conviction on a writ of coram nobis after evidence emerged that the government had suppressed reports showing Japanese Americans posed no security threat. The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 formally apologised and paid reparations to surviving internees. In 2018, in Trump v. Hawaii, Chief Justice John Roberts repudiated the decision, writing that Korematsu "was gravely wrong the day it was decided" and "has no place in law under the Constitution."
