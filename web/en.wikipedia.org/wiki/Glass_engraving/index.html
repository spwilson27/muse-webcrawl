Glass engraving

Glass engraving is a form of decorative glasswork that involves engraving a pattern or design into the surface of a glass object, either by holding it against a rotating wheel or by using a laser. It is one of the techniques of glass art, distinct from glass etching, which uses acid to produce the artwork.

Techniques

Wheel engraving uses small rotating wheels of copper or stone, fed with an abrasive such as carborundum, to cut into the glass. It permits deep, sculptural intaglio work. Stipple engraving builds up an image from thousands of tiny dots tapped into the surface with a diamond point. Sandblasting directs abrasive grit at the glass through a stencil. Modern workshops also use diamond-point drills and laser engraving machines.

History

Engraved glass was produced in the Roman world, and the craft flourished in Renaissance Venice and Bohemia. In 17th-century England, diamond-point stipple work was practised by Dutch émigré craftsmen. The 19th century saw a revival of copper-wheel engraving in Britain and Bohemia, producing elaborate exhibition pieces. Notable 20th-century engravers include Laurence Whistler, who decorated windows and goblets with stipple technique.
