Wacker process
TypeCatalytic oxidation of alkenes
Principal productAcetaldehyde (from ethylene)
CatalystsPalladium(II) chloride, copper(I) chloride, oxygen
DeveloperWacker Chemie, 1950s; first plant 1959

The Wacker process or Hoechst-Wacker process is the oxidation of ethylene to acetaldehyde in the presence of a palladium chloride catalyst. It is one of the first examples of homogeneous catalysis to be applied on a large industrial scale, and it largely displaced the older mercury-catalysed hydration of acetylene for acetaldehyde manufacture.

In the overall reaction, ethylene and oxygen react in water to give acetaldehyde. The palladium(II) is reduced to palladium(0) as it oxidises the alkene; copper(I) chloride reoxidises the palladium, and oxygen in turn reoxidises the copper, so that the only stoichiometric oxidant consumed is molecular oxygen:

CH2=CH2 + PdCl2 + H2O → CH3CHO + Pd + 2 HCl
Pd + 2 CuCl2 → PdCl2 + 2 CuCl
2 CuCl + ½ O2 + 2 HCl → 2 CuCl2 + H2O

Mechanism

The key steps are coordination of ethylene to Pd(II), nucleophilic attack by water on the coordinated alkene (hydroxypalladation), β-hydride elimination and reductive elimination to release the carbonyl product. Mechanistic studies of the Wacker process, including work by Henry and others, helped establish the general principles of palladium-catalysed oxidation chemistry, and variants (the "Wacker oxidation") are widely used in organic synthesis to convert terminal alkenes into methyl ketones.

Industrial practice

Two plant designs exist: the one-stage process, in which ethylene and oxygen react together, and the two-stage process, in which the oxidation and catalyst regeneration take place in separate reactors. The reaction can be extended to higher alkenes, which are oxidised to ketones following Markovnikov selectivity; propylene, for example, gives acetone.
