Cross-country skiing is a form of skiing where skiers rely on their own locomotion to move across snow-covered terrain, rather than using ski lifts or other forms of assistance. Cross-country skiing is widely practiced as a sport and recreational activity; however, it is still used for transportation in many rural, snow-clad areas, particularly in northern Europe and Canada.

Related disciplines include biathlon, which combines cross-country skiing with rifle shooting, and ski jumping and Nordic combined, which together form the Nordic skiing family. Cross-country skiing has been part of the Winter Olympic Games since the first Games in Chamonix in 1924.

History

Skiing originated as a means of winter transport in Scandinavia and Siberia several thousand years ago; wooden skis dating back thousands of years have been found preserved in peat bogs. In the 19th century, Norwegians such as Sondre Norheim developed skiing into a sport, and Norwegian emigrants spread the practice to North America and the Alps. The word "ski" comes from the Old Norse skíð, meaning a split piece of wood.

The first cross-country skiing competition was held in Norway in the 19th century, and the famous Holmenkollen festival in Oslo has hosted races since 1892. Long-distance citizen races such as Sweden's Vasaloppet (first held in 1922, commemorating Gustav Vasa's 1520 journey) and Norway's Birkebeinerrennet became national institutions.

Techniques

Classic style

In the classic technique, the skis move parallel to one another in groomed tracks, and propulsion comes from a diagonal stride with a grip zone — treated with grip wax or patterned with scales — under the foot. Double-poling, in which both poles are planted simultaneously, is increasingly used even on climbs by elite racers.

Skate skiing

Skate skiing, adopted widely in the 1980s after American skier Bill Koch popularized skating techniques, resembles ice skating: the skier pushes off the edge of alternating skis in a V pattern on a wide groomed lane. Skate skis are shorter and stiffer than classic skis, with smooth bases that are glide-waxed along their whole length. Races are designated as either classic or freestyle (skating permitted) events.

Equipment

Cross-country skis are long, narrow, and light, with tips that curve upward. Boots are flexible and attach to the binding only at the toe, leaving the heel free to lift — a key difference from alpine skiing. Poles are longer than alpine poles, reaching roughly to the skier's shoulder for classic and the chin or lips for skating. Clothing is typically light, breathable layers, since the sport generates considerable body heat and is among the most aerobically demanding of all endurance sports.

Competitive skiing

The FIS Cross-Country World Cup has been held annually since the early 1980s, and major championships include the FIS Nordic World Ski Championships and the Winter Olympics. Race formats include interval-start time trials, mass starts, skiathlons (combining classic and freestyle halves), team and individual sprints, and relays. Distances range from sprints of about 1.5 km to 50 km marathons. Leading nations historically include Norway, Sweden, Finland, Russia, and Italy.

Recreational skiing

Recreational skiers use groomed trail networks at Nordic centers or make their own tracks in backcountry terrain. Touring variants use slightly wider skis, sometimes with metal edges, for ungroomed snow. Cross-country skiing is valued as low-impact, full-body exercise and as a quiet way to travel through winter landscapes.
