Floating-point arithmetic

In computing, floating-point arithmetic (FP) is arithmetic that represents real numbers approximately, using a formulaic representation with a fixed number of digits and an exponent: a number is stored as a significand (or mantissa) multiplied by a base raised to an exponent:

significand × baseexponent

For example, the decimal number 12.345 can be represented as 12345 × 10−3. Floating point is analogous to scientific notation and allows computers to represent a very wide range of magnitudes — from the subatomic to the astronomical — with a fixed-size data type, at the cost of exactness: most decimal fractions, such as 0.1, have no exact binary representation.

IEEE 754

The modern standard for floating-point computation is IEEE 754, first published in 1985 and revised in 2008 and 2019. It defines formats including binary32 (single precision: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 fraction bits) and binary64 (double precision: 1, 11 and 52 bits respectively), as well as arithmetic operations, rounding rules, special values (positive and negative infinity and NaN, "not a number") and exception handling. Subnormal numbers fill the gap around zero.

Accuracy problems

Floating-point arithmetic is subject to rounding error, catastrophic cancellation when subtracting nearly equal numbers, and accumulation of error in long computations. Famous failures attributed to floating-point or related numeric errors include the explosion of the Ariane 5 rocket in 1996 (an integer overflow during a conversion from 64-bit floating point) and the Patriot missile timing drift in 1991. Numerical analysis studies how to design algorithms that remain stable despite these effects, and languages provide tools such as arbitrary-precision arithmetic and decimal types where exactness matters.

History

Floating-point concepts were pioneered by Konrad Zuse in his Z1 and Z3 machines, and the principles of binary floating point with subnormals and careful rounding were championed by William Kahan, the principal architect of IEEE 754, who received the Turing Award in 1989.
