Japanese theatre

Japanese theatre encompasses traditional forms — Noh, Kyōgen, Kabuki and Bunraku (puppet theatre) — that developed over centuries and are recognised by UNESCO, as well as modern shingeki and contemporary experimental theatre. Traditional forms combine music, dance, elaborate costume and stylised movement, with roots in religious ritual and court entertainment.

Noh and Kyōgen

Noh, perfected by Kan'ami and his son Zeami in the 14th–15th centuries under shogunal patronage, is a slow, poetic masked drama drawn from literature and legend, performed on a bare roofed stage with a pine backdrop, accompanied by flute and drums and a chorus. Comic Kyōgen plays are performed between Noh pieces, using colloquial language and everyday characters.

Kabuki and Bunraku

Kabuki arose in Kyoto in the early 1600s, tradition crediting the dancer Izumo no Okuni. After women and then young men were banned, adult male onnagata played female roles. Kabuki features spectacular staging, revolving stages, trapdoors, the hanamichi runway, bold kumadori make-up and frozen mie poses. Bunraku, developed in Osaka, uses large puppets operated by three visible puppeteers, narrated by a tayū chanter with shamisen accompaniment; the great playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon wrote for both forms.

Modern theatre

Western-style shingeki ("new theatre") emerged in the Meiji era with translations of Ibsen and Shakespeare, followed by postwar avant-garde (angura) directors such as Tadashi Suzuki and companies like the Suzuki Company of Toga, alongside commercial musical theatre and Takarazuka Revue.
