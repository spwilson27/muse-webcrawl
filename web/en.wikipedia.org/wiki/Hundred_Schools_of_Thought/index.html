Hundred Schools of Thought

The Hundred Schools of Thought (Chinese: 諸子百家; pinyin: zhūzǐ bǎijiā) were philosophies and schools of thought that flourished from the 6th century to 221 BCE during the Spring and Autumn period and the Warring States period of ancient China. The term "hundred" is figurative; the schools actually numbered in the dozens, but the era was one of great cultural and intellectual expansion, sometimes called a golden age of Chinese philosophy.

Competing teachers and schools debated statecraft and ethics across the Warring States.

Origins

The fragmentation of Zhou authority left competing states eager for advisers on government, warfare, and social order. Itinerant scholars — the shi class — travelled from court to court offering doctrines. The historian Sima Tan (died 110 BCE) classified the schools into six lineages, and the bibliographic chapter of the Book of Han later listed ten major schools.

Principal schools

  • Confucianism (Rujia) — founded on the teachings of Confucius and developed by Mencius and Xunzi; emphasised ritual, filial piety, humaneness (ren), and rule by virtue.
  • Taoism (Daojia) — associated with Laozi and Zhuangzi; advocated of wu wei (non-action) and harmony with the natural order.
  • Legalism (Fajia) — developed by Shang Yang, Shen Buhai, Shen Dao, and synthesised by Han Fei; stressed strict laws, administrative technique, and the power of the ruler. It became the governing philosophy of Qin.
  • Mohism (Mojia) — founded by Mozi; taught impartial care, meritocracy, frugality, and defensive warfare.
  • School of Yin-Yang (Yinyangjia) — associated with Zou Yan; explained the cosmos through yin-yang and the five phases.
  • School of Names (Mingjia) — logicians such as Hui Shi and Gongsun Long, famous for paradoxes like "a white horse is not a horse".
  • School of Diplomacy (Zonghengjia), the School of Agriculture (Nongjia), the School of the Military (Bingjia), eclectics (Zajia), and the minor talks (Xiaoshuojia) complete the traditional ten.

Legacy

After the Qin unification of 221 BCE and the Han dynasty's elevation of Confucianism, most rival schools declined or were absorbed, but their texts and ideas — Legalist statecraft, Taoist cosmology, Mohist logic — permanently shaped Chinese thought.
