Sabbatarianism

Sabbatarianism advocates the observance of the Sabbath in Christianity, in keeping with the Ten Commandments. Sabbatarians hold that one day each week is set apart by God for rest and worship, and that believers should refrain from ordinary work and recreation on that day in order to devote it to religious duties.

First-day Sabbatarianism

Most Christian Sabbatarians observe the Sabbath on Sunday, the first day of the week, identified with the day of Christ's resurrection and often called the "Lord's Day" after Revelation 1:10. Strict Sunday observance was particularly developed among English and Scottish Puritans in the 16th and 17th centuries, who argued that the fourth commandment is of perpetual moral obligation, with the day of observance changed from the seventh to the first day. The doctrine was codified in the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), which requires resting the whole day from worldly employments and recreations except for works of necessity and mercy. Sabbatarian convictions underpinned "Sunday closing" (blue) laws restricting commerce, sport and entertainment, which persisted in many jurisdictions into the 20th century.

Seventh-day Sabbatarianism

Seventh-day Sabbatarians hold that the Sabbath remains the seventh day, Saturday, as in Judaism, and that the day was never changed by scriptural authority. Groups holding this view include the Seventh-day Adventist Church (founded in the 19th century and notable for making Sabbath-keeping a defining doctrine), Seventh Day Baptists (dating to 17th-century England), the Worldwide Church of God and its offshoots, and various Church of God groups. Seventh-day Adventists in particular regard Saturday worship as a mark of loyalty to God.

Non-Sabbatarian view

Other Christians — including many Lutherans, Roman Catholics in their distinction between the ceremonial and moral law, and dispensationalists — hold that the Sabbath commandment as such was part of the Mosaic ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ, and that Sunday worship rests on apostolic practice rather than on a transferred Sabbath obligation. The debate turns on differing readings of passages such as Colossians 2:16–17, Romans 14:5, and the creation pattern of Genesis 2.
