In geometry, a stereographic projection is a mapping (function) that projects a sphere onto a plane. The projection is defined on the entire sphere, except at one point — the projection point (often the north pole). Intuitively, one draws a line from the projection point through each point of the sphere; where that line meets the plane is the image of the point.

Properties

Stereographic projection is conformal: it preserves angles at which curves cross, though it distorts areas, increasingly far from the point of tangency. It also has the elegant property that every circle on the sphere maps to a circle on the plane, except circles passing through the projection point, which map to straight lines. Formulas (for a unit sphere and projection onto the equatorial plane) give the coordinates of the image point as (X, Y) = (x/(1−z), y/(1−z)), with an inverse that underlies the construction of the Riemann sphere.

Applications

The projection was known in antiquity — it underlies the planispheric astrolabe, and its angle-preserving property was noted by Hipparchus and Ptolemy. In cartography, polar stereographic maps are used for polar regions and form the basis of the Universal Polar Stereographic grid. In crystallography and structural geology, stereonets (Schmidt and Wulff nets) plot the orientations of crystal faces and rock planes. In complex analysis, stereographic projection identifies the extended complex plane with the Riemann sphere, giving a geometric meaning to the point at infinity. "Little planet" panoramas in photography are also made with this projection.
