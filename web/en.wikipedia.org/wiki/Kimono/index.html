Kimono

The kimono (Japanese: 着物, literally "thing to wear") is a traditional Japanese garment and the national dress of Japan. It is a wrapped-front, T-shaped, flat-collared garment with long, wide sleeves, worn with a wide sash called an obi. Kimono are traditionally made from a single bolt of fabric called a tanmono, cut into straight pieces and sewn together.

History

Kimono-like garments were introduced to Japan from China through cultural exchange in the Kofun period. During the Heian period (794–1185), court ladies developed the elaborate layered style known as jūnihitoe. Over the following centuries the kosode, originally an undergarment, became the standard outer garment. During the Edo period (1603–1868), kimono design flourished under the shogunate's sumptuary laws, and distinctive styles emerged for samurai, merchants and townspeople.

Modern use

Following the Meiji Restoration, Western clothing was widely adopted for everyday life, and today kimono are worn mainly on formal or ceremonial occasions such as weddings, tea ceremonies, Coming of Age Day and funerals. Lighter unlined cotton kimono known as yukata are commonly worn at summer festivals. Types of formal kimono include the tomesode, furisode with long swinging sleeves for young women, and the men's hakama.

Construction

Kimono are made of silk, wool, cotton or modern synthetic fibres. The garment is always worn left side over right (except when dressing the dead), secured by the obi, and accompanied by traditional footwear such as geta or zōri sandals and split-toe tabi socks. The art of wearing kimono is called kitsuke.
