Purkinje effect

The Purkinje effect (sometimes called the Purkinje shift) is the tendency for the peak luminance sensitivity of the human eye to shift toward the blue end of the colour spectrum at low illumination levels as part of dark adaptation. This effect introduces a difference in colour contrast under different levels of illumination: for instance, while in bright daylight, red geranium flowers appear vivid against the dull green of their leaves, or adjacent blue flowers; but at dusk the same plant appears dark red or black, and its leaves relatively bright, since scotopic vision is most sensitive to blue-green light.

Explanation

Human vision uses two systems. In bright light, photopic vision relies on cone cells, whose combined luminous efficiency peaks near 555 nm (yellow-green). In dim light, scotopic vision relies on rod cells containing rhodopsin, with peak sensitivity near 507 nm (blue-green) and little sensitivity to deep red. As illumination falls, the visual system transitions between the two (through an intermediate, mesopic range), so blue-green objects gain relative brightness while red objects darken dramatically. Astronomers exploit the effect: faint red light preserves dark adaptation because rods are nearly blind to it, while averted vision uses the rod-rich peripheral retina to detect faint stars.

Discovery

Jan Evangelista Purkyně described the phenomenon in 1825 after noticing, while walking at dawn in Bohemia, how the colours of flowers changed as the light strengthened — red poppies that were bright at sunrise had appeared dark against bright blue cornflowers in the earlier twilight. The effect was later quantified in the standard luminosity functions adopted by the CIE.

Applications

The Purkinje shift informs lighting design (mesopic photometry for road lighting), the use of red lamps in observatories, ships' bridges and aircraft cockpits, and the rendering of night scenes in visual art, where blue tones signal darkness — the "day for night" technique in cinema.
