The Sultanate of Banten was an Islamic trading sultanate in western Java (present-day Banten province, Indonesia) that flourished from the sixteenth to the early nineteenth century. Founded after the Muslim conquest of the port of Banten from the Sundanese kingdom of Pajajaran, it became one of the great pepper-trading emporia of maritime Southeast Asia and a centre of Islamic learning.

Founding and growth

Banten was conquered around 1526–1527 by Fatahillah (Faletehan), a commander acting for the Demak Sultanate, and was then ruled by Maulana Hasanuddin, son of the saint Sunan Gunung Jati, who is regarded as the first sultan. Hasanuddin extended Banten's control over the pepper-producing regions of Lampung across the Sunda Strait in southern Sumatra, giving the sultanate a grip on the pepper trade that drew merchants from China, India, the Middle East and Europe.

Golden age

Under Sultan Ageng Tirtayasa (reigned 1651–1683), Banten reached its height. Ageng developed irrigation and rice agriculture, welcomed trade with the English and Danes as a counterweight to the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in nearby Batavia, and made Banten a haven for Muslim scholars. The rivalry with Batavia culminated in a civil war in which Ageng's son, Sultan Haji, allied with the Dutch; Ageng was captured in 1683, and the subsequent treaties reduced Banten to a Dutch vassal, ceding its claims over Cirebon and the pepper trade.

Decline and abolition

Through the eighteenth century Dutch control tightened, and the sultanate's authority shrank to its capital region. After unrest in 1808, Governor-General Daendels destroyed the sultan's palace; the sultanate was formally abolished by the British administration under Raffles in 1813, and Banten was annexed into the colonial state. The old capital, Banten Lama, preserves the Great Mosque of Banten (built c. 1560s), the ruins of the Surosowan palace, and the Dutch fort Speelwijk.
