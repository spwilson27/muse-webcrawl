Rustication (architecture)

Rustication
TypeArchitectural masonry treatment
PeriodRoman antiquity; revived in the Renaissance

In classical architecture, rustication is a decorative masonry treatment in which the visible faces of stone blocks are deliberately left rough, or worked to a rough texture, while the joints between blocks are deeply recessed and emphasised. It gives a wall an appearance of rugged strength and was typically used on the ground floor or basement storey of a building, where it contrasts with the smooth ashlar of the floors above.

History

Although the technique has ancient Roman precedents, rustication was revived and codified during the Italian Renaissance. The Medici palaces of Florence — notably the Palazzo Medici (begun 1444), the Palazzo Strozzi, and the Palazzo Pitti — made graduated rustication a signature of Florentine palace design: rough, almost rock-faced blocks at the lowest level, becoming progressively smoother on each higher storey. Sebastiano Serlio's treatise on architecture (1537) illustrated several types of rustication and helped spread the device across Europe.

Types

Several variants are distinguished. In diamond rustication, each block is cut to a low pyramidal point, as on the Palazzo dei Diamanti in Ferrara. Vermiculated rustication has a texture like worm-eaten wood or a mass of small squiggles. In other forms the joints alone are chamfered or bevelled while the block faces remain flat, producing a subtler effect. Imitation rustication could also be rendered in stucco or brick scored to resemble stone.

Use

Rustication became a standard feature of Renaissance, Baroque, and Neoclassical palaces, banks, prisons, and government buildings, where its associations with fortification and solidity were valued. Examples include the Palazzo Pitti in Florence, the Louvre's Tuileries wing in Paris, and countless 19th-century civic buildings in Britain and America. The term is unrelated to the practice of "rustication" meaning temporary suspension from a university.
