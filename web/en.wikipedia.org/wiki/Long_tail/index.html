The Long Tail

The Long Tail is a business concept and statistical pattern describing how, in some markets, a large number of niche products each selling in small volumes can collectively match or exceed the market of a small number of bestsellers. The term was popularised by Wired editor Chris Anderson in a 2004 article and his 2006 book The Long Tail: Why the Future of Business Is Selling Less of More.

In a power-law (Pareto) distribution of demand, the "head" consists of a few hits accounting for most sales, while the "tail" extends far down to thousands or millions of obscure items each selling rarely. Anderson argued that three forces of the digital economy — democratised tools of production, democratised distribution (near-zero inventory and shelf-space costs online), and search and recommendation tools connecting supply and demand — allow businesses such as Amazon, Netflix, and iTunes to profitably serve the tail.

Examples and criticism

Classic examples include online bookstores carrying millions of titles no physical shop could stock, and streaming services monetising deep catalogues. Subsequent research has qualified the argument: analyses of sales data have found that while the tail lengthens, hits may capture an even larger share of revenue (the "superstar effect"), and that niche markets may also become harder for individual creators to monetise. The long tail remains an influential framework for understanding digital distribution, media economics, and search behaviour.

Statistical meaning

In statistics, a long-tailed distribution has a tail that decays slowly (often as a power law) rather than exponentially, so that rare, extreme observations contribute a meaningful share of the total. Long tails appear in phenomena ranging from word frequencies and city sizes to wealth distribution and web traffic.
