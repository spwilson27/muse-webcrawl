The Battle of Santiago (Italian: Battaglia di Santiago) is the nickname of a notoriously violent football match between host nation Chile and Italy, played on 2 June 1962 at the Estadio Nacional in Santiago during the 1962 FIFA World Cup. Chile won 2–0, and the match became a byword for on-field violence.

Background

Relations between the teams were poisoned before kickoff. Italian journalists had written disparaging articles about Santiago and Chile — describing poverty and disorder in the capital — which were republished in the Chilean press, infuriating the home public. With both sides needing a result in a tight group, the atmosphere in the stadium was feverish.

The match

The first foul came within seconds, and the first dismissal after about twelve minutes, when Italy's Giorgio Ferrini was sent off for a foul on Honorino Landa; he refused to leave and had to be removed by police. English referee Ken Aston — who later invented the red and yellow card system partly in response to such chaos — struggled to keep control. Chile's Leonel Sánchez punched Italian captain Humberto Maschio, breaking his nose, without being dismissed, while Sánchez himself survived a later incident; Italy's Mario David was sent off shortly before half-time for a wild kick at Sánchez. Police intervened repeatedly. In the second half, with Italy down to nine men, Jaime Ramírez and Jorge Toro scored to give Chile victory.

Aftermath

Italy were eliminated in the group stage, while Chile went on to finish third in their home tournament. When the match was broadcast in Britain, commentator David Coleman introduced it as "the most stupid, appalling, disgusting and disgraceful exhibition of football, possibly in the history of the game". The "Battle of Santiago" remains one of the most infamous matches in World Cup history and helped spur formalised disciplinary procedures in football.
