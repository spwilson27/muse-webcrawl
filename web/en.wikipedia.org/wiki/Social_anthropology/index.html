Social anthropology

Social anthropology is the branch of anthropology concerned with the study of human societies and cultures, particularly their social structures, kinship systems, rituals, beliefs and institutions. It is the dominant form of anthropology in the United Kingdom and much of Europe and the Commonwealth, and closely overlaps with cultural anthropology as practised in the United States.

History and method

The discipline's defining method is ethnography based on long-term participant observation, established by Bronisław Malinowski's fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands and codified in Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922). A. R. Radcliffe-Brown promoted a comparative "social structural" approach modelled on Durkheim, while later generations turned to structuralism (Claude Lévi-Strauss), interpretive approaches (Clifford Geertz's "thick description") and critical and postcolonial perspectives.

Topics

Classic topics include kinship and marriage, gift exchange (Marcel Mauss's The Gift), religion and ritual, political organisation, economic life, gender, and the anthropology of the modern world — media, migration, science and bureaucracy. Social anthropologists increasingly study their own societies as well as distant ones.
