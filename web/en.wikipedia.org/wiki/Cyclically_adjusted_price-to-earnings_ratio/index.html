The cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, commonly known as CAPE, the Shiller P/E, or P/E 10, is a valuation measure usually applied to a broad equity market, defined as price divided by the average of ten years of earnings (the moving average), adjusted for inflation. It smooths short-run fluctuations in corporate profits over the business cycle and is used to assess likely long-term returns.

History

The approach descends from Benjamin Graham and David Dodd's suggestion in Security Analysis (1934) of using multi-year average earnings for valuation. Economists John Y. Campbell and Robert J. Shiller formalised the measure in papers in the late 1980s and 1990s, showing that it has predictive power for subsequent long-run stock returns. Shiller prominently warned of overvaluation before the dot-com peak of 2000, when the U.S. CAPE reached its all-time high of about 44.

Interpretation and criticism

A historically average CAPE for the U.S. market is around 16–17; high values suggest lower expected long-run returns and low values higher returns. Critics note that CAPE can stay elevated for decades, that accounting changes affect comparability, and that it is a poor market-timing tool even if it informs long-horizon expectations.
