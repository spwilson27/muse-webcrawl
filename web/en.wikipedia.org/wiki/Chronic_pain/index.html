Chronic pain

Chronic pain
SpecialtyPain medicine, neurology, anaesthesiology
DurationPain lasting or recurring for more than 3 months
CausesArthritis, back problems, nerve damage, cancer, fibromyalgia, persistent post-surgical pain; sometimes no identifiable cause

Chronic pain is pain that lasts or recurs for more than three months, beyond the usual course of healing of an acute injury or illness. Unlike acute pain, which serves as a protective warning signal, chronic pain may persist or arise without ongoing tissue damage and is regarded in modern classifications — including ICD-11 — as a disease in its own right, as well as a symptom of other conditions. It affects roughly one in five adults worldwide and is a leading cause of disability.

Classification

The International Association for the Study of Pain distinguishes chronic primary pain (e.g. fibromyalgia, chronic primary low back pain, complex regional pain syndrome) from chronic secondary pain linked to an underlying condition such as osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, neuropathy or cancer. Pain is further described as nociceptive (from tissue damage), neuropathic (from damage to the nervous system itself), or nociplastic (arising from altered nociception without clear tissue or nerve injury).

Mechanisms

Persistent pain involves peripheral and central sensitisation: nociceptors become hyper-responsive, and neurons of the spinal cord and brain amplify pain signalling, so that normally innocuous stimuli (allodynia) or minor stimuli cause exaggerated pain (hyperalgesia). Psychological and social factors — mood, sleep, beliefs, work and relationships — interact with these biological processes, giving rise to the biopsychosocial model of pain.

Impact

Chronic pain is strongly associated with depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, reduced mobility, loss of employment and social withdrawal. In the United States it contributes to a substantial share of disability and health-care costs, and its treatment with long-term opioid therapy has been implicated in the opioid epidemic.

Management

Guidelines favour multidisciplinary management: graded exercise and physical therapy, psychological approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy, and education in self-management. Medications include paracetamol, NSAIDs, and certain antidepressants and anticonvulsants for neuropathic pain; long-term opioids are discouraged for most chronic primary pain because of limited benefit and risks of dependence. Interventional procedures and neuromodulation are used in selected cases.
