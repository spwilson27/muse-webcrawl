Aerial archaeology

Aerial archaeology is the study of archaeological remains by examination of them from the air. It is one of the oldest methods of archaeological survey, dating to the early twentieth century when pioneers such as O. G. S. Crawford in Britain and Antoine Poidebard in Syria photographed sites from aircraft and balloons.

Principles

The method relies on the fact that buried features affect the surface above them in subtle ways. A filled-in ditch retains more moisture than surrounding soil, so crops growing over it are taller and greener, producing a cropmark. Conversely, buried walls stunt crop growth, producing parchmarks visible in dry summers. Low sun can also cast long shadows across slight earthworks, creating shadow marks best seen in winter mornings and evenings.

Practice

Modern aerial archaeology uses light aircraft, drones, satellite imagery, and airborne laser scanning (lidar), which can strip away vegetation digitally to reveal earthworks under forest canopy. Rectification software maps oblique photographs onto modern mapping so that newly discovered sites can be recorded in national inventories.
