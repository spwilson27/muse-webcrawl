Avocado

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A halved avocado showing green flesh and the large brown seed]

The avocado (Persea americana) is a tree of the laurel family, native to south-central Mexico, and its fruit — a large berry with a single big seed — prized for its buttery, nutty, pale-green flesh. Unlike most fruits, avocados are rich in fat (mostly monounsaturated oleic acid) and low in sugar, making them a savoury staple: mashed into guacamole, sliced onto toast and salads, blended into smoothies and sushi rolls. The Hass variety, with dark pebbly skin, dominates world trade.

History

Avocados were domesticated in Mesoamerica several thousand years ago; the name comes from the Nahuatl āhuacatl. Spanish writers described them in the 16th century, but the fruit spread slowly until 20th-century breeding and marketing — especially the Hass, patented in California in 1935 by Rudolph Hass from a chance seedling — created a shippable, reliable crop. Mexico is by far the largest producer.

Cultivation and ripening

Avocados do not ripen on the tree; they are picked hard and mature, then soften afterward — a climacteric fruit whose ripening is driven by ethylene, which is why an avocado ripens faster in a paper bag with a banana. Trees are usually grafted to preserve variety quality. The flesh browns quickly once cut (enzymatic oxidation); acid such as lime juice slows this.

Popularity and issues

Global demand surged in the 2010s ("avocado toast" became a cultural shorthand), bringing prosperity to growers but also pressures: avocados are water-hungry, orchards have displaced some forests in Michoacán, and organised crime has targeted the trade in Mexico. Guacamole consumption peaks in the United States around the Super Bowl and Cinco de Mayo.
