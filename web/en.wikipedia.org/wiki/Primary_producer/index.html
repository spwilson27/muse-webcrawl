Primary producers are organisms that produce organic compounds from inorganic sources, forming the base of almost every food chain. Most primary producers are photoautotrophs — green plants, algae and cyanobacteria — that use sunlight for photosynthesis. A smaller contribution comes from chemoautotrophs, such as the bacteria and archaea around deep-sea hydrothermal vents, which oxidise inorganic chemicals for energy.

Primary production

Gross primary production (GPP) is the total rate at which producers fix carbon; net primary production (NPP) is what remains after the producers' own respiration, and represents the energy available to consumers. Globally, NPP is on the order of 100 billion tonnes of carbon per year, split roughly evenly between land and ocean despite the ocean's vastly greater area — marine production is dominated by microscopic phytoplankton.

Ecological role

Primary producers support all heterotrophs, from herbivores to apex predators, and oxygenic photosynthesis by producers created and maintains Earth's oxygen-rich atmosphere — the cyanobacteria-driven Great Oxidation Event transformed the planet's chemistry over two billion years ago. Productivity varies with light, water and nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and iron; nutrient-poor open oceans are biological deserts compared with upwelling zones and coastal waters.

Measurement

Scientists estimate primary production with oxygen-evolution and carbon-14 uptake experiments, and globally with satellite measurements of ocean colour and terrestrial vegetation indices.
