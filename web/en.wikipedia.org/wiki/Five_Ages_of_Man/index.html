The Five Ages of Man are the five successive ages or races of humanity described by the Greek poet Hesiod in his poem Works and Days (lines 109–201). In the myth, the gods create a sequence of distinct races of men — Gold, Silver, Bronze, Heroic and Iron — each (with one exception) worse than the one before, in a narrative of moral and physical decline from an original golden age of peace and plenty to Hesiod's own iron age of toil and misery.


The decline of the ages

The ages

In the Golden Age, under Cronos, men lived like gods, free from work, sorrow and old age, while the earth produced food spontaneously. The Silver Age race was far inferior: children remained immature for a hundred years, adults lived briefly in suffering, and they were destroyed by Zeus for their impiety. The Bronze Age men were violent giants who loved war and destroyed one another. Uniquely, the fourth race — the Heroic Age of demigods and heroes who fought at Thebes and Troy — was better than the one before it, and its heroes dwell in the Isles of the Blessed. Finally, Hesiod laments that he himself lives in the Iron Age, an age of labour, pain, corruption and the breakdown of family and social bonds, which Zeus will in time destroy as well.

Parallels and influence

The scheme resembles the four metallic ages of later writers: Ovid reduced the races to four (gold, silver, bronze, iron) in his Metamorphoses, omitting the heroic age. Similar sequences of declining world-ages appear in other traditions, including the four yugas of Hindu cosmology and the statue of metals in the Book of Daniel. Hesiod's account is one of the earliest expressions of the idea of historical decline and of a lost golden age in Western literature.
