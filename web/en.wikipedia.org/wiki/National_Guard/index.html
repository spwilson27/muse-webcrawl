National Guard

The National Guard is a state-based military force in the United States that becomes part of the U.S. military's reserve components when activated for federal missions. It is a joint reserve component of the United States Army and the United States Air Force, comprising the Army National Guard and the Air National Guard, with units maintained in all 50 states, three territories, and the District of Columbia.

Dual status

The defining feature of the National Guard is its dual mission. Under state authority, Guard units answer to their governor and are routinely called out for disaster relief, search and rescue, and civil support — the most visible recent examples including hurricane responses and the COVID-19 pandemic. Under federal authority (Title 10), the President may mobilize the Guard for overseas combat and support missions, in which role Guard units served extensively in the Iraq and Afghanistan wars.

History

The Guard traces its lineage to the colonial militias, conventionally dated to 13 December 1636, when the Massachusetts Bay Colony organized its citizen soldiers into regiments. State militias fought in the Revolutionary War, the War of 1812, and the Civil War. The Militia Act of 1903 and the National Defense Act of 1916 standardized the militias as the National Guard and made them the Army's primary organized reserve. The Air National Guard was created as a separate reserve component in 1947.

Organization

The National Guard Bureau, a joint bureau of the Departments of the Army and the Air Force, administers federal funding and policy; its chief is a four-star general who sits on the Joint Chiefs of Staff. In each state the adjutant general commands the Guard when not in federal service. Total strength in recent years has been roughly 430,000–450,000 personnel, the large majority in the Army National Guard.
