Etiology

Etiology (also spelled aetiology) is the study of causation or origination. The word is derived from the Greek aitiología, "giving a reason for". In medicine, etiology refers to the cause or causes of a disease or condition — the factors whose presence gives rise to the illness. In wider usage the term covers the investigation of causes in fields such as philosophy, law, mythology and epidemiology.

In medicine

An etiologic agent may be a pathogen (such as a bacterium, virus or parasite), a genetic defect, an environmental exposure, a nutritional deficiency or a combination of factors. Diseases may be classified by etiology, for example as infectious, hereditary, physiological or deficiency diseases. Where no cause is known, a condition is described as idiopathic. Establishing causation historically depended on Koch's postulates for infectious disease; modern frameworks such as the Bradford Hill criteria weigh the strength, consistency, temporality and plausibility of an association before inferring cause.

History

Ancient medicine explained disease through humoral imbalance or divine displeasure, and many cultures developed etiological myths to explain the origins of customs, names and natural phenomena. The germ theory of disease, developed in the nineteenth century by figures such as Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, placed etiology on an experimental footing and transformed public health. Twentieth-century chronic-disease epidemiology broadened the concept to networks of interacting risk factors, sometimes called the "web of causation."

In other fields

In mythology, an etiological tale (or aition) explains the origin of a ritual practice, a place name or a feature of the world. In law and philosophy, etiology overlaps with the study of causation itself — the relation between an event and the conditions that produce it.
