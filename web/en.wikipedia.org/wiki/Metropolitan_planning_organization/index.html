Metropolitan planning organization

A metropolitan planning organization (MPO) is a federally mandated and federally funded transportation policy-making organization in the United States, made up of representatives from local governments and transportation agencies. Federal law requires that every urbanized area with a population of more than 50,000 designate an MPO to carry out a continuing, cooperative, and comprehensive ("3C") metropolitan transportation planning process. Federal funding for transportation projects in the area flows through plans and programmes adopted by the MPO. There are more than 400 MPOs in the United States.

History

The requirement originated in the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1962, which made federal highway aid conditional on a regional planning process, and was formalised by later legislation, notably the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1973 (which coined the MPO designation role for governors) and the Intermodal Surface Transportation Efficiency Act of 1991 (ISTEA), which significantly expanded MPO authority.

Functions

MPOs produce two key documents:

  • The Metropolitan Transportation Plan (or Long-Range Transportation Plan) — a 20-plus-year, fiscally constrained vision for the region's transport system.
  • The Transportation Improvement Program (TIP) — a short-term (four-year) programme of funded projects, which must be consistent with the long-range plan.

MPOs are governed by boards of elected local officials, with technical staff support; larger areas (over 200,000 population) are designated Transportation Management Areas with additional powers and responsibilities, including congestion management. Critics note that MPO boards are often weighted toward suburban jurisdictions and automobile-oriented investment, though reforms have periodically sought to rebalance representation and priorities.
