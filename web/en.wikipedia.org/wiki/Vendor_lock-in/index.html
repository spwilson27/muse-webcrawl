Vendor lock-in

Vendor lock-in
Lock-in makes switching suppliers costly

In economics and business, vendor lock-in, also known as proprietary lock-in or customer lock-in, makes a customer dependent on a vendor for products, unable to use another vendor without substantial switching costs. The use of open standards and alternative options makes such systems "decoupled" from the lock-in and provides a benefit to the customer. Lock-in is often used by vendors as a means of generating recurring revenue and discouraging customers from moving to competitors.

Mechanisms

Lock-in can arise from proprietary file formats and protocols that competitors cannot read, from contractual terms such as long subscriptions and termination fees, from the cost of retraining staff and migrating data, and from network effects, where a product becomes more valuable as more people use it. In computing, classic examples include proprietary document formats, printer manufacturers' ink cartridges, games consoles tied to exclusive titles, and cloud computing services whose proprietary APIs and data-egress charges discourage migration.

Types

The economist Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian catalogued forms of lock-in including contractual commitments, durable purchases with consumables, brand-specific training, information and databases stored in proprietary formats, specialised suppliers, and loyalty programmes. Lock-in may be deliberate strategy, but it also arises incidentally from investment in a technology.

Avoidance

Customers counter lock-in by insisting on open standards, data portability, interoperability testing and exit clauses in contracts. Regulators have sometimes intervened, for example by mandating charger standardisation or requiring dominant platforms to permit interoperability. In software, the use of open-source software and open file formats is a common mitigation.
