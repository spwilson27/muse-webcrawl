A mirrorless camera (formally a mirrorless interchangeable-lens camera, MILC, or digital single-lens mirrorless, DSLM) is a digital camera with an interchangeable lens mount that, unlike a digital single-lens reflex camera (DSLR), has no reflex mirror or optical viewfinder. Light passes through the lens directly to the image sensor, and the photographer composes using an electronic viewfinder (EVF) or the rear display, which show a live preview from the sensor. Removing the mirror box allows smaller, lighter bodies and shorter flange focal distances.

History

The first commercial mirrorless interchangeable-lens camera was the Panasonic Lumix DMC-G1, released in 2008, which launched the Micro Four Thirds system developed with Olympus. Sony's NEX series (2010) brought APS-C sensors to the format, and the Sony α7 (2013) was the first full-frame mirrorless camera. Through the 2010s, mirrorless systems steadily displaced DSLRs: in 2018 Canon and Nikon — the dominant DSLR makers — launched their full-frame mirrorless systems (EOS R/RF and Z-mount respectively), and both subsequently wound down DSLR development. By the early 2020s mirrorless cameras accounted for the overwhelming majority of interchangeable-lens camera sales.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include compact size and weight; a live exposure preview in the EVF; fast, accurate on-sensor autofocus with subject and eye detection; silent electronic shutters and very high burst rates; and in-body image stabilisation. Traditional disadvantages — shorter battery life than DSLRs, EVF lag and blackout, and smaller native lens catalogues — narrowed rapidly as the systems matured.

Sensor formats and mounts

Mirrorless systems span medium format (Fujifilm GFX, Hasselblad X), full-frame (Sony E, Canon RF, Nikon Z, L-Mount), APS-C (Fujifilm X, Canon EF-M/RF-S, Sony E), and Micro Four Thirds (Panasonic and OM System). Their short flange distances allow many legacy lenses to be adapted, which has encouraged a large market in mount adapters.

Market impact

The shift to mirrorless restructured the camera industry: Sony rose from a minor player to the leading full-frame maker, while mirrorless video features (high-resolution recording, advanced autofocus) made these cameras standard tools for hybrid photo/video creators.
