Bryaxis (Greek: Βρύαξις) was a Greek sculptor of the 4th century BC, one of the four masters — with Scopas, Leochares and Timotheus — employed on the sculptural decoration of the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus. He is also associated with the creation of the cult image of the god Serapis in Alexandria.

Mausoleum at Halicarnassus

According to Pliny the Elder, the four sculptors each carved one side of the Mausoleum, the tomb of King Mausolus of Caria completed around 350 BC; Bryaxis was responsible for the north side. Vitruvius, in his list of the Mausoleum's sculptors, names a "Bryaxis" among them. The Mausoleum friezes — an Amazonomachy, a Centauromachy and a chariot race — cannot be divided among the four hands with certainty, but the north-side work was counted in antiquity among the sculptor's masterpieces, and the monument's fame kept his name alive.

Serapis of Alexandria

Bryaxis is frequently credited with the colossal cult statue of Serapis for the Serapeum of Alexandria, the great temple founded under Ptolemy III (3rd century BC). The statue — Zeus-like in form, bearded, wearing the modius (grain-measure) headdress and accompanied by the three-headed Cerberus — became the canonical image of Serapis throughout the Roman world, copied in marble and bronze everywhere. Some scholars, however, attribute it to a younger namesake, a "Bryaxis the Younger," since the Serapeum image seems to post-date the elder sculptor's Mausoleum work by two or three generations; the question remains unresolved.

Other attributions

Pliny also records works by Bryaxis including statues of Asclepius and Seleucus, and a group of five colossal gods at Antioch is sometimes assigned to him. A signed base from Athens suggests he (or his family) worked in Attica as well. Almost none of his work survives in identifiable form, and his reputation, like that of several 4th-century masters, rests on literary testimony.
