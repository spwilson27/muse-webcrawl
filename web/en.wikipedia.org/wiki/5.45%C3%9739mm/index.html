The 5.45×39mm cartridge is a rimless bottlenecked intermediate cartridge developed in the Soviet Union and introduced into service in 1974 with the AK-74 assault rifle. It replaced the 7.62×39mm round as the standard service cartridge of the Soviet Union and its allies, paralleling NATO's move to the small-calibre 5.56×45mm cartridge.

History

Soviet designers studied the American 5.56 mm round's performance in Vietnam and sought a lighter cartridge permitting more ammunition to be carried, with lower recoil and a flatter trajectory than 7.62×39mm. The result, designed under Viktor Sabelnikov, entered service alongside Mikhail Kalashnikov's AK-74.

Design

The cartridge uses a tapered steel case 39.82 mm long with a bottleneck, firing a 5.6 mm-diameter bullet of about 3.2–3.4 g at roughly 880–900 m/s from an AK-74 barrel. The standard 7N6 projectile has a steel core behind a hollow air pocket in the nose, which shifts the centre of mass rearward.

Variants

Service loadings include the 7N6 and improved 7N10 armour-piercing rounds, the 7N22, the more recent 7N39 armour-piercing round with a carbide penetrator, the 7T3 tracer, and blank and training variants.

Performance debate

During the Soviet–Afghan War, Afghan mujahideen nicknamed the round the "poison bullet", believing its wounds were toxic; in fact the bullet's tendency to yaw early in tissue can produce severe wounding. Terminal-effectiveness comparisons with 5.56×45mm NATO remain a perennial subject of small-arms debate.
