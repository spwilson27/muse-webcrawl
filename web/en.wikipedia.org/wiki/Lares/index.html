Lares

Lares (Latin: Larēs) were guardian deities in ancient Roman religion. Their origin is uncertain; they may have been hero-ancestors, guardians of the hearth, hero-men, or field deities. They were believed to observe, protect, and bless what their domain encompassed: the home, crossroads, farmland, roads, and the state.

Household Lares, the Lares Familiares, were honoured daily at the household shrine, the lararium, usually a small niche or cupboard in the atrium, with offerings of food, wine, and incense. Small bronze or terracotta statues of the Lares, depicted as youthful dancers in short tunics holding a drinking horn and a patera, were kept in the shrine.

Types of Lares

  • Lares Familiares — protectors of the household and its members.
  • Lares Compitales — guardians of crossroads, honoured at the Compitalia festival.
  • Lares Permarini — protectors of sailors and sea voyages.
  • Lares Praestites — guardians of the city and state of Rome.
  • Lares Augusti — the Lares of the emperor, introduced under Augustus.

Worship

The head of the household led daily prayers before the lararium. On special occasions such as weddings, births, and departures, the Lares received particular honours. Together with the Penates and the Genius of the paterfamilias, the Lares formed the core of Roman domestic religion.
