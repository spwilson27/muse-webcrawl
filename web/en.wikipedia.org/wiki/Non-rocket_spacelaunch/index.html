Non-rocket spacelaunch

Non-rocket spacelaunch refers to theoretical concepts for reaching space in which much of the speed and altitude needed is provided by a propulsion technique that does not rely on rockets, or by ground-based infrastructure, rather than the vehicle carrying large quantities of its own propellant. Such methods could in principle reduce launch costs dramatically, because rockets must expend most of their energy accelerating their own fuel.

Static structures

Space elevators propose a tether anchored at the equator extending beyond geostationary orbit, along which climbers ascend. Orbital rings and space fountains are related megastructure concepts. All require materials or engineering far beyond present capability.

Dynamic and kinetic systems

Mass drivers (electromagnetic catapults) could accelerate payloads along a track to orbital velocity, practical mainly in airless environments such as the Moon. Space guns were investigated by projects such as the US HARP programme of the 1960s. The launch loop (Lofstrom loop) envisions a magnetically levitated belt hundreds of kilometres long. Skyhooks — rotating or non-rotating tethers in orbit — could exchange momentum with suborbital vehicles, as in the rotovator concept.

Hybrid approaches

Other proposals combine conventional rockets with assistance: air launch from carrier aircraft, maglev launch-assist tracks, and laser or microwave beamed-energy propulsion. As of the 2020s no non-rocket system has delivered a payload to orbit, though spin-launch and railgun research continue at small scales.
