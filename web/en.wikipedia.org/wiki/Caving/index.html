Caving

Caving – also known as spelunking in the United States and Canada and potholing in the United Kingdom and Ireland – is the recreational pastime of exploring wild cave systems (as distinguished from show caves). In contrast, speleology is the scientific study of caves and the cave environment.

Practices

Cavers explore caves for the physical challenge, for exploration and surveying of new passages, and for the scientific and photographic interest of cave formations and fauna. Trips range from walking-size horizontal passages to demanding expeditions involving vertical pitches descended by single-rope technique, tight squeezes, crawling, and sump diving undertaken by specialist cave divers.

Equipment

Standard equipment includes a helmet with a mounted light (now usually LED, historically carbide lamps), protective clothing such as oversuits and wellington boots or sturdy footwear, gloves, and back-up light sources. Vertical caving uses harnesses, ascenders, descenders and static ropes. Cavers follow a conservation ethic of "take nothing but photographs, leave nothing but footprints, kill nothing but time", since cave environments and formations recover extremely slowly from damage.

Safety

Hazards include flooding, hypothermia, falls, rockfall, getting lost and bad air; organised cave rescue volunteer teams exist in most major caving regions. Famous caving areas include the Yorkshire Dales and Mendip Hills in England, the Mammoth Cave system in Kentucky, the caves of the Nullarbor Plain in Australia, and the deep systems of the Picos de Europa in Spain and the Arabika massif in Georgia, where Veryovkina Cave exceeds 2,200 metres in depth.
