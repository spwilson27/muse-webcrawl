Platform economy

The platform economy is economic and social activity facilitated by platforms—typically online sales or technology frameworks. Underlying the concept is the rise of digital matchmakers: businesses that connect two or more distinct groups of users and capture value from the interactions they enable, rather than by producing goods or services directly.

Overview

A platform is a business model that creates value by facilitating exchanges between two or more interdependent groups, usually consumers and producers. Classic pre-digital examples include shopping malls, newspapers supported by advertising, and credit card networks. The internet dramatically reduced the cost of building such intermediaries, enabling companies such as Google, Amazon, Facebook (Meta), Apple, Alibaba, Uber, and Airbnb to reach enormous scale.

Platform businesses benefit from network effects: the service becomes more valuable as more people use it. Because of these effects, platform markets often tend toward winner-take-most outcomes, in which one or a few firms dominate a category. Platforms act as private regulators of their markets, setting rules of access, ranking, pricing, and dispute resolution for participants.

Types of platforms

  • Transaction platforms (marketplaces) facilitate exchanges: e-commerce sites, app stores, ride-hailing, and accommodation booking.
  • Innovation platforms provide a technological base on which others build, such as operating systems and cloud services.
  • Integrated platforms combine both functions, as with Apple (App Store plus iOS) or Google (Play plus Android).
  • Investment platforms aggregate portfolios of platform businesses.

History

The economics of two-sided markets was formalized in the early 2000s by researchers including Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole, and Geoffrey Parker and Marshall Van Alstyne, who described network effects and platform competition. The smartphone era after 2007, cheap cloud infrastructure, and widespread mobile internet access allowed platform models to spread into transport, lodging, food delivery, finance, education, and healthcare.

Criticism and regulation

Critics argue that platforms can entrench monopoly power, shift risks onto workers in the "gig economy," erode privacy through large-scale data collection, and avoid taxes or sectoral regulation. Nick Srnicek's Platform Capitalism (2016) popularized analysis of platforms as a new form of capitalist organization based on data extraction. Governments have responded with measures such as the European Union's Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act, antitrust cases in the United States, and various national rules reclassifying gig workers as employees.
