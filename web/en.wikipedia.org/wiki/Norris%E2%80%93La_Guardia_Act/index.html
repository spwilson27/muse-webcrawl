Overview

The Norris–La Guardia Act (also known as the Anti-Injunction Bill) is a 1932 United States federal law that curtailed the power of federal courts to issue injunctions against nonviolent labor disputes and outlawed so-called "yellow-dog contracts", agreements under which employees promised not to join a union. It was a landmark victory for organised labor during the Great Depression.

Background and provisions

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, employers routinely obtained court injunctions to break strikes and boycotts, and yellow-dog contracts were widely used to exclude union members from employment. Sponsored by Senator George W. Norris of Nebraska and Representative Fiorello H. La Guardia of New York, the Act declared yellow-dog contracts unenforceable in federal courts and sharply restricted the circumstances under which injunctions could be granted in labor disputes.

The Act also guaranteed workers the right to organise, to choose their own representatives, and to engage in concerted activities without employer interference.

Legacy

The Norris–La Guardia Act laid the groundwork for the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act), which established a comprehensive federal framework for collective bargaining. Its restrictions on labor injunctions remain part of United States law.
