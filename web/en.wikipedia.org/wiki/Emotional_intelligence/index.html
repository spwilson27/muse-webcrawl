Emotional intelligence

Emotional intelligence (EI) is defined as the ability to perceive, use, understand, manage, and handle emotions. People with high emotional intelligence can recognize their own emotions and those of others, use emotional information to guide thinking and behavior, discern between different feelings and label them appropriately, and adjust emotions to adapt to environments. The term first appeared in 1964, gained research form in the work of Peter Salovey and John Mayer (1990), and was popularized by psychologist and journalist Daniel Goleman's 1995 best-selling book Emotional Intelligence.

The four-branch ability model of emotional intelligence.

Models

Three main families of models compete:

  • Ability model (Mayer, Salovey, Caruso) — EI as a cognitive ability with four branches: perceiving emotions, using emotions to facilitate thought, understanding emotions, and managing emotions. Measured with performance tests such as the MSCEIT.
  • Mixed models (Goleman; Bar-On) — broad competencies combining abilities with traits and social skills: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills (Goleman); measured largely by self-report and 360° instruments (EQ-i).
  • Trait model (Petrides) — EI as a constellation of emotion-related self-perceptions and dispositions, located within personality frameworks.

Measurement and criticism

EI research faces criticism over construct validity: self-report ("trait") measures correlate heavily with established personality traits (especially low neuroticism and high extraversion/agreeableness), raising the question whether they measure anything new; scoring of ability tests relies on consensus or expert norms whose correctness is debatable; and popular claims — such as EI accounting for most of job success — exceed the evidence. Meta-analyses find ability EI modestly predicts job performance, academic outcomes, and mental and physical health, with incremental validity beyond IQ and personality that is real but modest. EI is generally higher in women on ability tests, and some aspects can be improved by training, though effects are debated.

Applications

EI concepts are widely applied in leadership development, education (social-emotional learning programs), psychotherapy, and organizational consulting. Goleman's writings in particular drove adoption in business, where EI training became a substantial industry despite the scientific debates.
