Naval operations in the Dardanelles campaign

The naval operations in the Dardanelles campaign (1915) were the Royal Navy and French Navy's attempt, in the First World War, to force the Dardanelles strait, capture Constantinople, and knock the Ottoman Empire out of the war, thereby opening a supply route to Russia through the Black Sea.

Background

The plan, championed by Winston Churchill, First Lord of the Admiralty, assumed that warships alone could silence the Turkish forts guarding the straits. A preliminary bombardment took place on 19 February 1915, followed by further attacks as minesweepers — mostly converted trawlers with civilian crews — struggled to clear the minefields under fire.

The attack of 18 March

On 18 March 1915 a fleet of British and French battleships, including the new dreadnought HMS Queen Elizabeth, advanced into the strait in three divisions. Unknown to the Allies, the Ottoman minelayer Nusret had laid a line of mines parallel to the Asian shore in Erenköy Bay on the night of 7–8 March, precisely where Allied ships manoeuvred. The French battleship Bouvet struck a mine and sank within minutes with most of her crew; the British battlecruiser HMS Inflexible and battleships HMS Irresistible and HMS Ocean were mined, the latter two sinking, and the French Gaulois was badly damaged. In total the Allies lost six capital ships sunk or put out of action in a single day.

Consequences

The failure convinced Allied commanders that the strait could not be forced by ships alone. The attempt shifted to a land campaign — the Gallipoli landings beginning 25 April 1915 — which also failed and was evacuated by January 1916. Submarines achieved some successes penetrating to the Sea of Marmara, where HMS E15, E11 and others sank Ottoman shipping, but the naval campaign's main object was never achieved.
