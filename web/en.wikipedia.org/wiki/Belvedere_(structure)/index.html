Belvedere (structure)

A belvedere (from Italian bel vedere, "beautiful view") is an architectural structure sited to take advantage of a fine or scenic view. The term may describe a purpose-built pavilion in a landscape or garden, a raised turret or cupola on a roof, or an open gallery — often with arches or colonnades — at the top of a building. Belvederes generally lack walls so that those inside can enjoy both the view and cooling breezes.

History

The idea of a viewing structure goes back to antiquity, but the belvedere flourished in Renaissance Italy. The Vatican's Belvedere Court, begun under Pope Innocent VIII in the 1480s and extended by Bramante, gave the type its name and fame: a villa and terraced garden built to display classical sculpture and command views over Rome. From Italy the belvedere spread through the great gardens of Europe, becoming a standard feature of Baroque and English landscape gardens.

Forms

In domestic architecture, a belvedere may take the form of a small glazed cupola or lantern on a roof — a feature of some Italianate and Second Empire houses in North America — used for ventilation as well as views. In gardens, belvederes overlap with gazebos, temples, and summer houses, but are distinguished by their siting on high ground or at the end of a vista to frame a prospect. Many palaces, such as the Belvedere in Vienna, took their names from the views they commanded.
