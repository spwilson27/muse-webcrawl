Praetor

A praetor (Latin: praetor) was a senior magistracy of the Roman Republic and later the Roman Empire, ranking below the consul and above the aedile and quaestor in the cursus honorum. Praetors held imperium and exercised judicial, administrative, and military functions. The title derives from praeire, "to go before".

History

The office was created in 366 BC, traditionally as a concession so that plebeians could access a chief magistracy; the first praetor was Spurius Furius Camillus. Initially a single praetor — the praetor urbanus — administered justice among Roman citizens in the city. In 242 BC a second praetor, the praetor peregrinus, was added to handle cases involving foreigners (peregrini). As Rome acquired provinces, additional praetors were created to govern them; the number rose to eight under Sulla and higher under Caesar.

Judicial role

The urban praetor supervised civil litigation at Rome and each year issued a praetor's edict setting out the principles he would follow in granting actions and defences. Successive edicts accumulated into a body of praetorian law (ius honorarium) that supplemented and corrected the strict civil law, introducing important innovations such as actions for fraud and duress and protection of possession. The edict was finally codified under Hadrian by the jurist Salvius Julianus.

Other functions

Praetors could command armies, convene the Senate, and govern provinces after their year in office as propraetors. Under the Empire the office retained prestige and judicial duties — including oversight of the treasury at times — but lost its political significance.
