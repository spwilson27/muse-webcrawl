Rainfed agriculture

Rainfed agriculture is farming that depends entirely on rainfall for water, without irrigation. It is practised on the majority of the world's cultivated land — roughly 80% of cropland — and produces a large share of food in developing countries, particularly cereals in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Yields, however, are typically lower and far more variable than under irrigation.

Characteristics

Rainfed systems range from dryland farming in semi-arid regions, where soil moisture conservation is paramount, to humid-zone farming where rainfall is abundant but drainage and erosion are the main concerns. Key risks are drought, erratic onset of the rainy season, and intense storms that damage crops and soil. Because output depends on weather, rainfed farmers face high production risk and often low incomes.

Improvement strategies

Techniques to raise and stabilise rainfed productivity include in-situ water harvesting (contour bunds, tied ridges, zaï pits), mulching and conservation tillage to preserve soil moisture, drought-tolerant and early-maturing varieties, agroforestry, integrated soil fertility management, and supplemental irrigation from small-scale water storage where feasible. Weather forecasting and index-based crop insurance are increasingly used to manage climate risk.

Importance

Because most future cropland expansion is limited, improving rainfed agriculture is central to global food security and climate adaptation: most of the world's poor farmers are rainfed smallholders.
