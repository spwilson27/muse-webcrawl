Vehicle recalls in the United States

In the United States, a vehicle recall is issued when a manufacturer or the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) determines that a vehicle model contains a safety-related defect or fails to comply with a federal safety standard. Under the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966, manufacturers must notify owners and remedy the defect free of charge, usually by repair, replacement or (rarely) repurchase.

Owners are notified by mail and can check open recalls by VIN on the NHTSA website.

How recalls work

Defects come to light through manufacturer testing, warranty data and consumer complaints filed with NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation. Manufacturers must report a safety defect to NHTSA within five working days of discovering it, then mail notification letters to owners of affected vehicles, identified by VIN. Dealers perform the remedy without charge. Completion rates average around 70–75% for passenger vehicles, leaving many recalled vehicles unrepaired; NHTSA publicises recalls and operates a VIN lookup tool to raise completion.

Major recalls

Some of the largest U.S. recalls include the 1980 Ford recall of about 21 million vehicles for transmission park-gear problems; the Firestone tyre/Ford Explorer crisis of 2000–2001; General Motors' 2014 ignition-switch recall, initially about 2.6 million small cars and ultimately some 30 million vehicles worldwide, linked to at least 124 deaths; and the Takata airbag recall, the largest in history, affecting roughly 67 million inflators in the U.S. alone because the propellant could degrade and rupture explosively.

Legal and regulatory framework

The TREAD Act of 2000, passed after the Firestone scandal, added "early warning" reporting requirements obliging manufacturers to share claims and warranty data with NHTSA. Regulators can levy civil penalties for delayed recalls — GM paid a then-record $35 million in 2014, later raised ceilings reaching $105 million for Toyota in an earlier case and $200 million for Takata-related violations. Recalls are distinct from technical service bulletins (non-safety guidance to dealers) and from "silent" or customer-satisfaction campaigns.

Statistics

In recent decades roughly 20–40 million vehicles per year have been recalled in the United States, with sharp spikes in 2014 (about 64 million) during the GM and Takata affairs. Software-related recalls, remedied increasingly by over-the-air updates, are a growing share as vehicles become more electronic.
