Impossible trinity

The impossible trinity (also known as the impossible trilemma, the monetary trilemma, or the unholy trinity) is a concept in international economics which states that it is impossible to have all three of the following at the same time:

  • a fixed foreign exchange rate,
  • free capital movement (absence of capital controls), and
  • an independent monetary policy.

The concept was developed from the Mundell–Fleming model by economists including Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming in the 1960s, and popularised as a "trilemma" in the 1990s. According to the theory, a government must choose two of the three policies and give up the third, because interest-rate arbitrage and capital flows will defeat any attempt to sustain all three.

The three corners

  • Fixed rate + free capital: monetary policy is surrendered, because domestic interest rates must track foreign rates to defend the peg. Currency boards such as Hong Kong's choose this corner.
  • Fixed rate + independent monetary policy: capital controls must restrict flows; the Bretton Woods system and, for decades, China's managed system approximated this combination.
  • Free capital + independent monetary policy: the exchange rate must float, as with the United States, Japan, and the post-1992 United Kingdom.

Historical episodes

Attempts to hold an untenable combination have repeatedly ended in crisis: the collapse of Bretton Woods in 1971–73, the European Exchange Rate Mechanism crises of 1992–93 (in which the United Kingdom and Italy were driven out), and the Asian financial crisis of 1997–98, in which fixed pegs combined with open capital accounts invited speculative attack. Countries responded in different ways—Malaysia imposed capital controls, others floated.

The euro can be seen as the extreme form of the first corner: member states share a single currency and central bank, permanently giving up national monetary policy in return for completely free capital movement within the union.
