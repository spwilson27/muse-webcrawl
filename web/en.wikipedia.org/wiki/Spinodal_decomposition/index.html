Spinodal decomposition

Spinodal decomposition is a mechanism by which a single thermodynamic phase spontaneously separates into two phases, without the nucleation barrier characteristic of ordinary phase separation. It occurs when a homogeneous mixture is brought into the unstable region of its phase diagram—the region inside the spinodal curve, where the second derivative of the free energy with respect to composition is negative. In this region, infinitesimal fluctuations in composition grow rather than decay, because diffusion proceeds "uphill", from regions of low concentration to regions of high concentration, lowering the total free energy.

Distinction from nucleation and growth

Between the binodal (coexistence) curve and the spinodal curve, a mixture is metastable: separation requires the formation of nuclei of the new phase large enough to overcome a surface-energy barrier, and proceeds by nucleation and growth, producing discrete droplets. Inside the spinodal, by contrast, decomposition is spontaneous and initially produces a characteristic, highly interconnected, quasi-periodic microstructure with a well-defined dominant wavelength. The amplified fluctuation spectrum was described theoretically by John W. Cahn and John E. Hilliard through the Cahn–Hilliard equation, which incorporates a gradient-energy term penalizing sharp composition gradients.

Coarsening and applications

Following the initial amplification stage, the microstructure coarsens: the characteristic length scale grows with time (approximately as t1/3 in the late stage) to reduce interfacial area, eventually producing two distinct macroscopic phases. Spinodal decomposition is exploited in materials science to produce fine two-phase microstructures with useful properties, for example in certain alloys, polymer blends, porous glasses such as Vycor, and phase-separated membranes.
