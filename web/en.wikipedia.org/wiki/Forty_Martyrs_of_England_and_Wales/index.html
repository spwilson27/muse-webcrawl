Forty Martyrs of England and Wales

The Forty Martyrs of England and Wales are a group of Catholic, lay and religious, men and women, executed between 1535 and 1679 for treason and related offences under various laws enacted by Parliament during the English Reformation. The individuals listed range from Carthusian monks who in 1535 declined to accept Henry VIII's Act of Supremacy, to seminary priests who were caught up in the alleged Popish Plot against Charles II in 1679.

Canonization

Following their beatifications between 1886 and 1929, the Forty Martyrs were canonized by Pope Paul VI on 25 October 1970. Many of them had previously been beatified, and their feast day in the Roman Catholic calendar of saints is kept on 4 May. In England, the feast of the Forty Martyrs is celebrated as a solemnity on 4 May; in Wales it is observed as a feast.

Background

The first wave of executions came with the rejection of papal authority by Henry VIII. The Act of Supremacy of 1534 declared the king supreme head of the Church of England, and refusal to take the prescribed oath became high treason. Carthusian martyrs such as John Houghton were among the first to die, hanged, drawn and quartered at Tyburn in 1535. Under Elizabeth I, recusancy laws and the statute making it treason for a Catholic priest ordained abroad to enter England brought a second wave, including Cuthbert Mayne, Edmund Campion and Margaret Clitherow, who was pressed to death at York in 1586.

List of martyrs

Among the forty are three laywomen (Margaret Clitherow, Margaret Ward and Anne Line), several secular priests, Franciscans, Benedictines, Augustinians, Jesuits, a Brigittine monk and a Carthusian. The best known include Edmund Campion, Robert Southwell, John Southworth and Philip Howard, Earl of Arundel, who died in the Tower of London in 1595.
