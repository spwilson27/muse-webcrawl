Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) is a hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy of the peripheral nervous system characterized by progressive loss of muscle tissue and touch sensation across various parts of the body. It is the most commonly inherited neurological disorder, affecting approximately 1 in 2,500 people. The disease is named after its discoverers, Jean-Martin Charcot and Pierre Marie in Paris and Howard Henry Tooth in Cambridge, who described it in 1886.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin in the first two decades of life and progress slowly. Weakness starts in the feet and lower legs, causing foot drop, frequent tripping, and the characteristic high-arched foot (pes cavus) and hammer toes; later the hands may be affected. Sensation to vibration, pain, and temperature can be diminished in a "stocking-glove" distribution. Despite the shared name, CMT is unrelated to Charcot joint or to multiple sclerosis.

Genetics and types

More than 100 genes are associated with CMT. The most common form, CMT1A, is caused by a duplication of the PMP22 gene on chromosome 17 and involves demyelination, markedly slowing nerve conduction. Axonal forms (CMT2) often involve MFN2. CMTX is X-linked, commonly involving GJB1 (connexin 32). Inheritance is most often autosomal dominant.

Management

There is no cure; management includes physiotherapy, occupational therapy, ankle-foot orthoses, orthopaedic surgery for deformities, and avoidance of neurotoxic drugs such as vincristine. Life expectancy is usually normal.
