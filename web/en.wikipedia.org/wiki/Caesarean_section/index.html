Caesarean section

Caesarean section
Other names: C-section, caesarean delivery
Specialty: Obstetrics
ICD-10: O82

A caesarean section, also known as a C-section, is a surgical procedure by which one or more babies are delivered through an incision in the mother's abdomen. It is often performed because vaginal delivery would put the baby or mother at risk.

Indications

Reasons for the operation include obstructed labour, twin pregnancy, high blood pressure in the mother, breech birth, and problems with the placenta or umbilical cord. A caesarean delivery may be planned (elective) or performed as an emergency during labour.

Procedure

The operation is usually performed under regional anaesthesia (spinal or epidural). A horizontal incision is made through the abdominal wall and the lower uterine segment; the baby is delivered, the cord is clamped and cut, and the placenta is removed before the uterus and abdomen are closed with sutures. The procedure typically takes about 45 minutes to an hour.

Risks and outcomes

Risks to the baby include breathing problems at birth; risks to the mother include infection, blood loss, and reactions to anaesthesia, as well as complications in future pregnancies. The World Health Organization has long considered a population rate of roughly 10–15% to be consistent with good outcomes, while rates are far higher in many countries.

History

Accounts of postmortem caesarean delivery are ancient, and Roman law (the lex caesarea) required extraction of a child from a dying mother. Successful operations on living mothers became survivable only with the development of anaesthesia, antisepsis and uterine suturing in the late nineteenth century.
