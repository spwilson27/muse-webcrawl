Folding@home (FAH or F@h) is a distributed computing project aimed to help scientists develop new therapeutics for a variety of diseases by simulating protein dynamics. This includes the process of protein folding and the movements of proteins, and is reliant on simulations run on the personal computers of volunteers. Folding@home is currently based at Washington University in St. Louis School of Medicine, led by Greg Bowman; it was founded by Vijay Pande at Stanford University, whose group led the project from 2000 to 2019.

How it works

Volunteers install a client program that downloads a "work unit" — a chunk of a molecular dynamics simulation — computes it using spare CPU and GPU capacity, and returns the results to project servers, earning points for their account or team. Because protein folding occurs on timescales far beyond what a single computer can simulate, the project uses methods such as Markov state models to stitch together many short trajectories contributed by thousands of machines.

Research

The project has contributed to research on Alzheimer's disease, Huntington's disease, Parkinson's disease, several cancers, antibiotic resistance, and infectious diseases. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, Folding@home focused on the SARS-CoV-2 virus and experienced an enormous surge of volunteers, becoming the first computing system to exceed one exaFLOPS of performance — at the time faster than the world's leading supercomputers combined. Its simulations of the viral spike protein helped reveal druggable pockets used in drug-design efforts.

Scale and recognition

Folding@home was recognised by Guinness World Records in 2007 as the most powerful distributed computing network. A version of the client shipped on Sony's PlayStation 3 from 2007 until 2012, contributing substantial computing power. The project has produced well over a hundred peer-reviewed scientific papers.
