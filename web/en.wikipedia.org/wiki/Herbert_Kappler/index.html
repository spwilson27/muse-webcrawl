Herbert Kappler (23 September 1907 – 9 February 1978) was a German SS officer and Gestapo chief in Rome during the Second World War. He was responsible for the Ardeatine massacre of March 1944, in which 335 Italian civilians and prisoners were murdered in reprisal for a partisan attack, and for the deportation of more than a thousand Jews from Rome to Auschwitz in October 1943. After the war he was convicted by an Italian military court and sentenced to life imprisonment; in 1977 he escaped from a Rome military hospital with the help of his wife and fled to West Germany, where he died the following year.

Early life and career

Kappler was born in Stuttgart and trained as an electrician. He joined the Nazi Party and the SA in 1931, transferring to the SS in 1933, and entered the SD, the SS intelligence service, in the mid-1930s. In 1939 he was posted to Rome as a police attaché at the German embassy and liaison officer to the Italian police, a position that gave him detailed knowledge of Italian affairs before Italy's defection from the Axis.

Occupation of Rome

After the German occupation of Rome in September 1943, Kappler became commander of the Security Police and SD in the city. In October 1943 he extorted 50 kilograms of gold from Rome's Jewish community under threat of deportation; despite payment, on 16 October 1943 his men rounded up more than 1,200 Jews, of whom 1,023 were deported to Auschwitz, where nearly all were murdered.

Ardeatine massacre

On 23 March 1944, Italian partisans killed 33 German military policemen in the Via Rasella bombing. Hitler ordered reprisals at a ratio of ten Italians for each German killed. Kappler supervised the selection of 335 victims — prisoners, Jews, and civilians seized from the streets — who were shot in the Ardeatine Caves on 24 March 1944. Five more victims were killed than the quota required.

Trial, imprisonment and escape

Kappler was tried by a British military court in 1947 for the extortion of the gold and by an Italian military court in 1948 for the Ardeatine massacre, receiving a life sentence. He was held at the military prison of Gaeta. In August 1977, seriously ill with cancer, he was transferred to the Celio military hospital in Rome, from which his wife Anneliese smuggled him out — according to the famous account, concealed in a large suitcase. He reached West Germany, which refused Italian requests for extradition, and died at Soltau on 9 February 1978.
