Mixed-use development

Mixed-use development is a type of urban development, urban design, urban planning and/or zoning that blends multiple uses, such as residential, commercial, cultural, institutional or entertainment, into one space, where those functions are to some degree physically and functionally integrated, and that provides pedestrian connections. Mixed-use development is often applied at the scale of a single building, a city block, or a neighbourhood, and is a central tenet of the New Urbanism movement.

Forms

Mixed use can be vertical — different uses stacked within one building, typically shops or offices at ground level with apartments above; horizontal — single-use buildings placed side by side within a walkable district; or a combination of the two. The traditional main street, with retail at pavement level and dwellings or offices above, is a classic vertical example that predates modern zoning.

History

Before the 20th century, most towns and cities mixed homes, workshops and commerce by default. Industrialisation brought nuisances such as noise, smoke and heavy traffic, prompting planners to separate uses; single-use zoning codes — exemplified by Euclidean zoning in the United States — institutionalised that separation, spreading low-density, car-dependent development. From the 1960s, critics such as Jane Jacobs argued that diverse, fine-grained mixtures of uses made streets safer and districts more lively. From the 1980s and 1990s, New Urbanists and transit-oriented development advocates pushed mixed use back into planning policy, and many cities reformed their codes to allow or encourage it.

Benefits and challenges

Advocates cite shorter trips and more walking and cycling, livelier streets across more hours of the day, more housing near jobs and transit, and more efficient use of land and infrastructure. Developers and lenders, however, often find mixed-use projects harder to finance, design and manage than single-use ones, because each use has different market cycles, building requirements and tenant expectations. Noise, servicing and parking can also create conflicts between uses that design and management must resolve.

Examples

Well-known large-scale mixed-use projects include Battery Park City in New York, the redevelopment of King's Cross in London, and transit-linked podium developments in Hong Kong, where housing towers rise above shopping centres and railway stations.
