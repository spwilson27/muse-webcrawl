Arthur Andersen LLP was an American accounting firm founded in Chicago in 1913. Once one of the "Big Five" accounting firms, with some 85,000 employees and revenue of over $9 billion, it collapsed in 2002 after its role in the Enron scandal.


Arthur Andersen's Chicago headquarters

History

Arthur E. Andersen, a Northwestern University professor, built the firm on a reputation for integrity — famously refusing, early in his career, to certify the accounts of a railroad client. Through the 20th century Andersen grew into a global partnership providing audit, tax and consulting services. Its consulting arm split off in 2000 after arbitration and became Accenture.

Enron and collapse

Andersen was auditor to Enron, which concealed massive debts in off-balance-sheet entities. When Enron failed in late 2001, Andersen personnel destroyed large quantities of Enron-related documents. The firm was convicted of obstruction of justice in June 2002, surrendered its audit licences, and effectively ceased to exist, with most offices absorbed by rival firms. The US Supreme Court unanimously overturned the conviction in 2005 (Arthur Andersen LLP v. United States), holding the jury instructions flawed — but by then the firm was gone.

Legacy

The collapse, together with Enron and WorldCom, prompted the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, which created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board and tightened auditor independence. The Big Five became the Big Four.
