The Two Cultures

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

"The Two Cultures" is the first part of an influential 1959 Rede Lecture by British scientist and novelist C. P. Snow, published as The Two Cultures and the Scientific Revolution. Its thesis was that "the intellectual life of the whole of western society" was split into two cultures — namely the sciences and the humanities — and that this was a major hindrance both to solving the world's problems and to education.

Argument

Snow argued that scientists and "literary intellectuals" had ceased to communicate, and that each group was ignorant of the other's achievements: literary intellectuals, he claimed, could rarely describe the Second Law of Thermodynamics, though its scientific equivalent in cultural importance was comparable to the works of Shakespeare. Conversely, he found scientists often had little engagement with literature. He blamed the British educational system for over-specialising too early and for privileging the humanities in positions of power, to the detriment of a world facing problems — poverty, overpopulation, nuclear weapons — that only science and technology could address.

Reaction

The lecture provoked a famous and vitriolic response from the literary critic F. R. Leavis, whose 1962 Richmond Lecture attacked Snow's credentials and the quality of his novels, generating a public controversy known as the Two Cultures controversy. Others, including Jacob Bronowski, responded more sympathetically. Snow revisited the argument in The Two Cultures: And a Second Look (1963), predicting the emergence of a "third culture" — a prediction later taken up for popular science writers who communicate directly with the public.

Legacy

The phrase "two cultures" entered common usage for the divide between arts and sciences, and the debate continues to frame discussions of STEM education, interdisciplinary study and science communication.
