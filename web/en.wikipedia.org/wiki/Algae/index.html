Algae

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Algae are a large and diverse group of photosynthetic organisms, mostly aquatic, ranging from microscopic single-celled phytoplankton to giant kelps tens of metres long. The term is informal — algae span several unrelated lineages, including green algae (close relatives of land plants), red algae, brown algae, diatoms, dinoflagellates and euglenids — united by living in water and making food from sunlight in chloroplasts.

Algae are ecological giants: marine phytoplankton produce a large share of Earth's oxygen — often estimated at about half — and form the base of most aquatic food webs. Diatoms' glass shells rain down to form sediments; coccolithophores build chalk cliffs. Some algae cause harmful blooms ("red tides") that poison shellfish; others are resources — seaweeds eaten across Asia, agar and carrageenan for food and laboratories, and candidate feedstocks for biofuel and carbon capture.

The evolutionary history of algae is the history of photosynthesis spreading through the oceans by endosymbiosis — repeatedly, as different hosts engulfed photosynthetic cells — which is why "algae" is a grade of life rather than a single family.
