Foreign concessions in China at their greatest extent

Concessions in China were territories within Chinese cities that were leased to, and governed by, foreign powers from the mid-19th century until the mid-20th century. Established under the so-called "unequal treaties" imposed after China's defeats in the Opium Wars, the concessions were enclaves in which foreign residents lived under their own laws and administrations, exempt from Chinese jurisdiction through the principle of extraterritoriality. The most important concessions were in Shanghai and Tianjin, but they also existed in Guangzhou (Canton), Hankou, Xiamen (Amoy), Zhenjiang, Jiujiang, Chongqing and other treaty ports.

The system began with the Treaty of Nanking (1842), which ended the First Opium War and opened five treaty ports to foreign trade. Land regulations issued in Shanghai in 1845 created the first foreign settlement there, and in 1863 the British and American settlements merged to form the Shanghai International Settlement, administered by the Shanghai Municipal Council. A separate French Concession was governed from Paris through the French consul. Tianjin eventually acquired up to nine foreign concessions — more than any other Chinese city — held by Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, Italy, Austria-Hungary and Belgium.

Origins

Following the First Opium War (1839–1842), the Qing dynasty was compelled to open ports to foreign commerce and to cede Hong Kong Island to Britain. The Treaty of the Bogue (1843) and the American Treaty of Wanghia and French Treaty of Whampoa (1844) added extraterritorial privileges for foreign nationals. After the Second Opium War (1856–1860), the Treaties of Tientsin and the Convention of Peking opened further ports, including Tianjin and Hankou, and foreign legations were established in Beijing.

Administration

Within the concessions, foreign municipal councils levied taxes, maintained police forces, built roads, docks and utilities, and operated their own courts for their nationals. Chinese residents, who often formed the majority of the population, were governed by these foreign administrations without representation for most of the period. Concessions became centres of banking, commerce and industry, and also refuges for Chinese dissidents and revolutionaries beyond the reach of Qing — and later warlord — authorities.

Retrieval and abolition

Chinese nationalism made retrocession of the concessions a central goal of both the Nationalist (Kuomintang) and Communist movements. Germany and Austria lost their concessions after the First World War, and Soviet Russia renounced its concessions in 1924. Britain returned its concessions in Hankou and Jiujiang in 1927 during the Northern Expedition. In 1943, during the Second World War, the United States and Britain formally relinquished extraterritoriality and their remaining concessions by treaty, while Japan "returned" concessions in occupied areas to the collaborationist Wang Jingwei regime for propaganda purposes. The French concessions were returned in 1946, and the last foreign concession, Italy's in Tianjin, was ceded to China under the 1947 peace treaty. The People's Republic of China, founded in 1949, recognised no foreign concessions.

Legacy

The concessions left a lasting architectural and urban legacy — the Bund in Shanghai, the former concession districts of Tianjin with their European-style villas and churches, and Shameen Island in Guangzhou remain prominent. In Chinese historical memory, the concessions symbolise the "century of humiliation", while historians also note their role in introducing modern municipal services, publishing and industry to China.
