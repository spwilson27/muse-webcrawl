Animal echolocation is the use of reflected sound by animals to locate objects and navigate — the biological form of sonar. It evolved independently in several lineages, most elaborately in microchiropteran bats and in toothed whales (dolphins, porpoises, sperm whales and their relatives), and in simpler forms in oilbirds, some cave swiftlets, and possibly shrews and tenrecs.

Bats

More than 1,000 species of bats echolocate, generating ultrasound in the larynx and emitting it through the mouth or the nose — nose-emitting species often have elaborate noseleaves that shape the beam. Calls range from about 9 kHz in some large species to over 200 kHz in small ones. During a hunt a bat's call pattern changes characteristically: a search phase of widely spaced calls, an approach phase of quickening pulses as it homes in, and a terminal feeding buzz of extremely rapid clicks just before capture. Many moths and other prey have evolved ears tuned to bat ultrasound and evasive behaviour, and some tiger moths even jam bat sonar with clicks of their own — an ongoing evolutionary arms race. Fruit bats (megabats) mostly lack laryngeal echolocation, relying on sight and smell; the Egyptian fruit bat instead clicks its tongue, and recent work shows some other megabats produce crude wing-clap echolocation.

Toothed whales

Odontocetes generate clicks in the nasal passages (phonic lips) and focus them through the fatty melon on the forehead; echoes are received mainly through the lower jaw and transmitted to the ear. Their clicks are among the most intense sounds made by any animal, and the system has extraordinary resolution — dolphins can distinguish objects differing by millimetres in wall thickness and can detect buried prey. Sperm whales use slow, powerful clicks for long-range sensing in the deep ocean, while river dolphins, living in murky water where eyes are nearly useless, rely on sonar almost entirely.

Birds and other animals

The oilbird of South America and several cave swiftlets of Asia nest in pitch-dark caves and navigate with audible clicks (roughly 1–15 kHz), a coarse system adequate for avoiding cave walls but not for catching prey. Shrews and tenrecs produce ultrasonic sounds that may serve simple orientation, though the evidence is debated.

History of research

In the 1790s Lazzaro Spallanzani showed that bats navigate by hearing rather than sight, but the mechanism remained unknown until 1938, when Donald Griffin and Robert Galambos demonstrated with ultrasonic microphones that bats produce and hear sounds above human hearing; Griffin coined the term "echolocation" in 1944. Parallel research on dolphins in the 1950s–60s (notably by Kenneth Norris and Arthur McBride's successors) established cetacean biosonar.
