Krakatoa (Indonesian: Krakatau) is a volcanic island group in the Sunda Strait between Java and Sumatra in Indonesia. It is famous for one of the most destructive volcanic eruptions in recorded history: on 26–27 August 1883 the volcano exploded in a series of eruptions (VEI 6) that destroyed most of the island, killed more than 36,000 people — most by the resulting tsunamis — and produced atmospheric effects observed worldwide for years. A new cone, Anak Krakatau ("Child of Krakatoa"), grew from the sea at the site in 1927 and remains active; its partial collapse in December 2018 triggered a further deadly tsunami.

Geography and earlier eruptions

Krakatoa sits on the convergent boundary where the Indo-Australian Plate subducts beneath the Sunda Plate. Before 1883 the archipelago comprised the main island of Krakatoa — with the cones Perboewatan, Danan and Rakata — plus Verlaten and Lang islands, the rim of an older caldera possibly formed in an eruption around AD 535 (a hypothesis linked by some authors to global cooling events recorded in 536). Verbeek's survey recorded an eruption in 1680; the islands were visited by Captain Cook's expedition in 1780.

The 1883 eruption

Precursory eruptions began on 20 May 1883 from Perboewatan. Activity intensified through the summer, and on the afternoon of 26 August the eruption entered its climactic phase. On the morning of 27 August four enormous explosions occurred; the third, at 10:02 local time, is estimated to have been one of the loudest sounds in recorded history — heard nearly 4,800 km away on Rodrigues Island near Mauritius, and across much of Australia. The explosions and caldera collapse generated tsunamis up to about 35 m high that devastated coastal towns around the strait, including Teluk Betung and Anjer; the official death toll was 36,417. Roughly 20 km³ of material was ejected, and about two-thirds of Krakatoa island vanished, leaving the surviving half of the Rakata cone.

Global effects

The eruption injected vast quantities of ash and sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere. A veil of aerosols circled the globe, lowering average global temperatures by about 1.2 °C for several years and producing spectacular red sunsets, blue and green suns, and "Bishop's rings" — phenomena recorded by observers and artists worldwide and studied in the Royal Society's 1888 report edited by G. J. Symons. Barographs recorded the pressure wave circling the earth multiple times. The disaster was among the first global news events reported in near real time by submarine telegraph cable.

Anak Krakatau

In 1927 submarine eruptions at the centre of the 1883 caldera built a new island, Anak Krakatau, which has grown steadily since, typically by several metres a year, with frequent Strombolian activity. On 22 December 2018 a flank collapse during an eruption reduced the cone's height from about 338 m to about 110 m and triggered a tsunami that killed more than 430 people on the Java and Sumatra coasts. The volcano has since rebuilt a new cone and crater lake and continues to be monitored by Indonesian authorities; the Krakatoa islands are part of the Ujung Kulon National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
