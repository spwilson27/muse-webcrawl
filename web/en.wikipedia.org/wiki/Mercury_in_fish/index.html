Mercury in fish

The presence of mercury in fish is a health concern for people who eat them, especially for women who are or may become pregnant, nursing mothers, and young children. Fish and shellfish concentrate mercury in their bodies, often as methylmercury, a highly toxic organic compound. Mercury enters waterways from natural sources and from industrial pollution, especially coal combustion; bacteria in sediments convert inorganic mercury to methylmercury, which then bioaccumulates and biomagnifies up the food chain.

Accumulation

Because methylmercury is eliminated slowly, long-lived predatory fish at the top of the food chain—such as shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish, marlin, and some tuna—build up the highest concentrations, sometimes exceeding 1 part per million. Smaller, shorter-lived fish such as sardines, anchovies, and salmon generally contain far less mercury.

Health effects

Methylmercury is a neurotoxin; prenatal exposure can impair cognitive development. The historical mass poisoning at Minamata, Japan, demonstrated the severe effects of industrial mercury discharge into seafood. Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. FDA and EPA issue consumption advisories recommending limits on high-mercury fish, particularly for pregnant women and children, while noting the nutritional benefits of low-mercury fish.

Regulation

Many countries set maximum mercury levels for commercial fish, and international agreements such as the Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013) aim to reduce anthropogenic mercury emissions and releases.
