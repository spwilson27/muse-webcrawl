Tachyphylaxis

FieldPharmacology
DefinitionRapid decrease in response to a drug after repeated administration
Classic examplesEphedrine; tyramine; topical nasal decongestants; nitrates
Distinguished fromDrug tolerance (slower onset)

Tachyphylaxis (from Greek tachys, "rapid", and phylaxis, "protection") is a medical phenomenon in pharmacology in which a patient shows a rapidly decreasing response to a drug following its initial administration, so that successive doses — sometimes within minutes to days — produce progressively smaller effects. It is distinguished from ordinary drug tolerance, which develops more slowly over days to weeks of exposure.

Mechanisms

Several mechanisms produce tachyphylaxis. Indirectly acting sympathomimetics such as ephedrine and tyramine work by releasing stored noradrenaline from nerve terminals; once those stores are depleted, further doses have little left to release. Other cases involve rapid receptor desensitization and internalization — the target receptor is uncoupled from its signalling pathway or removed from the cell surface — as with some responses to nicotine, serotonin agonists and gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogues. Depletion of a downstream mediator, such as reduced availability of nitric-oxide pathway substrates in nitrate tolerance, is also proposed.

Clinical examples

Familiar cases include the diminishing effect of repeated ephedrine boluses during anaesthesia; rhinitis medicamentosa, the rebound congestion caused by overuse of topical decongestant sprays such as oxymetazoline; attenuation of response to repeatedly dosed LSD, where tachyphylaxis sets in within a day; and reduced response to continuous nitroglycerin infusions, managed clinically by a daily nitrate-free interval. Because the response usually recovers after the drug is withheld for a period, drug holidays and intermittent dosing schedules are standard countermeasures.

Related concepts

Tachyphylaxis is one form of the broader phenomenon of desensitization; its slower cousin is tolerance, and its physiological mirror image is sensitization, in which repeated exposure increases a drug's effect.
