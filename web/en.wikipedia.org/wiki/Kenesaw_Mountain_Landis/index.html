Kenesaw Mountain Landis

Placeholder image: Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis.

Kenesaw Mountain Landis (November 20, 1866 – November 25, 1944) was an American jurist who served as a United States federal judge from 1905 to 1922 and as the first Commissioner of Baseball from 1920 until his death in 1944. He was named for Kennesaw Mountain, where his father was wounded in the American Civil War.

Federal judge

Appointed to the Northern District of Illinois by President Theodore Roosevelt, Landis gained national fame in 1907 by fining Standard Oil of Indiana more than $29 million for accepting freight rebates (later overturned on appeal). During World War I he presided over trials of draft resisters and Industrial Workers of the World leaders.

Commissioner of Baseball

Following the Black Sox Scandal, team owners granted Landis near-absolute authority to restore public confidence in the game. He banned the eight accused Chicago White Sox players for life despite their acquittal in court, and ruled the sport with an authoritarian hand for nearly a quarter-century, cracking down on gambling and barnstorming. His tenure is also criticised for his role in maintaining baseball's colour line, as no Black player appeared in the major leagues while he was commissioner.

Legacy

Landis was elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame shortly after his death. The Most Valuable Player Award bore his name until 2020.
