The Qumran Caves are a series of natural and artificial caves in the Judean Desert near the archaeological site of Khirbet Qumran, on the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea. They are famous as the hiding place of the Dead Sea Scrolls, a collection of nearly a thousand ancient manuscripts discovered between 1946/47 and 1956 in eleven principal caves, with further caves surveyed in later decades.

Discovery

According to the well-known account, Bedouin shepherds of the Ta'amireh tribe stumbled upon the first cave (Cave 1) in 1946 or 1947, finding clay jars containing leather and papyrus scrolls. Systematic exploration by archaeologists, notably Roland de Vaux of the École Biblique, followed, and between 1951 and 1956 ten more scroll-bearing caves were identified near the settlement ruins. Cave 4, an artificial cave cut into the marl terrace, held the bulk of the collection — roughly three quarters of all the manuscripts, in tens of thousands of fragments.

The caves

Caves 1, 2, 3 and 11 are natural caves in the limestone cliffs, while Caves 4 through 10 were hewn into the soft marl near the settlement. Cave 3 contained the Copper Scroll, an inventory of hidden treasures engraved on copper sheets. Cave 11, discovered in 1956, yielded well-preserved manuscripts including the Temple Scroll, the longest of all the scrolls. Surveys in 2017 announced evidence of a twelfth cave, from which looters had apparently removed scrolls, leaving storage jars, parchment and other artefacts.

Contents

The scrolls date from roughly the third century BCE to the first century CE and include the oldest known manuscripts of the Hebrew Bible, sectarian works such as the Community Rule and the War Scroll, commentaries (pesharim), prayers, and legal texts. Most scholars associate the collection with the nearby settlement and a Jewish sect, often identified with the Essenes.
