Perkins School for the Blind

The Perkins School for the Blind is a school for students who are blind and visually impaired, located in Watertown, Massachusetts, United States. Founded in 1829 in Boston, it was the first school for the blind established in the United States, and it is renowned in the history of special education, particularly for the education of Helen Keller and for the work of its teacher Anne Sullivan.

History

Perkins was incorporated in 1829 and opened in 1832 under its first director, Samuel Gridley Howe. It was named for Thomas Handasyd Perkins, a Boston merchant and benefactor who donated his mansion for the school's use and left it a bequest. Howe modelled the school on European institutions he had visited and made it a comprehensive centre for education, teacher training, and the production of books in raised type.

The school achieved international fame in the 1830s and 1840s through Laura Bridgman, a deafblind girl who learned language at Perkins — the first deafblind person known to do so — an achievement that inspired the later education of Helen Keller. Keller studied at Perkins from 1888, and Anne Sullivan, herself partially sighted and a Perkins graduate, became Keller's teacher and lifelong companion. In 1912 the school moved from South Boston to its present campus in Watertown.

Present day

Perkins today serves students from infancy through age 22 on its Watertown campus, including a deafblind programme, and reaches many more through international programmes, teacher training, and assistive technology. The Perkins Brailler, a durable mechanical braille typewriter developed at the school in the mid-twentieth century, became a worldwide standard. Its parent organisation, Perkins School for the Blind (now often styled Perkins), also supports a library service supplying braille and talking books across New England.
