Moussaka

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A baked moussaka with golden béchamel top, cut to show its layers]

Moussaka is a layered casserole of the Balkans and eastern Mediterranean, best known in its Greek form: slices of fried or baked eggplant layered with spiced minced meat in tomato, crowned with a thick béchamel that bakes to a golden crust. The Greek version was codified in the early 20th century by the chef Nikolaos Tselementes, whose French-influenced cookbooks deliberately "refined" Greek home cooking — the béchamel lid is his era's signature. Older and plainer moussakas live all around: Turkish musakka is an unlayered eggplant-and-mince stew with no béchamel at all, and Balkan versions often build on potatoes.

Name and older forms

The name comes from Arabic musaqqaʿa — "chilled" or "moistened" — carried through Ottoman kitchens across the empire's table. Eggplant dishes in sauce predate the casserole form by centuries; what Tselementes added was architecture and French sauce-craft, turning a homely stew into the dish Greek tavernas serve to the world. Cinnamon or allspice in the meat, a pinch of sugar in the tomato, egg yolks enriching the béchamel — the details define family versions argued over ever since.

Making and serving

Modern cooks salt or bake the eggplant to cut its oil appetite, brown the meat sauce until nearly dry, and let the finished moussaka rest — a fresh-from-the-oven moussaka slumps; rested, it cuts into proud squares. Vegetarian versions with lentils or mushrooms are now standard, and the dish's structure (vegetable, ragù, white sauce) invites parallel dishes from shepherd's pie to eggplant parmigiana, its Italian-American cousin in spirit.
