Chinese alchemy

Chinese alchemy is an ancient Chinese scientific and philosophical approach to alchemy, centred on the search for an elixir of immortality. Rooted in Taoism, it developed two main branches: waidan (外丹, "external alchemy"), the compounding of elixirs from minerals and metals, especially cinnabar and gold; and neidan (內丹, "internal alchemy"), the cultivation of an elixir within the practitioner's own body through meditation, breath control and inner visualisation. Practised since at least the 4th century BCE, Chinese alchemy contributed to chemistry, medicine and the invention of gunpowder.

External alchemy (waidan)

Waidan practitioners sought to create a pill or potion of immortality by heating substances such as cinnabar (mercuric sulphide), realgar, sulphur and gold in sealed reaction vessels, cycling them through repeated sublimations. The Cantong qi, attributed to Wei Boyang, is the earliest known alchemical treatise, and the 4th-century master Ge Hong's Baopuzi preserves extensive recipes. Because many elixirs contained mercury, lead and arsenic, elixir poisoning killed numerous devotees — including, by tradition, several Tang dynasty emperors — which helped drive the shift toward internal methods.

Internal alchemy (neidan)

From about the Tang and Song dynasties, neidan became dominant. It reinterpreted the alchemist's laboratory symbolically: the body is the furnace, and the "ingredients" are the Three Treasures — essence (jing), breath/vital energy (qi) and spirit (shen) — refined through stages to form an inner elixir or "golden pill" associated with longevity and spiritual transcendence. Neidan lineages, including the Quanzhen school, remain integral to religious Taoism.

Legacy

Alchemical experimentation produced lasting knowledge of minerals, salts and reactions; the quest for elixirs led Tang-era alchemists to mix sulphur, saltpetre and charcoal, yielding early gunpowder. Chinese alchemy also influenced medicine and pharmacology, notably the great materia medica tradition culminating in Li Shizhen's Bencao Gangmu.
