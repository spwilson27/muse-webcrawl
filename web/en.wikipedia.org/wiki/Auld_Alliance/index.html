Auld Alliance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Auld Alliance (Scots: Auld Allyance; French: Vieille Alliance; Scottish Gaelic: An Seann-chaidreachas) was an alliance between the kingdoms of Scotland and France against England, first formalised by the Treaty of Paris of 1295. Built on shared hostility toward England, it shaped Scottish and French foreign policy for more than 250 years and was renewed by nearly every successive monarch on both sides.

The alliance was formalised in 1295.

History

The alliance began when John Balliol and Philip IV of France agreed mutual support in 1295, as Edward I of England pressed his claim of overlordship over Scotland. Its terms obliged each kingdom to attack England if the other was invaded, forcing England into recurring two-front wars. Scottish armies fought in France during the Hundred Years' War — notably at Baugé (1421) and in the campaigns that relieved Orléans alongside Joan of Arc — and the Garde Écossaise served as bodyguards to French kings. In return France supported Scotland during the Wars of Independence and the Rough Wooing.

Royal marriages and decline

The alliance was cemented by marriages, culminating in Mary, Queen of Scots' marriage to the Dauphin Francis in 1558. Its effective end came with the Treaty of Edinburgh (1560), by which French troops withdrew from Scotland; the Reformation Parliament aligned Scotland with Protestant England, though Scots retained privileges in France — including, reputedly, dual nationality — that some argue were never formally revoked until modern times. Charles de Gaulle in 1942 called it "the oldest alliance in the world".

Legacy

The alliance left traces in Scots law, architecture, cuisine and vocabulary (such as ashet from assiette), in Bordeaux's Scottish wine trade, and in the enduring cultural affinity invoked by both countries, including in modern Franco-Scottish commemorations.
