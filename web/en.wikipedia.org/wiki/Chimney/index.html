Chimney

A chimney is an architectural ventilation structure made of masonry, clay or metal that isolates hot toxic exhaust gases or smoke produced by a boiler, stove, furnace, incinerator or fireplace from human living areas. Chimneys are typically vertical, or as near as possible to vertical, to ensure that the gases flow smoothly, drawing air into the combustion — the stack effect — and expelling the gases to the outside.

How it works

A chimney works because hot flue gases are less dense than the surrounding cooler air, so they rise through the flue, creating a partial vacuum that draws more air into the fire. The taller the chimney and the hotter the gases, the stronger this draught. The interior passage is called the flue; most masonry chimneys contain a flue liner of clay tile, metal or cast concrete to protect the structure and improve performance.

History

Early dwellings vented smoke through holes in the roof. True chimneys became common in northern Europe in the 12th century and transformed domestic architecture, allowing multi-room, multi-storey houses with fireplaces on several floors. Industrial chimneys grew enormously during the Industrial Revolution; the tallest chimney in the world is that of the GRES-2 power station in Ekibastuz, Kazakhstan, at 419.7 metres. Chimneys are traditionally cleaned by a chimney sweep to remove creosote deposits that can cause chimney fires.
