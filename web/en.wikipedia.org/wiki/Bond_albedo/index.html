Bond albedo

The Bond albedo (also called spheric albedo, planetary albedo or bolometric albedo), named after the American astronomer George Phillips Bond (1825–1865), is the fraction of power in the total electromagnetic radiation incident on an astronomical body that is scattered back out into space. Because it accounts for all wavelengths and all phase angles, it is a fundamental quantity for characterising a planet's energy balance and temperature.

For Earth, the Bond albedo is approximately 0.30 — that is, about 30 percent of incoming solar radiation is reflected back to space by clouds, the atmosphere and the surface. Saturn's moon Enceladus has one of the highest known Bond albedos, around 0.8 or more, owing to its fresh ice surface, while most asteroids have low albedos of around 0.1 or less.

Relation to other albedos

The Bond albedo differs from the geometric albedo, which measures brightness at zero phase angle relative to a perfectly diffusing disk. The two are related through the phase integral; the Bond albedo is the quantity that enters planetary equilibrium-temperature calculations, since only the absorbed fraction (1 − A) of incident sunlight heats the body.
