Great Divergence

The Great Divergence is the socioeconomic shift in which the Western world (especially Western Europe and its settler offshoots) overcame pre-modern growth constraints and emerged during the 19th century as the most powerful and prosperous region of the world, eclipsing the previously dominant or comparable civilizations of Asia, including Qing China, Mughal India and the Ottoman Empire. The term was popularized by the historian Kenneth Pomeranz in his 2000 book The Great Divergence.

The debate

Scholars dispute both the timing of the divergence and its causes. Earlier generations of historians attributed Europe's rise to deep institutional and cultural advantages: secure property rights, scientific rationalism, representative government or a distinctive work ethic. Pomeranz and the "California School" argued instead that the most developed regions of China, such as the Yangtze Delta, were comparable to the most developed parts of Europe in markets, life expectancy and consumption as late as 1750, and that the decisive break came from contingent advantages: Britain's abundant, conveniently located coal deposits, and the land and resources of the New World colonies — "ghost acres" of sugar, cotton and timber that relieved Europe's ecological constraints.

Alternative explanations

Other explanations emphasize state competition and warfare in a fragmented Europe, colonial extraction and the Atlantic slave economy, high wages that incentivized labour-saving invention (Robert Allen's account of the Industrial Revolution), or human capital and the culture of useful knowledge. Estimates of historical GDP per capita by Angus Maddison and later researchers suggest some divergence in incomes began earlier, in the early modern period, even if it became dramatic only after 1800. The question remains central to economic history and to debates about global inequality.
