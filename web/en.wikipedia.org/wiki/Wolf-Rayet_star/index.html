Wolf-Rayet star

Wolf-Rayet stars, often abbreviated as WR stars, are a rare heterogeneous set of stars with unusual spectra showing prominent broad emission lines of ionised helium and highly ionised nitrogen or carbon. They are among the most luminous, massive and hottest stars known.

History

The class is named after French astronomers Charles Wolf and Georges Rayet, who discovered the first examples in 1867 in the constellation Cygnus using visual spectroscopy. Their spectra lacked the hydrogen lines typical of other hot stars and instead showed broad emission bands.

Characteristics

Wolf-Rayet stars have surface temperatures typically between 30,000 K and over 200,000 K. They lose mass at very high rates through powerful stellar winds, with velocities of 1,000–3,000 km/s. Many are surrounded by nebulae formed from ejected material.

Classification

WR stars are divided principally into WN stars, showing nitrogen and helium lines, and WC stars, showing carbon and oxygen lines. A rarer WO sequence shows strong oxygen features. Modern classification uses ionisation subclasses such as WN2 to WN11 and WC4 to WC9.

Evolution

Most Wolf-Rayet stars are understood as evolved massive stars that have shed their outer hydrogen envelopes, either through their own winds or through binary interaction. They represent a late stage before core collapse, and many are expected to end as Type Ib or Type Ic supernovae, potentially leaving black holes.

Examples

Notable examples include Gamma Velorum (the brightest Wolf-Rayet star in the sky), WR 104, and the Quintuplet cluster stars. Around 700 Wolf-Rayet stars are catalogued in the Milky Way, with many more known in the Magellanic Clouds and other nearby galaxies.
