History of the Jews in Iraq

The history of the Jews in Iraq is documented over more than 2,600 years, beginning with the Babylonian captivity of the sixth century BCE. The Babylonian Jewish community produced the Babylonian Talmud and remained a major centre of Jewish learning for over a millennium. In the mid-twentieth century Jews formed a significant part of Baghdad's population, but nearly the entire community emigrated in 1950–51, principally to Israel.

Ancient period

Jews were first brought to Babylonia after Nebuchadnezzar II's conquests of Judah (597 and 586 BCE). Many remained after Cyrus the Great permitted return to Judea in 538 BCE. Under Parthian and Sasanian rule the community flourished; the Exilarch led the diaspora community, and the academies of Sura and Pumbedita compiled the Babylonian Talmud (completed c. 500 CE).

Islamic era

Under the Abbasid caliphate, Baghdad's Jews prospered as merchants, physicians and officials, and the Geonim of Babylonia were recognised authorities for Jews worldwide. The community endured periods of restriction and, in the thirteenth century, the Mongol invasions. Under the Ottoman Empire (1534–1917) the community's fortunes varied, with Baghdad, Basra and Mosul hosting important congregations; Iraqi Jews established trading networks across India and the Far East.

Modern Iraq and the Farhud

At the founding of the Kingdom of Iraq, Jews — perhaps a third of Baghdad's population — played prominent roles in commerce, administration and culture. The rise of Arab nationalism and Nazi propaganda worsened their position. On 1–2 June 1941, the Farhud, a pogrom in Baghdad, killed at least 175 Jews and injured many more. After the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, Zionism was made a capital offence and Jews were subjected to arrests and economic restrictions.

Exodus

In 1950 Iraq passed a law permitting Jews to emigrate if they renounced their citizenship. In Operation Ezra and Nehemiah (1950–51), some 120,000–130,000 Iraqi Jews were airlifted to Israel, leaving their property frozen. Only a small remnant stayed; further persecution after 1967 and the public hanging of nine Jews in Baghdad in 1969 reduced the community to a handful. Today only a few Jews remain in Iraq, while Iraqi Jewish culture survives in Israel and the wider diaspora.
