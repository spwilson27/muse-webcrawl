Star system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A star system or stellar system is a small number of stars that orbit each other, bound by gravitational attraction. A system of two stars is a binary star; systems of three or more are called multiple star systems. (A much larger group of stars bound by gravitation is a star cluster or galaxy.)

Hierarchical structure

Most multiple systems are organised hierarchically: a close pair may be orbited at a great distance by a third star, which may itself have a companion, forming nested binary orbits. This hierarchy keeps systems dynamically stable over long times. Examples include Alpha Centauri, a triple system in which the close binary Alpha Centauri A and B is orbited by the red dwarf Proxima Centauri; Castor, a sextuple system in the constellation Gemini; and Algol, a triple system known since antiquity for its eclipses. The seven-star system of Nu Scorpii and the quadruple Mizar–Alcor illustrate how apparent single stars resolve into many components.

Terminology and frequency

Systems are described as triple (ternary), quadruple, quintuple, sextuple, septuple and so on according to the number of stars. Multiplicity is common: a large fraction of Sun-like stars have at least one companion, though the fraction of triples and higher-order systems is smaller. Components are designated with capital letters (e.g. Alpha Centauri A, B, C) in order of discovery.

Observation

Components may be resolved visually with telescopes, detected spectroscopically through Doppler shifts in their spectra, or inferred from eclipses and astrometric wobbles, so some "stars" known since antiquity were only much later revealed as multiple systems.
