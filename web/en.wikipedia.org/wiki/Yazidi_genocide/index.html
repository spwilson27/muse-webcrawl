Yazidi genocide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Yazidi genocide
LocationSinjar region, Nineveh Governorate, Iraq
DateAugust 2014 onwards
TargetYazidi people
PerpetratorsIslamic State of Iraq and the Levant (ISIL)
OutcomeThousands killed; thousands of women and children enslaved; mass displacement; recognised as genocide by the UN and numerous states

The Yazidi genocide was the mass murder, enslavement, and expulsion of the Yazidi people of northern Iraq by the Islamic State (ISIL) beginning in August 2014. When ISIL captured the Sinjar region on 3 August 2014 after the withdrawal of Kurdish Peshmerga forces, tens of thousands of Yazidis fled to Mount Sinjar, where they were besieged without food or water until a corridor was opened by Syrian Kurdish forces. Those who could not flee were killed or captured: men and older boys who refused conversion were massacred, and thousands of women and girls were enslaved and subjected to systematic sexual violence.

Scale

Estimates based on community records and demographic research suggest that around 5,000 Yazidis were killed and some 6,000–7,000, mostly women and children, were abducted. Hundreds of thousands were displaced, the majority to camps in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, where many remain. Mass graves continue to be exhumed around Sinjar, and roughly 2,700 captives were still unaccounted for a decade later. Boys were forcibly converted and trained as child soldiers; captured women were sold in slave markets in Mosul and Raqqa, a practice ISIL openly justified in its propaganda as religiously sanctioned.

Recognition and accountability

In June 2016 a UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that ISIL was committing genocide against the Yazidis. The genocide has since been formally recognised by, among others, the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Canada, Australia, and the Iraqi parliament, which in 2021 passed the Yazidi Female Survivors Law providing reparations. The UN investigative team UNITAD documented evidence for prosecutions, and in November 2021 a German court convicted former ISIL member Taha al-Jumailly of genocide — the first such conviction worldwide for the crimes against the Yazidis.

Survivors' advocacy

Nadia Murad, a survivor of enslavement who escaped in late 2014, became a leading international advocate and was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2018 jointly with Denis Mukwege. The Sinjar region remains contested and largely unreconstructed, impeding the return of displaced families.
