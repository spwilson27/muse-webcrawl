The Second Anglo-Dutch War (1665–1667) was the second of four naval wars fought between England and the Dutch Republic in the 17th and 18th centuries, principally over trade and maritime supremacy. It ended with a Dutch victory sealed by the Treaty of Breda.

Background

Commercial rivalry intensified after the First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654). English seizures of Dutch possessions in North America (New Netherland, 1664) and West Africa, Dutch recapture of forts on the Guinea coast, and the ambitions of the restored Charles II and his brother James, Duke of York, pushed both countries toward war, declared in March 1665.

Course of the war

The opening Battle of Lowestoft (June 1665) was an English victory in which the Dutch commander Van Wassenaer Obdam was killed, but England failed to exploit it amid plague, the Great Fire of London, and financial exhaustion. In 1666 the Dutch under Michiel de Ruyter fought the Four Days' Battle (1–4 June 1666), generally counted a Dutch victory, though the English won at St. James's Day Battle (25 July). France and Denmark entered against England. With the English fleet laid up for lack of money in 1667, de Ruyter sailed up the Thames and Medway in June 1667 — the Raid on the Medway — burning and capturing much of the fleet at Chatham, including the flagship Royal Charles. It remains one of the worst defeats in Royal Navy history and hastened peace.

Treaty of Breda

The Treaty of Breda (31 July 1667) broadly restored the status quo: England kept New Netherland (renamed New York), while the Dutch retained Suriname and gained a relaxation of the Navigation Acts; the Dutch also kept the spice island of Run in the East Indies.
