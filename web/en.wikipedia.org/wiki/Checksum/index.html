Checksum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Diagram showing data and its checksum being transmitted and verified]

A checksum is a small block of data derived from another block of digital data for the purpose of detecting errors that may have been introduced during its transmission or storage. By recomputing the checksum on receipt or retrieval and comparing it with the stored value, a system can detect accidental changes to raw data with high probability.

Algorithms

Checksum algorithms range from simple parity bits and modular sums to position-dependent schemes such as Fletcher's checksum and Adler-32, and polynomial-based cyclic redundancy checks (CRCs), which offer stronger detection of burst errors. Cryptographic hash functions such as MD5 and SHA-256 are also used as checksums, since they additionally resist deliberate tampering, unlike simple checksums which only guard against accidental corruption.

Applications

Checksums are embedded throughout computing: the IPv4, TCP, and UDP headers each carry a checksum field; files downloaded from the internet are frequently published with an accompanying hash so users can verify integrity; filesystems such as ZFS and Btrfs checksum data blocks to detect silent corruption ("bit rot"); and credit card numbers and ISBNs use check digits — a simple form of checksum — to catch transcription errors.

Limitations

A simple checksum cannot detect all error patterns: swapping two bytes leaves a plain sum unchanged, for example. Nor does an ordinary checksum protect against malicious modification, since an attacker can simply recompute it — integrity against adversaries requires cryptographic authentication.
