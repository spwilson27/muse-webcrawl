Samaññaphala Sutta

The Samaññaphala Sutta is the second of the 34 suttas of the Dīgha Nikāya (the "long discourses" of the Buddha in the Pāli Canon). Its name means "The Fruits of the Contemplative Life": it presents a narrative in which the Buddha describes, progressively more advanced, the "fruits" — the visible benefits in this very life — of the life of a Buddhist monk. The sutta is a standard source for the gradual training (anupubbasikkhā) of the Buddhist path and for the doctrines of the Buddha's heterodox contemporaries.

King Ajātasattu, troubled by his patricide, visits the Buddha in Jīvaka's mango grove and asks about the fruits of the contemplative life.

Summary

The discourse begins at Rājagaha, where King Ajātasattu of Magadha — who had murdered his father, King Bimbisāra — is tormented by remorse. His minister Jīvaka leads him at night to the Buddha. The king asks whether the Buddha can point out a fruit of the contemplative life visible in this present life, as the crafts and professions yield visible fruits for servants, soldiers, farmers, and artisans.

The Buddha first recounts how the same question was put to six rival teachers — Pūraṇa Kassapa, Makkhali Gosāla, Ajita Kesakambalī, Pakudha Kaccāyana, Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta, and Sañjaya Belaṭṭhaputta — whose answers, ranging from amoralism and fatalism to materialism and agnosticism, are an important early record of non-Buddhist Indian philosophies. The Buddha then gives his own graded answer: the joy of a servant freed into the holy life; morality; contentment; sense restraint; mindfulness and clear comprehension; abandoning of the five hindrances; the four jhānas; insight knowledge; the supernormal powers; and finally the knowledge of the ending of the defilements — liberation. Each stage is illustrated with similes, such as the lotus pond and the mountain stream.

Moved, Ajātasattu takes refuge in the Triple Gem and confesses his crime; the Buddha remarks that had he not killed his father, he would that night have attained the Dhamma-eye.

Significance

The Samaññaphala Sutta provides one of the fullest canonical statements of the structure of Buddhist practice, from ethics through meditation to liberating insight, and its framework recurs throughout the Nikāyas. Parallel versions survive in Chinese translation in the Dīrgha Āgama and in Sanskrit fragments.
