Gastric acid, or stomach acid, is a digestive fluid formed in the stomach, composed principally of hydrochloric acid (HCl), with potassium chloride and sodium chloride. It activates the enzyme pepsin from its precursor pepsinogen, provides the acidic environment in which pepsin digests proteins, and acts as a barrier killing most ingested microorganisms.

Secretion

Acid is secreted by parietal cells in the gastric glands of the stomach lining via the H+/K+-ATPase "proton pump." Secretion proceeds through cephalic, gastric, and intestinal phases, stimulated by the hormone gastrin, by histamine acting at H2 receptors, and by acetylcholine. The stomach protects itself with a mucus–bicarbonate barrier.

Regulation and disorders

Somatostatin and prostaglandins inhibit acid secretion. Excess or misplaced acid contributes to gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcers, and Zollinger–Ellison syndrome. Acid-suppressing drugs — H2 receptor antagonists and proton pump inhibitors such as omeprazole — are among the most widely used medicines in the world. Conversely, too little acid (hypochlorhydria) can impair digestion and vitamin B12 absorption.
