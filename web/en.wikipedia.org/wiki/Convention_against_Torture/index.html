Convention against Torture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Nations Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CAT), adopted in 1984 and in force from 1987, is a treaty under international human rights law aimed at preventing torture and other acts of cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment around the world. Nearly all states are parties (around 170 or more).

Obligations

The Convention defines torture (severe pain or suffering, intentionally inflicted for such purposes as obtaining information or punishment, by or with the acquiescence of a public official), forbids it absolutely — "no exceptional circumstances whatsoever" (Article 2(2), the clause tested by post-2001 "ticking bomb" apologias), prohibits refoulement to torture (Article 3), requires criminalisation and universal jurisdiction over torturers found in a party's territory (the provision under which Pinochet was arrested in London in 1998), and obliges redress for victims. The Committee against Torture reviews states and hears complaints; the 2002 Optional Protocol (OPCAT) created preventive visiting to places of detention by an international subcommittee and national preventive mechanisms. Persistent practice despite near-universal ratification — documented by the Committee, special rapporteurs and NGOs — makes CAT a standing case study in the distance between norm adoption and norm compliance, and in the monitoring technologies built to narrow it.
