The bağlama (Turkish: [baːɫaˈma]) is a family of plucked long-necked lutes used in Turkish folk music, and more broadly the principal instrument of the ashik bard tradition across Anatolia, the Caucasus and the Balkans. It is often referred to generically as the saz (from Persian sāz, "instrument"), a word that covers the whole family of long-necked lutes, and it is regarded as a national symbol of Turkey.

Description

The bağlama has a pear-shaped bowl body — traditionally carved from a single block of mulberry wood or built from ribs of maple, mahogany or juniper — a long fretted neck, and seven strings arranged in three courses of two, two and three. The lowest course includes an octave string. Its frets are movable ties of gut or nylon, allowing the many microtonal (perde) positions required by Turkish makam melodies. Tuning systems vary by region and style; the best known are the bağlama düzeni and bozuk düzen. The instrument is played with a long thin plectrum (mızrap or tezene), traditionally of cherry bark or plastic, or with the fingers in the şelpe style.

History

The bağlama descends from the kopuz, the lute of the Central Asian Turkic ozan bards, which travelled west with the Oghuz Turks into Anatolia. Over the centuries it evolved from the smaller kopuz into the larger Anatolian instruments. The instrument is central to Alevi religious and cultural life, where it is treated as sacred — the "stringed Qur'an" of some communities — and accompanies the cem ceremony and the songs of poet-saints such as Pir Sultan Abdal. In the twentieth century, masters such as Âşık Veysel, Muharrem Ertaş, Neşet Ertaş and Arif Sağ brought the bağlama to national and international prominence; an electrified version, the elektro bağlama, became a hallmark of Anatolian rock in the 1970s.

Family of instruments

Sizes range from the small cura through the standard short-neck and long-neck bağlama to the large divan bağlama used to accompany slow, weighty songs. Related instruments across the region — the Greek bouzouki, the Balkan tambura, the Arab buzuq and the Central Asian dombra — preserve the shared heritage of the long-necked lute.
