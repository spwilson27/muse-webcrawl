Fair Lending Act

Fair lending in the United States refers to the body of federal law that prohibits discrimination in credit transactions. The principal statutes are the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA, 1974) and the Fair Housing Act (1968), together commonly described under the umbrella of fair lending law. Enforcement is shared among the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the Department of Justice, the Federal Reserve and other banking regulators.

Protected classes

ECOA prohibits discrimination on the basis of race, colour, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, receipt of public-assistance income, or exercise of consumer-protection rights. The Fair Housing Act additionally covers familial status and disability in mortgage lending and housing-related credit.

Forms of discrimination

Fair lending doctrine recognises overt discrimination, disparate treatment (treating similarly situated applicants differently), and disparate impact — facially neutral policies that disproportionately harm a protected class without sufficient business justification. Historical practices targeted by the laws include redlining, in which lenders denied credit to neighbourhoods based on their racial composition.

Related laws

The Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (1975) requires data reporting that makes fair-lending analysis possible, and the Community Reinvestment Act (1977) encourages banks to serve all segments of their communities.
