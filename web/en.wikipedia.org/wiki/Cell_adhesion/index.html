Cell adhesion

Cell adhesion is the process by which cells interact and attach to neighbouring cells or to the extracellular matrix through specialised cell surface molecules called cell adhesion molecules (CAMs). It is essential for the assembly of cells into tissues, for communication and regulation, and for the maintenance of tissue architecture; its disruption contributes to diseases including cancer.

Adhesion molecules

Four major families of CAMs mediate adhesion. Cadherins mediate calcium-dependent homophilic cell–cell adhesion and anchor adherens junctions and desmosomes to the cytoskeleton. Integrins are the principal receptors for extracellular matrix proteins such as collagen and fibronectin and also mediate some cell–cell contacts, linking the matrix to actin at focal adhesions. Selectins bind carbohydrate ligands and mediate the transient rolling of leukocytes on endothelium during inflammation. Members of the immunoglobulin superfamily, such as NCAM and ICAM, mediate calcium-independent adhesion important in the nervous and immune systems.

Junctions

Animal cells are physically linked by specialised junctions: tight junctions seal epithelia; adherens junctions and desmosomes provide mechanical strength; gap junctions allow direct exchange of small molecules; and hemidesmosomes anchor cells to the basement membrane.

Significance

Regulated adhesion and deadhesion drive embryonic development, wound healing and immune surveillance. Loss of cadherin-mediated adhesion is a hallmark of the epithelial–mesenchymal transition in metastasis, while inherited or acquired defects in adhesion molecules cause blistering skin diseases, bleeding disorders and immunodeficiencies such as leukocyte adhesion deficiency.
