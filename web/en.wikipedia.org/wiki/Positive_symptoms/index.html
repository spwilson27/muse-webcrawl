Positive symptoms

Positive symptoms are symptoms of a mental disorder that represent an excess or distortion of normal functions — experiences and behaviours that are added to a person's normal repertoire. The term is used principally in relation to schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders, where positive symptoms are contrasted with negative symptoms, which represent a loss or reduction of normal functions.

Types

  • Hallucinations — perceptions in the absence of an external stimulus, most commonly hearing voices ("auditory hallucinations"), but also visual, olfactory, gustatory or tactile experiences.
  • Delusions — fixed false beliefs held despite contradictory evidence, including persecutory, grandiose, referential and somatic delusions.
  • Disorganised speech — formal thought disorder shown by derailment, tangentiality, incoherence or illogicality.
  • Disorganised or abnormal motor behaviour — including agitation, bizarre mannerisms and catatonic features.

History of the concept

The positive/negative distinction was popularised in the 1980s, notably by Nancy Andreasen's rating scales (SAPS and SANS) and by Timothy Crow's Type I/Type II model of schizophrenia, which linked positive symptoms to dopamine overactivity and acute episodes, and negative symptoms to structural brain changes and chronic impairment.

Treatment

Positive symptoms generally respond better to antipsychotic medication than negative symptoms, which is one reason the distinction remains clinically important. They are also the main target of standard diagnostic criteria for psychotic episodes.
