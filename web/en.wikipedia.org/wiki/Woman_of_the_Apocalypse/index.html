Woman of the Apocalypse

The Woman of the Apocalypse (Greek: γυνὴ περιβεβλημένη τὸν ἥλιον, "woman clothed with the sun") is a figure described in chapter 12 of the Book of Revelation in the New Testament. She appears as "a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet and a crown of twelve stars on her head" (Revelation 12:1), pregnant and crying out in the pains of childbirth, pursued by a great red dragon with seven heads and ten horns who seeks to devour her child.

The narrative

The woman gives birth to a male child destined to rule the nations; the child is caught up to God, and the woman flees into the wilderness to a place prepared for her, where she is nourished for 1,260 days (or "a time, times, and half a time"). Michael and his angels then war against the dragon, who is cast down to earth; the dragon pursues the woman, spewing a flood from his mouth, but the earth opens and swallows the flood, and the woman is given "two wings of a great eagle" to escape. Enraged, the dragon goes off to make war on "the rest of her offspring".

Interpretations

The Church / people of God

Many interpreters, ancient and modern, read the woman as a corporate symbol: the people of God — Israel giving birth to the Messiah, and/or the Church persecuted through history. The twelve stars suggest the twelve tribes and the twelve apostles.

Mary, mother of Jesus

Catholic tradition frequently identifies the woman with Mary, the mother of Jesus, an interpretation attested from late antiquity and standard in medieval Mariology; the imagery underlies depictions of the Immaculate Conception and of Our Lady of Guadalupe, who appears clothed with the sun's rays, standing on the moon, pregnant. Pope Pius X and later popes endorsed the Marian reading, although Catholic scholars note the figure can bear several levels of meaning simultaneously.

Other readings

Some scholars detect echoes of ancient Near Eastern and Greco-Roman mythic motifs — the goddess (Isis, Leto) pursued by a serpent or dragon (Typhon, Python) who threatens her divine child. Historicist interpreters in the Protestant tradition variously mapped the woman onto the medieval Church, while dispensational readings often take her as national Israel.

In art

The figure is ubiquitous in medieval Apocalypse manuscripts and sculpture (e.g. the tympana of Romanesque churches), in Albrecht Dürer's celebrated 1498 woodcut from his Apocalypse series, and in later Marian imagery throughout Catholic Europe and Latin America.
