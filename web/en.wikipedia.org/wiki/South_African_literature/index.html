South African literature

South African literature is the literature of South Africa, written in the country's many languages, including Afrikaans, English, the Bantu languages (such as Zulu, Xhosa and Sotho), and the oral traditions of San and Khoi peoples that precede written literature by millennia.

Oral traditions and early writing

Indigenous oral storytelling, praise poetry (izibongo), and rock art narratives form the earliest literary heritage. Written literature began with Dutch colonial records in the 17th century. Thomas Pringle and other settler poets wrote in English in the 19th century, while Olive Schreiner's The Story of an African Farm (1883) became the first major South African novel.

Twentieth century

Sol Plaatje's Mhudi (1930) was the first novel in English by a black South African. The 1950s Sophiatown renaissance produced writers such as Peter Abrahams, Es'kia Mphahlele, and the Drum magazine circle. During apartheid, literature became a vehicle of protest: Nadine Gordimer, J. M. Coetzee, André Brink, Athol Fugard, and the poets of the Soweto era wrote against censorship and oppression. Gordimer (1991) and Coetzee (2003) both won the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Post-apartheid literature

Since 1994, writers including Zakes Mda, Antjie Krog (Country of My Skull), Ivan Vladislavić, Marlene van Niekerk, Damon Galgut (Booker Prize winner for The Promise, 2021), and Tsitsi Dangarembga's contemporaries in the region have explored memory, reconciliation, land, and inequality in a democratic but divided society.
