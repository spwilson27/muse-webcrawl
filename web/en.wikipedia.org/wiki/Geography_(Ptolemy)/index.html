Geography (Ptolemy)

The Ptolemaic world map, 15th-century reconstruction

Author: Claudius Ptolemy

Written: c. 150 AD

The Geography (Greek: Γεωγραφικὴ Ὑφήγησις, "Guide to Geography"), also known by its Latin names Geographia and Cosmographia, is a gazetteer, atlas and treatise on cartography compiled by the Greco-Roman scholar Claudius Ptolemy in Alexandria around 150 AD. It codified the geographical knowledge of the Roman Empire and dominated Western and Islamic geography for over a thousand years.

Contents

The work is divided into eight books. Book I is a theoretical treatise on map-making, discussing the construction of globes and map projections — Ptolemy describes two projections of his own devising. Books II–VII list some 8,000 localities with their latitudes and longitudes, covering Europe, Africa and Asia. Book VIII describes how to draw regional maps of the inhabited world (the oikoumene).

Sources and errors

Ptolemy built on the lost work of Marinus of Tyre and on travellers' itineraries. His coordinates contain systematic errors: he underestimated the Earth's circumference (adopting Poseidonius's smaller value rather than Eratosthenes' accurate one) and overestimated the east–west extent of Asia — errors that later encouraged Columbus to believe Asia was reachable by sailing west.

Transmission

The text was preserved by Byzantine scholars, translated into Arabic in the 9th century, and reintroduced to Western Europe when it was translated from Greek into Latin by Jacopo d'Angelo around 1406–1409. Printed editions with reconstructed maps appeared from the 1470s and were enormously influential during the Renaissance and the Age of Discovery.
