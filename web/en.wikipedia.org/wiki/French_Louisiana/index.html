French Louisiana (French: La Louisiane) was the French colonial district of New France encompassing the Mississippi River basin and the Gulf Coast of North America. Named for King Louis XIV, it was claimed in 1682 and governed, with interruptions, until France sold it to the United States in the Louisiana Purchase of 1803.


French Louisiana and the Mississippi basin

Exploration and settlement

In 1682 René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, descended the Mississippi to its mouth and claimed the entire watershed for France. Permanent settlement began in 1699 under Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville on the Gulf Coast. New Orleans was founded in 1718 by Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville and became the capital in 1723. The colony was administered first by chartered companies — notoriously John Law's Mississippi Company, whose collapse in the "Mississippi Bubble" of 1720 ruined investors — and from 1731 directly by the Crown.

Society

French Louisiana was thinly populated and strategically held through forts and alliances with Native nations such as the Choctaw. Its economy rested on fur trading, deerskins, indigo and tobacco; enslaved Africans, first imported in 1719, were governed under the Code Noir and became central to plantation labour around New Orleans and the lower river. "Upper Louisiana" (the Illinois Country) supplied grain to the lower colony.

Transfers

Defeated in the Seven Years' War, France ceded Louisiana west of the Mississippi plus New Orleans to Spain by the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau (1762), while Britain took the east bank. Spain returned Louisiana to France by the Treaty of San Ildefonso in 1800. Napoleon, needing money and having lost Saint-Domingue, sold the territory to the United States in 1803 for $15 million, doubling the size of the young republic.

Legacy

French law, language and culture left deep marks: Louisiana's civil-law tradition, parish system, Creole and Cajun cultures, and the French Quarter of New Orleans all descend from the colonial period.
