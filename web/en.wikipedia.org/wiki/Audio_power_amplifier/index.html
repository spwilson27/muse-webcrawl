Audio power amplifier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An audio power amplifier (informally, an amp) is an electronic amplifier that amplifies low-power electronic audio signals, such as the signal from a preamplifier or audio source, to a level suitable for driving loudspeakers. It is the final stage in a typical audio playback chain, before the loudspeakers.

Amplifier classes

Power amplifier designs are categorised by the portion of the input cycle during which the output devices conduct:

  • Class A — devices conduct for the full cycle; excellent linearity but low efficiency (at best ~50%, often less) and high heat.
  • Class B — two devices each conduct for half the cycle; efficient but prone to crossover distortion.
  • Class AB — a compromise dominating analogue hi-fi design, biasing class B devices slightly on to suppress crossover distortion.
  • Class D — switching amplifiers using pulse-width modulation; efficiencies above 90%, enabling compact high-power designs in PA, car audio and active speakers.

Key specifications

Important parameters include output power (watts RMS into a stated load, typically 8 or 4 ohms), total harmonic distortion, signal-to-noise ratio, frequency response, damping factor and slew rate. Power ratings are governed by standards such as the FTC amplifier rule in the United States.

Technology

Early amplifiers used vacuum tubes; the transistor, demonstrated in consumer amplifiers from the 1960s, largely replaced them, though tube amplifiers persist in guitar amplification and high-end hi-fi. Modern designs commonly use MOSFET or bipolar output stages, op-amp-based front ends, and switch-mode power supplies in class D products.
