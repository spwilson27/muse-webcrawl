Eavesdropping

Eavesdropping is the act of secretly or stealthily listening to the private conversation or communications of others without their consent. The word derives from the practice of standing under the eaves of a house — in the eavesdrop, the line along which water drips from the roof — in order to hear what was said inside. Eavesdropping ranges from casually overhearing conversation to sophisticated electronic surveillance.

Techniques

Traditional methods include concealed listening devices ("bugs"), wiretapping of telephone lines, and parabolic microphones. On computer networks, eavesdropping takes the form of packet sniffing, in which an attacker intercepts traffic on a shared or compromised network; the classic defence is encryption, which renders intercepted traffic unintelligible. So-called man-in-the-middle attacks combine eavesdropping with active interception and relay of messages.

Law

Most legal systems restrict eavesdropping. In the United States, the Federal Wiretap Act generally prohibits intercepting private communications, though jurisdictions differ on whether one party to a conversation may record it ("one-party consent") or all parties must agree ("two-party consent"). Law-enforcement interception is typically authorised only by court order. State surveillance programmes, revealed in detail by the 2013 disclosures of Edward Snowden, prompted worldwide debate on the boundaries of lawful eavesdropping.

In nature

Biologists also use the term for animals that exploit the signals of others — for instance, predator species that home in on the mating calls of frogs or insects, or rival males that assess opponents by listening to their contests — a process called eavesdropping in behavioural ecology.
