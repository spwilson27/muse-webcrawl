Inertial frame of reference

In classical physics and special relativity, an inertial frame of reference (also called an inertial space or Galilean reference frame) is a frame of reference in which objects exhibit inertia: a body with no net force acting on it moves in a straight line at constant speed, and the laws of physics take their simplest form. Any frame moving with constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is itself inertial; frames that accelerate or rotate are non-inertial and exhibit fictitious forces such as the centrifugal and Coriolis forces.

Background

The concept grew out of Galileo's principle of relativity (1632), illustrated by his ship thought experiment: no mechanical experiment performed below deck can reveal whether a ship is at rest or moving uniformly. Newton codified the idea in the Principia (1687), grounding inertial motion in the notion of absolute space, and defended its detectability through the rotating bucket argument. Ernst Mach later criticised absolute space, arguing that inertia should be defined relative to the matter of the universe — an idea that influenced Einstein.

Newtonian mechanics

In Newtonian mechanics all inertial frames are related by Galilean transformations, time is absolute, and Newton's laws are invariant across them. The surface of the Earth is only approximately inertial because of Earth's rotation and orbital motion; for many purposes star-fixed frames (such as the International Celestial Reference Frame) provide better approximations.

Special and general relativity

Einstein's special relativity (1905) recast the inertial frame as one in which the speed of light in vacuum is invariant, replacing Galilean with Lorentz transformations while retaining the defining idea: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames. General relativity generalises this: globally inertial frames do not exist in curved spacetime, but locally, in free fall, spacetime is approximately flat and special relativity applies — the content of the equivalence principle.
