Wichita people

The Wichita people are a confederation of Southern Plains Native American tribes historically centred in present-day Kansas and Oklahoma. The Wichita and Affiliated Tribes (Wichita, Keechi, Waco, and Tawakoni) are a federally recognized tribe headquartered in Anadarko, Oklahoma. Their autonym is Kitikiti'sh, commonly rendered "people of the raccoon eyes", referring to a traditional practice of tattooing around the eyes.

Wichita grass houses were a distinctive feature of their villages.

History

The Wichita lived for centuries along the rivers of the central and southern Great Plains, in villages of large beehive-shaped grass houses. They were farmers growing corn, beans, squash, and tobacco, supplementing agriculture with seasonal buffalo hunts. Archaeologists associate Great Bend aspect sites in Kansas with ancestral Wichita communities.

In 1541, Francisco Vázquez de Coronado encountered the Wichita (the people of Quivira) in present-day Kansas. In the eighteenth century, pressure from the Osage and the arrival of European trade goods drew many Wichita bands south to the Red River and into Texas, where they built fortified villages such as the twin villages on the Red River. Smallpox epidemics severely reduced their population in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

In the nineteenth century the Wichita were displaced repeatedly: they were removed to Indian Territory, fled to Kansas during the Civil War, and in 1867 settled near the Washita River in present-day Oklahoma, where their descendants remain. The Wichita language, a Caddoan language, is now dormant; the last fluent first-language speaker, Doris Lamar McLemore, died in 2016, though revitalisation efforts continue.

Culture

Wichita society was matrilineal and organised into bands led by chiefs. Villages combined farming and hunting, and the Wichita were known for extensive tattooing, finely made pottery, and trade with both Plains and Pueblo peoples. Their grass houses, built by women, could reach 30 to 50 feet in diameter.
