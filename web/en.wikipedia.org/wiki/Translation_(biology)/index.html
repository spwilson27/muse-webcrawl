Translation (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In molecular biology, translation is the process by which ribosomes build proteins using the instructions carried by messenger RNA. The ribosome reads the mRNA sequence in three-letter codons, and transfer RNA molecules bring the matching amino acids, which are linked into a growing chain that folds into a protein.

Process

Translation has three phases. In initiation, the small ribosomal subunit, mRNA and an initiator tRNA assemble at the start codon (usually AUG). In elongation, aminoacyl-tRNAs enter the ribosome's A site, peptide bonds form between amino acids, and the ribosome steps along codon by codon — a cycle driven by elongation factors and GTP. Termination occurs at a stop codon, where release factors free the completed protein. A single mRNA is usually translated by many ribosomes at once (a polysome).

The genetic code and fidelity

The correspondence between codons and amino acids — the genetic code — is nearly universal across life. Proofreading by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and the ribosome keeps error rates low. Many antibiotics work by jamming bacterial ribosomes, exploiting their difference from human ones, and newly made proteins are folded by chaperones and often modified in the Endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.
