Chlamydia is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis. It is the most commonly reported bacterial sexually transmitted infection in many countries, including the United States. Most infections cause no symptoms, which allows the infection to spread unknowingly and to cause complications before it is detected. When symptoms occur, they may include abnormal genital discharge and a burning sensation when urinating.

Signs and symptoms

The majority of infected people — roughly 70% of women and 50% of men in many estimates — have no symptoms. In women, symptomatic infection may cause cervicitis, abnormal vaginal discharge, and bleeding between periods. In men, it can cause urethritis with discharge and painful urination. Infection can also occur in the rectum and throat, and conjunctivitis may result from eye exposure.

Complications

Untreated chlamydia in women can ascend to cause pelvic inflammatory disease, which may lead to chronic pelvic pain, ectopic pregnancy, and tubal-factor infertility. In men it can cause epididymitis. Infection during pregnancy is associated with premature birth and can be passed to the newborn, causing neonatal conjunctivitis and pneumonia. Certain invasive strains of C. trachomatis (serovars L1–L3) cause lymphogranuloma venereum.

Cause and transmission

Chlamydia trachomatis is an obligate intracellular bacterium. It spreads through vaginal, anal, or oral sex with an infected person and can be transmitted even without ejaculation. Because reinfection is common, retesting after treatment is recommended.

Diagnosis and screening

Diagnosis is by nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) of urine or swab samples, which is highly sensitive and specific. Health authorities recommend annual screening of sexually active women under 25 and of older women and men at increased risk. Screening programs have been shown to reduce the incidence of pelvic inflammatory disease.

Treatment and prevention

Chlamydia is curable with antibiotics; doxycycline taken for seven days is the preferred regimen in many guidelines, with azithromycin as an alternative, including in pregnancy. Sexual partners should be treated, and patients should abstain from sex until treatment is complete. Consistent condom use reduces transmission risk. Unlike some infections, having had chlamydia does not confer lasting immunity.

Epidemiology

The World Health Organization has estimated well over 100 million new chlamydial infections annually worldwide among adults, making it one of the four most common curable sexually transmitted infections alongside trichomoniasis, gonorrhea, and syphilis. Reported rates are highest among adolescents and young adults.
