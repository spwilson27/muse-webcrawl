Eugen Goldstein (5 September 1850 – 25 December 1930) was a German physicist, an early investigator of electrical discharges in gases and a pioneer of the discharge-tube experiments from which atomic physics emerged. He coined the term "cathode ray" (Kathodenstrahlen) and in 1886 discovered the canal rays — streams of positively charged particles moving through perforated cathodes — an observation that led eventually to the discovery of isotopes and to mass spectrometry. His work laid groundwork for J. J. Thomson's identification of the electron and is sometimes credited with an early, undeveloped suggestion of the proton.

Life and career

Goldstein studied at Breslau and Berlin, earning his doctorate in 1881 under Hermann von Helmholtz. He spent much of his career at the Berlin Observatory and later headed an astrophysics section at the Potsdam Observatory, pursuing electrical-discharge research largely as a private passion in his own laboratory. Despite the importance of his discoveries he never obtained a full university professorship, a situation often attributed to discrimination against Jewish scientists in Imperial and Weimar Germany.

Research

In the 1870s Goldstein showed that cathode rays travel in straight lines, cast sharp shadows and could be deflected, concluding they were a wave-like phenomenon — a view that set up the German–British debate later resolved in favour of particles by Thomson. In 1886, using a tube with a perforated cathode, he observed luminous streams passing through the holes in the direction opposite to the cathode rays: the Kanalstrahlen, or canal rays. Wilhelm Wien and Thomson later showed these were positive ions of the residual gas; their study by Thomson and Francis Aston revealed isotopes. Goldstein also studied the spectra of gases and salts under discharge, and the phosphorescence produced by cathode rays.

Legacy

Though long overshadowed by Thomson and Rutherford, Goldstein's canal rays are recognised as the first observation of positive ions, foundational both for atomic theory and for the mass spectrograph. A crater on the Moon is named in his honour.
