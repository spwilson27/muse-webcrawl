Engine efficiency

Engine efficiency of thermal engines is the percentage of energy input that the engine can convert to useful work. For a heat engine, efficiency is the ratio of net work output to heat input from the hot reservoir; no heat engine can exceed the Carnot efficiency determined by the temperatures between which it operates, as required by the second law of thermodynamics.

Internal combustion engines

Typical gasoline (petrol) spark-ignition engines convert about 20–35% of the chemical energy in fuel into useful work, with the rest lost as exhaust heat, coolant heat, friction, and pumping losses. Diesel engines are more efficient, generally 30–45%, because of their higher compression ratios and lean combustion; the largest marine diesels can exceed 50% efficiency. Efficiency of internal combustion engines is often expressed through brake specific fuel consumption and indicated by the brake thermal efficiency.

Other engines

Steam turbines in modern power plants achieve efficiencies of around 40%, and combined-cycle gas turbine plants, in which exhaust heat from a gas turbine drives a steam turbine, can reach 60% or more. Jet engines measure their performance by propulsive and thermal efficiency combined as overall efficiency.

Improving efficiency

Engine efficiency can be improved by higher compression ratios, turbocharging and downsizing, direct fuel injection, variable valve timing, reduced friction, waste heat recovery, and hybridisation, in which an electric motor and battery recover braking energy and allow the engine to operate in its most efficient range.
