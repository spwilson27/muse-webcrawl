Lunar New Year

Lunar New Year
Observed byChina, Taiwan, Vietnam, Korea, and diaspora communities worldwide
DateFirst day of the lunisolar (lunar) calendar; falls between 21 January and 20 February
DurationUp to 15 days (to the Lantern Festival)
Also calledSpring Festival (China), Tết (Vietnam), Seollal (Korea)

Lunar New Year is the beginning of a new year based on lunar or lunisolar calendars, celebrated in many East and Southeast Asian cultures. The most widely observed version is Chinese New Year, known in China as the Spring Festival; related festivals include Vietnamese Tết and Korean Seollal. The date falls on the second new moon after the winter solstice (with some variation), between 21 January and 20 February.

Customs

Celebrations centre on family reunion. In China, the reunion dinner on New Year's Eve is the year's most important meal, traditionally including foods symbolising prosperity such as dumplings, fish, and rice cakes (niangao). Homes are cleaned beforehand to sweep away ill fortune and decorated with red paper cuttings, couplets, and lanterns — red being associated with luck and the mythical beast Nian. Elders give children money in red envelopes (hongbao). Lion and dragon dances, firecrackers, and fireworks are traditional. The festival season concludes on the fifteenth day with the Lantern Festival.

Zodiac

Each year is associated with one of twelve animals of the Chinese zodiac — Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon, Snake, Horse, Goat, Monkey, Rooster, Dog, and Pig — cycling in combination with the five elements and the yin-yang polarity, giving a sixty-year cycle.

Around the world

Large public celebrations take place in Chinatowns and Asian diaspora communities in cities such as London, New York, San Francisco, Sydney, and Singapore. The annual migration of workers returning home in China for the holiday, chunyun, is the world's largest periodic human migration.
