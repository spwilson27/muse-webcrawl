Cider

Cider (IPA: /ˈsaɪdər/) is an alcoholic beverage made from the fermented juice of apples. Cider is widely available in the United Kingdom — the world's largest market — and in Ireland, France (especially Brittany and Normandy), Spain (Asturias and the Basque Country), and parts of North America. In the United States, unfiltered non-alcoholic apple juice is often called "apple cider", while the alcoholic drink may be called "hard cider". Alcohol content ranges from about 1.2% to 8.5% ABV or more for traditional English ciders.

History

Fermented apple drinks were known in the ancient world; the Romans encountered cider-making in Britain around 55 BC. By the Norman era, cider production was established in northern France and England. In the English West Country, farmhouse "scrumpy" was traditionally made from unselected bittersweet cider apples rich in tannins. In colonial America cider was a staple drink, and Johnny Appleseed (John Chapman) spread apple orchards largely for cider production. Prohibition in the United States badly damaged the industry, from which it recovered only in the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

Production

Cider apples are washed, milled into a pomace, and pressed; in traditional methods the pomace is wrapped in cloth "cheeses" and pressed in a screw or hydraulic press. The juice ferments with natural or cultured yeasts, sometimes slowly through the winter ("keeving" in French practice produces a naturally sweet, sparkling cider). The finished cider may be bottled still or carbonated, blended, or distilled into apple brandy such as Calvados or applejack.

Styles

French cidre is typically low-alcohol, sweet, and highly carbonated; Spanish sidra is tart and traditionally poured from a height to aerate it; English West Country cider is strong, tannic, and often still. Modern commercial ciders are usually made from culinary apple juice concentrates and are sweet and fizzy. Ice cider, inspired by Quebec ice wine, is made from frozen apples.
