German nationalism

Germania, a personification of the German nation

German nationalism (German: Deutschnationalismus) is an ideological notion that promotes the unity of Germans and German speakers into a single nation-state. It emphasises German culture, language and descent, and first emerged as a mass political force during the Napoleonic Wars, when French occupation stimulated a German national consciousness in opposition to foreign rule.

Origins

Thinkers such as Johann Gottlieb Fichte, whose Addresses to the German Nation (1808) celebrated the German language and spirit, and movements like the Burschenschaften student fraternities fed the demand for a unified national state to replace the fragmented Holy Roman Empire. The liberal nationalists of the 1848–49 revolutions failed to achieve unification, which was instead accomplished "from above" by Otto von Bismarck through the wars of 1864–71, producing the German Empire under Prussian leadership — the "lesser German" (kleindeutsche) solution excluding Austria.

Radicalisation

In the late 19th century, nationalism became increasingly völkisch and expansionist, tied to colonial ambitions and organisations such as the Pan-German League. After defeat in 1918, nationalist resentment over the Treaty of Versailles and the stab-in-the-back myth fuelled radical movements, culminating in the racial nationalism of Nazism under Adolf Hitler, whose regime pursued Lebensraum and pan-German annexations.

After 1945

After the catastrophe of the Second World War, mainstream West German politics embraced a restrained "constitutional patriotism", while reunification in 1990 was achieved peacefully. Debates about national identity, immigration and the far right continue to shape discussion of German nationalism today.
