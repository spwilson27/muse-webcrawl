Agricultural origins

Origins of agriculture
PeriodBegan c. 12,000 years ago, early Holocene
Major centresFertile Crescent, China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, Andes–Amazonia, eastern North America, Sahel, Ethiopia

The origins of agriculture, sometimes called the Neolithic Revolution, were the wide-scale transition of many human cultures during the Neolithic period from a lifestyle of hunting and gathering to one of agriculture and settlement, making an increasingly large population possible. Agriculture was invented independently in at least eleven regions of the world, as foraging communities began to cultivate and eventually domesticate plants and animals.

Centres of origin

The earliest well-documented transition occurred in the Fertile Crescent of Southwest Asia, where by about 9500 BCE communities were farming emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, peas, and flax, and herding sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs. In China, rice was domesticated in the Yangtze valley and millet in the Yellow River valley; in highland New Guinea, taro and banana cultivation began remarkably early. In the Americas, squash, maize (from teosinte), beans, potatoes, and quinoa were domesticated in Mesoamerica and the Andes–Amazonia region, while the Eastern Agricultural Complex of North America produced native crops such as sunflower and goosefoot. Africa saw independent domestications in the Sahel (pearl millet, sorghum, African rice) and the Ethiopian highlands (teff, coffee).

Causes and consequences

Proposed causes include the warmer, more stable and CO2-richer climate of the Holocene, population pressure on wild resources, and social competition and feasting. Whatever the triggers, farming spread by migration and adoption across Eurasia and beyond. Its consequences were transformative and double-edged: storable surpluses supported population growth, sedentism, craft specialization, social hierarchy, and states, but early farmers often suffered poorer health, heavier labour, and greater vulnerability to famine and epidemic disease than the foragers they replaced.
