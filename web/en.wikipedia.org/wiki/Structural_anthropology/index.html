Structural anthropology

Structural anthropology is a school of sociocultural anthropology based on the work of Claude Lévi-Strauss, who argued that culture, like language, is built from underlying structures of the human mind. Drawing on the structural linguistics of Ferdinand de Saussure and Roman Jakobson, Lévi-Strauss proposed that cultural phenomena — myths, kinship systems, rituals, and rules of exchange — can be analysed as systems of relations governed by universal mental structures, often organised as binary oppositions (raw/cooked, nature/culture, life/death).

Key works and ideas

In The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) Lévi-Strauss analysed marriage rules as systems of exchange, generalising Marcel Mauss's theory of the gift. Tristes Tropiques (1955) made him famous beyond academia. His four-volume Mythologiques (1964–71) analysed hundreds of Amerindian myths as transformations of shared structures, and The Savage Mind (1962) examined "primitive" classification as a rigorous intellectual system — the bricolage of concrete thought.

Influence and criticism

Structural anthropology deeply influenced structuralism across the humanities and social sciences, including the work of Louis Althusser, Roland Barthes, and Michel Foucault. Critics charged it with neglecting history, agency, and material conditions, and later movements — post-structuralism, practice theory (Bourdieu), and interpretive anthropology (Geertz) — defined themselves partly against it.
