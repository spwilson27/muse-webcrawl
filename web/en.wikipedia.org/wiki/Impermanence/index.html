Impermanence

Impermanence, also known as the philosophical problem of change, is a concept in a variety of religions and philosophies. In Eastern philosophy it is notable for its role in the Buddhist three marks of existence (tilakkhaṇa), where Sanskrit anitya (Pali: anicca) asserts that all conditioned existence, without exception, is "transient, evanescent, inconstant". It is also a central doctrine in Hinduism and Jainism.

In Buddhism

According to the doctrine of impermanence, all of phenomenal existence arises, changes, and passes away; nothing—including the self—endures unchanged. Together with dukkha (suffering or unsatisfactoriness) and anattā (non-self), anicca is one of the three characteristics of all conditioned things. The Buddha taught that clinging to impermanent things as though they were permanent is a root cause of suffering, and that direct insight into impermanence, cultivated in meditation (for example in vipassanā practice), leads toward liberation. The doctrine extends to the aggregates (skandhas) constituting a person, who changes from moment to moment like a flowing river.

In Hinduism and Jainism

In Hindu thought, the material world (samsara) is likewise impermanent and subject to cycles of creation and dissolution, in contrast with the eternal ātman and Brahman. Jain philosophy emphasises the manifold, changing nature of reality (anekāntavāda), holding that every substance persists through origination and decay of its modes.

In Greek philosophy

In ancient Greek philosophy, Heraclitus made change the centrepiece of his teaching: everything flows (panta rhei), and one cannot step into the same river twice. Parmenides took the opposite view, arguing that change is an illusion—an early formulation of the problem of change that later philosophers, from Aristotle (with his analysis of substance, form and the potential/actual distinction) to modern metaphysicians debating endurance and perdurance, have continued to address.

Cultural expressions

Impermanence has inspired aesthetic ideals such as the Japanese mono no aware—a gentle sadness at the transience of things, embodied in the appreciation of cherry blossoms—and art forms like Tibetan sand mandalas, which are ritually destroyed after completion. In the Western tradition the theme appears as vanitas painting and the memento mori.
