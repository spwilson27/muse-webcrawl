Priscillian

Placeholder image: a late antique church in Hispania

Priscillian (died c. AD 385) was a wealthy Roman nobleman from Gallaecia in Hispania who promoted a rigorous form of Christian asceticism. He became Bishop of Ávila, was accused of heresy and sorcery, and was executed at Trier under the usurper Magnus Maximus — generally regarded as the first Christian executed for heresy by a Christian authority. The movement he founded, Priscillianism, survived in Iberia for two centuries.

Teachings

Priscillian advocated celibacy, fasting, study of apocryphal scriptures, and lay and female participation in prayer meetings, attracting clergy and laity alike. Opponents accused his followers of Gnostic and Manichaean doctrines, magic, and immoral nocturnal rites — charges modern scholars, aided by the rediscovery of his writings at Würzburg in 1885, consider largely unfounded: his surviving tractates appear substantially orthodox.

Trial and execution

Condemned in absentia by a synod at Zaragoza (380), Priscillian appealed to Rome and Milan without success. His chief accuser, Bishop Ithacius, pursued the case at the court of Magnus Maximus, where Priscillian was tortured, convicted of sorcery (maleficium), and beheaded with several followers, including the poetess Euchrotia, around 385. Martin of Tours protested against bishops invoking the secular sword, and the affair became a byword for clerical overreach.

Priscillianism after his death

His body was returned to Gallaecia and venerated as a martyr's; Priscillianist communities persisted until councils at Toledo (400) and Braga (561, 572) suppressed the movement.
