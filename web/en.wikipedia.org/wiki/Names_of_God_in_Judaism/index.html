Names of God in Judaism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The names of God in Judaism are numerous, and many are treated with great reverence in Jewish tradition. The most important and holy name is the Tetragrammaton, the four-letter name YHWH (יהוה), which observant Jews do not pronounce; in prayer and reading it is replaced by Adonai ("my Lord"), and in ordinary speech often by HaShem ("the Name").

The Tetragrammaton

YHWH appears nearly 7,000 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its original pronunciation was lost in antiquity — traditionally rendered "Yahweh" by scholars, and the source of the later form "Jehovah". Because of the prohibition on pronouncing the name (rooted in the Third Commandment and Leviticus 24), scribes marked it with the vowels of Adonai, and printed texts and Torah scrolls handle it with special care.

Other names and titles

Other significant names include El, a generic Semitic word for god used also as a name; Elohim, the plural-form name used throughout Genesis 1; Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh ("I Am that I Am"), revealed to Moses at the burning bush; El Shaddai, often translated "God Almighty"; El Elyon ("God Most High"); El Olam ("the Everlasting God"); Adonai Tzevaot ("Lord of Hosts"); and HaKadosh Baruch Hu ("the Holy One, Blessed be He"). Rabbinic tradition also uses descriptive epithets such as HaMakom ("the Place") and Rachmana ("the Merciful One").

Seven names requiring care

The Talmud lists seven names of God that may not be erased once written — the Tetragrammaton, El, Elohim, Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh, Shaddai, Tzevaot, and Adonai — a rule that governs the work of scribes (soferim) and the disposal of worn sacred texts in a genizah. Kabbalistic tradition elaborates further systems of divine names, including the 42-letter and 72-letter names.
