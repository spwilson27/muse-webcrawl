Hieros gamos

Hieros gamos (Greek ἱερὸς γάμος, "sacred marriage") is a sacred marriage that is ritually enacted, in myth or in cult practice, between a god and a goddess, or between human participants representing them. In the ancient Near East the rite was associated with fertility and the renewal of kingship: in Sumerian and Babylonian tradition the king ritually united with a priestess representing the goddess Inanna/Ishtar during the New Year (Akitu) festival, legitimating his rule and ensuring the fertility of the land.

In Greek religion

In Greek myth the prototypical hieros gamos is the union of Zeus and Hera, celebrated on Samos and elsewhere; their wedding was commemorated in festivals such as the Athenian Theogamia during the month Gamelion ("wedding month"). Scholars have also interpreted elements of the Eleusinian Mysteries and the Athenian Anthesteria — in which the wife of the archon basileus was ritually "married" to Dionysos — as forms of sacred marriage.

Interpretation

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship, particularly that influenced by James George Frazer's The Golden Bough, treated sacred marriage rites as a widespread feature of ancient fertility religion, though later scholars have cautioned against over-generalising and questioned how literally such unions were physically consummated. In analytical psychology, Carl Jung used hierosgamos for the union of opposites within the psyche, and the concept recurs in alchemy as the coniunctio.
