Interfaith dialogue

Interfaith dialogue refers to cooperative, constructive interaction between people of different religious traditions or spiritual beliefs, at both individual and institutional levels. Its aims commonly include mutual understanding, respect for religious diversity, conflict prevention and resolution, and joint action on shared concerns such as poverty and peace. Dialogue is often distinguished from syncretism and proselytism: participants typically do not seek to merge religions or to convert one another, but to learn and to cooperate while retaining their distinct identities.

History

Although encounters between religions are ancient, organised interfaith dialogue is largely a modern movement. The 1893 Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago is often taken as its starting point. In the 20th century, bodies such as the World Congress of Faiths (founded 1936) and, within the World Council of Churches, programmes on dialogue with people of living faiths promoted structured encounters. The Roman Catholic Church's declaration Nostra aetate (1965), issued at the Second Vatican Council, transformed Catholic relations with Judaism and other religions, rejecting antisemitism and affirming truth in other traditions; the Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue followed. Jewish–Christian dialogue developed after the Holocaust through organisations such as the International Council of Christians and Jews (1947).

Forms and initiatives

Scholars commonly distinguish the dialogue of life (everyday neighbourly relations), the dialogue of action (joint social projects), the dialogue of theological exchange, and the dialogue of religious experience (shared prayer and contemplation). Prominent initiatives include the 2007 open letter A Common Word Between Us and You from Muslim scholars to Christian leaders; the United Nations World Interfaith Harmony Week, observed since 2011; King Abdullah's interfaith centres; and local interfaith councils and scriptural reasoning groups, in which Jews, Christians and Muslims study one another's scriptures together. Dialogue has been central to peacebuilding in conflicts with religious dimensions, though critics question whether elite dialogues reach grassroots communities or gloss over real doctrinal differences and power asymmetries.
