Image schema

Image schema
FieldCognitive linguistics, cognitive science
Key figuresMark Johnson, George Lakoff
Key worksThe Body in the Mind (1987); Metaphors We Live By (1980)

An image schema is a recurring dynamic pattern of our perceptual interactions and motor programmes that gives coherence and structure to our experience. The concept was introduced in cognitive linguistics by Mark Johnson in The Body in the Mind (1987) and George Lakoff in Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things (1987). Image schemas are preconceptual structures that arise from embodied experience — manipulating objects, moving through space, perceiving forces — and are later projected, via metaphor, onto abstract domains.

Examples

  • CONTAINER: a boundary distinguishing an interior from an exterior (a cup, a room), underlying expressions like "in trouble" or "out of sight".
  • SOURCE–PATH–GOAL: movement from a starting point along a trajectory to an endpoint, structuring talk of purposes and progress.
  • LINK, BALANCE, FORCE, UP–DOWN, FRONT–BACK, PART–WHOLE, CENTER–PERIPHERY: further basic schemas grounded in bodily experience.

Role in thought and language

Because image schemas have an internal structure — a container has an inside, boundary and outside — they support inferences. Johnson and Lakoff argue that abstract reasoning is largely metaphorical: we understand time through motion schemas, causation through force schemas, and categories through container schemas. Image schemas are therefore proposed as a bridge between bodily experience and higher cognition, and they figure in embodied cognition research, developmental psychology and studies of gesture.

Criticism

Critics have asked how schemas are individuated, whether they are genuinely preconceptual or derived from language itself, and whether experimental evidence supports their psychological reality. Nevertheless the concept remains influential in cognitive semantics and construction grammar.
