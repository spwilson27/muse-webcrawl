Frank Furness

Frank Heyling Furness (1839–1912) was an American architect of the Victorian era, based in Philadelphia. Known for bold, muscular, eclectic designs in an idiosyncratic Gothic Revival and Queen Anne manner, he designed more than 600 buildings, mentored Louis Sullivan, and was later championed as a forerunner of modernism.

Frank Furness
Born: 12 November 1839, Philadelphia
Died: 27 June 1912
Notable works: Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts, Fisher Fine Arts Library, Baldwin Locomotive Works

Life and career

Son of abolitionist minister William Henry Furness, Furness trained with John Fraser and in Richard Morris Hunt’s New York atelier, and won the Medal of Honor in the Civil War. With partners including George Hewitt (Furness & Hewitt) and later Allen Evans, he built banks, stations, churches, libraries and houses noted for compressed forms, oversized details, rich materials and inventive ironwork.

Reputation

His work fell from fashion and many buildings were demolished, but from the 1960s critics and architects — notably Robert Venturi, who praised the University of Pennsylvania’s Fisher Fine Arts Library (1891) — revived his reputation as one of America’s most original architects.
