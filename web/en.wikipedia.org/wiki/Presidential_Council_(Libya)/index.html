Presidential Council (Libya)

TypeHead of state (collective)
SeatTripoli
Formation12 March 2016 (first council)
BasisLibyan Political Agreement (2015)

The Presidential Council (Arabic: المجلس الرئاسي) is the collective head of state of Libya under the Libyan Political Agreement signed at Skhirat, Morocco, on 17 December 2015. The council was intended to lead a Government of National Accord (GNA) and unify the rival institutions that emerged after the second Libyan civil war, balancing representation among Libya's regions and political factions.

First council (2016–2021)

The original council had nine members — a president, five vice presidents and three ministers of state — chosen to reflect Libya's three historic regions, Tripolitania, Cyrenaica and Fezzan. Fayez al-Sarraj, a former parliamentarian, chaired the council and served as prime minister of the GNA. The council arrived in Tripoli at the end of March 2016 and operated from the Abu Sittah naval base before moving into the capital. Several members resigned or boycotted sessions over disputes about security arrangements and appointments, and the council's authority in eastern Libya was never accepted by the House of Representatives or Khalifa Haftar's Libyan National Army.

Second council (2021–present)

In February 2021 the Libyan Political Dialogue Forum, convened by the United Nations, elected a new three-member Presidential Council chaired by Mohamed al-Menfi, a former ambassador from Tobruk, with Abdullah al-Lafi and Mossa al-Koni as deputies. The council was paired with a Government of National Unity led by Prime Minister Abdul Hamid Dbeibeh and was meant to govern only until national elections planned for December 2021. Those elections were postponed indefinitely, and the council has remained in office, exercising head-of-state functions including the formal role of supreme commander of the armed forces, receiving foreign ambassadors, and issuing decrees by consensus of its members.

Powers

Under the Political Agreement the council acts collectively. Its functions include appointing senior military commanders, declaring states of emergency with other institutions' approval, accrediting diplomats, and signing legislation promulgated by the legislature into effect. Decisions are taken unanimously among the chair and deputies, a rule designed to force consensus but one that has also contributed to paralysis during crises.
