Alfred Binet (8 July 1857 – 18 October 1911) was a French psychologist best known for developing, with his collaborator Théodore Simon, the first practical intelligence test, the Binet–Simon scale. His work laid the foundation for modern IQ testing, although Binet himself intended the scale as a tool to identify schoolchildren needing educational support, not as a fixed measure of innate ability.

Life and career

Binet was born in Nice and studied law in Paris before turning to natural science and psychology at the Sorbonne. He worked at the Salpêtrière Hospital under Jean-Martin Charcot, studying hypnosis, then in 1894 became director of the Laboratory of Experimental Psychology at the Sorbonne. In 1895 he co-founded L'Année psychologique, the first French journal of psychology. His early research used his two daughters, Marguerite and Alice, as subjects in studies of individual differences in perception, memory and reasoning.

The Binet–Simon scale

In 1904 the French Ministry of Public Education commissioned Binet to devise a method for identifying children whose poor school performance reflected intellectual disability rather than laziness. With Simon he published a scale in 1905, revised in 1908 and 1911, grouping tasks by the age at which a typical child could perform them and yielding the concept of "mental age". Lewis Terman at Stanford later adapted the scale into the Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales and introduced the intelligence quotient (IQ) formulation proposed by William Stern.

Views and legacy

Binet warned against treating intelligence as a single, fixed quantity and emphasised that his scale was a rough guide for classification, not a ranking of worth. Later misuse of intelligence tests in eugenics and immigration policy ran contrary to his stated intentions.
