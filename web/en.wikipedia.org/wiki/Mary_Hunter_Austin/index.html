Mary Hunter Austin (September 9, 1868 – August 13, 1934) was an American writer. One of the early nature writers of the American Southwest, Austin is best known for her 1903 collection The Land of Little Rain, a classic account of the Mojave Desert and Owens Valley country of California. Her writing describes the land of California and the High Sierra, the Indigenous people, sheepherders, miners, and settlers she encountered, and foreshadows the attitudes of modern environmentalism: she argued that the desert had an intrinsic worth and that human use of water in arid lands should respect ecological limits.

Life

Born in Carlinville, Illinois, she graduated from Blackburn College in 1888 and moved with her family to a homestead in California's San Joaquin Valley. Her marriage to Stafford Wallace Austin took her to the Owens Valley, where she taught school, studied the Paiute and Shoshone people, and began publishing stories. After her marriage ended she joined the artists' colonies at Carmel-by-the-Sea and later Santa Fe, New Mexico, associating with figures such as Jack London, Willa Cather, and Ansel Adams, and campaigning for Native American arts, water rights in the Owens Valley, and women's suffrage. Mount Mary Austin, in the Sierra Nevada near Independence, California, is named in her honour.

Works

Austin wrote more than 30 books, including essay collections such as The Land of Journeys' Ending (1924) and The Land of the Sun (1927), the novel Isidro (1905), the play The Arrow Maker (1911), retellings of Native tales in The American Rhythm (1923), and her autobiography Earth Horizon (1932), written in the third person. Her work helped define regional literature of the West and modern American nature writing.
