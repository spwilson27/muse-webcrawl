NASA Clean Air Study

The NASA Clean Air Study was a project led by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in association with the Associated Landscape Contractors of America (ALCA) in 1989 to research ways to clean the air in sealed environments such as space stations. Its results showed that certain common indoor plants may provide a natural way of removing volatile organic pollutants (benzene, formaldehyde, and trichloroethylene were tested).

Plants tested in sealed chambers removed measurable amounts of airborne chemicals over 24 hours.

Background

The study, principally authored by B. C. Wolverton, grew out of research into life-support systems for future lunar bases and observations of "sick building syndrome" in energy-efficient sealed buildings, including NASA's own Biohome experiment. Wolverton investigated how plants and the microorganisms in their root zones could break down synthetic organic chemicals.

Method and findings

Selected houseplants were placed in sealed Plexiglas chambers into which known quantities of benzene, formaldehyde, or trichloroethylene were injected; concentrations were measured over 24 hours. Many plants removed significant fractions of the chemicals, with removal partly attributable to microorganisms in the potting soil, which could adapt to metabolise the pollutants. Species frequently cited from the study's list include English ivy (Hedera helix), spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum), golden pothos (Epipremnum aureum), peace lily (Spathiphyllum), Chinese evergreen (Aglaonema), snake plant (Dracaena trifasciata), and the florist's chrysanthemum.

Criticism and later research

Later analyses have cautioned that the sealed-chamber results do not translate directly to real buildings: a 2019 review calculated that dozens to hundreds of plants per square metre would be needed to match the air-cleaning effect of ordinary ventilation. Nonetheless, the study remains hugely influential in popular culture and the houseplant industry, and its core finding — that plant–microbe systems metabolise VOCs — is not in dispute.
