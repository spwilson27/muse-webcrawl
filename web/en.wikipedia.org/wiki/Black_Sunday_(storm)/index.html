Black Sunday (storm)

Black Sunday
DateApril 14, 1935
LocationOklahoma Panhandle and Texas Panhandle, U.S.
TypeDust storm ("black blizzard")

Black Sunday refers to a particularly severe dust storm that occurred on April 14, 1935, as part of the Dust Bowl in the United States. The storm, one of the worst of the decade, swept across the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles and neighbouring areas, turning day into night and displacing an estimated 300,000 tons of topsoil by some accounts.

The storm

After a calm, sunny morning, a cold front drove a massive wall of dust southward across the plains. Witnesses described a black cloud thousands of feet high that blotted out the sun; static electricity from the storm reportedly stalled car engines and made metal objects spark. People caught outdoors could not see their hands in front of their faces. Associated Press reporter Robert E. Geiger, writing from Guymon, Oklahoma, popularised the term "Dust Bowl" in his dispatches about the storm.

Impact

Black Sunday intensified national attention on the ecological disaster of the Dust Bowl and on the plight of farming families, many of whom migrated westward in its aftermath. Folksinger Woody Guthrie, who was in Pampa, Texas, during the storm, commemorated it in his song "The Great Dust Storm" on the album Dust Bowl Ballads (1940).
