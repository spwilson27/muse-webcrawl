Marie-Georges-Jean Méliès (8 December 1861 – 21 January 1938) was a French magician, actor, and film director. He led many technical and narrative developments in the earliest days of cinema, primarily in the fantasy and science fiction genres. A pioneering innovator of special effects, Méliès pioneered the use of multiple exposures, time-lapse photography, dissolves, and hand-painted colour in his work.

Early life and stage magic

Méliès was born in Paris into a family of shoe manufacturers. Drawn to puppetry, drawing, and stage magic, he worked in London as a magician's assistant and, in 1888, bought the Théâtre Robert-Houdin in Paris, where he staged elaborate illusion spectacles. In December 1895 he attended the Lumière brothers' first public film screening and immediately sought to acquire a camera; refused a sale of the Cinématographe, he obtained a projector in London and built his own camera.

Film career

Méliès began filming in 1896 and by 1897 had built one of the world's first film studios — a glass-roofed studio at Montreuil — and founded the Star Film Company. Legend holds that he discovered the stop trick when his camera jammed during filming, and he went on to master substitution splices, superimpositions, and theatrical trick effects. Between 1896 and 1913 he directed more than 500 films.

His most famous work, A Trip to the Moon (Le Voyage dans la Lune, 1902), shows a group of astronomers travelling to the Moon in a cannon-fired capsule; its image of the capsule landing in the Man in the Moon's eye is one of the most iconic in cinema history. Other major films include The Impossible Voyage (1904), The Kingdom of the Fairies (1903), and The Conquest of the Pole (1912). He also made one of the first horror films, The Haunted Castle (1896), and a noted reconstruction of the Dreyfus Affair.

Decline and rediscovery

Under pressure from larger studios, piracy of his films (notably by Edison's company), and changing tastes, Méliès's finances collapsed; he stopped filmmaking in 1913 and later ran a toy and candy stall at the Gare Montparnasse. In the late 1920s film historians and surrealist admirers rediscovered him; he was awarded the Legion of Honour in 1931. He died in Paris in 1938. His life inspired Martin Scorsese's film Hugo (2011).
