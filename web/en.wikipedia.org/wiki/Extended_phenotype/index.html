Extended phenotype

The extended phenotype is a biological concept, most fully developed by Richard Dawkins in his 1982 book The Extended Phenotype, according to which a phenotype should not be limited to biological processes such as protein biosynthesis or tissue growth, but extended to include all effects that a gene has on its environment, inside or outside the body of the individual organism.

The argument

Dawkins argued that the effects of a gene can reach far beyond the cell or body in which it sits. His celebrated examples include the houses built by caddis-fly larvae (which vary with the larva's genes and affect its survival), the dams of beavers, and the manipulated behaviour of hosts by parasites — for example, flukes that make snails behave in ways that benefit the parasite's life cycle. If a parasite's gene changes its host's behaviour in ways that increase the parasite gene's propagation, that behaviour is part of the parasite gene's extended phenotype.

Central theorem

Dawkins summarised the idea in his "central theorem of the extended phenotype": an animal's behaviour tends to maximise the survival of the genes "for" that behaviour, whether or not those genes happen to be in the body of the particular animal performing it. The book also introduced the idea that the organism itself is a "vehicle" or survival machine constructed by replicating genes.

Reception

Dawkins considered the extended phenotype his most important contribution to evolutionary theory. The concept has influenced work on niche construction, parasite manipulation of hosts, and discussions of where the boundaries of an individual organism lie.
