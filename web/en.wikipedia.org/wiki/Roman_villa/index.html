A Roman villa was a country house built for wealthy Romans during the Republic and Empire. Villas ranged from working agricultural estates (villa rustica), producing wine, olive oil and grain with slave and tenant labour, to luxurious leisure retreats (villa urbana, or villa maritima on the coast) where senators escaped the politics and heat of Rome.

Architecture

The typical villa was arranged around courtyards — an atrium and a colonnaded peristyle garden — with reception rooms, dining rooms (triclinia), private baths and bedrooms decorated with frescoes and mosaic floors, heated by hypocausts. The estate complex included a pars rustica with barns, presses, granaries and slave quarters. Famous examples include Hadrian's Villa at Tivoli, the Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum, and the palatial Villa Romana del Casale in Sicily with its celebrated mosaics.

Villas in the provinces

Thousands of villas spread across the western provinces — Gaul, Spain, Britain, North Africa — as the working centres of landed estates and a focus of Romanization in the countryside. Many declined in the 4th and 5th centuries amid insecurity and economic change, though some were converted into churches or early medieval settlements.
