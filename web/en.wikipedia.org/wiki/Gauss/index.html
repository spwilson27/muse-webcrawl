Gauss

Gauss (symbol: G) — CGS unit of magnetic flux density. Named after Carl Friedrich Gauss. Named 1936 by the IEC.

The gauss, symbol G (sometimes Gs), is a unit of measurement of magnetic induction, also known as magnetic flux density. The unit is part of the Gaussian system of units, which inherited it from the older centimetre–gram–second electromagnetic units (CGS-EMU) system. It was named after the German mathematician and physicist Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1936.

Definition

One gauss is defined as one maxwell per square centimetre. As the CGS system has been superseded by the International System of Units (SI), the use of the gauss has been deprecated by standards bodies, but is still regularly used in various subfields of science. The SI unit for magnetic flux density is the tesla (symbol T), which corresponds to 10,000 gauss.

The gauss is defined in terms of the CGS system's base units. The magnetic flux density B in gauss can be related to the magnetic field strength H in oersteds. In vacuum, B = H in CGS units, whereas in SI units B = μ₀H.

Unit conversions

1 G = 1 Mx/cm², and 1 G = 10⁻⁴ T. The Earth's magnetic field at its surface ranges from about 0.25 to 0.65 gauss. Smaller magnetic fields are often measured in milligauss or microgauss. The gaussmetre is an instrument used to measure magnetic fields in gauss.

History

The unit's namesake, Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855), made fundamental contributions to the theory of magnetism and, with Wilhelm Eduard Weber, developed one of the first magnetometers. The IEC officially adopted the name "gauss" for the unit in 1936, replacing earlier usage of "line per square centimetre."
