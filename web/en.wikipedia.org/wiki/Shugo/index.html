Shugo (守護), commonly rendered "military governor", "constable" or "protector", was a title given to certain officials in feudal Japan. Appointed by the shōgun, a shugo was responsible for one or more provinces and combined military, police and limited judicial functions. The office was created in 1185 by Minamoto no Yoritomo, founder of the Kamakura shogunate, as part of the new dual system of rule in which the imperial court in Kyoto retained civil administration while the shogunate controlled military affairs.

The shugo's provincial headquarters (shugo-sho) became centres of warrior government in the provinces.

Duties

The shugo's original, formally defined tasks — the so-called "three commissions" (ōbansai) — were to organise the local warriors (gokenin) for guard duty in Kyoto, to hunt down rebels, and to punish serious crimes such as murder and robbery. Shugo worked alongside the jitō (land stewards) appointed to individual estates, and frictions between the two offices were a recurring feature of Kamakura politics.

Rise of the shugo daimyō

During the Muromachi period (1336–1573), the Ashikaga shogunate greatly expanded shugo authority: they gained rights to a share of estate revenues (hanzei), power to adjudicate land disputes, and control over provincial taxation. Powerful shugo houses such as the Yamana, Hosokawa, Shiba and Hatakeyama accumulated multiple provinces and became shugo daimyō, territorial warlords in all but name. Their competition for influence in Kyoto helped ignite the Ōnin War (1467–1477) and the Sengoku period, during which many shugo were overthrown by their own deputies (shugodai) or by rising local lords, while others transformed themselves into Sengoku daimyō.
