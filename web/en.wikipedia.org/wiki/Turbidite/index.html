Turbidite

A turbidite is the geologic deposit of a turbidity current — a dense, sediment-laden underwater flow that moves rapidly downslope under gravity, typically down a continental slope. Turbidite deposits characteristically show graded bedding, with coarse grains at the base fining upward as the current slows and drops its load.

Bouma sequence

The classic description of a complete turbidite bed is the Bouma sequence (1962), a five-part vertical succession from massive or graded sandstone at the base through parallel lamination, ripple cross-lamination, and silt, to mud at the top. Individual beds may be incomplete depending on the flow's energy and distance travelled.

Significance

Thick turbidite successions (flysch) accumulate in deep-marine fans and foreland basins, and are now mostly interpreted as products of repeated turbidity currents triggered by earthquakes, storms, or slope failure. Turbidite sandstones form important petroleum reservoirs in offshore basins such as the North Sea, the Gulf of Mexico, and offshore Brazil and West Africa.
