Sociology of emotions

The sociology of emotions studies how feelings are shaped by social structures, norms, and interactions.

The sociology of emotions is a subfield of sociology that studies emotions as social phenomena: how they are shaped by social structures, cultural norms, roles, and interactions, and how they in turn sustain or disrupt social order. Although classical sociologists including Marx, Weber, Durkheim, and Simmel wrote about feelings such as alienation, charisma, collective effervescence, and shame, the field crystallized as a named specialty only in the 1970s and 1980s, with a section of the American Sociological Association established in 1986.

Major approaches

Dramaturgical and cultural approaches, associated with Erving Goffman and especially Arlie Russell Hochschild, examine feeling rules — social norms about what one should feel and display — and emotion work, the effort of managing one's feelings. Hochschild's The Managed Heart (1983) introduced the concept of emotional labour, performed for a wage by service workers such as flight attendants and bill collectors. Structural theories, such as Theodore Kemper's power–status model, derive emotions from the outcomes of interactions along dimensions of power and status. Interaction ritual theory, developed by Randall Collins from Durkheim, treats shared emotion ("emotional energy") as the central product of successful social rituals. Affect control theory uses computational models to predict how identities and behaviours generate sentiments.

Topics

Sociologists of emotion study, among other subjects, empathy and sympathy markets; love and intimacy; shame, guilt, anger, and grief in social movements and politics; the emotions of organizations and professions; and the commercialization of feeling in service economies. The field draws on, and debates with, psychology and neuroscience over the extent to which emotions are socially constructed versus biologically based.
