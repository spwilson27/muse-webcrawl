Jiaozi

Jiaozi (simplified Chinese: 饺子; traditional Chinese: 餃子; pinyin: jiǎozi) are Chinese dumplings consisting of a thinly rolled piece of dough filled generally with ground meat and finely chopped vegetables, crimped into a crescent shape. They are boiled, steamed or pan-fried, eaten with a dipping sauce of black vinegar and chilli oil or soy sauce, and are a staple of northern Chinese cuisine and of Chinese New Year celebrations.

Jiaozi
Place of origin: China
Region: Northern China
Main ingredients: Wheat dough, ground pork, cabbage or chives
Variations: Shuijiao (boiled), zhengjiao (steamed), jianjiao / gyoza (pan-fried)

History

Dumplings resembling jiaozi are depicted in Han dynasty tombs, and jiaozi-shaped gold ornaments and actual preserved dumplings have been found in Tang-dynasty tombs at Turpan. Legend credits the Han physician Zhang Zhongjing with wrapping medicinal "ear-shaped" dumplings (jiao'er) to treat frostbitten ears. By the Ming and Qing dynasties jiaozi were the standard New Year's Eve food in the north, their shape echoing silver ingots (sycee) and promising wealth; a coin is sometimes hidden in one for luck.

Preparation and types

Unleavened wheat dough is rolled into small circles, filled — classically with pork and Chinese cabbage or garlic chives, also beef, lamb, shrimp, egg or vegetables — pleated shut, then cooked: boiled shuijiao, steamed zhengjiao, or pan-fried jianjiao, called guotie ("potstickers") when crisped in a pan. The Japanese gyōza and Korean mandu descend from jiaozi. Related Chinese dumplings include wontons, xiaolongbao and har gow.
