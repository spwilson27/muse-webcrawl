Swap (finance)

In finance, a swap is a derivative contract through which two counterparties exchange cash flows or liabilities arising from different financial instruments. Most swaps involve cash flows based on a notional principal amount, such as a loan or bond, although the instruments can be almost anything. Usually the principal does not change hands. Each cash flow comprises one "leg" of the swap. The first swaps were traded in the early 1980s — a landmark 1981 currency swap between IBM and the World Bank is usually cited — and the market has grown to hundreds of trillions of dollars in notional value outstanding.

Types of swaps

  • Interest rate swaps — exchange fixed-rate for floating-rate interest payments in the same currency; by far the largest segment of the market.
  • Currency swaps — exchange principal and interest in different currencies, used to hedge foreign-currency borrowing.
  • Credit default swaps (CDS) — the buyer pays a premium for protection against default on a reference debt; they played a prominent role in the 2008 financial crisis.
  • Commodity swaps — exchange a fixed price for a floating market price of oil, gas, metals or agricultural goods.
  • Equity swaps — exchange the return on a stock or index for a fixed or floating rate.
  • Total return swaps — exchange the total economic performance of an asset for a fixed cash flow.

Uses and risks

Swaps are used to hedge risk (converting floating-rate debt to fixed, for example), to obtain cheaper financing through comparative advantage, and to gain exposure to assets or markets without buying them. Because they were historically traded over-the-counter (OTC) under ISDA master agreements, swaps create counterparty credit risk; after the 2008 crisis, reforms such as the U.S. Dodd–Frank Act pushed standardised swaps onto central clearing houses and exchanges, and benchmark reform replaced the scandal-hit LIBOR reference rate with near-risk-free rates such as SOFR and €STR.
