Myoglobin

Myoglobin

Type: Heme protein (globin)

Location: Muscle tissue

Function: Oxygen storage and facilitated diffusion

First protein structure solved: 1958 (John Kendrew)

Ribbon diagram of myoglobin; the heme group sits in a hydrophobic pocket.

Myoglobin (symbol Mb) is an iron- and oxygen-binding protein found in the muscle tissue of vertebrates. A single polypeptide chain of about 153 amino acids folded into eight alpha-helices, it contains one heme group that binds a single oxygen molecule. Myoglobin stores oxygen in muscle and releases it when blood oxygen runs low during exertion; it is especially abundant in the muscles of diving mammals such as whales and seals, allowing them to stay submerged for long periods.

Structure and history

Myoglobin was the first protein to have its three-dimensional structure determined by X-ray crystallography, work by John Kendrew and colleagues on sperm whale myoglobin reported in 1958, which earned Kendrew a share of the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Unlike haemoglobin, with its four subunits and cooperative S-shaped binding curve, myoglobin is monomeric and binds oxygen hyperbolically with very high affinity — suitable for taking oxygen from haemoglobin and holding it in muscle. Its bright red pigment gives meat its colour: the red liquid in raw beef is largely water mixed with myoglobin, not blood.

Clinical significance

When muscle is damaged — by crush injury, severe exertion, or heart attack — myoglobin escapes into the bloodstream. Massive release (rhabdomyolysis) can obstruct the kidneys and cause acute kidney failure, and blood or urine myoglobin was historically used as an early, though non-specific, marker of myocardial infarction.
