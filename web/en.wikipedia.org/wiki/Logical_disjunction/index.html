In logic, disjunction, also known as logical disjunction or logical or (or inclusive or), is a truth-functional operator which produces a result that is true if at least one of its operands is true. The disjuncts are the operands of the disjunction. The logical connective that represents this operator is typically written as ∨ or +. Thus, A ∨ B — read "A or B" — is true if A is true, if B is true, or if both are true, and false only when both are false.

Inclusive and exclusive or

Logical disjunction as standardly defined is inclusive: "A or B" does not rule out both. Natural language "or" is often exclusive ("either A or B but not both"), which logic treats as a separate connective, XOR, expressible as (A ∨ B) ∧ ¬(A ∧ B). Logicians regard ambiguity between the two as a matter of pragmatics — often analysed following Grice as a conversational implicature — rather than of the meaning of "or" itself.

Properties and rules

Disjunction is commutative and associative, idempotent (A ∨ A ≡ A), and distributes over conjunction. It figures in the inference rules of disjunction introduction (from A, infer A ∨ B for any B) and disjunction elimination (proof by cases). In Boolean algebra, disjunction corresponds to the OR operation and, over sets, to union; in electronics it is realised by the OR gate. Disjunction also interacts with conjunction through De Morgan's laws: ¬(A ∨ B) ≡ ¬A ∧ ¬B.

Notation and computing

Notation varies by field: ∨ in logic (chosen by Russell and Whitehead, from Latin vel), + in Boolean algebra and engineering, and || or or in programming languages, where it typically short-circuits — the second operand is not evaluated if the first is true.
