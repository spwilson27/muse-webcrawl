Service-learning is an educational approach that combines learning objectives with community service in order to provide a pragmatic, progressive learning experience while meeting societal needs. It integrates meaningful community service with instruction and reflection to enrich the learning experience, teach civic responsibility, and strengthen communities.

Definition and components

Service-learning is commonly defined by three key components: (1) service that meets a genuine community need, undertaken in partnership with community organisations; (2) explicit connection of the service to academic learning objectives in a course or curriculum; and (3) structured reflection by students on the experience. It is distinguished from volunteering and community service by this deliberate integration with learning, and from internships by its emphasis on reciprocity and civic outcomes as much as on skills.

History

The term "service-learning" was coined in 1966 by Robert Sigmon and William Ramsay in the context of the Southern Regional Education Board. The approach drew on earlier educational philosophy, notably John Dewey's ideas on experiential education and the link between education and democracy. It expanded rapidly in the United States in the 1980s and 1990s, supported by organisations such as Campus Compact and by federal programmes including Learn and Serve America.

Outcomes and criticism

Studies associate service-learning with gains in academic achievement, civic engagement, and personal development, though effect sizes vary with programme quality. Critics argue that poorly designed programmes can burden community partners, reinforce stereotypes, or substitute charity for structural change; advocates respond that partnership-based, critically reflective designs mitigate these risks.
