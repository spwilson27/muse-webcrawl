Colorism

Colorism, or shadeism, is a form of prejudice or discrimination in which people of the same race or ethnic group are treated differently based on the shade of their skin color — typically, with lighter skin receiving preferential treatment over darker skin. The term was popularized by writer Alice Walker in 1982, who defined colorism as "prejudicial or preferential treatment of same-race people based solely on their color." Colorism operates both within communities and on a global scale, and is analytically distinct from racism, which involves discrimination between perceived racial groups.

History and causes

Scholars link colorism to colonialism and slavery: in many colonized societies, lighter-skinned people — often those of mixed European ancestry — were granted intermediate status, education, and employment advantages, a hierarchy that persisted after independence. In the Americas, the "brown paper bag test" was infamously used by some institutions to exclude darker-skinned African Americans, while practices such as enslaved house servants receiving marginally better conditions deepened intra-community divisions. In South and Southeast Asia, preferences for fair skin predate European colonialism in some accounts but were reinforced by it, and sustain a large skin-lightening industry; similar patterns are documented in Latin America, the Caribbean, the Middle East, and parts of Africa, where skin bleaching remains a public-health concern.

Effects

Research in the United States has found correlations between darker skin tone and disparities in wages, educational attainment, sentencing outcomes, and perceived attractiveness within the same racial group. Media representation is also implicated: casting, photography lighting, and beauty advertising have historically favored lighter skin.

Responses

Campaigns such as "Dark is Beautiful" in India and movements within African American and Afro-Latino communities have challenged colorist norms, and several major cosmetics companies have renamed or discontinued products marketed with terms such as "fair" and "whitening."
