Thirty-two-bar form, also called AABA form or ballad form, is a song structure that was the dominant form of American popular song in the first half of the twentieth century, the era of Tin Pan Alley and the Broadway musical. Thousands of standards of the Great American Songbook are built on it, and its chord progressions became foundational vocabulary for jazz.

Structure

The form consists of four sections of eight bars each. The main theme (A) is stated, repeated (with the same music and often partly different lyrics, or as a repeated refrain), and then contrasted with a contrasting section (B), called the bridge — also known as the middle eight, release, or channel — before the main theme returns for a final A section. The B section typically moves to a different harmonic area, often beginning on the subdominant or a related key, providing tension that the returning A resolves.

In recorded performance the 32-bar chorus was usually preceded by a verse, which jazz and cabaret performers often omit, and the chorus itself may be repeated many times as a framework for improvisation.

Examples and legacy

Classic examples include "I Got Rhythm" (1930) by George and Ira Gershwin — whose chord progression, "rhythm changes," underlies countless jazz compositions — as well as "Blue Moon," "Body and Soul," "What Is This Thing Called Love?," and "Over the Rainbow." The Beatles' early songs also frequently used the form, for example "From Me to You" and "I Want to Hold Your Hand."

As rock and roll and later pop styles rose in the 1950s and 1960s, the verse–chorus form displaced the 32-bar chorus as the standard popular-song structure, but AABA remains a core form in jazz performance and music theory teaching.
