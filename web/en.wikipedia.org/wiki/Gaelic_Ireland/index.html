Gaelic Ireland

Gaelic Ireland (Irish: Éire Ghaelach) was the Gaelic political and social order, and associated culture, that existed in Ireland from the late prehistoric era until the 17th century. For most of its history it was not a unified state but a patchwork of independent kingdoms and lordships (túatha) bound by a common language, law, and culture, with a nominal High King (Ard Rí) whose authority varied greatly over time.

Society

Gaelic society was kin-based and hierarchical, ordered around the extended family group (derbfine). Kings and lords ruled territories whose inhabitants owed them tribute and military service. Learned classes — jurists (brehons), poets and historians (filí), physicians, and craftsmen — enjoyed high status and often hereditary professions. Brehon law, a sophisticated body of customary law administered without central enforcement, governed contracts, compensation for injury (éraic), and status.

History

Early medieval Ireland developed a distinctive monastic culture of scholarship and art — producing works such as the Book of Kells — and Irish missionaries were influential across early medieval Europe. From 795 the island endured Viking raids and settlements, and in 1169 began the Norman invasion, which brought much of the east and south under the Lordship of Ireland. Gaelic lordships nonetheless recovered ground in the 14th and 15th centuries. The Tudor conquest, completed after the Nine Years' War (1594–1603), the Flight of the Earls (1607), and the plantations that followed, destroyed the Gaelic order; by the mid-17th century it had effectively ceased to exist as a political system.

Culture

Gaelic culture centred on the Irish language, oral and written literature (including the great saga cycles), bardic poetry, music, and sports such as hurling that survive in modified form today.
