Trust deed (real estate)

In real estate in the United States, a trust deed or deed of trust is a legal instrument used in place of a mortgage in many states. It transfers the legal title in real property to a trustee, typically a title company, which holds it as security for a loan between a borrower (trustor) and a lender (beneficiary). The equitable title remains with the borrower, as does possession of the property.

How it works

Three parties are involved: the trustor (borrower), the beneficiary (lender) and the trustee. The borrower signs a promissory note promising repayment, and the deed of trust is recorded in the county land records. When the loan is fully repaid, the beneficiary directs the trustee to execute a deed of reconveyance, restoring full legal title to the borrower.

Foreclosure

The principal advantage of the trust deed for lenders is the power of sale clause: if the borrower defaults, the trustee may sell the property at a public auction through a non-judicial foreclosure, without going to court, subject to statutory notice requirements. This process is generally much faster than judicial mortgage foreclosure — in some states only a few months. Some jurisdictions, however, require judicial foreclosure even for deeds of trust, or restrict deficiency judgments after a non-judicial sale.

Comparison with mortgages

A mortgage involves two parties and, in lien-theory states, merely creates a lien rather than conveying title. A deed of trust is preferred in states such as California, Arizona, Colorado, Texas and Virginia, while traditional mortgages prevail in much of the Midwest and East. The practical outcome for a performing borrower is much the same; the difference matters mainly on default.
