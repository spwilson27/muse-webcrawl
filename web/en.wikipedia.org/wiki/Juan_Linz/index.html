Juan Linz

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Juan José Linz Storch de Gracia (24 December 1926 – 1 October 2013) was a Spanish sociologist and political scientist, Sterling Professor at Yale University, whose work on authoritarianism, totalitarianism and democratic breakdown shaped comparative politics. Born in Bonn to a German father and Spanish mother and raised in Spain, he wrote his Columbia dissertation on Spanish politics and became the leading scholar of Franco's regime.

Linz's distinction between authoritarian regimes — limited pluralism, no guiding ideology, political demobilisation — and totalitarian ones structured the study of dictatorships. His essay “The Perils of Presidentialism” (1990) argued that presidential constitutions are more prone to breakdown than parliamentary ones, because of dual democratic legitimacy (president and legislature both claim election), fixed terms that make crises rigid, and winner-take-all stakes; it provoked a large literature, with critics citing selection effects and the survival of many presidential democracies. With Alfred Stepan he wrote Problems of Democratic Transition and Consolidation (1996), a framework for how democracies are established and made “the only game in town”.

Legacy

Linz's concepts — Linzian regime types, the perils thesis, democratic consolidation — remain standard teaching. Spain's successful transition after Franco, which he advised on indirectly through scholarship, is often read through his framework.
