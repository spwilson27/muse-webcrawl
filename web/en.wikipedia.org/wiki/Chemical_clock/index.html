Chemical clock

A chemical clock (or clock reaction) is a complex mixture of reacting chemical compounds in which the onset of an observable property — most often a dramatic colour change — occurs after a predictable induction period. Clock reactions are classic demonstrations in chemical kinetics because the delay before the change depends sharply on the concentrations of the reactants.

The iodine clock

The best-known example is the iodine clock reaction, in which hydrogen peroxide oxidises iodide to iodine under acidic conditions while a reducing agent such as thiosulfate immediately converts the iodine back. Only when the reducing agent is exhausted does free iodine accumulate and react with starch to give the characteristic blue-black colour. The original version was described by Hans Heinrich Landolt in 1886, and the reaction is often called the Landolt clock.

Other clock reactions

Other examples include the formaldehyde clock, the chlorite–iodide clock, and "Old Nassau," a Halloween-themed variant in which the solution turns orange and then black in succession. Clock reactions are closely related to autocatalysis and to oscillating reactions such as the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, in which the colour change repeats periodically rather than occurring once.
