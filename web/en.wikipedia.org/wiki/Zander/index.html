Zander

Zander (Sander lucioperca)
FamilyPercidae (perch family)
Native rangeFresh and brackish waters of Eurasia, from the Elbe and Baltic drainages to the Caspian and Aral basins
SizeCommonly 40–70 cm; can exceed 100 cm and 15 kg
DietPiscivorous — smaller fish
ConservationLeast Concern (IUCN)

The zander (Sander lucioperca) is a species of predatory freshwater fish in the perch family, Percidae. It is the largest member of the family in Europe and is closely related to the North American walleye (Sander vitreus). The name "zander" derives from the German Zander; the fish is also called pike-perch for its elongated, pike-like body combined with the spiny dorsal fin of a perch.

Description

Zander have a long, streamlined body, greenish-grey to brown coloration with dark vertical markings in juveniles, and two separate dorsal fins, the first spiny. Their eyes are adapted for low-light hunting, with a reflective layer (tapetum lucidum) like that of the walleye, and they possess prominent canine teeth for seizing prey fish such as roach, bleak, and young perch.

Distribution and habitat

Native to lakes and large, slow rivers across central, eastern, and northern Europe and western Asia, zander favour deep, turbid waters. They have been widely introduced beyond their native range — including to England (from Woburn Abbey stockings in the 1870s and 1960s), Spain, and Anatolia — where they are valued but sometimes controversial for their impact on native fish.

Fishing and cuisine

Zander are prized both as a sporting fish and as a food fish; their firm, white, delicately flavoured flesh with few bones makes them one of the most valued freshwater table fish in Europe. Commercial fisheries operate in the Baltic region and the great lakes of eastern Europe, and the species is increasingly farmed in recirculating aquaculture systems.
