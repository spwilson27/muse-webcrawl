Marine reserve

A marine reserve is a type of marine protected area in which all extractive activities — fishing, mining, drilling, and harvesting — are prohibited by law. Also called no-take zones or ecological reserves, marine reserves are the most strongly protected category of ocean area and are used both to conserve marine biodiversity and to support fisheries through spillover of fish and larvae into surrounding waters.

Effects

Scientific studies of well-enforced reserves consistently find that fish inside them become more numerous, larger, and more diverse than in comparable fished areas, often within a few years. A landmark 2017 synthesis of 124 reserves found average biomass of fish assemblages roughly 670% greater in fully protected areas than in nearby unprotected ones. These changes arise because animals survive to older ages and larger sizes, producing disproportionately more offspring. "Spillover" of adults and export of larvae can benefit adjacent fisheries, though the magnitude of fishery benefits is debated and depends on reserve size, placement, and enforcement.

History and examples

New Zealand created one of the world's first no-take marine reserves at Cape Rodney–Okakari Point (the Leigh Marine Reserve) in 1975. Notable examples include the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park zoning in Australia, the Papahānaumokuākea and Ross Sea protected areas (among the largest on Earth), California's network of marine protected areas, and the Leigh and Poor Knights Islands reserves in New Zealand. Globally, fully or highly protected reserves cover only a small fraction of the ocean — around 3% — compared with about 8% in all marine protected areas combined.

Design and debate

Conservation scientists generally recommend that reserves be no-take, well enforced, old (more than ten years), large, and isolated by deep water or sand — conditions associated with the strongest ecological effects. Establishing reserves can be controversial with fishing communities because of short-term loss of fishing grounds; successful reserves often involve local communities in design and management.
