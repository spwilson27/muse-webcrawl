Spherical harmonics

In mathematics and physical science, spherical harmonics are special functions defined on the surface of a sphere. They form a complete, orthonormal set of functions and are the angular portion of a set of solutions to Laplace's equation separated in spherical coordinates. Spherical harmonics are denoted Yℓm(θ, φ), where ℓ = 0, 1, 2, … is the degree and m, with −ℓ ≤ m ≤ ℓ, is the order; θ is the polar (colatitudinal) angle and φ the azimuthal angle.

Definition

The spherical harmonics can be written in terms of the associated Legendre polynomials Pℓm(cos θ):

Yℓm(θ, φ) = N eimφ Pℓm(cos θ),

where N is a normalisation constant chosen so that the functions are orthonormal over the unit sphere. Any reasonably behaved function on the sphere can be expanded as a series of spherical harmonics, in analogy with a Fourier series on a circle.

Applications

Spherical harmonics are ubiquitous in physics and engineering. They describe the angular dependence of atomic orbitals in quantum mechanics (the s, p, d and f orbitals correspond to ℓ = 0, 1, 2, 3), solutions of the Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom, expansions of gravitational and magnetic fields (for example in geomagnetism and geodesy), multipole expansions in electromagnetism, and the analysis of the cosmic microwave background. In computer graphics, low-order spherical harmonics are used to represent diffuse lighting environments.

History

Spherical harmonics were first investigated by Pierre-Simon Laplace in the 1780s in connection with gravitational potentials, building on Adrien-Marie Legendre's work on the associated polynomials. They have since become a standard tool wherever problems with spherical symmetry arise.
