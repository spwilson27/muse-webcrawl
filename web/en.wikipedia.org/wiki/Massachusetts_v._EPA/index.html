Massachusetts v. EPA

Massachusetts v. Environmental Protection Agency, 549 U.S. 497 (2007), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court in which the Court held that carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases are "air pollutants" under the Clean Air Act, and that the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has authority — and, on proper findings, a duty — to regulate them. The case is regarded as the most important environmental decision in the Court's history.

Background

In 1999, environmental groups petitioned the EPA to regulate greenhouse gas emissions from new motor vehicles under section 202 of the Clean Air Act. In 2003, under the George W. Bush administration, the EPA denied the petition, arguing that it lacked authority to regulate greenhouse gases and that, in any event, regulation would be unwise. Massachusetts, eleven other states, several cities, and environmental organisations sued.

Decision

Writing for a 5–4 majority, Justice John Paul Stevens held, first, that Massachusetts had standing to sue: states are entitled to "special solicitude" in standing analysis, and Massachusetts faced a concrete injury from sea-level rise eroding its coastal land. Second, the Court held that greenhouse gases fit the Clean Air Act's "capacious" definition of air pollutant. Third, the EPA's stated reasons for refusing to regulate — concern about piecemeal approaches and foreign policy — were not grounded in the statute; if the EPA wished to decline regulation, it had to base its decision on whether greenhouse gases endanger public health or welfare.

Consequences

In December 2009, following the ruling, the EPA issued an "endangerment finding" that greenhouse gases endanger public health and welfare, paving the way for vehicle emissions standards and later regulation of power plants, including the Clean Power Plan. Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Scalia filed dissents disputing standing and the statutory interpretation, respectively. The case remains the foundational legal authority for federal climate regulation in the United States.
