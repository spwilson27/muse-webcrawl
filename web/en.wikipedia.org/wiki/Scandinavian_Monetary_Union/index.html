Scandinavian Monetary Union

MembersSweden, Denmark, Norway
Established1873 (Sweden–Denmark); Norway joined 1875
CurrencyKrone/krona on the gold standard
DissolvedDe facto 1914; formally wound up by 1924

The Scandinavian Monetary Union (Danish/Norwegian: Skandinaviske møntunion; Swedish: Skandinaviska myntunionen) was a monetary union formed by Sweden and Denmark in 1873, joined by Norway in 1875, that created a common currency unit — the krone — based on the gold standard. Coins and later banknotes of the member states circulated at par throughout the union.

Formation

The union grew out of the pan-Scandinavian movement and practical concerns about the fragmented silver-based currencies of the region. Under the 1873 convention, the krone was defined as 1/2480 of a kilogram of pure gold, subdivided into 100 øre. Gold coins of the three kingdoms were struck to a common standard and accepted as legal tender across borders; from 1894, Norwegian and Swedish central bank notes also circulated in each other's territory, making the union function much like a full currency area.

Dissolution

The outbreak of the First World War in 1914 broke the union: gold convertibility was suspended, Sweden prohibited gold exports, and the currencies began to trade at divergent rates, with the Swedish krona appreciating strongly. Attempts to restore parity after the war failed, and by 1924 the union was effectively dead, though all three countries retain currencies named krone/krona to this day. The union is remembered as one of the more successful historical currency unions between sovereign states.
