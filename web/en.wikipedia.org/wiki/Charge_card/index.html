A charge card is a type of credit card that requires the cardholder to pay the balance in full upon receipt of the statement, usually every month. Unlike a revolving credit card, a charge card does not permit a balance to be carried over from month to month, so no interest is charged (late fees and penalties apply instead). Charge cards traditionally have no preset spending limit, with purchasing power adjusted to the cardholder's usage and payment history.

History

Charge cards trace their origin to early 20th-century department-store and oil-company cards, then to the Diners Club card of 1950 — the first multipurpose charge card — followed by American Express (1958) and Carte Blanche. "Travel and entertainment" (T&E) cards were aimed at business travellers and often carried annual fees in exchange for services and rewards.

Differences from credit cards

Because balances cannot revolve, issuers earn revenue from annual fees and merchant discount fees rather than interest. Charge cards may not report a credit limit to credit bureaus, which can affect utilization calculations differently from credit cards. Many modern American Express products blur the distinction with "pay over time" features.
