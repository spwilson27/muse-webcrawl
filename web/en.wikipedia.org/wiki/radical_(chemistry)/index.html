Radical (chemistry)

TypeReactive chemical species
FeatureUnpaired valence electron
ExamplesHydroxyl radical (•OH), methyl radical (•CH₃)

In chemistry, a radical, also known as a free radical, is an atom, molecule, or ion that has at least one unpaired valence electron. With some exceptions, these unpaired electrons make radicals highly chemically reactive.

Formation

Radicals form by homolytic bond cleavage, in which a covalent bond breaks symmetrically and each fragment keeps one electron. Heat, ultraviolet light, and peroxides promote radical initiation, leading to chain reactions such as radical polymerization and combustion.

Stability

Most radicals are short-lived, but resonance delocalization, steric shielding, and captodative effects can stabilize some: triphenylmethyl, discovered by Moses Gomberg in 1900, was the first radical identified, and molecular oxygen is itself a stable diradical.

Biological role

Free radicals generated by metabolism can damage DNA, proteins, and lipids, contributing to aging and disease; antioxidants such as glutathione and vitamin E scavenge them. Radicals are also essential intermediates in synthesis and atmospheric chemistry — see organic chemistry.
