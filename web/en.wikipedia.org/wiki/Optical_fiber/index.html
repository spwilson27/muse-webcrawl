Optical fiber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of a bundle of optical fibres glowing blue at their tips]

An optical fiber is a flexible glass or plastic strand constructed to guide light along its length by total internal reflection. Bundled into cables, optical fibres are the physical medium of the modern Internet: nearly all long-distance traffic — and increasingly the 'last mile' to homes — travels as pulses of light in glass.

The physics was demonstrated in the 19th century (light guided by water jets), but practical fibre communications required two breakthroughs: Charles Kao's 1966 insight that sufficiently pure glass would make fibre viable (recognised by a 2009 Nobel Prize), and Corning's low-loss fibre of 1970. A modern fibre is about 125 micrometres across including coating, with a core of ~9 micrometres for long-haul single-mode transmission; erbium-doped amplifiers boost the signal every 80 km or so without converting it to electricity.

Wavelength-division multiplexing — many colours of light, each an independent channel — multiplies capacity so that a single fibre pair can carry tens of terabits per second, and a submarine cable contains several pairs. The economics are transformative: once the glass is in the ground or on the seabed, capacity upgrades come from the electronics at the ends, which is why the world's bandwidth keeps compounding.
