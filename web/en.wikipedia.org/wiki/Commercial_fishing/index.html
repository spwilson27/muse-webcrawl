Commercial fishing

Commercial fishing is the activity of catching fish and other seafood for commercial profit, mostly from wild fisheries. It provides a large quantity of food to many countries around the world, but those who practise it as an industry must often pursue fish far into the ocean under adverse conditions. Large-scale commercial fishing is also known as industrial fishing.

Scale and methods

Global capture fisheries produce on the order of 90 million tonnes of fish annually, with China the largest producer; the most valuable and voluminous fisheries include anchoveta, Alaska pollock, skipjack tuna, and various squid and shrimp. Major methods include:

  • Trawling — dragging nets (bottom or midwater) behind vessels.
  • Purse seining — encircling schools of fish, common for tuna and sardines.
  • Longlining — kilometres of line with baited hooks, used for tuna, swordfish, and Patagonian toothfish.
  • Gillnetting, traps and pots — important for crab, lobster, and coastal fisheries.
  • Dredging — for scallops and other shellfish.

The workforce

Tens of millions of people work in capture fisheries and processing worldwide, the great majority in small-scale and artisanal operations in developing countries. Commercial fishing is among the most dangerous occupations, and labour abuses — including forced labour on distant-water vessels — are a documented concern.

Sustainability

According to the FAO, roughly a third of assessed fish stocks are fished at biologically unsustainable levels. Problems include overfishing, bycatch of non-target species, habitat damage from bottom trawling, illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing, and "ghost gear." Management tools include catch quotas and total allowable catches, individual transferable quotas, marine protected areas, gear restrictions, certification schemes such as the Marine Stewardship Council, and regional fisheries management organisations. Aquaculture surpassed capture fisheries as a source of aquatic animals for human consumption in the 2010s–2020s.
