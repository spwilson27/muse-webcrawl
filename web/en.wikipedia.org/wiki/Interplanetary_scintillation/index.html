Interplanetary scintillation

Interplanetary scintillation (IPS) refers to random fluctuations in the intensity of radio waves from a distant celestial source, on a time scale of a few seconds. It is analogous to the twinkling of stars in the night sky, but instead of atmospheric turbulence it is caused by irregularities in the solar wind as the radio waves propagate through interplanetary space.

Discovery

IPS was discovered in the early 1960s by a team at the Mullard Radio Astronomy Observatory in Cambridge led by Antony Hewish. While studying quasars, they found that compact radio sources showed rapid intensity variations. Investigating these fluctuations led directly to the construction of the Interplanetary Scintillation Array, with which Jocelyn Bell Burnell discovered the first pulsar in 1967.

Uses

Because the scintillation pattern depends on the speed and density of the solar wind, IPS observations are used to measure solar wind velocity, map its three-dimensional structure, and track coronal mass ejections travelling toward Earth, contributing to space weather forecasting. Sources that scintillate strongly must be very compact — smaller than about one arcsecond — so IPS also serves as a tool for identifying compact radio sources such as quasars.
