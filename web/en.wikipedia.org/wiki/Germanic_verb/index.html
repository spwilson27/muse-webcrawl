Germanic verb

Reconstructed Proto-Germanic verb paradigms

The Germanic verb system, inherited from Proto-Indo-European via Proto-Germanic, is characterised by the division of verbs into strong and weak classes. Strong verbs mark the past tense by ablaut (vowel alternation in the root), while weak verbs — a Germanic innovation — form the past with a dental suffix, the ancestor of English -ed.

Strong verbs

Strong verbs fall into seven classes according to the pattern of ablaut in their principal parts: infinitive, past singular, past plural and past participle. Examples surviving in English include sing–sang–sung (class III) and drive–drove–driven (class I). Over time many strong verbs have shifted to the weak conjugation by analogy.

Weak verbs

Weak verbs form their preterite with a dental suffix (Proto-Germanic *-dē or similar). They are grouped into several classes by their present-stem formation. Because the pattern is productive, new verbs entering Germanic languages are conjugated weakly.

Other categories

The system also includes preterite-present verbs, whose present tense has the form of an old strong preterite — these include the ancestors of modal auxiliaries such as can, shall and may — and a small group of irregular verbs including the verb "to be" and "to do". Germanic largely lost the Indo-European aspect system, retaining a two-tense (present/past) framework, and much of the old subjunctive survives as the optative mood.
