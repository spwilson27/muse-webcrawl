John Dryden

John Dryden (19 August [O.S. 9 August] 1631 – 12 May [O.S. 1 May] 1700) was an English poet, literary critic, translator, and playwright who was appointed England's first Poet Laureate in 1668. He dominated Restoration literature so thoroughly that the period is sometimes called the Age of Dryden, noted for his heroic couplets, satires, and translations of Virgil and other classics.

Life

Educated at Westminster School and Trinity College, Cambridge, Dryden worked for Cromwell's government, then welcomed the Restoration with Astraea Redux (1660). He wrote successful plays for the King's Company, married Lady Elizabeth Howard, and became Poet Laureate and historiographer royal. Converting to Catholicism under James II, he lost his offices after the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and supported himself by translation.

Works

Major poems include Annus Mirabilis (1667), the political satire Absalom and Achitophel (1681), Religio Laici (1682), The Hind and the Panther (1687), and Alexander's Feast (1697). His critical prose, including An Essay of Dramatic Poesy (1668), founded much English literary criticism. His translations of Virgil (1697) and Fables, Ancient and Modern (1700) were highly influential.

Legacy

Dryden refined the closed heroic couplet later perfected by Alexander Pope, and influenced satire, criticism, and translation for a century.
