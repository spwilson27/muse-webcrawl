Morphogenesis

Morphogenesis (from the Greek morphê, shape, and genesis, creation) is the biological process that causes a cell, tissue or organism to develop its shape. It is one of the three fundamental aspects of developmental biology, along with control of tissue growth and regulation of cell differentiation. Morphogenesis also describes the development of unicellular life forms and the formation of structures in plants.

Mechanisms

Morphogenesis arises from changes in cell behaviour: cells alter their shape, migrate, adhere to one another, proliferate, or die (apoptosis) in coordinated patterns. These behaviours are driven by gene regulatory networks and by signalling molecules called morphogens — substances such as retinoic acid, Sonic hedgehog, Wnt, and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) that form concentration gradients and specify cell fates in a dose-dependent manner. Physical forces, extracellular matrix mechanics, and cell–cell adhesion gradients also play central roles; differential adhesion can cause cell populations to sort and layers to fold, as in gastrulation and neural tube formation.

History

D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson's On Growth and Form (1917) emphasised physical forces in shaping organisms. Alan Turing's 1952 paper "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" proposed reaction–diffusion systems in which interacting chemicals spontaneously generate patterns such as spots and stripes — a mechanism since supported in contexts from animal coat markings to digit patterning. Conrad Waddington's concept of the epigenetic landscape framed development as canalised progression through stable states.

Examples

Classic examples include the formation of limbs in vertebrate embryos, branching morphogenesis of the lung and kidney, the folding of the neural plate into the neural tube, and the patterning of Drosophila segments by morphogen gradients of proteins such as Bicoid.
