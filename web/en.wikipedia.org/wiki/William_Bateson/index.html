William Bateson (8 August 1861 – 8 February 1926) was an English biologist who was the first person to use the term genetics to describe the study of heredity, and the chief populariser of the ideas of Gregor Mendel following their rediscovery in 1900 by Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak.

Bateson was educated at Rugby School and St John's College, Cambridge, where he studied zoology and made research trips to the United States and Central Asia to study variation in animals. In his 1894 book Materials for the Study of Variation he emphasised discontinuous variation in evolution. After 1900 he became Mendel's most energetic advocate in the English-speaking world, translating Mendel's paper into English and defending Mendelism against the biometric school of Karl Pearson and W. F. R. Weldon.

Career

Bateson worked at Cambridge for much of his career and in 1910 became the first director of the John Innes Horticultural Institution at Merton, which under him became a leading centre for genetic research. His collaborators and students included Reginald Punnett, co-developer of the Punnett square, and Edith Rebecca Saunders.

Mendelism and genetics

In a 1905 letter to Adam Sedgwick, Bateson proposed the word "genetics" for the new science of heredity and variation; the term was publicly aired at the Third International Conference on Plant Hybridisation in London in 1906. His book Mendel's Principles of Heredity (1902, expanded 1909) systematised the Mendelian rules. Together with Punnett he discovered genetic linkage (coupling) in sweet peas, though he initially resisted Thomas Hunt Morgan's chromosomal interpretation of the phenomenon.

Later work

Bateson remained sceptical of the chromosome theory for many years and proposed his own "vibratory" model of heredity. He nonetheless built the institutional foundations of British genetics and helped found the Genetical Society in 1919.

Legacy

The Bateson Prize lectureship at the John Innes Centre and the term "epistasis", which he coined, commemorate his influence. His son was the anthropologist Gregory Bateson.
