Space food

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Space food
Early formsTubes (USSR), squeeze tubes and cubes (Mercury/Gemini)
ModernThermo-stabilised, freeze-dried and fresh-supply items
FacilitiesFood labs at Johnson Space Center and Star City
Dinner aboard the ISS.

Space food is the specially produced and packaged food flown for consumption in spaceflight. Its history tracks the programmes: Yuri Gagarin ate from tubes (beef and chocolate pastes) in 1961; Mercury astronauts endured cubes and tubes they despised; Gemini added freeze-dried items rehydrated with a water gun; Apollo got 'spoon-bowl' packs and, on Apollo 8, a famous Christmas dinner of thermostabilised turkey and gravy. The Space Shuttle brought a galley and near-normal trays; the International Space Station runs a multinational menu on roughly nine-day cycles, with bonus containers of crew-chosen items.

The constraints are unglamorous: crumbs become floating hazards (hence tortillas instead of bread), salt and spices drift (hence liquid salt and pepper), taste dulls in fluid-shifted heads (hence crews' famous cravings for hot sauce), and everything must survive months at ambient temperature with mass and trash minimised.

Signature items

Tang — a commercial drink mix flown on Gemini — became so associated with spaceflight its makers advertised on it for decades (much to Buzz Aldrin's later, colourful annoyance). The corned-beef sandwich John Young smuggled aboard Gemini 3 in 1965 triggered a congressional inquiry and, indirectly, crumb-control rules ever since. Fresh food — fruit, vegetables, the chilies and lettuces grown aboard the ISS itself — is the modern morale frontier.
