In mathematics, a Padé approximant is the "best" approximation of a function near a point by a rational function of given order. Under this technique, the approximant's power series agrees with the power series of the function it is approximating. The technique was developed around 1890 by the French mathematician Henri Padé, but goes back to the German mathematician Georg Frobenius, who introduced the idea and investigated the features of rational approximations of power series.

Definition

Given a function f and two integers m ≥ 0 and n ≥ 1, a Padé approximant of order [m/n] is the rational function

R(x) = P(x)/Q(x), with deg P ≤ m and deg Q ≤ n,

whose Taylor expansion matches that of f to the highest possible order — namely through the term xm+n. Equivalently, f(x) − R(x) = O(xm+n+1). Arranging the approximants of all orders in a table gives the Padé table; the diagonal and near-diagonal approximants (m = n or m = n ± 1) are used most often.

Properties and applications

Because a rational function can reproduce poles, Padé approximants frequently give far better approximations than truncated Taylor series of the same information content, often converging well outside the Taylor series' radius of convergence and providing a form of analytic continuation. They are intimately connected with continued fractions (the C-fraction expansions) and with the epsilon algorithm used to accelerate convergence of sequences. Drawbacks include possible spurious poles and Froissart doublets (close pole–zero pairs) when the input series is noisy.

Padé approximants are used throughout applied mathematics and physics: summming divergent perturbation series in quantum mechanics and quantum field theory (including the long-distance behavior of QCD correlators), computing special functions in numerical software, model-order reduction of dynamical systems, signal processing, and extrapolation of sequences in statistical mechanics and critical-phenomena research.

Examples

The [3/2] approximant of ex is (1 + (3/5)x + (3/20)x² + (1/60)x³) / (1 − (2/5)x + (1/20)x²), which matches the exponential series through x5 and stays accurate far beyond the range of a five-term Taylor polynomial. Similarly, rational approximants to sin x, log(1 + x) and the gamma and error functions appear in classical references and computer-algebra systems.
