Undersampling

In signal processing, undersampling (also called bandpass sampling) is a technique whereby a bandpass-filtered signal is sampled at a sample rate below the Nyquist rate for its highest frequency component, yet the original signal can still be recovered without aliasing distortion — provided the signal's bandwidth, rather than its absolute frequency range, satisfies the sampling criterion.

Principle

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem requires a sample rate of at least twice the highest frequency only for baseband signals extending from zero frequency. A bandpass signal occupying a narrow band between frequencies fL and fH has bandwidth B = fH − fL; it can be sampled at rates a little above 2B if the sample rate is chosen so that the spectral copies produced by sampling do not overlap. Undersampling then has the effect of mixing the band down to baseband (or an intermediate frequency), a property exploited for direct digital downconversion.

Applications

Undersampling is widely used in software-defined radio and digital receivers, where an intermediate-frequency or even radio-frequency signal is digitised directly without an analogue mixer stage. It also appears in oscilloscopes (equivalent-time sampling of repetitive waveforms), in stroboscopic measurement, and in undersampling-based analogue-to-digital conversion for communications. The technique requires a sharp anti-alias bandpass filter before the sampler, low sampling jitter (aperture uncertainty degrades performance at high input frequencies), and careful selection of the sampling frequency to position the aliased band in a clear region.

Contrast with accidental undersampling

Sampling a signal too slowly by accident produces aliasing, in which high-frequency components masquerade as low frequencies (as in the wagon-wheel effect). Deliberate bandpass undersampling avoids this by exploiting prior knowledge that the intervening spectrum is empty, turning the aliasing process into a controlled frequency translation.
