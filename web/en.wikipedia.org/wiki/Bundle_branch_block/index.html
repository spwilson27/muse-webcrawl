A bundle branch block is a defect in the heart's electrical conduction system in which one of the two bundle branches — the pathways that carry impulses from the bundle of His to the right and left ventricles — fails to conduct properly. The affected ventricle is then activated indirectly through the heart muscle from the other side, which is slower, producing a widened QRS complex (120 milliseconds or more) with a characteristic shape on the electrocardiogram (ECG). Blocks are classified as right bundle branch block (RBBB) or left bundle branch block (LBBB), and may be complete or incomplete.

Anatomy and mechanism

Normally, the electrical impulse generated at the sinoatrial node travels through the atria to the atrioventricular node, then down the bundle of His, which divides into right and left bundle branches running through the interventricular septum, and finally into Purkinje fibres that rapidly activate both ventricles almost simultaneously. If one branch is blocked, the impulse descends the intact branch and spreads cell-to-cell across the septum to the blocked side, delaying and desynchronising ventricular contraction.

Right bundle branch block

RBBB produces an rSR' pattern ("M-shaped" complex) in lead V1 and a wide slurred S wave in lateral leads. It is relatively common and can occur in otherwise healthy people, but it may also reflect right ventricular strain, congenital heart disease (such as atrial septal defect), pulmonary embolism, or coronary disease. Isolated RBBB in an asymptomatic person often requires no treatment beyond evaluation.

Left bundle branch block

LBBB produces broad, notched or monophasic R waves in lateral leads and predominantly negative complexes in V1. It is less common in healthy people and more often signals underlying structural heart disease — hypertension, aortic stenosis, cardiomyopathy, or coronary artery disease. New LBBB with symptoms suggestive of heart attack is treated as an emergency, although modern guidelines rely on specific criteria (such as the Sgarbossa criteria) because LBBB itself obscures the usual ECG signs of myocardial infarction. LBBB also causes ventricular dyssynchrony that can worsen heart failure, which is treated in selected patients with cardiac resynchronisation therapy (a biventricular pacemaker).

Causes and significance

Causes include ischaemic heart disease, long-standing hypertension, valvular disease, cardiomyopathies, myocarditis, congenital abnormalities, and age-related degeneration of the conduction system (Lev's and Lenegre's diseases). Bundle branch blocks increase in prevalence with age and are associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular events, particularly when combined (bilateral or bifascicular blocks), which can progress to complete heart block.

Management

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause; there is no therapy that directly repairs a blocked bundle. Patients with syncope and bifascicular block may require a pacemaker, and those with heart failure and LBBB may benefit from cardiac resynchronisation therapy. Incidental asymptomatic single bundle branch block generally needs only periodic monitoring.
