The corvus (Latin for "crow" or "raven") was a Roman naval boarding device used in sea battles against Carthage during the First Punic War. It consisted of a hinged gangplank fitted with a heavy iron spike at its far end, mounted on a pole at the prow of a Roman warship so that it could be raised, swung and dropped onto an enemy deck.

Description

The historian Polybius describes the corvus in detail. A pole about 7.3 m (24 ft) tall stood at the prow; around it fitted a boarding bridge some 10.9 m (36 ft) long and 1.2 m (4 ft) wide with low rails on each side. A pulley and rope raised the bridge, and when an enemy ship came alongside it was dropped: the iron beak underneath its end bit into the enemy's deck, locking the two ships together. Roman marines — legionaries fighting at sea — then charged across the bridge two abreast, turning a naval battle into the kind of infantry combat at which Rome excelled.

Use and decline

The corvus was decisive in early Roman victories, above all at the Battle of Mylae (260 BC), Rome's first naval victory, and subsequently at Ecnomus (256 BC) and other engagements. It compensated for Carthage's superior seamanship. However, the device raised a ship's centre of gravity and caught the wind, making vessels less seaworthy; catastrophic storm losses in 255 and 249 BC are sometimes partly blamed on it. By the later stages of the war the Romans, having developed an experienced navy, appear to have abandoned the corvus, relying instead on speed, seamanship and the grapnel.
