The Black–Scholes model is a mathematical model for the dynamics of a financial market containing derivative investment instruments, most famously used to price European-style options. First published by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes in their 1973 paper "The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities," and extended by Robert C. Merton, the model yields a closed-form formula for the price of a call or put option based on the underlying asset's price, the strike price, time to expiry, the risk-free rate, and the asset's volatility.

Assumptions

  • The underlying asset price follows geometric Brownian motion with constant drift and volatility.
  • There are no transaction costs or taxes, and trading is continuous.
  • The risk-free interest rate is constant and known.
  • There are no arbitrage opportunities.
  • The underlying pays no dividends (in the original version; later versions incorporate them).
  • Options are European, exercisable only at expiry.

The formula

For a call option, the price is C = S·N(d₁) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂), where S is the spot price, K the strike, T the time to expiry, r the risk-free rate, and N(·) the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The terms d₁ and d₂ depend on the log-moneyness of the option and on volatility σ. The corresponding partial differential equation is the Black–Scholes equation.

Use and limitations

The model transformed options trading after the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened in 1973 and remains the market's standard language: traders quote options in terms of the model's implied volatility. Its limitations are well documented — real markets exhibit volatility smiles and skews, fat-tailed returns, discrete trading, and jumping prices — spawning extensions such as local-volatility and stochastic-volatility models. Scholes and Merton shared the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for the work (Black had died in 1995).
