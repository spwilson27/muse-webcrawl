SpecialtyCardiology
DefinitionResting heart rate below 60 beats per minute
SymptomsOften none; fatigue, dizziness, syncope, breathlessness

Bradycardia, also called bradyarrhythmia when it results from an abnormal rhythm, is a slow resting heart rate, conventionally defined in adults as fewer than 60 beats per minute. It is the opposite of tachycardia. A slow rate is not necessarily pathological: trained athletes commonly have resting rates in the 40s or lower, and heart rate normally falls during sleep. Bradycardia becomes a clinical problem when it causes inadequate cardiac output with symptoms such as fatigue, dizziness, fainting (syncope), chest pain or heart failure.

Classification and causes

Bradycardias are usually classified by the level of the conduction problem. In sinus bradycardia the impulse originates normally in the sinoatrial node but at a slow rate; sinus node dysfunction (sick sinus syndrome) includes sinus pauses and chronotropic incompetence, and is common in older adults. In atrioventricular block, impulses are delayed or blocked between the atria and ventricles, ranging from first-degree block (prolonged PR interval) to complete heart block, in which the ventricles rely on a slow escape rhythm.

Extrinsic causes include beta blockers, calcium channel blockers and other antiarrhythmic drugs, hypothyroidism, hypothermia, raised intracranial pressure (part of Cushing's triad), electrolyte disturbance, and heightened vagal tone.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on the electrocardiogram and correlation of the rhythm with symptoms, often using ambulatory monitoring. Asymptomatic bradycardia usually requires no treatment. Symptomatic episodes are treated first with atropine; if unresponsive, temporary transcutaneous or transvenous pacing or catecholamine infusions are used while the cause is corrected, and recurrent or irreversible symptomatic bradycardia is an indication for a permanent pacemaker.
