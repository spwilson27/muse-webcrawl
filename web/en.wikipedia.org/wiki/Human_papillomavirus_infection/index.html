Human papillomavirus infection

SpecialtyInfectious disease, gynaecology, dermatology, oncology
SymptomsOften none; warts, including genital warts
CausesHuman papillomavirus, spread by close skin-to-skin contact
PreventionHPV vaccines, condoms

Human papillomavirus infection (HPV infection) is caused by a DNA virus from the Papillomaviridae family. Many HPV infections cause no symptoms and resolve spontaneously within one to two years as the immune system clears the virus. In some cases, however, infection with particular types persists and results either in warts or in precancerous lesions, which can progress to cancer of the cervix, anus, penis, vagina, vulva, or oropharynx.

More than 200 types of HPV are recognized. They are classified as low-risk types, which mainly cause warts — for example types 6 and 11, responsible for most genital warts — and high-risk types, including 16 and 18, which together cause about 70 percent of cervical cancers. HPV is the most common sexually transmitted infection worldwide, and most sexually active people acquire it at some point.

Signs and symptoms

Most infections are subclinical. Cutaneous types cause common warts on the hands and feet, including plantar warts. Genital infection may cause genital warts (condylomata acuminata). Persistent high-risk infection of the cervix can cause cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, detectable by cervical screening (Pap smear or HPV testing), which may develop into cervical cancer over years if untreated.

Transmission and prevention

HPV spreads by direct skin-to-skin and skin-to-mucosa contact, most commonly sexual contact. Barrier contraception reduces but does not eliminate transmission. Prophylactic vaccines — Gardasil, Gardasil 9, and Cervarix — protect against the most important oncogenic and wart-causing types and are recommended in adolescence before exposure. Vaccination programmes, as in Australia, have produced dramatic declines in genital warts and cervical precancer, with evidence of herd protection.

Diagnosis and treatment

There is no treatment that eradicates the virus itself; management targets the lesions it causes. Warts may be treated with topical agents, cryotherapy, or surgical removal. Cervical precancer is treated by excision or ablation, and cancers are managed according to type and stage with surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy.
