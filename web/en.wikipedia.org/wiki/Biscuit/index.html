Biscuit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A plate of assorted biscuits — digestives, shortbread and custard creams — beside a cup of tea]
A plate of assorted biscuits — digestives, shortbread and custard creams — beside a cup of tea

A biscuit is a flour-based baked food product. The word means different things in different Englishes: in British and Commonwealth usage, a biscuit is a small, crisp, often sweet baked good (called a 'cookie' in North America); in American usage, a biscuit is a soft, leavened quick bread similar to a plain Scone. The name derives from the Latin bis coctus, 'twice cooked', for the hard ship's biscuit that kept sailors (and armies) alive for months.

British biscuit

Britain industrialised the sweet biscuit in the nineteenth century — Huntley & Palmers of Reading was the world's largest biscuit factory — producing the canonical tea-dunking repertoire: digestives (1839), rich tea, bourbons, custard creams, Jammie Dodgers, Jaffa Cakes (whose biscuit-or-cake status was litigated for VAT purposes in 1991 — ruled a cake) and the chocolate digestive, regularly voted the nation's favourite. Dunking science (and its public feuds) is a small British cultural institution.

American biscuit

The US biscuit is a quick bread leavened with baking powder or soda, made flaky by cutting cold fat into flour; buttermilk biscuits anchor Southern cooking — biscuits and gravy, the base of chicken and dumplings, and the fast-food breakfast sandwich. Beaten biscuits of Kentucky, cathead biscuits and drop biscuits are regional forms, descended from the same hardtack lineage via soda chemistry.
