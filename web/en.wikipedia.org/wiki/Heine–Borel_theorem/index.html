Heine–Borel theorem

In mathematical analysis, the Heine–Borel theorem, named after Eduard Heine and Émile Borel, states that for a subset S of Euclidean space ℝn, the following two statements are equivalent:

  • S is closed and bounded; and
  • S is compact, that is, every open cover of S has a finite subcover.

In the particular case of the real line, the theorem says that a subset of the reals is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded—for example, every closed interval [a, b] is compact. The theorem is fundamental because compactness guarantees, among other things, that a continuous real-valued function on such a set attains its maximum and minimum (the extreme value theorem) and is uniformly continuous.

History

The history of what today is called the Heine–Borel theorem starts in the 19th century, with the search for solid foundations of real analysis. Statements equivalent to the theorem for intervals were known in connection with Heine's work on uniform continuity (1872) and with ideas of Pincherle and others. Émile Borel stated the theorem for countable covers of a closed interval in his 1895 thesis; the extension to arbitrary open covers and to higher dimensions was developed by Pierre Cousin, Lebesgue and Schoenflies around 1895–1904. In general metric spaces, closed and bounded is necessary but not sufficient for compactness (as the unit ball of an infinite-dimensional space shows), and the full equivalence characterises finite-dimensional normed spaces; the abstract definition of compactness that generalises the theorem was given by Fréchet and formalised in Bourbaki.
