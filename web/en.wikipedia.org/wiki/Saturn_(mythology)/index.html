Saturn (mythology)

Saturn (Latin: Sāturnus) was a god in ancient Roman religion, presiding over agriculture, sowing and harvest, wealth, and the passage of time. He was identified with the Greek god Cronus and was often depicted as an old man with a sickle or scythe. In Roman myth, Saturn ruled the world during a mythical Golden Age of abundance and equality, and his festival, the Saturnalia, was the most popular holiday of the Roman year.

Mythology

According to the Roman telling, Saturn, having been driven from the heavens by his son Jupiter, fled to Latium in Italy, where he was received by the god Janus and taught the people agriculture and civilisation. His reign was remembered as a Golden Age in which the earth gave freely, no one laboured, and there were no slaves or private property. The hill later occupied by Rome's Capitol was said to have been called the Saturnian Hill, and the Temple of Saturn at the foot of the Capitoline housed the state treasury (aerarium).

Cult and festival

Saturn's temple in the Forum, among the oldest in Rome, stood on the Clivus Capitolinus. His statue was bound with woolen bands that were loosed only during the Saturnalia, held each December. During the festival normal order was inverted: slaves dined with (or were waited on by) their masters, gifts were exchanged, gambling was permitted, and a "king of the Saturnalia" presided over the merriment — customs often seen as forerunners of Christmas and Carnival traditions. The planet Saturn, visible to the naked eye and the slowest-moving of the classical planets, bears his name, as does Saturday (Latin dies Saturni).
