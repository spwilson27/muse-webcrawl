Democratic backsliding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Democratic backsliding is the process by which a democratic political system weakens or loses its democratic qualities, typically gradually and often through legal means, rather than by a sudden coup. It is also called democratic erosion, de-democratisation or autocratisation. Backsliding governments characteristically capture courts and electoral bodies, pressure or buy out independent media, tilt electoral rules, harass civil society and opposition, and concentrate power in the executive — while continuing to hold elections, which become progressively less fair.

The term gained prominence in the 2010s as analysts described developments in countries including Hungary, Venezuela, Turkey, Poland (2015–2023) and others; classification of particular countries is contested by their governments and some scholars. Organisations such as Freedom House and the V-Dem Institute have reported more countries autocratising than democratising in recent years — a “third wave of autocratisation” in V-Dem's phrase.

Causes and remedies

Explanations emphasise polarisation (which makes voters tolerate undemocratic behaviour by their own side), weak institutional guardrails and informal norms of tolerance and forbearance (Levitsky and Ziblatt's How Democracies Die, 2018), economic insecurity, and disinformation. Proposed remedies include cross-party democratic coalitions, court and electoral-commission independence, media pluralism and international conditionality; backsliding has sometimes been reversed at the ballot box, as in Poland in 2023.
