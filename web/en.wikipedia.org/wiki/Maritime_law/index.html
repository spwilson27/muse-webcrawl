Maritime law

Illustration: Modern shipping governed by maritime law (placeholder image)

Maritime law, also known as admiralty law, is the body of law governing nautical issues and private maritime disputes, including shipping, navigation, commerce by sea, salvage, marine insurance, and the carriage of goods and passengers by water. It is distinct from the law of the sea, which is public international law governing states' rights and duties in maritime zones, though the two fields overlap in practice.

History

Maritime law is among the oldest bodies of law: customs of the sea were codified in the ancient Rhodian Sea Law, in medieval collections such as the Rolls of Oléron, the Consolato del Mare of Barcelona and the laws of Wisby, and were administered by specialised admiralty courts. In England, the High Court of Admiralty exercised jurisdiction for centuries, while in the United States admiralty is assigned to the federal courts. Characteristic doctrines include general average (shared sacrifice for the common safety, traced to Rhodian law), salvage awards, maritime liens, limitation of shipowners' liability, and the treatment of the ship itself as a juridical person liable in rem.

Modern framework

Much of modern maritime law is harmonised by international conventions negotiated under bodies such as the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and the Comité Maritime International (founded 1897), including the Hague-Visby Rules on bills of lading, the SOLAS convention on safety of life at sea, the MARPOL convention on pollution, conventions on collision and salvage (1989), and the Arrest Conventions allowing ships to be detained to secure claims. Seafarers' rights are addressed by the Maritime Labour Convention (2006). National systems still govern areas such as cabotage, personal injury (e.g. the U.S. Jones Act), and registration under national or "flag of convenience" registries such as Panama, Liberia and the Marshall Islands.

Scope

Practitioners divide the field into "wet" work (casualties, collision, salvage, pollution) and "dry" work (charterparties, bills of lading, ship sale and finance, marine insurance at markets such as Lloyd's of London). Arbitration, particularly in London, resolves a large share of international shipping disputes.
