Operation Tempest (Polish: akcja "Burza", sometimes called Operation Storm) was a series of anti-Nazi uprisings conducted during World War II by the Polish Home Army (Armia Krajowa, AK), the dominant force in the Polish resistance movement in German-occupied Poland. The operation's aims were twofold: to harass the retreating German army, and to liberate Polish territory before the advancing Red Army so that representatives of the Polish government-in-exile could take power as hosts rather than supplicants.

The plan

Approved by the Polish government in London in late 1943, Tempest envisaged local AK units surfacing in sequence as the Eastern Front moved west across Poland, attacking German rear guards, protecting infrastructure, and establishing civil administration loyal to the London government. The plan assumed tactical cooperation with the Red Army but political independence — an assumption that proved tragically optimistic given Stalin's intention to install a communist government in Poland.

Course of the operation

Tempest began in Volhynia in January 1944, where the AK's 27th Infantry Division fought alongside Soviet forces. In July, AK units helped liberate Wilno (Operation Ostra Brama) and Lwów; in both cities the Soviet NKVD then arrested the Polish commanders and disarmed or conscripted their men. The largest single action of Tempest was the Warsaw Uprising (1 August – 2 October 1944), which the Red Army did not support; it ended with the destruction of the capital's AK force and the systematic demolition of Warsaw by the Germans.

Legacy

Operation Tempest demonstrated the Home Army's strength and the legitimacy of the London government, but it could not prevent the communist takeover of Poland. Its veterans were persecuted in the post-war years, and the operation remained a suppressed memory in communist Poland; since 1989 it has been commemorated as a symbol of Polish resistance to both totalitarian occupiers.
