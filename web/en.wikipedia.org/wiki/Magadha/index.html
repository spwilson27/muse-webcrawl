Magadha

RegionSouth Bihar, middle Ganges plain, India
CapitalsRajgir (Rajagriha), later Pataliputra
Periodc. 6th century BCE – 4th century CE (as imperial core)
Major dynastiesHaryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda, Maurya, Gupta

Magadha was an ancient Indian kingdom and cultural region in the fertile middle Ganges valley, corresponding roughly to southern Bihar. From the 6th century BCE it rose to dominate northern India, and its successive dynasties built the subcontinent's first great empires. Magadha was also the cradle of both Buddhism and Jainism: the Buddha attained enlightenment at Bodh Gaya within its territory and spent much of his teaching career there, as did Mahavira, the 24th Jain tirthankara.

Rise to power

Magadha's advantages were agricultural and strategic: rich alluvial rice land, access to timber and elephants from eastern forests, iron deposits to the south, and control of river trade on the Ganges. Under the Haryanka kings Bimbisara and Ajatashatru (5th century BCE), the kingdom expanded from its hill-girt capital Rajgir (Rajagriha) and shifted to the river port of Pataliputra — modern Patna — which remained India's imperial capital for centuries. The Shishunaga and then the wealthy, low-born Nanda dynasty followed, creating one of the largest armies in the ancient world.

Imperial heartland

Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the Nandas around 321 BCE and founded the Maurya Empire, which under Ashoka embraced nearly the whole subcontinent. After centuries of fragmentation, Magadha again became the seat of empire under the Gupta dynasty (4th–6th centuries CE), whose rule is often called India's classical age, and Pataliputra remained its capital. The region's learning was famed: Nalanda, the great Buddhist monastic university, flourished nearby under Gupta and later Pala patronage.
