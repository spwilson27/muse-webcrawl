The pronghorn (Antilocapra americana) is a species of even-toed ungulate mammal indigenous to interior western and central North America. Though not a true antelope, it is often called the American antelope, prong buck or pronghorn antelope. It is the only surviving member of the family Antilocapridae and the fastest land mammal in the Americas, able to sustain high speeds over long distances.

Description

Adults stand 81–104 cm at the shoulder and weigh 36–70 kg. The coat is reddish tan above and white below, with white bands on the neck and a distinctive white rump patch that can be flared to signal alarm. Both sexes bear horns — unique among horned animals, the pronghorn's horn consists of a bony core covered by a keratinous sheath that is shed and regrown annually, and the sheath is forked (pronged) in males. Pronghorns have very large eyes and excellent vision, an oversized windpipe, heart and lungs, and hollow guard hairs that insulate against temperature extremes.

Speed and evolution

The pronghorn can reach speeds of nearly 88 km/h and is exceeded in top speed only by the cheetah, which it far outlasts in endurance. Its speed is widely thought to be an evolutionary legacy of predation by the now-extinct American cheetah (Miracinonyx) — an example of an evolutionary "ghost" of predators past. Modern predators (coyotes, wolves, cougars, bobcats, golden eagles) take mainly fawns.

Behaviour and ecology

Pronghorns inhabit grasslands, sagebrush steppe and desert from southern Canada through the western United States to northern Mexico. They are mixed feeders, eating forbs, shrubs, cacti and grasses. Social organisation varies seasonally, from territorial bachelor males in summer to mixed winter herds. The rut occurs in late summer and autumn; females typically give birth to twins in late spring.

Conservation

Once numbering perhaps 35 million, pronghorns were reduced by market hunting and habitat loss to about 13,000 by 1924. Hunting regulation and habitat management under the North American Model of Wildlife Conservation restored the population to roughly one million. Some subspecies remain in trouble: the Sonoran pronghorn (A. a. sonoriensis) and peninsular pronghorn (A. a. peninsularis) are endangered, and migration corridors are increasingly fragmented by fences, roads and energy development.
