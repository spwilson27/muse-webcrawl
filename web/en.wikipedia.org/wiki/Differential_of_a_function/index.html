Differential of a function

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In calculus, the differential represents the principal part of the change in a function y = f(x) with respect to changes in the independent variable. The differential dy is defined as

dy = f′(x) dx,

where f′(x) is the derivative of f with respect to x, and dx is an additional real variable. The differential thus captures the linear approximation to the change in f: for a small increment Δx, Δy ≈ dy, with the error vanishing faster than Δx itself.

History

The notion of the differential was central to the original formulations of calculus by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Leibniz treated dx and dy as infinitesimal quantities — differences smaller than any finite number yet not zero. Although this picture was enormously fruitful, its logical status troubled critics such as Bishop Berkeley. Nineteenth-century analysis replaced infinitesimals with limits, and the differential was reinterpreted as a function of two variables, x and dx. In the twentieth century, Abraham Robinson's nonstandard analysis rehabilitated infinitesimals rigorously, while differential geometry generalised the concept into that of a differential form.

Differentials in several variables

For a function of several variables, z = f(x1, …, xn), the total differential is

dz = (∂f/∂x1) dx1 + … + (∂f/∂xn) dxn.

A function is called differentiable at a point when such a linear approximation exists with error little-o of the distance from the point. The total differential also underlies the multivariate chain rule and the technique of implicit differentiation.

Higher-order differentials

Second and higher differentials can be defined by iterating the operation. The second differential, d2y = f′′(x)(dx)2 (with dx held constant), appears in Taylor's theorem and in the analysis of maxima and minima. Care is needed because higher differentials are not invariant under change of variables in the same simple way as the first differential.

Applications

Differentials are used throughout science and engineering for error propagation, for deriving the equations of physics from variational principles, and for the linearisation of nonlinear systems. In economics they appear in marginal analysis; in geometry, their generalisation to differential forms underpins integration on manifolds via Stokes' theorem.
