Émile Bernard

Émile Henri Bernard (28 April 1868 – 16 April 1941) was a French Post-Impressionist painter and writer. Working in Pont-Aven, Brittany, alongside Paul Gauguin, Bernard developed the style known as Cloisonnism — flat areas of colour bounded by dark outlines — and, with Gauguin, the broader approach called Synthetism. His later career took him to Egypt and back toward a more classical, religious art.

Early career

Born in Lille, Bernard entered the Paris atelier of Fernand Cormon in 1884, where he met Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec and Louis Anquetin. Expelled in 1886, he travelled on foot through Brittany and settled in Pont-Aven, joining the artists' colony there.

Pont-Aven and Synthetism

In the summer of 1888 Bernard painted Breton Women in the Meadow, a landmark of Cloisonnism inspired by stained glass and Japanese prints. His discussions with Gauguin that season shaped Synthetism, though the two later quarrelled over priority for the style. Bernard also knew Vincent van Gogh, who admired his work and exchanged paintings and letters with him.

Later life

After travels in Italy, Bernard lived in Egypt from 1893 to 1904, marrying there and turning increasingly to religious subjects. He returned to France, founded the journal La Rénovation esthétique, and spent his later years defending a classical, tradition-based art. He died in Paris in 1941.
