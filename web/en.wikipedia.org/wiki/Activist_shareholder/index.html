Activist shareholder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An activist shareholder is an investor — typically a hedge fund, private equity firm or wealthy individual — who acquires a significant stake in a public company and uses that shareholding to press management and the board for changes, in order to increase the value of the shares. The practice is known as shareholder activism or activist investing.

Methods

Activists commonly demand board seats, replacement of executives, spin-offs or sales of divisions, return of cash through dividends or buybacks, cost reductions, or the sale of the whole company. Tactics range from private engagement and public letters to media campaigns and full proxy fights, in which the activist solicits other shareholders' votes to elect its own nominees at the annual meeting. In the United States, investors crossing ownership thresholds must disclose their stake and intentions on Schedule 13D filings, which often triggers a rise in the target's share price.

Notable activists

Well-known activist investors include Carl Icahn, Daniel Loeb (Third Point), Bill Ackman (Pershing Square), Nelson Peltz (Trian), Paul Singer (Elliott Management) and ValueAct Capital. Famous campaigns include Icahn's interventions at numerous firms, Elliott's campaign at AT&T, and Engine No. 1's 2021 success in winning board seats at ExxonMobil on a climate-focused platform — an example of activism driven partly by environmental, social and governance (ESG) concerns.

Debate

Supporters argue activism improves governance and unlocks value; critics contend it can encourage short-term financial engineering at the expense of long-term investment and employees. Empirical studies find mixed but often modestly positive average effects on target company performance.
