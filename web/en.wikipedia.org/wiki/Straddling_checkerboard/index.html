Straddling checkerboard

A straddling checkerboard is a device for converting plaintext letters into digits, giving a fractionated output in which the most frequent letters are represented by a single digit and the rest by two digits. It was widely used in twentieth-century manual ciphers, because it converts text into numbers suitable for subsequent transposition or addition of a key, and because its variable-length encoding frustrates simple frequency analysis.

Construction

The board is a grid of three rows. The top row, unnumbered, holds eight of the most common letters beneath eight of ten digits. Two further rows, headed by the two digits left blank above, each hold the remaining letters and characters. A letter in the top row enciphers as its column digit; a letter in a lower row enciphers as the row digit followed by the column digit.

0123456789
ETAONRIS
2BCDFGHJKLM
6PQUVWXYZ./

In this example, A enciphers as 3, but B is 20 and K is 27. Because the row labels "straddle" the arrangement, the decoder can always tell whether a digit begins a one- or two-digit group: digits 2 and 6 announce a pair, all others stand alone.

Use

The checkerboard formed the fractionation step of the VIC cipher used by Soviet spy Reino Häyhänen in the 1950s — considered one of the most complex hand ciphers ever used — and appears in various agent ciphers of the Second World War and Cold War, often combined with columnar transposition and lagged Fibonacci digit keys. Since frequent letters take one digit, messages expand only modestly, by roughly 30–40%.
