The Tunisian campaign (also known as the Battle of Tunisia) was a series of battles in Tunisia during the North African campaign of the Second World War, between Axis forces and Allied forces mainly the British. The Allies consisted of British Imperial Forces, including a Greek contingent, with American and French corps. The battle opened with initial success by the German and Italian forces but the massive supply and numerical superiority of the Allies led to the decisive defeat of the Axis. Over 250,000 German and Italian troops were taken as prisoners of war, including most of the Afrika Korps, sometimes compared in scale to the German capitulation at Stalingrad.

Background

Following Operation Torch, the Allied landings in Morocco and Algeria on 8 November 1942, and the British Eighth Army's advance westward after the Second Battle of El Alamein, Axis forces rushed reinforcements into Tunisia and established a bridgehead around Tunis and Bizerte. The Allied "Run for Tunis" in late 1942 failed in mud and determined Axis resistance, and the campaign settled into a winter stalemate.

Course of the campaign

In February 1943, Erwin Rommel's armoured forces struck the inexperienced American II Corps at the Battle of Kasserine Pass, inflicting a sharp defeat before Axis supply problems and Allied air power forced a withdrawal. Thereafter the Allies reorganised under General Harold Alexander's 18th Army Group, with George S. Patton taking command of II Corps. In March, Montgomery's Eighth Army breached the Mareth Line in southern Tunisia following a flanking march through the Tebaga Gap, while American forces advanced to Gafsa and Maknassy, threatening to cut the Axis position in two.

Through April 1943 the Allies ground forward against the contracting Axis perimeter (Operations Vulcan and Strike). Tunis fell on 7 May, Bizerte the same day, and organised resistance ended on 13 May 1943 with the surrender of General Giovanni Messe's Italian First Army.

Aftermath

The victory cleared North Africa of Axis forces and opened the way for the Allied invasion of Sicily (Operation Husky) in July 1943. The huge number of prisoners led the campaign's end to be nicknamed "Tunisgrad" in the press.
