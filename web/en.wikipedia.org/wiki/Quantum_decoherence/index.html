Quantum decoherence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of a quantum system becoming entangled with its environment, losing interference]
Decoherence: entanglement with the environment suppresses interference.

Quantum decoherence is the loss of quantum coherence: the process by which a quantum system's behaviour changes from one that can be explained by quantum mechanics to one explainable by classical mechanics, as phase relations between its states leak into correlations with its environment.

Developed from the 1970s (H. Dieter Zeh, 1970; Wojciech Zurek and others in the 1980s), decoherence theory explains why macroscopic objects never exhibit interference: a dust grain in sunlight decoheres in far less than a trillionth of a second through collisions with photons and air molecules, while carefully isolated atoms and photons can stay coherent for seconds or longer. Decoherence selects preferred 'pointer states' (robust under environmental monitoring), explains the disappearance of interference in the Double-slit experiment when paths are monitored, and is the central obstacle to Quantum computing. It does not by itself explain why a single outcome occurs — interpretations differ on that (Copenhagen interpretation, Many-worlds interpretation) — but it explains why outcomes look classical.

Evidence and implications

Experiments in cavity QED (Serge Haroche's group, 1996) watched a mesoscopic 'cat' state decohere in real time at the predicted rate, and matter-wave interferometry shows interference fading as molecules grow larger and hotter, exactly as decoherence predicts. Environment-induced selection ('einselection') also supplies, within many-worlds, the branching structure of worlds — decoherence defines when branches form — making the concept central to modern foundations whichever interpretation one prefers.
