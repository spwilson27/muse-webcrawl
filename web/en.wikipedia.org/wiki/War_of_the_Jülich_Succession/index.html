War of the Jülich Succession

The War of the Jülich Succession (1609–1614) was a conflict within the Holy Roman Empire over the succession to the United Duchies of Jülich-Cleves-Berg after the death of Duke John William in 1609 without direct heirs. Because the duchies occupied a strategically vital position on the Lower Rhine, the dispute drew in the Dutch Republic, Spain, France and the Emperor, and is often seen as a prelude to the Thirty Years' War.

The claimants

The principal claimants were John Sigismund, Elector of Brandenburg, married to Anna of Prussia, eldest daughter of Duke John William's eldest sister, and Wolfgang Wilhelm, Count Palatine of Neuburg, son of another sister. Initially the two claimants agreed, in the Treaty of Dortmund (1609), to rule the duchies jointly as the "possessing princes," defying an imperial order to sequester the territories.

Conflict

The alliance broke down as the two men fell out, partly over religion: John Sigismund converted to Calvinism and Wolfgang Wilhelm to Catholicism, each seeking powerful allies. Fighting followed, including the siege of Jülich in 1610 with Dutch and French involvement, and renewed hostilities in 1614 involving Spanish troops under Ambrogio Spinola and Dutch forces under Maurice of Nassau.

Settlement

The Treaty of Xanten (1614) provisionally partitioned the inheritance: Brandenburg received Cleves, Mark and Ravensberg, while Neuburg received Jülich and Berg. The partition, confirmed at Cleves in 1666, shaped the map of the Rhineland for centuries, giving Brandenburg-Prussia its first substantial territories in western Germany.
