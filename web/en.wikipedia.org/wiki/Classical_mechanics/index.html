Classical mechanics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that describes the motion of macroscopic objects — from projectiles and machine parts to astronomical bodies — under the influence of forces. It is one of the oldest branches of physics, systematised by Isaac Newton in the 17th century and reformulated in the 18th and 19th centuries by Lagrange, Hamilton, and others. Classical mechanics remains an excellent approximation for everyday scales: it fails only at very small scales (where quantum mechanics applies), at speeds near the speed of light (special relativity), and in very strong gravity (general relativity).

Newtonian mechanics

Newton's three laws of motion form the foundation: a body persists in its state of motion unless acted on by a force; force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma); and every action has an equal and opposite reaction. Together with the law of universal gravitation, published in the Principia Mathematica (1687), they unify terrestrial and celestial motion and underpin the concepts of momentum, energy, work, and angular momentum.

Analytical mechanics

Lagrangian mechanics (1788) reformulates the theory using the principle of least action and generalised coordinates, applying a scalar function — kinetic minus potential energy. Hamiltonian mechanics (1833) recasts it in terms of positions and momenta in phase space and reveals deep connections to symmetry and conservation laws, later formalised in Noether's theorem. These formulations are indispensable in advanced dynamics, control theory, and as the bridge to quantum mechanics.

Branches and applications

The field includes statics, kinematics, dynamics, fluid mechanics, continuum mechanics, and celestial mechanics. Its applications span engineering design, ballistics, orbital mechanics and spaceflight, robotics, and biomechanics. Chaos theory grew out of the classical three-body problem, showing that even deterministic classical systems can be unpredictably sensitive to initial conditions.

Limits

Classical mechanics is the limiting case of both relativity and quantum mechanics. Its deterministic worldview dominated physics until the early 20th century and still provides the working framework for most engineering.
