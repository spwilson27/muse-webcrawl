The Portuguese Empire was a global maritime empire built by the Kingdom of Portugal from the fifteenth century onward, widely regarded as the first global empire in history and one of the longest-lived, lasting from the capture of Ceuta in 1415 until the handover of Macau to China in 1999. At its height it comprised territories in South America (Brazil), Africa (Angola, Mozambique, Guinea, Cape Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe), and Asia (Goa, Daman and Diu, Malacca, Macau, East Timor, and trading posts from Hormuz to Nagasaki). Its legacy includes the Portuguese language, today spoken by more than 250 million people, and the Community of Portuguese Language Countries (CPLP).

Origins (1415–1498)

Portugal, united under the Avis dynasty, pioneered Atlantic exploration driven by the search for gold, spices, slaves, and a sea route to India, as well as crusading ambition and rivalry with Castile. The capture of Ceuta in North Africa (1415), the settlement of Madeira and the Azores, and progressively longer voyages down the African coast — supported by Prince Henry the Navigator — culminated in Bartolomeu Dias rounding the Cape of Good Hope in 1488. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the non-Christian world between Portugal and Spain along a meridian in the Atlantic.

The Asian trading empire (1498–1580)

Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498, opening the Cape Route. Under viceroys such as Francisco de Almeida and Afonso de Albuquerque, Portugal seized a chain of fortified ports — Goa (1510), Malacca (1511), Hormuz (1515) — creating the Estado da Índia, a thalassocracy that levied protection fees (the cartaz system) on Indian Ocean shipping and funnelled pepper and spices to Lisbon. Portuguese ships reached China in 1513 and Japan in 1543, founded Macau in 1557, and opened Brazil after Pedro Álvares Cabral's landfall in 1500.

Union with Spain and decline (1580–1640)

After King Sebastian died at Alcácer Quibir in 1578, Philip II of Spain claimed the Portuguese crown, uniting the two empires from 1580 to 1640. The union exposed Portuguese possessions to Spain's enemies: the Dutch seized much of Brazil's sugar coast, Malacca (1641), and positions in Africa and Asia, while the English took Hormuz (1622). A revolt in 1640 restored Portuguese independence under John IV of Braganza, but the eastern empire never recovered its former extent.

Brazilian gold and the territorial empire (1640–1822)

The empire's centre of gravity shifted to Brazil, where sugar, then the gold and diamond rushes of Minas Gerais in the 1690s, financed the metropole, and to Angola, the chief source of enslaved Africans transported across the Atlantic — Portugal and Brazil carried more enslaved people than any other colonial system over the trade's history. In 1808, fleeing Napoleon's invasion, the Portuguese court transferred to Rio de Janeiro, making Brazil the seat of empire. Brazil declared independence under Pedro I in 1822.

The "third empire" and decolonisation (1822–1999)

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Portugal concentrated on Africa, expanding inland from Angola and Mozambique during the Scramble for Africa, checked by the British Ultimatum of 1890 over the "Pink Map" corridor. The authoritarian Estado Novo regime clung to the colonies, fighting colonial wars in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea-Bissau from 1961 to 1974. The Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 brought rapid decolonisation: Angola and Mozambique became independent in 1975, and East Timor was invaded by Indonesia the same year (independence restored 2002). Goa had been annexed by India in 1961. The handover of Macau on 20 December 1999 closed the empire's five-century history.

Legacy

The empire created the first truly global trade network linking four continents, spread Portuguese language and Catholicism, carried crops (maize, cassava, chillies, pineapple) between continents in the Columbian exchange, and left creole languages, fortifications, and architectural heritage around the world. It also left the legacies of the transatlantic slave trade, in which Portugal was the earliest and one of the largest participants, and of colonial underdevelopment debated by historians today.
