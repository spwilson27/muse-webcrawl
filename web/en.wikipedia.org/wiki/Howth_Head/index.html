Howth Head

The cliffs of Howth Head from the cliff path.

Howth Head (Irish: Ceann Bhinn Éadair) is a headland on the northern side of Dublin Bay in County Dublin, Ireland, forming the northeastern boundary of the bay. The rocky peninsula, connected to the mainland at Sutton, rises to about 171 metres (561 ft) at its summit and is largely covered by heathland of heather and gorse. It is distinct from the fishing village and suburb of Howth, which occupies the northern slopes of the peninsula.

Features

At the southeastern tip of the head stands the Baily Lighthouse, first established in the 17th century and rebuilt in 1814, long a key navigational mark for shipping entering Dublin Bay. A popular cliff path circles the head, passing the site of an early Christian church and the promontory fort area near the Nose of Howth. The headland commands views across Dublin Bay to the Wicklow Mountains and north toward Ireland's Eye, a small offshore island and bird sanctuary.

Ecology and protection

Howth Head is designated a Special Area of Conservation for its sea cliffs and dry heath, habitats that support breeding seabirds including razorbills, guillemots, fulmars and kittiwakes, as well as peregrine falcons. The heathland has been shaped by periodic gorse fires. The headland features prominently in Dublin's literary geography, appearing in the works of James Joyce, whose characters look across the bay from "the head of Howth".
