Portland Vase

The Portland Vase is a Roman cameo glass amphora, dating from roughly 1–25 AD, decorated with white glass figures cut in relief against a dark blue glass ground. It is one of the most famous surviving works of ancient glass and is now in the British Museum in London.

Description

The vase is about 25 cm high and depicts two scenes separated by sphinx-like figures, generally understood to show mythological subjects involving love and marriage — most often interpreted as the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, though interpretations have varied widely since the 17th century. The base disc, showing a figure usually identified as Paris, is now thought to be a later addition.

History

The vase was recorded in Rome by the early 1600s, apparently in the collection of Cardinal del Monte and then of the Barberini family. It was bought in the 18th century by Sir William Hamilton, British envoy to Naples, who sold it to Margaret Cavendish-Harley, Duchess of Portland — hence its name. It was later owned by the dukes of Portland, who lent it to the British Museum, where in 1845 it was smashed by a deranged visitor; it was painstakingly reconstructed and has been restored several times since. The museum purchased it in 1945.

Influence

The vase inspired many copies and imitations, most famously the glass replicas and jasperware versions produced by Josiah Wedgwood, whose first successful jasperware copy of 1790 was a landmark of British ceramics.
