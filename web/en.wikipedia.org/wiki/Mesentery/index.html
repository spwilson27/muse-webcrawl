Mesentery

The mesentery is an organ of the digestive system consisting of a double fold of peritoneum that attaches the intestines to the posterior abdominal wall. It supports the jejunum and ileum, and carries the blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatics that supply the intestines. In 2016, researchers argued the mesentery should be classified as a single continuous organ, a view popularised by surgeon J. Calvin Coffey and colleagues.

Structure

The root of the mesentery, about 15 cm long, attaches diagonally across the posterior abdominal wall from the duodenojejunal flexure to the right sacroiliac joint. The mesentery fans out to an intestinal border roughly six metres long, encompassing the length of the small intestine. Related structures include the transverse mesocolon, sigmoid mesocolon, and mesoappendix, which along with the mesentery proper are now understood as parts of one continuous structure.

Function

The mesentery holds the intestines in place while allowing them mobility for digestion. It transmits the superior mesenteric vessels and their branches, autonomic nerves, and lymphatic vessels and nodes draining the gut. It also stores fat and contains immune tissue that helps respond to intestinal bacteria.

Clinical significance

Disorders involving the mesentery include volvulus (twisting of the bowel on its mesenteric axis), mesenteric ischaemia from compromised blood flow, mesenteric cysts and tumours, and involvement in Crohn's disease, where "creeping fat" wraps around inflamed bowel segments.
