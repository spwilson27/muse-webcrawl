Kamiokande

Kamiokande (Kamioka Nucleon Decay Experiment) was a neutrino and proton-decay observatory located 1,000 metres underground in the Mozumi Mine near Hida (formerly Kamioka), Gifu Prefecture, Japan. Operating from 1983, its detections of neutrinos from supernova SN 1987A and from the Sun founded the field of neutrino astronomy and contributed to the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics awarded to Masatoshi Koshiba.

History

Built as a 3,000-tonne tank of pure water watched by photomultiplier tubes to detect Cherenkov light from proton decay, Kamiokande found no proton decays, setting lower limits on the proton lifetime. Upgraded in 1985 as Kamiokande-II with improved electronics and timing, it could detect low-energy neutrinos. On 23 February 1987 it recorded a burst of 11–12 neutrino events over 13 seconds from supernova SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud — the first neutrinos ever detected from beyond the solar system. In 1989 it confirmed the deficit of solar neutrinos first seen by the Homestake experiment, and it also recorded an excess of atmospheric muon-neutrino events that later proved evidence for neutrino oscillation.

Legacy

Kamiokande was succeeded in 1996 by the much larger Super-Kamiokande in the same mine. The original site now hosts experiments including the KamLAND reactor-neutrino detector (in the Kamiokande cavity's mine area) and the KAGRA gravitational-wave observatory.
