Self-cooling can

A self-cooling can (or self-chilling can) is a beverage can that refrigerates its contents on activation, without external power or ice. Such cans have been developed repeatedly since the mid-twentieth century, but cost, size overhead, and environmental objections have kept them a niche product.

Mechanisms

Several physical principles have been used:

  • Evaporative/adiabatic expansion: early designs released a compressed refrigerant gas; the rapid expansion absorbed heat. Prototypes in the 1990s used HFC-134a, a potent greenhouse gas, which drew strong environmental criticism and led to redesigns using carbon dioxide.
  • Endothermic dissolution: mixing water with salts such as ammonium nitrate or calcium chloride lowers the temperature of the mixture.
  • Vacuum evaporation and adsorption: later designs, such as the "Chill Can" developed by the Joseph Company, used a heat-exchange unit in which water evaporates into a desiccant-filled vacuum chamber, drawing heat from the drink and lowering its temperature by roughly 15–17 °C within a few minutes.

History and commercialization

Patents for self-cooling containers date back decades, and Pepsi among others tested concepts in the 1990s. The Joseph Company's Chill Can reached limited commercial release in the United States in 2018 in partnership with 7-Eleven for iced coffee, marketed as "Fizzics Sparkling Cold Brew" trials in the Los Angeles area. The cans chilled in about 75–90 seconds. Persistent obstacles include the extra volume and weight of the cooling unit, higher unit cost than ordinary cans, recycling complications, and the availability of refrigerated retail as a substitute — keeping self-cooling cans a novelty outside specialist uses such as military rations, remote locations, and promotional products.
