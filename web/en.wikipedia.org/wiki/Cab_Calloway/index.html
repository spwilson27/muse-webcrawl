Cabell Calloway III (December 25, 1907 – November 18, 1994) was an American jazz singer and bandleader. He was a regular performer at the Cotton Club in Harlem, where he became a popular vocalist of the swing era. His niche of mixing jazz and vaudeville won him acclaim during a career that spanned over 65 years.

Career

Raised in Baltimore, Calloway moved through Chicago's jazz scene in the late 1920s before taking over the Missourians band in New York, which became the Cab Calloway Orchestra. At the Cotton Club his band alternated with Duke Ellington's, and nationwide radio broadcasts made him a star. His 1931 recording of "Minnie the Moocher" — with its call-and-response "hi-de-hi-de-ho" scat refrains — became one of the biggest hits of the era and his trademark.

Calloway was famous for his flamboyant showmanship: white tailcoats, dramatic conducting with an oversized baton, gliding dance steps that anticipated the moonwalk, and a mastery of scat singing and "jive" slang, which he codified in The Hepster's Dictionary (1938). His orchestra at various times employed Dizzy Gillespie, Ben Webster and Cozy Cole.

Later years and legacy

After the big-band era faded, Calloway performed in musical theatre — including Porgy and Bess and Hello, Dolly! — and reached a new generation with his appearance in the 1980 film The Blues Brothers, performing "Minnie the Moocher." He was inducted into the Big Band and Jazz Hall of Fame in 1982 and received the National Medal of Arts in 1993.
