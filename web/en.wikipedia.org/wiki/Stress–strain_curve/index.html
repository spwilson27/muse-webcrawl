Stress–strain curve

In engineering and materials science, a stress–strain curve for a material gives the relationship between stress and strain. It is obtained by gradually applying load to a test coupon and measuring the deformation, from which the stress and strain can be determined. These curves reveal important material properties such as the modulus of elasticity, yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and ductility.

Stages of the curve

For a ductile metal, the curve begins with a linear elastic region where stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law); the slope is Young's modulus. At the yield point the material begins to deform plastically. Continued loading causes strain hardening until the ultimate tensile strength is reached, after which the specimen necks and the engineering stress falls until fracture. Brittle materials, by contrast, show little plastic deformation and fracture shortly after the elastic region.

Engineering vs true curves

The engineering stress–strain curve uses the specimen's original cross-sectional area, while the true stress–strain curve uses the instantaneous area and therefore continues to rise until fracture. See also strain and tensile testing.
