Host–guest chemistry

Host–guest chemistry is the branch of supramolecular chemistry in which a larger molecule, the host, binds a smaller molecule or ion, the guest, in its cavity through non-covalent interactions. It mimics the binding of substrates by enzymes and underpins molecular recognition.

Principles

Binding is driven by combinations of hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, hydrophobic effects, electrostatic interactions, and coordination bonds. Key concepts include preorganization and complementarity (emphasized by Donald Cram), and selectivity arising from the match of size, shape, and interaction sites between host and guest. Common hosts include crown ethers, cryptands, cyclodextrins, calixarenes, cucurbiturils, and cyclophanes.

History and applications

The field grew from Charles Pedersen's discovery of crown ethers (1967) and the work of Jean-Marie Lehn and Donald Cram, who shared the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Pedersen. Applications include drug delivery, sensors, catalysis, separations, and molecular machines.
