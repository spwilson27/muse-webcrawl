Entorhinal cortex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The entorhinal cortex (EC) is an area of the brain's allocortex, located in the medial temporal lobe. It serves as the main interface between the hippocampus and the neocortex, and plays a central role in memory formation, navigation, and the perception of time.

Anatomy and function

The entorhinal cortex receives input from widespread areas of the neocortex and projects to the hippocampus via the perforant path, whose fibers terminate in the dentate gyrus and hippocampal subfields. It is divided into medial and lateral areas, which carry spatial and non-spatial (object and olfactory) information respectively.

In 2005, researchers including Edvard Moser and May-Britt Moser discovered grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex — neurons that fire in a hexagonal pattern as an animal moves through space — a finding that contributed to the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning the brain's positioning system. The entorhinal cortex also contains head-direction cells and border cells.

Clinical significance

The entorhinal cortex is among the earliest brain regions affected in Alzheimer's disease; neurofibrillary tangle pathology in the Braak staging scheme begins in the transentorhinal region before spreading to the hippocampus and neocortex. Atrophy of the entorhinal cortex is an early biomarker of the disease.
