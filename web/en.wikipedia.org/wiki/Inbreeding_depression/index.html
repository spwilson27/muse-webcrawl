Inbreeding depression

Inbreeding depression is the reduced biological fitness in a population resulting from inbreeding — the mating of closely related individuals. It typically manifests as lower fertility, higher juvenile mortality, reduced growth, smaller adult size, and increased susceptibility to disease and environmental stress. The phenomenon was systematically documented by Charles Darwin, whose experiments on self-fertilised plants showed marked reductions in vigour compared with cross-fertilised controls.

Inbred lines of many crop species show conspicuous loss of vigour.

Causes

The principal genetic cause is increased homozygosity: inbreeding raises the chance that an individual inherits two copies of the same allele from a common ancestor. This unmasks deleterious recessive alleles that are normally hidden in heterozygotes (the dominance hypothesis) and also reduces any advantage conferred by heterozygosity itself (the overdominance hypothesis). Most evidence supports dominance as the primary mechanism. The severity of depression is often quantified using the inbreeding coefficient and lethal equivalents.

Consequences and management

Inbreeding depression is a central concern in conservation biology, because small, isolated populations of endangered species accumulate inbreeding rapidly; famous cases include the Florida panther, whose health improved after outcrossing with Texas cougars. In agriculture and animal breeding, controlled inbreeding is used to fix desirable traits, but must be balanced against depression; hybrid vigour (heterosis) in cross-bred offspring is the mirror image of the phenomenon. Repeated inbreeding accompanied by selection can purge part of the recessive load, though purging is unreliable for mildly deleterious alleles.
