Hyades (mythology)

In Greek mythology, the Hyades (Ancient Greek: Ὑάδες, "the rainy ones") were a sisterhood of nymphs who brought rain. They were daughters of the Titan Atlas (or, in some accounts, of Erechtheus or Hyas) and sisters of Hyas and of the Pleiades. As stars, the Hyades form the V-shaped cluster in the constellation Taurus, with their rising and setting in antiquity marking the rainy season in Greece.

The Hyades cluster, the rainy stars of Taurus.

Myth

The most common story told that the Hyades were the nurses of the infant Dionysus on Mount Nysa; for this service Zeus placed them among the stars. Another version made their transformation an act of pity: their brother Hyas was killed by a lion (or a boar) while hunting, and the sisters grieved so inconsolably that the gods turned them into stars. Their names are given variously — commonly five or seven: Ambrosia, Eudora, Pedile, Coronis, Polyxo, Phyto (Phaio), and Thyone (Dione) appear among the lists in Hyginus and other sources.

Astronomy and weather lore

The Hyades' heliacal rising and setting were seasonal markers for Greek and Roman farmers and sailors; their name's association with hyein, "to rain", matched their appearance at the onset of the winter rains. Hesiod and later Aratus mention them with the Pleiades and Orion as weather signs, and Virgil calls them the "wet Hyades" (pluvias Hyadas). The cluster remains the nearest open star cluster to the Sun, dominated visually by orange giant stars forming the head of Taurus around — though not physically associated with — the bright star Aldebaran.
