Solid South

The Solid South was the electoral support of the Democratic Party in the Southern United States from the end of Reconstruction (1877) through the mid-1960s. During this period, the former Confederate states voted almost uniformly for Democratic candidates at every level of government, and the region functioned as a one-party system in which the Democratic primary was the real election. The pattern rested on the disenfranchisement of Black voters and the politics of white supremacy, and it dissolved as the national Democratic Party embraced civil rights and the Republican Party made inroads with white Southern voters.

Origins

After the Civil War and Reconstruction, white Southerners associated the Republican Party with Union occupation and emancipation. From the late 1870s, "Redeemer" governments restored Democratic control. Between 1890 and 1910 the Southern states adopted new constitutions and laws — poll taxes, literacy tests, and white primaries — that effectively disenfranchised nearly all Black citizens and many poor whites, locking in Democratic dominance. In presidential elections the region delivered its electoral votes to the Democratic nominee in election after election; a common saying held that Southerners would vote for a "yellow dog" before voting Republican (hence "Yellow Dog Democrat").

Power in Congress

One-party rule produced long congressional tenure, and Southern Democrats accumulated seniority that let them chair key committees in the House and Senate. This bloc repeatedly blocked anti-lynching legislation, federal civil-rights enforcement, and other measures, while supporting much of the New Deal's economic programme — provided it did not disturb the racial order. The 1948 walkout of the "Dixiecrats" (States' Rights Democratic Party), led by Strom Thurmond in protest at a civil-rights plank, was an early crack in the edifice.

Breakup

The Solid South began to fragment in presidential voting after the Second World War and collapsed in the 1960s. Democratic presidents John F. Kennedy and Lyndon B. Johnson backed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965; Barry Goldwater carried five Deep South states for the Republicans in 1964, George Wallace's American Independent candidacy took much of the region in 1968, and by the 1980s–90s the Republicans had become competitive and then dominant across most of the South at every level — a realignment historians call the "Southern strategy" era. The restoration of Black voting rights under the Voting Rights Act transformed the electorate even as partisan allegiances inverted.
