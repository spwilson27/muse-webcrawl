The rabies vaccine is a vaccine used to prevent rabies, a viral disease that is almost invariably fatal once symptoms appear. It can be given before exposure to people at high risk — veterinarians, animal handlers, laboratory workers and travellers to endemic regions — and, crucially, after exposure, because the virus's long incubation period allows immunity to develop before it reaches the brain.

History

Louis Pasteur and Émile Roux developed the first rabies vaccine from desiccated infected rabbit spinal cord and used it in July 1885 on nine-year-old Joseph Meister, who had been mauled by a rabid dog; the boy survived. Early nerve-tissue vaccines carried significant risks of neurological reactions. Modern vaccines, grown in human diploid cells, chick embryo cells or Vero cells and inactivated, are far safer and are produced by companies such as Sanofi Pasteur and Novartis (Rabipur/RabAvert, Imovax Rabies, Verorab).

Use

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) combines thorough wound washing, a series of vaccine doses (commonly four doses over two weeks for immunocompetent people, per the Essen regimen or abbreviated schedules) and, for previously unvaccinated patients, rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated around the wound. Timely PEP is essentially 100% effective. Oral rabies vaccines in baits are used to immunise wildlife — foxes, raccoons and coyotes — and have eliminated fox rabies from much of Western Europe; dogs are vaccinated to control the main reservoir for human cases, which kill an estimated 59,000 people a year, mostly in Asia and Africa.
