Antisemitism in the Russian Empire

Antisemitism in the Russian Empire encompassed a broad range of official policies, legal restrictions, and popular violence directed against Jews from the late eighteenth century, when the partitions of Poland brought a large Jewish population under Russian rule, until the fall of the Romanov dynasty in 1917. By the late nineteenth century the empire contained the largest Jewish population in the world—over five million people—most of whom were subject to severe discrimination.

Most Jews in the empire were confined to the Pale of Settlement in the west

Pale of Settlement

Beginning with decrees of Catherine the Great in 1791, Jewish residence was largely restricted to the Pale of Settlement, a territory in the western borderlands of the empire comprising much of present-day Poland, Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, and Bessarabia. Jews were generally barred from living in the interior of Russia, with limited exceptions for certain merchants, artisans, veterans, and university graduates. Within the Pale, Jews were often prohibited from owning rural land and, after 1887, faced quotas in secondary and higher education.

Conscription and the cantonists

Under Nicholas I, the 1827 conscription statute subjected Jewish communities to military draft quotas, and Jewish boys—sometimes as young as twelve—were taken as cantonists for lengthy terms of service during which many were pressured to convert to Christianity. The cantonist system was abolished under Alexander II in 1856.

May Laws and pogroms

Following the assassination of Alexander II in 1881, waves of pogroms swept the Pale, and the government blamed the Jews for the violence. The "Temporary Rules" of May 1882 (the May Laws) restricted Jewish settlement in rural areas, limited property ownership, and tightened educational quotas; though nominally temporary, they remained in force until 1917. Further pogroms, notably at Kishinev in 1903 and across the empire in 1905–1906, killed thousands and destroyed homes and businesses, while the tsarist secret police produced and promoted the fraudulent Protocols of the Elders of Zion. The 1911–1913 Beilis trial in Kiev, in which a Jewish factory clerk was prosecuted on a ritual-murder (blood libel) charge, attracted international outrage; Beilis was acquitted.

Emigration and end of the regime

Persecution and poverty drove mass Jewish emigration: roughly two million Jews left the Russian Empire between 1881 and 1914, most bound for the United States, with others going to Palestine, Western Europe, and Argentina. The Pale and nearly all legal disabilities were abolished by the Provisional Government after the February Revolution of 1917.
