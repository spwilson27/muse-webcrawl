Marinoan glaciation

The Marinoan glaciation was a severe period of worldwide glaciation in the Cryogenian Period that lasted from approximately 650 to 632.3 million years ago, at the end of the Proterozoic Eon. It was the second and more recent of the two great "Snowball Earth" events, following the Sturtian glaciation. The glaciation takes its name from Marino, a suburb of Adelaide, South Australia, near where its type deposits were described.

Evidence

Evidence for the Marinoan glaciation includes glacial deposits (tillites and dropstones) found on nearly every continent, many of them deposited at tropical palaeolatitudes, as indicated by palaeomagnetic measurements. These low-latitude glaciers imply ice sheets reached sea level near the equator — the central observation behind the Snowball Earth hypothesis, in which the entire ocean may have frozen over.

Marinoan glacial deposits are characteristically overlain by "cap carbonates" — distinctive layers of dolostone and limestone deposited rapidly in the warm, alkaline oceans that followed the thaw. The end of the glaciation is dated by uranium–lead dating of volcanic ash beds to about 635 million years ago, which also defines the base of the Ediacaran Period.

Mechanism

Under the Snowball Earth model, a runaway ice–albedo feedback carried the planet into a global glaciation as ice advanced toward the equator and reflected ever more sunlight. Escape from the snowball state required the slow build-up of volcanic carbon dioxide in the atmosphere over millions of years — with silicate weathering shut down by the ice cover — until greenhouse warming triggered catastrophic melting, intense weathering, and the deposition of cap carbonates.

Significance

The end of the Marinoan glaciation immediately precedes the appearance of the Ediacara biota, the first large, complex multicellular organisms in the fossil record. Whether the extreme environmental stresses of the snowball events helped spur the evolution of complex life remains an active topic of research.
