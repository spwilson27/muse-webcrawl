Dental fluorosis

Dental fluorosis is a developmental disturbance of tooth enamel caused by excessive fluoride intake during enamel formation, generally in children under eight years of age. It ranges from barely visible white flecks and lacy markings in mild cases to brown staining, mottling, and pitting of the enamel in severe cases. Because fluorosis is determined while teeth are forming, it does not develop in teeth that have already erupted.

History and epidemiology

The condition was recognized in the early 20th century as "Colorado brown stain" and "mottled enamel", studied by dentist Frederick McKay and chemist H. V. Churchill, who traced it to naturally high fluoride in drinking water. The same investigations — especially H. Trendley Dean's epidemiological studies using his fluorosis index — led to the discovery that moderate fluoride levels protect against dental caries, the basis for community water fluoridation. Fluorosis remains common worldwide where groundwater fluoride is naturally high, as in parts of India, China, and the East African Rift, sometimes accompanied by skeletal fluorosis at higher exposures.

Mechanism and management

Excess fluoride disrupts ameloblast function during the maturation stage of enamel formation, causing retention of enamel proteins and hypomineralization of subsurface enamel; the porous enamel then takes up stains after eruption. Mild fluorosis is purely cosmetic. Treatment of cosmetically objectionable cases includes enamel microabrasion, bleaching, resin infiltration, veneers, or crowns. Prevention consists of monitoring total fluoride intake in young children — from water, toothpaste (using a pea-sized amount and discouraging swallowing), and supplements.
