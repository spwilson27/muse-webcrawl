Pattern 1914 Enfield

The Pattern 1914 Enfield (P14) was a British bolt-action service rifle of the First World War period. Developed from the experimental Pattern 1913 Enfield, which had been designed around a powerful experimental .276 Enfield cartridge, the P14 was re-chambered for the standard British .303 British round and manufactured in the United States by Winchester, Remington, and Eddystone to supplement British rifle production during the war.

The P14, recognisable by its long barrel, volley sights, and distinctive stock profile.

Design

Unlike the Lee-Enfield, the P14 used a Mauser-style action with a front-locking bolt and a one-piece bolt body, combined with an aperture rear sight protected by prominent steel "ears" on the receiver. It was a long, heavy, and notably accurate rifle, fitted with volley sights for ranged area fire. Approximately 1.2 million were produced in the United States before production ended in 1917.

Service

Because the Short Magazine Lee-Enfield remained the standard British infantry rifle, the P14 was used mainly as a sniper rifle — the Winchester-made rifles, fitted with telescopic sights as the Pattern 1914 Mk I W (T), were considered the most accurate British sniping rifles of the war — and for training and second-line duties. Its design was adapted by the United States as the M1917 Enfield, chambered in .30-06, which became the most widely issued American rifle of the First World War. P14s later served with the Home Guard in the Second World War and were sold or supplied to several allied nations.
