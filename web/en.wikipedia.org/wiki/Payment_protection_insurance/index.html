Payment protection insurance

Payment protection insurance (PPI), also known as credit insurance, credit protection insurance, or loan repayment insurance, is an insurance product that enables consumers to ensure repayment of credit if the borrower dies, becomes ill or disabled, loses a job, or faces other circumstances that may prevent them from earning income to service the debt. It is typically sold alongside loans, credit cards, mortgages and car finance.

The UK mis-selling scandal

PPI became the subject of the largest consumer redress exercise in British financial history. From the 1990s, banks and other lenders sold millions of PPI policies, many to customers who did not need them, could not claim on them (for example the self-employed or those with pre-existing conditions), or did not know they had bought them. Premiums were often added as a single upfront sum to the loan, generating large commissions that were not disclosed.

Regulatory investigations by the Office of Fair Trading, the Financial Services Authority and the Competition Commission led to reforms, a ban on single-premium policies, and a judicial review in 2011 that the banks lost. The Supreme Court's 2014 decision in Plevin v Paragon Personal Finance widened grounds for complaint to undisclosed commission. The Financial Conduct Authority set a deadline of 29 August 2019 for PPI complaints; by the end of the scheme, lenders had paid out more than £38 billion in compensation, with the total cost including administration exceeding £50 billion.

How PPI works

A typical policy covers loan repayments for a limited period (often 12 months) in the event of accident, sickness or unemployment, and pays off the outstanding balance on death. Policies generally carry exclusions and waiting periods, which were a frequent source of mis-selling complaints.
