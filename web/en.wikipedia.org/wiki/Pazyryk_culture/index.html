The Pazyryk culture is an Iron Age archaeological culture (c. 6th to 3rd centuries BCE) identified by excavated artifacts and mummified humans found in the Siberian permafrost of the Altay Mountains. The culture is associated with the eastern Scythian world of mounted nomadic pastoralists described in broad terms by Herodotus.

Excavations

The great burial mounds (kurgans) of the Pazyryk valley were excavated by M. P. Gryaznov in 1929 and by Sergei Rudenko's expeditions of 1947–1949. Water seeping into looted tombs had frozen into permanent ice lenses, preserving organic materials — wood, leather, textiles, food, and bodies — in extraordinary condition for more than two thousand years.

Finds

The five large Pazyryk barrows contained log burial chambers with chieftains, sacrificed horses wearing elaborate bridles, saddles, and antlered "reindeer" masks. Among the most celebrated finds is the Pazyryk carpet, the world's oldest surviving knotted-pile carpet, now in the Hermitage Museum in St. Petersburg, along with appliquéd felt hangings, a swan-decorated tent, and mummies bearing elaborate animal-style tattoos.

Later discoveries

Excavations on the Ukok Plateau in the 1990s, led by Natalia Polosmak, uncovered the "Ice Maiden" (also called the Princess of Ukok), a tattooed woman buried with six horses, whose repatriation to the Altai Republic became a celebrated cultural and political issue. The Pazyryk sites are now part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of the Golden Mountains of Altai.
