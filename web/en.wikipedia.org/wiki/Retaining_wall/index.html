Retaining wall

A retaining wall is a structure designed and constructed to resist the lateral pressure of soil, when there is a desired change in ground elevation that exceeds the angle of repose of the soil. Retaining walls are used to bound soils between two different elevations, often in terrain where the landscape needs to be shaped severely for construction, roads, or agriculture such as terrace farming.

Types

Gravity walls depend on their own weight to resist earth pressure and are typically built of stone or unreinforced concrete. Cantilever walls of reinforced concrete use an L- or inverted T-shaped footing so that the weight of the backfill itself contributes to stability. Sheet pile walls are driven into the ground and used in soft soils and waterfront construction. Anchored walls are held by cables or rods anchored in rock or soil behind the wall. Gabion walls are built of wire cages filled with rock, and mechanically stabilized earth walls use layers of geogrid or metal strips within compacted fill.

Design considerations

The design of a retaining wall must account for lateral earth pressure, which depends on the soil's unit weight, friction angle, and cohesion, and follows theories developed by Coulomb and Rankine. Drainage is critical: hydrostatic pressure from water accumulating behind a wall is a leading cause of failure, so walls are provided with drainage material, drainage pipes, and weep holes. Walls must be checked against overturning, sliding, bearing-capacity failure, and global slope instability.
