The Ising model is a mathematical model of ferromagnetism in statistical mechanics. It consists of discrete variables ("spins") that can be in one of two states (+1 or −1), arranged on a lattice and interacting with their nearest neighbours. Despite its simplicity, the model displays a rich phase transition between a magnetised (ordered) state at low temperature and a disordered state at high temperature, and it has become one of the most widely studied models in physics, with applications from neural networks and image processing to social behaviour.

Definition

For a configuration σ of spins on a lattice Λ, the energy is given by the Hamiltonian

H(σ) = −J Σ<ij> σiσj − h Σi σi

where the first sum runs over neighbouring pairs, J is the interaction strength (J > 0 favours alignment, i.e. ferromagnetism), and h is an external magnetic field. Configurations occur with Boltzmann probability proportional to exp(−βH), with β = 1/(kBT). The magnetisation — the average spin — serves as the order parameter.

History and solutions

The model was proposed by Wilhelm Lenz in 1920 and solved in one dimension by his student Ernst Ising in his 1924 doctoral thesis; the 1D model has no phase transition at any positive temperature, which led Ising to conclude (incorrectly) that the model would not exhibit magnetism in higher dimensions either. Rudolf Peierls showed in 1936 that the two-dimensional model does order. Lars Onsager's exact solution of the 2D square-lattice model in zero field (1944) — a landmark of mathematical physics — gave the critical temperature sinh(2J/kBTc) = 1, i.e. kBTc/J ≈ 2.269, and the spontaneous magnetisation formula published by C. N. Yang (1952). No exact solution exists in three dimensions; the 3D model is studied by Monte Carlo simulation (e.g. the Metropolis and Wolff algorithms), series expansions and renormalisation group methods, which yield critical exponents in the same universality class as the liquid–gas critical point.

Phase transition and critical phenomena

Below Tc the system spontaneously breaks symmetry, settling into a mostly-up or mostly-down state; at Tc, fluctuations appear on all scales, producing fractal-like spin clusters and diverging correlation length. The Ising model provided the paradigmatic example for the theory of critical phenomena and universality developed by Kadanoff, Wilson and others.

Applications beyond physics

Ising-like models appear as Hopfield networks in neuroscience and machine learning, in Markov random fields for image segmentation, in lattice-gas and opinion-dynamics models in sociology and economics, and as the problem class of quantum annealers: finding the ground state of a general Ising spin glass is NP-hard, making the model a benchmark for optimisation hardware.
