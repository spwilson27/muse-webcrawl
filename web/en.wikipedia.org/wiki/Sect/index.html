Sect

A sect is a subgroup of a larger religious or philosophical tradition.

A sect is a subgroup of a religious, political, or philosophical belief system, usually an offshoot of a larger group. The word originally referred neutrally to a following or school of thought, but in modern usage it often carries connotations of a group deviating from orthodox teaching, and in popular speech it can be pejorative, shading toward the term cult.

Etymology

The term derives from the Latin secta, meaning "a way, road, mode of life, school of philosophy" or "a heresy" — probably from sequi, "to follow". In classical usage it simply denoted the adherents of a philosophical school or political faction.

Sociological usage

In the sociology of religion, sect is one term in a classic typology running from church to denomination to sect to cult, developed from the work of Max Weber and Ernst Troeltsch. A sect, in this schema, is a relatively small, voluntary, exclusive body that breaks away from a larger religious tradition over doctrinal disputes, maintains tension with surrounding society, demands high commitment from members, and often arises among the socially disadvantaged. Over generations a sect may soften and become a denomination, or it may dissolve. Examples frequently analysed as sects in this technical sense include the early Methodists, Quakers, and many Anabaptist groups.

Usage in other traditions

The word is also used neutrally of schools within religions: the sects of Islam (Sunni, Shia, and others), the sects of Buddhism, the several sects of ancient Judaism named by Josephus (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, Zealots), and the sects or sampradayas of Hinduism. In several European languages the cognate word (e.g. French secte, German Sekte) is more strongly pejorative than English "sect" and is applied to groups regarded as manipulative or dangerous, which has made the term contentious in public policy debates.
