Goal-line technology (GLT) is the use of electronic aid in association football to determine whether a goal has been scored — that is, whether the whole of the ball has wholly crossed the goal line. The system signals the referee, typically via a vibrating watch display within about one second, without otherwise interrupting play.

History

Calls for technology grew after a series of high-profile incidents, notably Frank Lampard's disallowed goal for England against Germany at the 2010 FIFA World Cup, when the ball clearly crossed the line. After trials, the International Football Association Board (IFAB) approved GLT on 5 July 2012 and it was written into the Laws of the Game. It was first used at the 2012 FIFA Club World Cup, adopted by the English Premier League for 2013–14, and used at the 2014 FIFA World Cup, where it confirmed a goal for France against Honduras in its first World Cup intervention.

Systems

Licensed systems fall into two families: camera-based systems such as Hawk-Eye and GoalControl, which triangulate the ball's position with multiple high-speed cameras, and magnetic-field systems such as GoalRef and Cairos, which use a modified ball with embedded electronics passing through a field generated around the goal. All licensed systems must meet FIFA Quality Programme accuracy standards. GLT is used alongside, and distinct from, the video assistant referee (VAR) system introduced later.
