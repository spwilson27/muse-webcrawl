Moon illusion


The full Moon appears larger near the horizon than overhead.

The Moon illusion is an optical illusion which causes the Moon to appear larger near the horizon than it does higher up in the sky. It has been known since ancient times and recorded by various cultures. The explanation of this phenomenon is still debated.

The angular size of the Moon as seen from Earth is about half a degree, and it does not appreciably change as the Moon crosses the sky — indeed, the Moon is slightly closer to an observer when it is overhead. Photographs prove the retinal image is the same size; the difference is entirely perceptual.

Proposed explanations

  • Apparent distance theory: the sky is perceived as a flattened dome, so objects near the horizon are judged farther away; with the same visual angle, a supposedly more distant Moon is perceived as larger.
  • Relative size theory: near the horizon the Moon is compared with trees, buildings and other objects of known size, making it appear large; high in the empty sky there is nothing to compare it with.
  • Angle of regard: raising the eyes to look overhead may influence size perception.

The phenomenon is related to the Ponzo illusion and to size constancy in human visual perception. The same illusion applies to the Sun and to constellations near the horizon.
