The proteome is the entire set of proteins that is, or can be, expressed by a genome, cell, tissue or organism at a given time. It is largely the set of expressed proteins in a given type of cell or organism, at a given time, under defined conditions. The study of the proteome is called proteomics.

Unlike the genome, which is roughly constant for an organism, the proteome varies from cell to cell and over time, because different genes are expressed in different cell types and because proteins are modified after translation. A single gene can give rise to many distinct protein species (proteoforms) through alternative splicing and post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation and glycosylation, so the proteome is far more complex than the genome.

History

The word "proteome" is a blend of protein and genome, coined by Marc Wilkins in 1994 while a PhD student at Macquarie University. Early proteomics relied on two-dimensional gel electrophoresis to separate thousands of spots on a gel; proteins were identified by Edman sequencing or peptide mass fingerprinting.

Methods

Modern proteomics is dominated by liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). In shotgun proteomics, proteins are digested with trypsin and the resulting peptides identified from their fragmentation spectra. Label-based strategies (SILAC, TMT, iTRAQ) and label-free approaches enable quantitative comparison between samples. Targeted methods and affinity reagents such as antibodies, used by the Human Protein Atlas, complement mass spectrometry. Large international efforts, including the Human Proteome Project, aim to identify at least one protein product for every human protein-coding gene.

Types of proteomes

Researchers speak of cellular, organellar, tissue, organismal and even "dark" proteomes — the latter referring to proteins whose structures or functions remain uncharacterized. The proteomes of body fluids such as plasma are of special interest for biomarker discovery.
