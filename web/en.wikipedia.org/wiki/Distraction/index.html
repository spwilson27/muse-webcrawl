Distraction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Distraction is the process of diverting the attention of an individual or group from a desired area of focus and thereby blocking or diminishing the reception of desired information. Distraction is caused by the lack of ability to pay attention, a lack of interest in the object of attention, the greater intensity or novelty of something other than the object of attention, or attributing interest to an object of attention that is not its object.

Causes and mechanisms

Attention is a limited cognitive resource. Distraction occurs when stimuli — external (noise, movement, notifications, conversation) or internal (pain, rumination, hunger, intrusive thoughts) — compete successfully for processing. Cognitive psychology distinguishes exogenous capture, in which salient events seize attention automatically, from failures of endogenous control, as when fatigue or divided attention undermines concentration. The phenomenon of inattentional blindness shows that focused attention itself can cause people to miss obvious events outside the focus.

Contexts

  • Driving: distracted driving — texting, phone use, adjusting controls — is a major cause of road accidents, and many jurisdictions restrict hand-held device use.
  • Education and work: multitasking with digital media is associated with poorer learning and task performance; task-switching imposes measurable "switch costs".
  • Medicine: distraction and interruption during procedures such as surgery and medication administration are studied as patient-safety risks.
  • Deliberate distraction: magicians, pickpockets, political communicators and military deception planners exploit misdirection; distraction is also used therapeutically, for example to reduce pain or anxiety.

Distraction and technology

Smartphones and social media are engineered to capture attention through notifications and variable rewards, prompting a research literature on "attention economy" effects and countermeasures such as notification batching, focus modes and digital-wellbeing tools. Individual differences matter: people with ADHD experience heightened distractibility, and distractibility also increases with sleep loss and ageing.
