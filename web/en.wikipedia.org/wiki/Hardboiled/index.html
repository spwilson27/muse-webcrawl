Hardboiled

Hardboiled (or hard-boiled) fiction is a literary genre that shares some of its characters and settings with crime fiction (especially detective stories and noir fiction). The genre's typical protagonist is a detective who battles the violence of organised crime that flourished during Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933) and its aftermath, while dealing with a legal system that has become as corrupt as the organised crime itself.

History

The style emerged in pulp magazines such as Black Mask in the 1920s. Dashiell Hammett, a former Pinkerton detective, brought authenticity with works like Red Harvest (1929) and The Maltese Falcon (1930), featuring Sam Spade and the Continental Op.

Raymond Chandler refined the form with Philip Marlowe in The Big Sleep (1939), adding lyrical prose and a moral code for his knight-like detective. James M. Cain explored the criminal's viewpoint in The Postman Always Rings Twice (1934).

Characteristics

Hardboiled prose is terse, unsentimental and colloquial, with sharp dialogue. Unlike the classical whodunit of Agatha Christie, it emphasises atmosphere, motive and social corruption over puzzle-solving. It strongly influenced film noir.

Later developments

Writers such as Mickey Spillane, Ross Macdonald, Elmore Leonard and contemporary neo-noir authors extended the tradition. The genre also shaped comic books, video games and hardboiled wonderland works in Japan.
