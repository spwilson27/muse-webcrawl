Veto power

Veto
OriginLatin: "I forbid"
TypesAbsolute, suspensive (qualified), pocket veto
Famous holdersU.S. President; UN Security Council permanent members; Roman tribunes

A veto is a legal power to unilaterally stop an official action. In the most typical example, a president or monarch vetoes a bill to stop it from becoming law, or a permanent member of the United Nations Security Council vetoes a resolution. The word comes from Latin veto, "I forbid", the formula used by the tribunes of the plebs in ancient Rome to block measures of the Senate and magistrates.

Forms

An absolute veto cannot be overridden — as with the Security Council veto held by China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States, the five permanent members. A suspensive or qualified veto merely delays a measure or can be overridden by a special majority: the President of the United States, for instance, may veto bills passed by Congress, but Congress can enact them anyway by a two-thirds vote of both houses. A pocket veto occurs when a president simply withholds signature from a bill after the legislature has adjourned, killing it without a formal veto. Some systems grant partial or "line-item" vetoes over individual provisions, and many heads of state hold a reserved veto they exercise rarely or on ministerial advice.

Uses and controversy

Vetoes serve as checks within separated powers, protecting minorities, constitutional limits or executive judgement against hasty legislation. They are equally criticised as undemocratic blocks: the Security Council veto has repeatedly prevented UN action in the face of mass atrocities, prompting long-running reform campaigns, while frequent presidential veto threats shape legislation before it is ever passed. In the European Union, the historical national veto in the Council has steadily given way to qualified majority voting as competences have expanded.
