The Markandeya Purana (Sanskrit: मार्कण्डेय पुराण, Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa) is a Sanskrit text of Hinduism, and one of the eighteen major Puranas. The text is named after the Vedic sage Markandeya, who appears as the narrator of several of its discourses. It is among the oldest of the Puranas, with core material generally dated by scholars to roughly the 3rd to 7th centuries CE, though it contains later additions.

Unlike most Puranas, the Markandeya Purana is not strongly sectarian: it does not consistently glorify one deity over others, and its chapters span topics including cosmology, mythology, genealogy of gods and kings, ethics, social customs, yoga, and theories of dharma and karma. Its most celebrated portion is the Devi Mahatmyam ("Glory of the Goddess"), a hymn of 700 verses in praise of the goddess Durga, which is recited during the festival of Navaratri and forms the scriptural core of Shaktism.

Contents

The text, in its standard recension, comprises 137 chapters and about 9,000 verses. It opens with a dialogue in which the birds of knowledge answer questions raised by the sage Jaimini, who had been left unsatisfied by explanations in the Mahabharata. The frame moves through creation myths, the dynasties of the solar and lunar races, descriptions of the Manvantaras (ages of Manu), and discourses on the afterlife.

Devi Mahatmyam

Chapters 78–90 form the Devi Mahatmyam, also called the Durga Saptashati or Chandi. It narrates the goddess's three great manifestations: as Mahakali, who destroys the demons Madhu and Kaitabha; as Mahalakshmi, who slays Mahishasura, the buffalo demon; and as Mahasarasvati, who defeats Shumbha and Nishumbha. The section presents the goddess as the supreme reality and is foundational for goddess worship in India.

Significance

The Markandeya Purana has been studied for its early references to social customs, geography, and the development of the goddess tradition. Many of its verses were quoted by medieval digest (nibandha) writers on ritual and law. It has been translated into English several times, most notably by F. Eden Pargiter in the early 20th century.
