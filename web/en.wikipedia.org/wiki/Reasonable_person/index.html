Reasonable person

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The reasonable person is a legal fiction in common-law systems: a hypothetical person of ordinary prudence, care and foresight whose conduct supplies the objective standard for negligence and other doctrines. The defendant is liable not for failing to be perfect, but for failing to act as the reasonable person would — the standard of Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks (1856) and of Judge Learned Hand's cost-benefit formula in United States v. Carroll Towing (1947) (liability where the burden of precaution is less than the probability of harm times its gravity).

The figure descends from the nineteenth-century “man on the Clapham omnibus” (Greer LJ, 1932) and the earlier “reasonable man”; modern usage prefers “person”. The standard is mostly objective — the defendant's own limitations (short of physical disability or childhood, where the standard adjusts to a reasonable child) do not excuse — and it recurs across the law: the reasonable bystander in contract, the person skilled in the art in patent law, the reasonable suspicion and reasonable doubt thresholds (the latter deliberately left more elusive). Critics argue the fiction can smuggle in majority norms and gendered or class assumptions; defenders reply that an administrable objective standard is indispensable to a law that must guide conduct in advance.
