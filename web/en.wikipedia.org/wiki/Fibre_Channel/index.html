Fibre Channel

Fibre Channel (FC) is a high-speed data transfer protocol providing in-order, lossless delivery of raw block data. It is primarily used to connect computer data storage to servers in commercial data centres, forming storage area networks (SANs). Despite its name, Fibre Channel can run over either optical fibre or copper cabling.

History

Work on Fibre Channel began in 1988, and the standard was approved by ANSI in 1994. It was designed to combine the channel semantics of mainframe I/O (as in ESCON) with networking flexibility, and to carry protocols such as SCSI (via FCP), IP and later NVMe (FC-NVMe) over a single infrastructure.

Architecture

Fibre Channel networks use host bus adapters (HBAs) in servers, FC switches forming a "fabric", and storage arrays. Devices are identified by 64-bit World Wide Names (WWNs). The protocol is organised in five layers (FC-0 through FC-4), from the physical media up to protocol mappings. Common speeds have doubled each generation, from 1 Gbit/s ("1GFC") through 32 Gbit/s, with 64 and 128 Gbit/s generations standardised later.

Topologies and zoning

Deployments are normally dual-fabric for redundancy. Switches support zoning (restricting which ports or WWNs can communicate) and LUN masking on arrays to control access. The arbitrated loop (FC-AL) topology, once used to connect disks directly, has largely been superseded by switched fabrics.

Comparison with iSCSI

Fibre Channel competes with iSCSI and Fibre Channel over Ethernet (FCoE). It remains widely used in enterprise SANs because of its predictable low latency and lossless delivery, while Ethernet-based storage has grown in smaller installations.
