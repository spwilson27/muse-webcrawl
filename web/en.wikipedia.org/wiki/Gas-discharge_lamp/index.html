Gas-discharge lamp

Gas-discharge lamps are a family of artificial light sources that generate light by sending an electric discharge through an ionised gas, a plasma. Typically, such lamps use a noble gas (argon, neon, krypton or xenon) or a mixture of these gases, and sometimes additional substances such as vaporised metals. The colour of the light depends on the gas and substances used: neon signs glow orange-red, sodium vapour lamps yellow, and mercury vapour lamps blue-green.

Different gases produce characteristic colours in discharge lamps.

History

The discharge phenomenon was studied from the 17th century with early electrostatic devices, and Heinrich Geissler's sealed glass tubes of the 1850s (Geissler tubes) demonstrated glowing gas discharges. Peter Cooper Hewitt's mercury-vapour lamp of 1901 was an early commercial success, and Georges Claude's neon lighting was displayed in Paris in 1910, launching the neon sign industry. Fluorescent lamps, in which ultraviolet discharge excites a phosphor coating, were commercialised in the late 1930s.

Operation

A discharge starts when the applied voltage ionises the gas, making it conductive. Because the discharge has negative resistance characteristics, gas-discharge lamps require a ballast to limit current. Most lamps need a warm-up period to reach full brightness, and high-intensity discharge (HID) lamps include metal-halide, high-pressure sodium and mercury-vapour types used in street lighting, stadiums and industrial spaces.

Types

  • Low-pressure lamps: fluorescent lamps, sodium-vapour (SOX) lamps, neon signs.
  • High-pressure lamps: mercury-vapour, metal-halide and high-pressure sodium lamps.
  • High-intensity discharge lamps: including xenon arc lamps used in projectors and searchlights.

Gas-discharge lamps generally offer higher luminous efficacy and longer life than incandescent lamps, though many applications are now being replaced by LED lighting.
