The Carnegie Steel Company was a steel-producing company created by the industrialist Andrew Carnegie to consolidate his steel ventures in the Pittsburgh area. Through vertical integration, aggressive cost control and adoption of the Bessemer process, it became the largest and most profitable steel company in the world, and in 1901 Carnegie sold it to the banker J. P. Morgan for about $480 million, forming the core of the new United States Steel Corporation.

History

Carnegie began in steel with the Edgar Thomson Steel Works at Braddock, opened in 1875, and expanded by acquiring rivals such as the Homestead Steel Works and the Duquesne works, together with coke fields (through the Frick Coke Company) and iron-ore interests. In 1892 these holdings were merged as the Carnegie Steel Company. Its partnership with Henry Clay Frick, who became chairman, drove efficiency but also fierce anti-union policies.

Homestead Strike

In 1892 the company's Homestead works was the scene of the Homestead Strike, one of the most violent labour disputes in American history. When negotiations with the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers broke down, Frick locked out the workforce and brought in Pinkerton detectives; a pitched battle on 6 July 1892 left about ten men dead. State militia occupied the town, the strike collapsed, and unionism in the American steel industry was set back for decades.

Sale to Morgan

By 1900 Carnegie Steel was producing more steel than the whole of Great Britain. In 1901 Carnegie, ready to retire to philanthropy, sold the company to Morgan, who combined it with other properties to create U.S. Steel, the first billion-dollar corporation. Carnegie used much of his fortune to fund libraries, universities and foundations (see Carnegie library).
