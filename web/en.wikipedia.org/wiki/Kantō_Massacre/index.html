Kantō Massacre

The Kantō Massacre was a mass murder in the Kantō region of Japan that followed the Great Kantō earthquake of 1 September 1923. Amid the chaos and fires caused by the quake, false rumours spread that ethnic Koreans were poisoning wells, committing arson and planning an uprising. Between September and November 1923, vigilante groups, police and military personnel murdered an estimated 6,000 people, overwhelmingly Koreans, along with Chinese residents and Japanese socialists, anarchists and others mistaken for Koreans.

Events

After martial law was declared, rumours were amplified by official channels and newspapers. Vigilantes set up checkpoints and used shibboleths — phrases difficult for Koreans to pronounce — to identify victims. Notable related incidents include the Amakasu Incident, in which the military police murdered the anarchist Ōsugi Sakae, his partner Itō Noe and his six-year-old nephew, and the Kameido Incident, in which labour activists were killed by police.

Legacy

The Japanese government at the time downplayed the killings, and for decades the massacre received little official acknowledgement. Memorial services organised by Korean and Japanese civic groups are held annually in Tokyo's Yokoamichō Park. In recent years, the commemoration has become politically contested, with Tokyo governors since 2017 declining to send the customary letter of condolence, and debates continuing over the historical record and the number of victims.
