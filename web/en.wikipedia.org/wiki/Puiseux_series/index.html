In mathematics, Puiseux series are a generalisation of power series that allow for negative and fractional exponents of the indeterminate. They were first introduced by Isaac Newton around 1676 and rediscovered by Victor Puiseux in 1850. A Puiseux series in a variable x has the form

f(x) = Σk≥k₀ ck xk/n

where the coefficients ck belong to some field, k₀ is an integer (possibly negative), and n is a positive integer, the ramification index. Equivalently, a Puiseux series is an ordinary Laurent series in a fractional power x1/n.

Puiseux's theorem

Puiseux's theorem states that the field of Puiseux series over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero is itself algebraically closed. A consequence is the local description of algebraic curves: near a point, the branches of a plane algebraic curve can be parametrised by Puiseux expansions. This is computed algorithmically by the Newton–Puiseux algorithm, which uses the Newton polygon of the defining polynomial.

Applications

Puiseux series occur in the study of singularities of algebraic curves, in tropical geometry, and in asymptotic analysis, where fractional-power expansions describe the behaviour of solutions of differential equations near branch points.
