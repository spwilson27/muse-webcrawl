Figure of speech

A figure of speech or rhetorical figure is a word or phrase that intentionally deviates from straightforward language use or literal meaning to produce a rhetorical or intensified effect (persuasive, poetic, memorable, or comic). In the distinction between literal and figurative language, figures of speech form a traditional category distinct from tropes — though the terms are often used flexibly. Figures have been catalogued since classical antiquity; the Roman rhetorician Quintilian discussed them at length in his Institutio Oratoria, and Renaissance handbooks such as Henry Peacham's The Garden of Eloquence (1577) listed nearly two hundred.

Schemes and tropes

A traditional division separates schemes, which rearrange the usual order or pattern of words, from tropes, which change the meaning of words. Schemes include anaphora (repetition at the beginnings of successive clauses), chiasmus (reversal of parallel structures, as in "ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country"), antithesis, parallelism, alliteration, and asyndeton. Tropes include metaphor, simile, metonymy, synecdoche (a part standing for the whole), irony, hyperbole (exaggeration), litotes (understatement), personification, and oxymoron (apparently contradictory terms, as in "deafening silence").

Functions

Figures of speech compress meaning, create emphasis and rhythm, make abstract ideas concrete, and engage the audience's imagination. In oratory they serve persuasion and memory; in poetry they are a central source of imagery and effect; in everyday speech they appear in idioms, proverbs, advertising slogans, and political catchphrases. Some figures "die" into literal language over time — dead metaphors such as "the leg of a table" are no longer perceived as figurative.

Classification

Modern scholars have classified figures in many ways: by linguistic level (phonetic, morphological, syntactic, semantic), by operation (addition, omission, transposition, substitution, as in the Groupe µ taxonomy), or by cognitive function. Cognitive linguistics, notably George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's Metaphors We Live By (1980), argues that figurative structures such as conceptual metaphor are fundamental to ordinary thought, not merely decorative.
