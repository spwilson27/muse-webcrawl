William Spratling (22 September 1900 – 7 August 1967) was an American-born silversmith, designer and writer who is credited with transforming the Mexican silver-craft industry and reviving the fortunes of the town of Taxco in the state of Guerrero.

Early life and move to Mexico

Spratling grew up in Alabama and studied architecture, teaching design at Tulane University in New Orleans, where he shared a house with William Faulkner and co-authored the sketches book Sherwood Anderson and Other Famous Creoles (1926). He first visited Mexico in 1926 and settled there in 1929, becoming a friend of artists including Diego Rivera and David Alfaro Siqueiros.

Taxco silver

In 1931 Spratling founded the Taller de las Delicias workshop in Taxco, training local goldsmiths to produce his modernist silver jewellery and tableware inspired by pre-Columbian motifs. His success attracted apprentices — among them Antonio Pineda and the Castillo brothers — and launched an industry of hundreds of workshops that made "Taxco silver" internationally famous. He promoted Mexican crafts with exhibitions in the United States and advised Alaska Native arts programmes in the 1940s.

Later life

Spratling's memoir Little Mexico (1932) and his autobiography File on Spratling (1967) record his Mexican years. He died in a car accident near Taxco in 1967. His ranch workshop is now the William Spratling Museum, and November 27 is celebrated in Taxco as the Day of the Silversmith in his honour.
