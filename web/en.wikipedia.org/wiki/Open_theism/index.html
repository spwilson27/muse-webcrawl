Open theism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Open theism, also known as openness theology, is a theological movement in Christianity holding that the future is partly "open" — that is, God does not fully determine or exhaustively foreknow every future event, because free creatures' future decisions do not yet exist to be known. Open theists maintain that this view takes seriously the biblical portrayal of God as relating to creation in time, responding to prayer, and experiencing genuine interaction with human beings.

Core claims

Open theists generally affirm that God is omniscient in the sense of knowing everything that can be known: God perfectly knows the past and present, knows all possibilities and probabilities, and knows the future insofar as it is settled by past causes or by God's own intentions — but the future free actions of creatures are not yet realities and therefore are not objects of knowledge in the same way. God is accordingly portrayed as taking risks, adjusting plans in response to creatures, and being genuinely affected by the relationship, while remaining supremely wise and resourceful in achieving ultimate purposes.

History

Precursors can be found in figures such as the Socinians and some Arminian and process theologians, and in the "free will theism" of various 19th-century writers. The movement gained prominence in evangelical theology with the 1994 book The Openness of God, authored by Clark Pinnock, Richard Rice, John Sanders, William Hasker, and David Basinger. Sanders' The God Who Risks (1998) and Gregory Boyd's God of the Possible (2000) became influential presentations.

Criticism and controversy

Open theism has been sharply criticised by proponents of classical theism and by many Calvinist and some Arminian theologians, who argue that it compromises divine omniscience, sovereignty, immutability, and the reliability of prophecy, and that it departs from the historic doctrine of God. The controversy led to debates and membership challenges within the Evangelical Theological Society in the early 2000s; attempts to expel Pinnock and Sanders narrowly failed. Supporters reply that exhaustive definite foreknowledge is a philosophical assumption read into Scripture, that their view safeguards creaturely freedom and the meaningfulness of prayer, and that classical attributes can be reinterpreted rather than denied.
