Language of thought hypothesis

The language of thought hypothesis (LOTH), sometimes called the mental language hypothesis, is a view in philosophy of mind and cognitive science holding that thought occurs in a mental language, often called Mentalese. The hypothesis proposes that mental representations have a combinatorial syntax and semantics analogous to those of natural languages: complex thoughts are built from simpler components, and thinking consists in causal transitions between these representations that are sensitive to their syntactic structure.

Overview

The hypothesis was articulated most influentially by the philosopher Jerry Fodor, beginning with his 1975 book The Language of Thought and developed further in LOT 2 (2008). Fodor argued that the best explanation of the productivity and systematicity of thought — our ability to entertain indefinitely many thoughts, and the fact that anyone who can think "John loves Mary" can also think "Mary loves John" — is that thought has a compositional, language-like structure.

Arguments

Productivity and systematicity

Just as a finite vocabulary and grammar generate infinitely many sentences, a finite stock of mental symbols and rules of combination would explain how thinkers can grasp novel thoughts they have never encountered before. Systematicity — the intrinsic connection between structurally related cognitive capacities — is presented as an empirical regularity that a language of thought explains naturally, whereas rival accounts (such as classical connectionism) allegedly treat it as accidental.

Intentional realism and processes

Fodor's argument is also computational: psychological explanations routinely attribute beliefs and desires (propositional attitudes) with semantic content, and mental processes appear to operate on representations in a way that respects their logical form. A mental language gives these posits a physical realisation: syntactic structures tokened in the brain, over which computations are defined.

Criticism

Critics have raised several objections. Connectionists such as Paul Smolensky argued that subsymbolic systems might explain cognition without sentence-like representations, though Fodor and Zenon Pylyshyn replied that such systems either implement a language of thought or fail to explain systematicity. Others question whether the hypothesis genuinely explains productivity, whether infants and non-human animals — who lack natural language — could think in Mentalese, and whether the posited syntax is empirically detectable. Daniel Dennett and others have suggested the hypothesis risks an explanatory regress requiring an inner interpreter.

Relation to other theories

The hypothesis is closely tied to the computational theory of mind and to representationalism about mental states. It contrasts with eliminative materialism, with embodied and enactive approaches to cognition, and with views on which concepts are prototypes or perceptual symbols rather than amodal symbols. Earlier anticipations of the idea appear in medieval philosophy, notably William of Ockham's notion of a mental language.
