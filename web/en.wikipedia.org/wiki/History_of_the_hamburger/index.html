History of the hamburger

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the hamburger traces the development of the ground-beef sandwich from 19th-century antecedents — including the Hamburg steak brought by German immigrants to the United States — to its emergence as an icon of American fast food in the 20th century.

Origins

Minced-meat dishes are ancient; medieval Tartars were (probably apocryphally) said to tenderize meat under their saddles. In the German port city of Hamburg, chopped beef was formed into a "Hamburg steak," and German immigrants carried it to America in the 1800s, where it appeared on menus as an inexpensive beefsteak dish. Placing the cooked patty in bread or a bun is credited to various claimants around 1885–1904: Charlie Nagreen of Seymour, Wisconsin; the Menches brothers of Akron, Ohio; Oscar Weber Bilby of Tulsa; and Louis Lassen of New Haven, Connecticut, whose Louis' Lunch still serves burgers today.

Rise of the hamburger stand

White Castle, founded in Wichita, Kansas in 1921 by Billy Ingram and Walter Anderson, standardized the small, steamed "slider" and the hygienic white-tiled restaurant, transforming hamburgers from suspect fairground food into a respectable mass product. Drive-ins and car culture spread the burger across postwar America.

Fast food era

Richard and Maurice McDonald redesigned their San Bernardino restaurant around a speedee assembly line in 1948; Ray Kroc franchised McDonald's nationwide from 1955. Competitors such as Burger King (1954) and Wendy's (1969) followed, and the hamburger became a global symbol of American cuisine, adapted worldwide with countless regional variations — from Japan's rice burgers to India's vegetarian McAloo Tikki.
