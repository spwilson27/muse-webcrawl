Deafness is the partial or complete inability to hear. It is usually described in terms of hearing loss measured in decibels, and may be classified as mild, moderate, severe, or profound. Deafness may be present at birth (congenital) or acquired later in life, and it may affect one ear (unilateral) or both (bilateral).

Types and causes

Conductive hearing loss results from problems in the outer or middle ear—such as earwax blockage, infection (otitis media), or damage to the ossicles—that prevent sound from reaching the inner ear. Sensorineural hearing loss arises from damage to the cochlea or auditory nerve, caused by aging (presbycusis), prolonged noise exposure, certain infections, genetic conditions, or ototoxic medications. A mixed loss combines both. Worldwide, hundreds of millions of people live with disabling hearing loss, according to the World Health Organization.

Diagnosis and management

Hearing is assessed with audiometry, which measures thresholds across frequencies. Depending on the cause and severity, management includes hearing aids, bone-anchored devices, middle-ear surgery, and cochlear implants, which electrically stimulate the auditory nerve. Early identification in infants is important for language development.

Deaf culture and language

Many people who are born deaf or become deaf early are part of Deaf culture (often written with a capital D), a linguistic and cultural community centered on sign languages such as American Sign Language (ASL) and British Sign Language (BSL). From this perspective, deafness is viewed as a difference in communication and identity rather than solely a medical disability, and there is a long history of Deaf education, advocacy, and debate over oral versus signing approaches.
