Fort Driant is a fortification near Metz in north-eastern France. Built by Germany during the annexation of Alsace-Lorraine and originally named Feste Kronprinz, it formed part of the second ring of forts (the Moselstellung) defending Metz. After the First World War it passed to France and was renamed for a French officer; despite French improvements in the 1930s, it was not integrated into the Maginot Line proper.

Second World War

Fort Driant is best known for its role in the Battle of Metz in the autumn of 1944, during the Lorraine campaign of General George S. Patton's US Third Army. Held by German officer cadets and troops, the fort withstood repeated American assaults in September and October 1944: attacks by the 5th Infantry Division were repulsed with heavy casualties, as the fort's artillery batteries and mutually supporting positions commanded the approaches and its garrison sheltered underground from bombing and shellfire. Patton's forces eventually bypassed and isolated the position; the garrison did not surrender until December 1944, after Metz itself had fallen.

Description

The fort is a large dispersed work (Feste) of the German school, with separate artillery batteries, infantry positions and barracks connected by underground galleries, surrounded by wire and anti-tank obstacles. Its principal armament included turreted 100 mm and 150 mm guns. Today the site is a French military area and is not generally open to the public.
