Unit train

Summary
TypeFreight rail operation
CargoSingle bulk commodity
Typical length100+ cars

A unit train, also called a block train or a trainload, is a train in which all cars (wagons) carry the same commodity and are shipped from the same origin to the same destination, without being split up or stored en route. This stands in contrast to traditional wagonload freight, in which cars of different commodities are assembled and reassembled in classification yards.

Operation

Unit trains shuttle back and forth between a loading point — such as a coal mine, oil terminal, grain elevator or iron ore port — and a single unloading point, often a power station, steelworks or export terminal. Because the train is never broken up, cars can be loaded and unloaded with specialised high-capacity equipment. Coal trains are commonly loaded through flood loaders or rapid-discharge silos, sometimes while the train creeps forward continuously, and unloaded using rotary car dumpers that tip entire wagons without uncoupling them.

Economics

Moving large volumes of a single commodity in dedicated trainsets dramatically reduces handling costs, yard time and per-ton rates compared with manifest freight. Railroads typically offer substantial discounts for unit train traffic. The concept became widespread in North America from the 1960s onward, particularly for coal from the Powder River Basin, and underlies heavy-haul operations worldwide, including iron ore railways in Australia and Brazil where trains may exceed 300 wagons and 40,000 tonnes.

Variants

Related concepts include the shuttle train, which runs on a simplified tariff between a fixed origin and destination with guaranteed car supply, and distributed power operation, in which locomotives placed within and at the rear of the train are controlled remotely to manage the stresses of very long consists. Container traffic is often moved in dedicated intermodal unit trains between ports and inland terminals.
