Mughal Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Taj Mahal at Agra, white marble mausoleum built by Shah Jahan]

The Taj Mahal at Agra, white marble mausoleum built by Shah Jahan

The Mughal Empire (1526–1857) was an early-modern empire in South Asia founded by Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia who defeated the Delhi Sultanate at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526. At its greatest extent under Aurangzeb around 1707 it ruled almost the entire subcontinent and some 150 million people — perhaps a quarter of the world's population and GDP. Its legacy includes the Taj Mahal, miniature painting, Urdu and Indo-Islamic architecture, and the administrative framework later inherited by British India.

The great emperors

Babur's son Humayun lost and regained the throne; his grandson Akbar (r. 1556–1605) built the empire in earnest — conquering Gujarat and Bengal, organising the mansabdari system of ranked officials, abolishing the jizya tax on non-Muslims and pursuing a policy of religious conciliation. Jahangir (1605–1627) patronised painting; Shah Jahan (1628–1658) raised the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort and Delhi's Jama Masjid, then was deposed and imprisoned by his son Aurangzeb (1658–1707), whose expansion to the far south and stricter Islamic policies stretched imperial resources and provoked Maratha resistance.

Decline

After Aurangzeb the empire fragmented under weak emperors, invasions by Nader Shah of Persia (1739, who carried off the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor diamond) and Afghan armies, and the rise of the Marathas, Sikhs and the British East India Company. After the Indian Rebellion of 1857, in which rebels proclaimed the aged Bahadur Shah II their emperor, the British exiled him to Burma and formally ended the dynasty in 1858.
