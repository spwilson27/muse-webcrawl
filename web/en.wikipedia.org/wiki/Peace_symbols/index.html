Peace symbols

A number of peace symbols have been used many ways in various cultures and contexts. The dove and the olive branch were used symbolically by early Christians and then eventually became used widely for peace by the end of World War II. In the 1950s the "peace sign", as it is known today (the "victory" or V sign used differently), was designed as the logo of the British Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament by Gerald Holtom, and rapidly adopted by the American anti-war movement and counterculture.

The CND symbol

The best-known peace symbol — a circle containing a vertical line with two diagonal lines descending from the centre (☮) — was designed in 1958 by the artist Gerald Holtom for the first Aldermaston March. Holtom combined the semaphore signals for the letters "N" and "D" (for nuclear disarmament) within a circle representing the Earth. It was adopted by the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament and spread worldwide; it was never copyrighted, which aided its diffusion.

Dove and olive branch

The dove with an olive branch derives from the biblical story of Noah and from early Christian art, and was used by Picasso, whose lithograph La Colombe became an emblem of the post-1949 peace congresses. The broken rifle, used by War Resisters' International since 1921, symbolises refusal of military service.

Other symbols

The rainbow peace flag (bandiera della pace) became popular in Italy from 1961 and spread internationally during the 2003 Iraq War protests. The white poppy, distributed in Britain since 1933, is an alternative to the red remembrance poppy. The crane, folded in paper (origami), became a peace symbol through the story of Sadako Sasaki, a victim of the Hiroshima bombing.
