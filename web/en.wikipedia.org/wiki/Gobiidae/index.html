Gobiidae is a family of fishes, commonly known as the true gobies, in the order Gobiiformes. It is one of the largest families of fishes, with more than 2,000 described species in over 200 genera. Gobies are mostly small bottom-dwelling fishes of coastal marine, brackish and fresh waters throughout the world, especially diverse in the tropical Indo-Pacific.

Characteristics

Most gobies are small, typically less than 10 centimetres long; the family includes some of the smallest vertebrates known, such as Trimmatom nanus and the dwarf pygmy goby, which mature at under a centimetre. A characteristic feature is the fusion of the pelvic fins into a disc-shaped sucker, used to cling to rocks and coral in currents. Gobies lack a swim bladder in most species and spend much of their time resting on the substrate. Many are brightly coloured, and males commonly guard eggs laid on the underside of shells or rocks until they hatch.

Ecology

Gobies occupy an extraordinary range of habitats: coral reefs, tide pools, estuaries, rivers, caves and even semi-terrestrial mudflats at the water's edge. Some species live in close symbiosis with alpheid pistol shrimps, sharing a burrow in which the shrimp digs and the sharp-eyed goby keeps watch; others clean parasites from larger fish, and some freshwater species, such as the waterfall-climbing gobies of Hawaii, migrate between streams and the sea. Certain marine gobies, including the round goby, have become invasive species when transported in ship ballast water.
