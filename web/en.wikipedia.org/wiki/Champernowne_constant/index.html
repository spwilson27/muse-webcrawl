Champernowne constant

D. G. Champernowne, creator of the constant

In mathematics, the Champernowne constant C10 is a transcendental real constant whose decimal expansion has important properties. It is named after the economist and mathematician D. G. Champernowne, who published it in 1933 while still an undergraduate at Cambridge.

It is defined by concatenating representations of successive integers:

C10 = 0.12345678910111213141516…

Normality

Champernowne proved that C10 is a normal number in base 10: every finite sequence of digits occurs in its expansion with the frequency expected for a uniformly random sequence. It was the first number explicitly constructed and proved to be normal; Émile Borel had shown in 1909 that almost all real numbers are normal, but had given no example. The analogous constant formed in any base b is normal in that base.

Despite its normality, C10 is far from random: its digits are generated by a very simple rule, and its first n digits can be compressed into a program much shorter than n digits. It is therefore not algorithmically random.

Transcendence

Kurt Mahler proved in 1937 that the Champernowne constant is a transcendental number, as part of a broader result about numbers formed by concatenating values of polynomials. The constant is also known to have an irrationality measure of exactly 10.

Expansions and generalisations

The constant can be written as a series involving nested sums over the blocks of integers with a given number of digits. Generalisations include constants formed by concatenating the sequence of prime numbers (which is not known to be normal) and the Copeland–Erdős constant 0.235711131719…, formed from the primes, which Copeland and Erdős proved in 1946 to be normal in base 10.
