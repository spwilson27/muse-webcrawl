Geotechnical engineering

Geotechnical engineering, also known as geotechnics, is the branch of civil engineering concerned with the engineering behaviour of earth materials — soil and rock. It applies the principles of soil mechanics and rock mechanics to design foundations, retaining structures, slopes, tunnels, dams, and earthworks, and to assess natural hazards such as landslides, soil liquefaction, and subsidence. It is one of the oldest engineering disciplines in practice, but its scientific basis dates largely from Karl Terzaghi's work in the 1920s.

Practice

A typical project begins with a site investigation: boreholes, trial pits, in-situ tests such as the standard penetration test (SPT) and cone penetration test (CPT), and laboratory testing of samples to determine stratigraphy, strength, compressibility, and groundwater conditions. The geotechnical engineer then designs shallow or deep foundations (footings, rafts, piles), analyses slope stability and settlement, and specifies ground improvement such as compaction, grouting, or geosynthetics where necessary.

Key concepts

Central ideas include Terzaghi's principle of effective stress, shear strength and the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion, consolidation theory, lateral earth pressure theories of Rankine and Coulomb, and bearing-capacity analysis. Because ground conditions are inherently uncertain, geotechnical design relies heavily on engineering judgement, precedent, and the observational method pioneered by Ralph Peck.

Sub-disciplines

Related fields include earthquake (geotechnical earthquake) engineering, offshore geotechnics for oil platforms and wind farms, mining geomechanics, pavement engineering, and environmental geotechnics dealing with landfill liners and contaminated land.
