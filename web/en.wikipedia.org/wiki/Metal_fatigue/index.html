Metal fatigue

Metal fatigue is the weakening of a metal caused by repeated cyclic loading. It is the progressive and localised structural damage that occurs when a material is subjected to fluctuating stresses, leading to the initiation and growth of cracks and eventual fracture — often at stress levels well below the material's static strength.

Mechanism

Fatigue damage typically begins at stress concentrators such as notches, holes, surface scratches or inclusions, where microscopic cracks nucleate at slip bands. The cracks then grow incrementally with each cycle — growth visible on fracture surfaces as beach marks and striations — until the remaining cross-section can no longer carry the load and sudden failure occurs.

Measurement and design

Fatigue behaviour is characterised by S–N curves, plotting stress amplitude against the number of cycles to failure; some materials such as steels exhibit an endurance limit below which fatigue failure does not occur. Engineers limit fatigue through design against stress concentrations, surface treatments such as shot peening, and inspection regimes. Fatigue has been implicated in major accidents, including the de Havilland Comet crashes of 1954, which drove modern fail-safe design practice.
