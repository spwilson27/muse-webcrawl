Batik

Batik is a technique of wax-resist dyeing applied to cloth, originating in Indonesia, particularly on the island of Java. Patterns are drawn on fabric with hot wax using a spouted tool called a canting or printed with a copper stamp called a cap; the cloth is then dyed, and the wax resists the dye so the pattern remains in the undyed (or previously dyed) colour. The process may be repeated with successive waxings and dye baths to build up multicoloured designs.

History

Wax-resist techniques are ancient and examples survive from Egypt, China, India and West Africa, but the art reached its highest development in Java, where batik became deeply embedded in court culture in the royal centres of Yogyakarta and Surakarta. During the Dutch colonial period, batik spread along Java's north coast (batik pesisir), absorbing Chinese and European motifs and brighter colours. In the nineteenth century, Dutch and later industrial producers developed roller-printing to imitate batik, and wax prints became hugely popular in West Africa.

Cultural significance

Traditional Javanese motifs carry symbolic meanings, and certain patterns were historically reserved for royalty. In Indonesia, batik is worn for formal occasions, and "Batik Friday" is observed in many offices. In 2009, UNESCO inscribed Indonesian batik on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Technique

The main variants are batik tulis (hand-drawn, the most laborious and prized), batik cap (stamped), and printed imitations. Natural dyes such as indigo and soga brown were traditional; synthetic dyes are now common.
