History wars

The history wars is a term used in Australia for a public and political debate, prominent from the 1990s into the 2000s, over the interpretation of the history of European colonisation of Australia and its effects on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. The debate concerned the scale of frontier violence, the treatment of Indigenous children (the Stolen Generations), and how national history should be taught and commemorated.

Background

From the 1960s, historians such as Henry Reynolds documented violent conflict on the Australian frontier, challenging older celebratory narratives of peaceful settlement. The 1968 Boyer Lectures by anthropologist W. E. H. Stanner had already criticised the "great Australian silence" about Indigenous history. The 1997 Bringing Them Home report on the Stolen Generations intensified public discussion.

The debate

The term "history wars" gained currency during the prime ministership of John Howard (1996–2007), who criticised what he called a "black armband view of history". Historian Geoffrey Blainey had earlier coined the phrase "black armband" for overly negative readings of the past. In 2002–2003 journalist Keith Windschuttle's The Fabrication of Aboriginal History disputed historians' estimates of Aboriginal deaths in Tasmania, provoking strong rebuttals from scholars including Robert Manne and Stuart Macintyre, whose 2003 book gave the debate its name. Prime Minister Kevin Rudd's 2008 apology to the Stolen Generations marked a shift in official commemoration, though arguments over curricula, museums and memorials continue.

Themes

  • Whether frontier killings should be classed as massacre or genocide, and how many deaths occurred
  • The intentions and effects of child-removal policies
  • Whether the national story should emphasise achievement or dispossession
  • The role of academic historians versus politicians and media in shaping public history
