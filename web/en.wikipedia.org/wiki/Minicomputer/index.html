Minicomputer

A minicomputer, or colloquially mini, is a type of general-purpose computer developed from the mid-1960s, in a size and cost range between mainframe computers and microcomputers. Minicomputers filled the gap between large mainframes and smaller dedicated systems, typically selling for well under US$25,000 (in 1960s terms), using integrated circuits and core memory, and supporting timesharing with multiple simultaneous users.

History

The term emerged in the mid-1960s. Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) launched the PDP-8 in 1965, widely regarded as the first successful minicomputer; it cost about US$18,500, occupied a small cabinet or desktop, and sold in the tens of thousands. DEC's later PDP-11 (1970) and VAX (1977) lines became industry standards. Competitors included Data General with its Nova and Eclipse lines, Hewlett-Packard's HP 2100 and HP 3000, Prime Computer, and Wang Laboratories.

Characteristics

Minicomputers typically had word lengths of 12, 16, or 32 bits, core or semiconductor memory, and support for peripherals such as teletypes, line printers, paper-tape and magnetic-tape drives, and later disk drives and CRT terminals. They were widely used for process control, laboratory automation, engineering computation, timesharing services, and business data processing, and they hosted influential operating systems such as DEC's RT-11, RSX-11 and VMS, and early versions of Unix at Bell Labs, where the system was developed on a PDP-7 and PDP-11.

Decline

The rise of microprocessor-based microcomputers and workstations in the 1980s eroded the minicomputer market. By the early 1990s most minicomputer vendors had failed, been acquired, or converted their product lines into servers. The term has largely fallen out of use, surviving in the "midrange" designation applied to systems such as IBM's AS/400 descendants.
