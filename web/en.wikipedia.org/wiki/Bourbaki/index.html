Nicolas Bourbaki is the collective pseudonym of a group of mathematicians, predominantly French, who write a series of books presenting an exposition of modern advanced mathematics, beginning in 1935. The group's aim was to ground mathematics on set theory and to present the subject with the greatest possible rigour and generality, restructuring it around the notion of mathematical structure.

Origins

The group was founded in Paris in late 1934 by young mathematicians — including André Weil, Henri Cartan, Jean Dieudonné and Claude Chevalley — dissatisfied with the existing analysis textbooks used in French universities. The pseudonym was borrowed from Charles-Denis Bourbaki, a French general of Greek origin, and the group surrounded "Nicolas Bourbaki" with an elaborate fictional biography, including a birthplace in the invented country of Poldavia. Members are required to retire at age 50, keeping the group perpetually renewed.

Éléments de mathématique

The group's treatise, the Éléments de mathématique (note the singular "mathématique"), proceeds in a strict logical order from set theory through algebra, topology, analysis, Lie groups and integration. Bourbaki introduced or popularised much modern notation and vocabulary, including the symbols ∅ for the empty set, and the terms injective, surjective and bijective. Drafts were debated at regular "Bourbaki congresses" and rewritten until unanimous approval was reached.

Influence and criticism

Bourbaki profoundly shaped 20th-century pure mathematics and influenced the "New Math" school reforms of the 1960s. Critics, including some former members, have charged the group with excessive abstraction, neglect of applications and geometry, and a rigid style that sidelined areas such as probability. Output slowed greatly after the 1970s, though occasional new volumes and revised editions have continued to appear.
