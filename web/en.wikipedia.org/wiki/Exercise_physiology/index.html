Exercise physiology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Exercise physiology is the physiology of physical exercise: the study of the acute responses and chronic adaptations of the body's systems — muscular, cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine, metabolic and nervous — to physical activity. It is an applied discipline drawing on anatomy, biochemistry, biomechanics and nutrition, and it underpins sports science, physical therapy, cardiac rehabilitation and occupational health.

Energy systems

Muscle contraction is powered by adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which must be continuously resynthesised. Three systems share the work: the phosphagen system (ATP–phosphocreatine), supplying maximal effort for a few seconds; anaerobic glycolysis, breaking down glucose without oxygen and dominating efforts of roughly 30 seconds to 2 minutes; and the oxidative (aerobic) system, using carbohydrates and fats in the mitochondria to sustain prolonged exercise. The proportion contributed by each depends on intensity and duration.

Responses and adaptations

During exercise, heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output rise, blood is redistributed to working muscles, ventilation increases, and sweating regulates temperature. Regular training produces characteristic adaptations: endurance training increases maximal oxygen uptake (VO2 max), mitochondrial density, capillarisation and blood volume, while resistance training increases muscle fibre size and strength. Key laboratory measures include VO2 max, the lactate (anaerobic) threshold, running economy and body composition.

History and applications

The field's modern foundations were laid in the early 20th century by A. V. Hill's work on oxygen uptake and by the Harvard Fatigue Laboratory (1927–1947), which studied workers, soldiers and athletes under stress, including at altitude and in extreme climates. Today exercise physiologists work in elite sport, hospitals and clinics prescribing exercise for cardiovascular, metabolic and respiratory disease, in the military and spaceflight — where countermeasures fight the deconditioning suffered by astronauts — and in research on ageing, obesity and diabetes.
