The Paris Observatory (French: Observatoire de Paris) is an astronomical observatory whose primary site is in the 14th arrondissement of Paris, with a second major site at Meudon. It is the largest French astronomical research centre and among the largest in the world. Founded in 1667, it is one of the oldest observatories still in operation.

History

The observatory was established under King Louis XIV, promoted by his minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert as part of a project of scientific prestige. The building, completed in 1672, was designed by architect Claude Perrault. Giovanni Domenico Cassini became its first prominent director, beginning a family dynasty of directors lasting over a century.

The Paris meridian, running through the observatory, was long a rival to the Greenwich meridian as the world's prime meridian; France adopted Greenwich Mean Time only in 1911, and then under the legal fiction of calling it "Paris mean time, retarded by nine minutes and twenty-one seconds".

Work and discoveries

Astronomers at the observatory measured the speed of light (Ole Rømer, using observations of Jupiter's moon Io in 1676), catalogued stars and nebulae (Charles Messier's catalogue was compiled here), and produced the first accurate map of the Moon. Urbain Le Verrier computed the position of Neptune from perturbations of Uranus, leading to its discovery in 1846.

Today the observatory's research spans solar physics, astrophysics, cosmology, and space instrumentation, and it publishes the Connaissance des Temps ephemeris, one of the world's oldest annual scientific publications (since 1679).
