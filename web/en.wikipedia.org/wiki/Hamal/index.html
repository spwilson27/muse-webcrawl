Hamal

Hamal (Alpha Arietis)
Constellation: Aries
Apparent magnitude: 2.00
Spectral type: K1 III
Distance: about 66 light-years
Radius: about 15 solar radii
Mass: about 1.5 solar masses

Hamal, designation Alpha Arietis (α Arietis, abbreviated Alpha Ari, α Ari), is the brightest star in the northern zodiacal constellation of Aries. It has an apparent visual magnitude of 2.00, making it among the brighter stars in the night sky. Based on parallax measurements, Hamal lies about 66 light-years (20 parsecs) from the Sun.

Name

The name Hamal derives from the Arabic Rās al-Ḥamal (رأس الحمل), "head of the ram", after its position marking the ram's head in the traditional depiction of the constellation. In 2016 the International Astronomical Union's Working Group on Star Names approved the name Hamal for this star. Its Flamsteed designation is 13 Arietis.

Properties

Hamal is an evolved giant star with a stellar classification of K1 III. It is thought to have exhausted the hydrogen at its core and left the main sequence. With an interferometrically measured angular diameter and its known distance, its physical radius is about 15 times that of the Sun, and it shines with roughly 90 times the Sun's luminosity from an outer atmosphere with an effective temperature near 4,480 K, giving it the orange hue characteristic of a K-type star. Spectroscopic measurements indicate a slight variability, of about 0.06 magnitudes, and the star is a mild candidate spectroscopic binary: a 2011 study found evidence for an unseen low-mass companion in a long-period orbit, though the result remains tentative. Like many red giants, Hamal is a source of X-ray emission from a hot corona.

In culture

Because of its position near the celestial equator and the ecliptic, Hamal was an important reference star in antiquity. Around 2000 BC it lay close to the vernal equinox point — the "First Point of Aries" — and in some Babylonian and Greek traditions it marked the beginning of the zodiac. Astrologers have associated it with violence and cruelty, following Ptolemy, who likened its influence to that of Mars and Saturn.
