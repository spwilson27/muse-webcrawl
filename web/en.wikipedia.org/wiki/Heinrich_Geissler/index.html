Heinrich Geissler

Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Geißler (26 May 1814, in Igelshieb, Thuringia – 24 January 1879, in Bonn) was a German physicist and glassblower, best known for his invention of the Geissler tube and the mercury vacuum pump that made its manufacture possible. Working mainly in Bonn, where he established a workshop for scientific instruments, Geissler was a master glassblower whose skills were in demand across European laboratories.

Work

Around 1857 Geissler perfected a vacuum pump using a column of mercury with no mechanical valves, achieving vacua far better than previous pumps. With it he produced sealed glass discharge tubes containing rarefied gases, with platinum electrodes fused through the glass. When connected to an induction coil, the tubes glowed with colours characteristic of the enclosed gas—the decorative and experimental Geissler tubes. These tubes became standard instruments in the study of electrical discharge in gases and were the direct ancestors of the cathode-ray tube; experiments with them by Julius Plücker in Bonn and by William Crookes in England led onward to the discovery of cathode rays, the electron and the neon lamp. Geissler also invented instruments such as the vapour-pressure thermometer and a form of hygrometer, and he taught glassblowing to many students.

Honours

The University of Bonn awarded him an honorary doctorate in 1868. The lunar crater Geissler is named after him.
