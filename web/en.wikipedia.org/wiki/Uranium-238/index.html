Uranium-238 (238U or U-238) is the most common isotope of uranium found in nature, making up more than 99% of natural uranium. Unlike uranium-235, it is non-fissile with thermal neutrons — that is, it cannot sustain a nuclear chain reaction in a thermal-neutron reactor — but it is fertile: neutron capture converts it into fissile plutonium-239. Uranium-238 has a half-life of about 4.47 billion years, roughly the age of the Earth, and its decay series is a major source of terrestrial heat and of natural radioactivity in rocks.

Properties and decay

With 92 protons and 146 neutrons, U-238 decays by alpha emission to thorium-234, beginning the uranium (4n+2) decay series that passes through radium-226 and radon-222 and terminates at stable lead-206. Because the ratios of uranium to lead isotopes change predictably over time, uranium–lead dating of minerals such as zircon provides some of the most precise ages in geochronology, including the age of the Earth itself.

Uses

U-238 is the feedstock for producing plutonium-239 in breeder reactors and for nuclear weapons programmes. Material from which most U-235 has been removed — depleted uranium — is used where extreme density is valuable: armour-piercing munitions, tank armour, aircraft counterweights, and radiation shielding. It is also the starting material for uranium enrichment; enrichment tails are overwhelmingly U-238. Pure U-238 is weakly radioactive (specific activity about 12.4 kBq/g), and its chemical toxicity as a heavy metal is generally considered the greater health concern in most exposure scenarios.

History

Uranium's isotopes were characterized in the 1930s; the identification of U-235 as the fissile isotope (by Arthur Dempster's mass spectrometry and the work of the Hahn–Meitner–Frisch circle on fission) set the course of the Manhattan Project, whose central industrial challenge was separating the rare U-235 from the abundant U-238.
