Anabaptism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anabaptism (from Neo-Latin anabaptista, from the Greek ἀναβαπτισμός, "re-baptism") is a Christian movement that traces its origins to the Radical Reformation of the 16th century. Anabaptists hold that baptism is valid only when the candidate freely confesses their faith in Christ and requests to be baptised — "believer's baptism" — and therefore reject the baptism of infants. The name was given to them by their opponents, who considered a second baptism to be a repetition; Anabaptists themselves maintain that infant baptism is not true baptism at all.

Modern descendants of the movement include the Amish, Hutterites, Mennonites, and many Brethren groups; together, Anabaptist churches counted roughly two million baptised members worldwide in the early 21st century.

Origins

Anabaptism emerged in Zurich, Switzerland, in the circle around Ulrich Zwingli. On January 21, 1525, Conrad Grebel, Felix Manz, and George Blaurock — frustrated that Zwingli would not break more radically with the city council over reform — baptised one another as adults, an act conventionally regarded as the founding of the movement. The Swiss Brethren, as they called themselves, spread quickly through German-speaking Europe. Their 1527 Schleitheim Confession set out distinctive positions: believer's baptism, the ban (church discipline), separation from "the world", refusal of oaths, and nonresistance — the refusal to bear the sword.

Persecution and the Münster rebellion

Anabaptists were persecuted ferociously by both Catholic and Protestant authorities, who regarded rebaptism as a capital offence under imperial law; thousands were executed by burning, beheading, or drowning — a bitter parody of their baptismal practice. Felix Manz was drowned in the Limmat at Zurich in 1527, and the Tyrolean leader Jakob Hutter was burned at the stake in 1536.

The movement's reputation was gravely damaged by the Münster rebellion of 1534–35, in which radical Anabaptists under John of Leiden seized the German city of Münster, proclaimed a theocratic "New Jerusalem", and were crushed after a siege amid atrocities on both sides. Mainstream Anabaptists repudiated the violence, and under leaders such as Menno Simons — a former Dutch priest whose name the Mennonites bear — the movement consolidated around pacifism and communal discipline. The Hutterites, organised by Jakob Hutter, practised full community of goods in Moravia.

Beliefs and practices

Historically characteristic Anabaptist convictions include:

  • Believer's baptism upon confession of faith
  • The church as a voluntary, disciplined community of believers, separated from the state
  • Nonresistance and pacifism; refusal of military service and (usually) of oaths
  • A strong emphasis on discipleship (Nachfolge Christi) and simple living
  • Congregational authority and, in many groups, mutual aid within the community

Groups such as the Amish (who split from the Mennonites under Jakob Ammann in 1693) maintain distinctive dress and limits on technology, while many Mennonite and Brethren congregations are largely indistinguishable in dress from their neighbours but retain the movement's peace witness.
