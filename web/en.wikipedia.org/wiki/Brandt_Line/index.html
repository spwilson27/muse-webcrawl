Brandt Line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Brandt Line is a visual depiction of the North–South divide between developed and developing countries, based on per-capita GDP. It was proposed by former German Chancellor Willy Brandt in the 1980s in the report of the Independent Commission on International Development Issues, commonly known as the Brandt Commission, chaired by Brandt. The report, North–South: A Programme for Survival (1980), called for large-scale transfers of resources from the developed "North" to the developing "South" to reduce global inequality.

The line

The line encircles the world at a latitude of approximately 30° N, dipping southwards to include some wealthier states and excluding others: it passes around the Mediterranean, looping down to place Australia and New Zealand in the North, and Japan, Singapore, and the Asian "tiger" economies in the North as well, while most of Africa, Asia south of the line's meanders, and Latin America fall in the South. The division captures the observation that the world's richest countries lie overwhelmingly in the temperate Northern Hemisphere.

Criticism and legacy

The Brandt Line has been criticised as an oversimplification: it was already somewhat dated on publication (for example, it grouped the Soviet bloc with the North despite lower living standards, and wealthy oil states of the Middle East with the South), and the rise of China, India, and other emerging economies has blurred it further. Nevertheless, it remains an influential and widely reproduced teaching device in geography and development studies, and the Brandt Commission's advocacy influenced debates on international development aid, debt relief, and fairer terms of trade.
