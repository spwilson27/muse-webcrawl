Hearth

A hearth is the place in a home where a fire is or was traditionally kept for heating and cooking, usually constituted by a horizontal hearthstone and often enclosed to varying degrees by a fireplace, oven, chimney or fireback. From the Neolithic onward the hearth was the centre of the household — archaeological sites are often identified by their hearths — and the word retains strong figurative meanings of home and family.

History

Early hearths were simple fire pits, sometimes stone-lined, in the centre of a dwelling with smoke escaping through the roof. Medieval halls kept a central open hearth until the adoption of wall fireplaces and chimneys from about the 12th century in northern Europe, which moved the hearth to an external wall. Hearths remained the main source of heat and the cooking place until stoves and central heating displaced them in the 19th and 20th centuries; the raised hearth, mantelpiece and fireside furnishings became decorative survivals of the working hearth.

Cultural significance

The hearth was sacred in many cultures: Hestia in Greek religion and Vesta in Rome personified it, and Rome's public hearth was tended by the Vestal Virgins. "Hearth and home" remains a set phrase in English; hearth taxes (levied in England from 1662) were assessed per hearth. In archaeology, hearth features preserve charcoal, burnt bone and stone that inform dating and reconstructions of diet and activity areas.
