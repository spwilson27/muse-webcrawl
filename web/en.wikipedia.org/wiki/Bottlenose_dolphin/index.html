Bottlenose dolphin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bottlenose dolphin leaping clear of the water beside a boat's bow wave]
A bottlenose dolphin leaping clear of the water beside a boat's bow wave

The common bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) is one of the best-known dolphin species, distributed in warm and temperate seas worldwide. Growing to 2–4 metres, it is highly intelligent, with a large brain, complex social life, and sophisticated echolocation. Bottlenose dolphins are the dolphins most often seen in aquariums and popular culture (such as the television series Flipper), and are used by some navies for mine detection.

Biology and behaviour

Bottlenose dolphins live in fission–fusion societies with long-term alliances — notably the multi-level male alliances of Shark Bay in Australia — and communicate with whistles (including individually distinctive 'signature whistles' functioning like names), clicks for echolocation and body language. They hunt cooperatively, strand-feed on mud banks in some regions, use tools — Shark Bay dolphins carry sponges to protect their rostrums while foraging — and exhibit culture passed between generations.

Cognition

Decades of research show self-recognition in mirrors, understanding of artificial symbol sequences, numerical competence, metacognition and social learning, placing dolphins among the most cognitively complex animals (see Animal cognition). Calves stay with mothers for years learning foraging specialisations; dialects and traditions differ between communities.

Conservation and human relations

The species as a whole is Least Concern, but coastal populations face bycatch, pollution, noise and habitat loss; some (such as the Mediterranean and Black Sea subspecies groupings) are threatened. Drive hunts (notably at Taiji, Japan) and captivity are controversial; the US and Indian governments have at times restricted military and display use on ethical grounds.
