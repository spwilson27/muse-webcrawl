Conspiracy theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conspiracy theory
DefinitionAn explanation for events invoking a secret plot by powerful actors, contrary to the mainstream account
Key scholarshipHofstadter, "The Paranoid Style" (1964); Popper, "conspiracy theory of society"
RelatedMisinformation, disinformation, pseudohistory, scapegoating

A conspiracy theory is an explanation for an event or situation that asserts the existence of a conspiracy by powerful and sinister groups, often political in motivation, when other explanations are more probable. The term generally carries a negative connotation, implying that the appeal rests on prejudice, emotional conviction or insufficient evidence rather than on verifiable fact. Conspiracy theories resist falsification: evidence against the conspiracy, or an absence of evidence for it, is itself reinterpreted as originating from the conspiracy.

Terminology and history

The phrase entered English in the late 19th century and acquired its pejorative sense in the mid-20th century. Real conspiracies do exist — Watergate, the Tuskegee experiment, COINTELPRO, and tobacco-industry suppression of research were all concealed plots later proven — and scholars distinguish such documented cases from speculative theories about events such as the JFK assassination (multiple official investigations notwithstanding), the Moon landing, 9/11, chemtrails, flat Earth, "birther" claims, and QAnon. Richard Hofstadter's 1964 essay "The Paranoid Style in American Politics" is the classic analysis of conspiracist rhetoric in democratic politics.

Psychology

Research links belief in conspiracy theories to pattern-seeking (apophenia), agency detection, proportionality bias (matching big events to big causes), distrust of authority, existential anxiety, and a need for uniqueness and control. Beliefs cluster: people who endorse one conspiracy theory tend to endorse others, even mutually contradictory ones, a pattern called monological belief. Education and analytic thinking modestly reduce susceptibility; personal involvement and perceived threat increase it.

Spread and consequences

Conspiracy theories flourish during crises and spread rapidly through mass media and social-network recommendation systems. They can have serious consequences: vaccine conspiracies undermine public health, election-fraud conspiracism corrodes democratic institutions, and targeted theories have inspired violence (for example, the 2016 "Pizzagate" shooting). Governments and platforms have responded with debunking, "prebunking" (inoculation against manipulation techniques), labelling, and — controversially — removal of content, raising free-speech debates.

Scholarly debate

Philosophers debate whether conspiracy theories should be rejected as a class or judged case by case ("particularism"), noting that the label can be used to stigmatize legitimate suspicion of official accounts, especially where institutions have conspired before. Historians generally treat the term as marking explanations unsupported by the weight of evidence and maintained by self-sealing reasoning.
