Healthcare-associated infection

A healthcare-associated infection (HAI), also called a nosocomial infection (from the Greek nosokomeion, "hospital"), is an infection that a patient acquires during the course of receiving medical treatment in a hospital or other healthcare facility, which was neither present nor incubating at the time of admission. HAIs also include infections acquired in hospital that appear after discharge, and occupational infections among healthcare staff.

Types

The most common types of HAI are:

  • Central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI)
  • Catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI) — among the most frequent HAIs overall
  • Surgical site infections (SSI)
  • Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and other hospital-acquired pneumonias
  • Clostridioides difficile infection, often following antibiotic exposure

Common causative organisms include Staphylococcus aureus (including methicillin-resistant strains), Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella species, enterococci (including vancomycin-resistant strains) and fungi such as Candida. Antimicrobial resistance is a major concern in HAIs.

Burden

HAIs are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Surveillance estimates suggest that roughly one in twenty to one in thirty hospital patients in developed countries acquires an HAI, with higher rates in intensive care units and in low-resource settings. They prolong hospital stays, increase costs, and contribute to the emergence of resistant pathogens.

Prevention

Prevention relies on infection control programmes: hand hygiene (historically pioneered by Ignaz Semmelweis), sterile technique for invasive devices, prompt removal of catheters and lines, isolation precautions, environmental cleaning, aseptic wound care and antimicrobial stewardship. Care "bundles" — small sets of evidence-based practices applied together — have been shown to markedly reduce device-associated infections.
