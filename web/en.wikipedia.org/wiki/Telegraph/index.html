Telegraph

A telegraph is a system for transmitting messages over long distances using coded signals, most famously the electrical telegraph of the nineteenth century, which sent text messages along wires using Morse code. The word derives from the Greek tēle ("far") and graphein ("to write"). Before electrical systems, optical telegraphs — chains of semaphore towers — relayed signals visually.

Optical telegraphs

The most extensive early network was built in France by Claude Chappe beginning in the 1790s. Towers with movable arms relayed coded messages across hundreds of kilometres; by the 1850s the French network exceeded 5,000 km of lines. Similar systems operated in Sweden, Britain, and elsewhere, but they were limited by daylight, weather, and the cost of staffing every tower.

Electrical telegraph

Experiments with electricity in the early 1800s culminated in practical systems by William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone in Britain (1837) and by Samuel Morse in the United States, whose first line between Washington, D.C., and Baltimore opened in 1844 with the message "What hath God wrought." Morse's code of dots and dashes, tapped out with a key and recorded or heard as clicks on a sounder, became the international standard.

Telegraph networks spread rapidly along railway lines and across continents. The first durable transatlantic cable was completed in 1866, collapsing communication times between Europe and America from weeks to minutes. Companies such as Western Union built vast commercial networks, and telegraphy transformed journalism, finance, warfare, and diplomacy — for the first time, information could travel far faster than any messenger.

Decline and legacy

The telephone, telex, fax, and ultimately the internet displaced the telegram through the twentieth century. Western Union sent its last telegram in 2006, and India's state service — among the world's last large networks — closed in 2013. The telegraph nevertheless established the template for all later telecommunications: coded, near-instant, networked communication, along with new social practices such as terse "telegraphic" prose and time-zone standardization.
