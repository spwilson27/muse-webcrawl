Waterfall model

The waterfall model proceeds through sequential phases, with progress flowing downward like a waterfall.

The waterfall model is a breakdown of development activities into linear sequential phases, where progress is seen as flowing steadily downwards (like a waterfall) through the phases of conception, initiation, analysis, design, construction, testing, deployment and maintenance. The model is a software development life cycle (SDLC) method historically used by many organizations, and is often cited as the traditional alternative to agile and iterative methodologies.

Origin

The model is commonly attributed to Winston W. Royce, who described it in a 1970 paper, "Managing the Development of Large Software Systems". Ironically, Royce presented the strictly sequential approach as flawed: he explicitly argued that it was risky and invited failure, and advocated adding feedback loops, prototyping and customer involvement — elements of the "iterative" model he also sketched. Despite this, the simplified sequential version became entrenched, partly through adoption in United States Department of Defense standard DOD-STD-2167A in the 1980s.

Phases and criticism

The canonical phases are requirements specification, system and software design, implementation and unit testing, integration and system testing, and operation and maintenance. Each phase is completed before the next begins, and its outputs (such as requirements and design documents) serve as the input to the next phase. The model works best when requirements are fixed and well understood, as in construction-like projects or safety-critical systems with strict documentation needs. It is criticized for inflexibility: errors or changed requirements discovered late in testing are expensive to fix, and working software only emerges at the end, delaying feedback. This critique motivated the rise of agile methodologies, iterative development and the spiral model.
