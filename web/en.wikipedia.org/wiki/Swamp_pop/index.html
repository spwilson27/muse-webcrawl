Swamp pop

Swamp pop is a Louisiana music genre created by Cajun and Creole musicians of the Acadiana region in south Louisiana and southeast Texas. It combines New Orleans-style rhythm and blues, country and western, and traditional French Louisiana musical influences. Characterized by highly emotional, often lovelorn vocals, honky-tonk piano, wailing saxophones, and a strong backbeat, swamp pop produced a string of national hits in the late 1950s and early 1960s by artists such as Bobby Charles, Jimmy Clanton, Rod Bernard, and Phil Phillips.

Swamp pop flourished in the dancehalls of Acadiana in the 1950s and 1960s.

Characteristics

The classic swamp pop recording features a slow-to-midtempo ballad or upbeat rocker with triplet piano figures, "crying" lead vocals influenced by Fats Domino and country singers alike, and lyrics — often in English, sometimes in French — about lost love and bayou life. The genre is distinguished from its close relative swamp blues by its pop orientation and romantic lyricism.

History

Swamp pop emerged as young Cajun musicians, themselves second-generation heirs to French Louisiana traditions, absorbed the R&B heard on radio from New Orleans and Nashville. Landmark hits include Bobby Charles's "See You Later, Alligator" (made famous by Bill Haley), Phil Phillips's "Sea of Love" (1959), Rod Bernard's "This Should Go on Forever" (1959), Jimmy Clanton's "Just a Dream" (1958), and Dale and Grace's "I'm Leaving It Up to You" (1963), a national number one. Recorded largely in small studios in Crowley and Ville Platte, the music faded from the charts after the British Invasion but remained a living regional tradition, celebrated at Louisiana festivals into the twenty-first century.
