Curtain wall (fortification)

A curtain wall is a defensive wall built between two towers, bastions or other strongpoints of a fortification. In a castle, the curtain walls form the enclosing circuit that links the towers and the gatehouse, sheltering the courtyard and buildings within. The term derives from the way the wall "hangs" between its towers, like a curtain between posts. Curtain walls are among the most characteristic features of medieval castles and walled towns.

Design

Curtain walls were built high and thick — often 2 to 3 metres thick at the base and 7 to 12 metres high — of rubble masonry faced with dressed stone, sometimes battered (sloped) at the foot to deflect missiles and frustrate mining. Along the top ran a wall walk (allure) protected by a parapet with battlements, through which defenders could shoot; later designs added overhanging machicolations and wooden or stone hoardings so missiles and liquids could be dropped on attackers at the base. Arrow slits pierced the wall at intervals, and the flanking towers allowed defenders to fire along the face of the curtain — the reason curtains were set between towers rather than left unbroken.

Development

Enclosing circuits of linked towers are ancient: Roman city walls and the fortifications of the ancient Near East used the same principle. In medieval Europe, castles progressed from timber palisades and the stone keeps of the 11th century to concentric designs — famously in the Crusader castle Krak des Chevaliers and Edward I's castles in Wales such as Conwy and Caerphilly — where inner and outer curtain walls created successive lines of defence. The spread of gunpowder artillery from the 15th century made tall thin curtains vulnerable; engineers responded with lower, massively thickened walls and angled bastions in the trace italienne style, in which the straight stretches between bastions continued to be called curtains.
