Kőnig's theorem (graph theory)

In the mathematical area of graph theory, Kőnig's theorem, proved by Dénes Kőnig (1931), describes an equivalence between the maximum matching problem and the minimum vertex cover problem in bipartite graphs. It was discovered independently, also in 1931, by Hungarian mathematician Jenő Egerváry in the more general case of weighted graphs; for this reason the result is also called the Kőnig–Egerváry theorem.

Statement of the theorem

A matching in a graph is a set of edges no two of which share a vertex; a vertex cover is a set of vertices that touches every edge of the graph. In any graph, the size of a maximum matching is at most the size of a minimum vertex cover: each edge of the matching needs a distinct vertex of the cover. Kőnig's theorem states that in a bipartite graph the two quantities are equal:

size of maximum matching = size of minimum vertex cover.

Proof

One proof constructs the vertex cover from a maximum matching. Let U be the set of unmatched vertices on the left side of the bipartition, and let Z be the set of vertices reachable from U by alternating paths (paths alternating between edges outside and inside the matching). The set K = (L \ Z) ∪ (R ∩ Z) is a vertex cover, and it has exactly one endpoint of each matched edge, hence size equal to the matching. Modern proofs often derive the theorem from the max-flow min-cut theorem by modelling the bipartite graph as a flow network: a maximum matching corresponds to a maximum flow, and a minimum vertex cover to a minimum cut.

Applications and related results

The theorem implies Hall's marriage theorem and is equivalent to several other min-max relations in combinatorics, including the Dilworth decomposition and the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem on doubly stochastic matrices. Applications include scheduling, assignment problems, and the analysis of bipartite networks. The theorem fails in general graphs: in a triangle, the maximum matching has size one while the minimum vertex cover has size two.
