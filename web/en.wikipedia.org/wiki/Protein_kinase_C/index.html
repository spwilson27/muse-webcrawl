Protein kinase C

Protein kinase C, commonly abbreviated to PKC (EC 2.7.11.13), is a family of protein kinase enzymes that are involved in controlling the function of other proteins through the phosphorylation of hydroxyl groups of serine and threonine amino acid residues on these proteins, or a member of this family. PKC enzymes in turn are activated by signals such as increases in the concentration of diacylglycerol (DAG) or calcium ions (Ca2+).

Isozymes

In humans there are at least 12 PKC isozymes, divided into three subfamilies: conventional (α, βI, βII, γ), requiring Ca2+, DAG and a phospholipid; novel (δ, ε, η, θ), requiring DAG but not Ca2+; and atypical (ζ, ι/λ), requiring neither.

Function

PKC enzymes play important roles in several signal transduction cascades, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, angiogenesis and tumour promotion. Phorbol esters, potent tumour promoters, activate PKC by mimicking DAG. Upon activation, PKC translocates to the plasma membrane and phosphorylates downstream targets such as the Raf–MEK–ERK pathway components.

Clinical significance

Dysregulation of PKC signalling is implicated in cancer, diabetes, heart failure and Alzheimer's disease. PKC inhibitors and activators have been investigated as therapeutics; bryostatin-1 is a natural PKC modulator studied in oncology and neurology.
