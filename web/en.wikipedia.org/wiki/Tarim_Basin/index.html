Tarim Basin

The Tarim Basin is a large endorheic basin in Xinjiang, Northwest China, occupying an area of about 1,000,000 km². It is dominated by the Taklimakan Desert, one of the largest sandy deserts in the world, and is ringed by the Tian Shan mountains to the north, the Kunlun Mountains to the south, and the Pamir Mountains to the west. The Tarim River, China's longest inland river, flows along the basin's northern edge. Historically, a chain of oasis cities around the desert's rim carried the northern and southern routes of the Silk Road.

Geography

The basin is one of the driest places in Eurasia: the surrounding mountain ranges block moist air, and annual rainfall in the interior is often below 50 mm, while evaporation is extreme. Meltwater from glaciers and snowfields in the encircling mountains feeds rivers that descend into the basin and historically sustained a necklace of oases before disappearing into the sand or terminating in salt lakes such as Lop Nur, a shifting terminal lake in the basin's east famous in Silk Road history.

History

The basin was inhabited in antiquity by Indo-European-speaking peoples, notably the Tocharians, whose mummified remains (the Tarim mummies) attest to early settlement. Oasis city-states such as Kashgar, Khotan, Kuqa, and Karashahr flourished on Silk Road trade, transmitting Buddhism, art, and goods between China, Central Asia, and the West. Control of the region alternated among the Han and Tang dynasties of China, Tibetan and Turkic empires, and local kingdoms. Turkic Uyghur peoples migrated into the basin from the 9th century onward, and Islam gradually became dominant from the 10th century. The region was incorporated into Qing China in the 18th century and today forms the southern part of the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region.

Resources and modern development

The basin holds major petroleum and natural gas reserves, exploited since the late 20th century, and its oases produce cotton, fruit (notably melons and grapes), and other crops under irrigation. Water scarcity, desertification, and the shrinking of the Tarim River's lower reaches are significant environmental concerns; the river's restoration has been a major ecological project.
