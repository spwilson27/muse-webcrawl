Kouros

A kouros (Ancient Greek: κοῦρος, plural kouroi) is the modern term given to free-standing ancient Greek sculptures that depict nude male youths. They first appear in the Archaic period in Greece and represent the type of the male votive statues, typically measuring up to life size. The female counterpart of the kouros is the kore.


The New York Kouros, c. 590–580 BC

Characteristics

Kouroi follow a strict canonical pose derived ultimately from Egyptian sculpture: the figure stands rigidly frontal, left foot advanced, arms held at the sides with fists clenched, head upright with the characteristic "Archaic smile". Early examples in marble and limestone echo Egyptian proportions and grid-based carving; over the sixth century BC the type becomes increasingly naturalistic, culminating in works such as the Kritios Boy (c. 480 BC), which introduces a relaxed, proto-Classical stance.

Functions

Kouroi served as votive offerings in sanctuaries — many are inscribed with dedications and the names of sculptors — and as grave markers in cemeteries. Some represent the god Apollo, to whom the type is often linked, while others commemorate individual youths. Famous examples include the Sounion Kouros, the Kroisos Kouros from Anavyssos, and the pair Cleobis and Biton at Delphi. The twin kouroi of Argos and the Getty kouros have generated scholarly debate, the latter over its authenticity.
