Fluid catalytic cracking

Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) is the most widely used conversion process in petroleum refineries for converting high-boiling, high-molecular-weight hydrocarbon fractions of crude oil — principally vacuum gas oil — into more valuable gasoline, olefinic gases (such as propylene) and other lighter products. The process employs a powdered catalyst, typically based on zeolite Y, maintained in a fluidised state by the hydrocarbon vapours, and was first commercialised in 1942.

An FCC unit, one of the largest process units in a modern refinery.

Process

Preheated feedstock is contacted with hot, regenerated catalyst in a riser reactor at around 535 °C. In a few seconds of contact, the heavy molecules crack into lighter ones. The vapours are separated from the spent catalyst in cyclones and sent to a fractionator. The catalyst, coated with coke deposited during reaction, flows into a regenerator where the coke is burned off with air at around 715 °C; the heat released drives the endothermic cracking reactions, making the process nearly self-sustaining. The regenerated catalyst then circulates back to the reactor.

Products

The principal product is FCC gasoline (typically around 40–50% of output), notable for its high octane number. Light olefins — especially propylene, an important petrochemical feedstock — butylene and isobutane are recovered as liquefied petroleum gas and alkylation feed. By-products include light cycle oil, clarified slurry oil and fuel gas. FCC supplies a large share of the world's gasoline pool and propylene production.

History

Catalytic cracking was pioneered by Eugène Houdry with fixed-bed reactors in the 1930s. The fluidised process, developed by a consortium led by Standard Oil of New Jersey (Esso) with researchers including Warren K. Lewis and Edwin R. Gilliland of MIT, was first operated at the Baton Rouge refinery in 1942 and became strategically vital to the Allied war effort by producing high-octane aviation gasoline. Zeolite catalysts introduced in the 1960s greatly improved selectivity and yield.
