Socket bayonet

A socket bayonet mounted on a musket

OriginFrance, late 17th century

A socket bayonet is a bayonet that attaches to a firearm by means of a hollow metal tube — the socket — which fits over the muzzle of the barrel and locks onto a stud or lug. Unlike the earlier plug bayonet, which was jammed into the barrel and prevented the weapon from being fired, the socket bayonet leaves the bore clear, so a soldier can shoot with the bayonet fixed and then use the musket-and-bayonet combination as a pike-length spear.

Development

The design is generally credited to the French military engineer Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban around 1687, and it was progressively adopted by European armies in the 1690s and early 1700s. A famous early demonstration of the plug bayonet's inadequacy came at the Battle of Killiecrankie (1689) and the Battle of Fleurus (1690), and by the War of the Spanish Succession socket bayonets were standard in French and, soon after, British service. The typical blade was triangular in cross-section — strong, light, and producing wounds that were notoriously difficult to stitch — with the blade offset from the socket so the soldier's hand could pass to load the musket.

Tactical effect

Because every musketeer could now defend himself against cavalry and act as his own pikeman, the pike disappeared from European infantry formations, ending the era of "pike and shot". Battalions became uniformly armed with musket and bayonet, and the bayonet charge became a decisive, if often more psychological than physical, tactic of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century warfare. Socket bayonets remained in service on military rifles into the twentieth century — for example on the Mosin–Nagant and early Lee–Enfield rifles — before being largely superseded by knife bayonets that could double as tools.
