Ferdinando Galiani

Born: 2 December 1728, Chieti, Kingdom of Naples
Died: 30 October 1787 (aged 58), Naples
Occupation: Economist, diplomat, philosopher

Ferdinando Galiani (2 December 1728 – 30 October 1787), known in French as Ferdinand Galiani, was an Italian economist, diplomat and philosopher of the Enlightenment. Although his writings were influential in his lifetime, he is best remembered today for his treatise on monetary theory, Della moneta (1751), written when he was only twenty-two, and for his Dialogues sur le commerce des blés (1770), a witty critique of the physiocratic doctrine of free trade in grain.

Life

Galiani was born in Chieti in the Kingdom of Naples and educated in Naples under the guidance of his uncle, Monsignor Celestino Galiani. He took minor orders and became known in Neapolitan intellectual circles as the "Abbé Galiani". His satirical work on the death of Naples' public executioner attracted attention, and his treatise on coinage, Della moneta, published anonymously in 1751, attracted the attention of King Charles of Naples, who appointed him to government posts.

In 1759 Galiani was sent to Paris as secretary of the Neapolitan embassy, where he remained for ten years and became a celebrated figure in the salons of Paris. He corresponded with Madame d'Épinay, Denis Diderot, and other philosophes. His forced return to Naples in 1769 — punishment, in part, for his conduct of a diplomatic dispute over grain exports — deeply disappointed him. Back in Naples he held senior administrative posts and wrote on economics, politics, and the Neapolitan dialect.

Works

In Della moneta, Galiani developed a subjective theory of value based on utility and scarcity, which anticipated later developments in marginalist economics; economists such as Friedrich Hayek later praised the work as remarkably modern. His Dialogues sur le commerce des blés (1770), written in French and polished by Diderot and Friedrich Melchior Grimm, attacked the physiocrats' call for unrestricted free trade in grain, arguing that policy must suit national circumstances. Voltaire called the book a cross between Plato and Molière. Galiani also wrote the satirical Socrate immaginario libretto and a treatise on the Neapolitan language.
