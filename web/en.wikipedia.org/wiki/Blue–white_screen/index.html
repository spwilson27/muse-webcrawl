Blue–white screen

[Hero image: an agar plate streaked with blue and white bacterial colonies after a blue–white screen]

The blue–white screen is a screening technique in molecular biology that allows for the rapid detection of recombinant bacteria in molecular cloning experiments. It is based on the principle of α-complementation of the lacZ gene, which encodes the enzyme β-galactosidase. Colonies containing a plasmid with an inserted DNA fragment appear white, while colonies with an uninterrupted vector appear blue on indicator plates.

Mechanism

Common cloning vectors (such as the pUC series) carry the lacZα fragment, which complements a defective β-galactosidase (the ω fragment) encoded by the host strain (e.g. E. coli DH5α). The cloning site lies within lacZα, so insertion of foreign DNA disrupts α-complementation. Plates are supplemented with the inducer IPTG and the chromogenic substrate X-gal. Functional β-galactosidase cleaves X-gal to produce an insoluble blue dye, so non-recombinant colonies turn blue; recombinants, lacking the enzyme, remain white.

Use

The screen greatly simplifies the identification of colonies carrying an insert, although inserts that are small, in-frame, or cloned outside the multiple cloning site may give light-blue colonies, and white colonies are not by themselves proof of a correct construct — confirmation by colony PCR, restriction analysis or sequencing is standard practice. The technique was developed in the early 1980s alongside the pUC vectors of Joachim Messing and co-workers.
