SubjectStop-and-frisk; Fourteenth Amendment; Fourth Amendment

Floyd v. City of New York, 959 F. Supp. 2d 540 (S.D.N.Y. 2013), was a United States federal civil rights class action challenging the New York City Police Department's (NYPD) use of "stop-and-frisk" under its stop, question and frisk policy. On August 12, 2013, Judge Shira Scheindlin held that the city's application of the practice violated the plaintiffs' rights under the Fourth Amendment (against unreasonable searches and seizures) and the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause, finding that the NYPD had engaged in indirect racial profiling of Black and Hispanic New Yorkers. She appointed a federal monitor to oversee reforms.

Background

Between January 2004 and June 2012, the NYPD conducted about 4.4 million Terry stops, of which roughly 80 percent were of Black or Hispanic people; in about 88 percent of stops, the person was neither arrested nor issued a summons. The named plaintiff, David Floyd, a medical student, had been stopped outside his Bronx home in 2007 while helping a tenant who had been locked out. The case, brought by the Center for Constitutional Rights, went to trial in the spring of 2013 alongside the related cases Ligon v. City of New York and Davis v. City of New York.

Decision and aftermath

The court found that the city's reliance on racial appearance as a proxy for criminality constituted indirect racial profiling, and that the NYPD had acted with deliberate indifference toward unconstitutional stops. Remedies included appointment of Peter L. Zimroth as federal monitor, immediate reforms to stop documentation, a pilot program for body-worn cameras, and development of a court-ordered remedial process with community input.

The city appealed under Mayor Michael Bloomberg, and on October 31, 2013, a Second Circuit panel removed Judge Scheindlin from the case, an unusual step criticising her public statements and application of the "related case rule"; the ruling itself was stayed pending appeal. After Bill de Blasio, who had campaigned against the practice, became mayor, the city withdrew the appeal in 2014 and accepted the reforms. The number of reported stops fell dramatically, from a peak of about 686,000 in 2011 to a small fraction of that level in subsequent years.
