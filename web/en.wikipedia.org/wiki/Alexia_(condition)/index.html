Alexia (condition)

Alexia
SpecialtyNeurology, neuropsychology
SymptomsLoss of ability to read following brain damage
CausesStroke or injury to the dominant (usually left) cerebral hemisphere

Alexia, also called acquired dyslexia, is a form of aphasia in which a previously literate person loses the ability to read following brain damage, usually from a stroke affecting the dominant hemisphere of the brain. It is distinguished from developmental dyslexia, in which reading difficulty is present from childhood.

Types

Pure alexia

Pure alexia, also known as alexia without agraphia or letter-by-letter reading, was described by Jules Dejerine in 1892. Patients can write fluently but cannot read back what they have written. Reading is laboriously reconstructed letter by letter, with reading time increasing in proportion to word length. It typically results from damage disconnecting the visual cortex from the left angular gyrus, often involving the splenium of the corpus callosum and the left occipital lobe.

Alexia with agraphia

In alexia with agraphia, reading and writing are both impaired. It is associated with lesions of the dominant angular gyrus and often occurs together with elements of Gerstmann syndrome.

Deep and surface alexia

In central alexias associated with aphasia, patients may make semantic errors when reading aloud (deep alexia), reading "cat" for "dog", or may lose the ability to read irregular words while retaining the ability to sound out words phonetically (surface alexia).

Treatment

Treatment is typically provided by speech-language therapists and involves reading rehabilitation strategies, including letter-by-letter reading training, tactile-kinaesthetic methods (tracing letters), and compensatory use of assistive technology such as text-to-speech software.
