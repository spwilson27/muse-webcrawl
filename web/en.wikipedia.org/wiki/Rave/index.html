A rave is a dance party at a warehouse, public or private property, club, or festival, featuring performances by DJs playing electronic dance music. The style is most associated with the early 1990s dance music scene that developed in the United Kingdom and spread internationally, in which partygoers danced all night to fast, repetitive electronic music in improvised venues. Raves are characterised by loud sound systems, laser light shows, projected images and visual effects, and an atmosphere intended to foster communal euphoria and freedom of expression.

History

The word "rave" was used in 1960s London counterculture and Northern Soul contexts ("rave-up"), but acquired its modern meaning after 1987–88, when British DJs and holidaymakers brought the Balearic, acid house sound home from Ibiza. The "Second Summer of Love" of 1988 saw acid house parties explode across London and Manchester (at clubs such as Shoom and The Haçienda) and out into warehouses and fields along the M25 orbital motorway, where promoters like Sunrise, Energy and Biology staged huge illegal parties publicised by pirate radio and word of mouth.

Large free parties and travelling sound systems, such as Spiral Tribe, kept the unlicensed tradition going, sometimes culminating in events like the week-long Castlemorton Common festival of 1992. The resulting political backlash produced the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, which famously targeted gatherings characterised by "repetitive beats". Commercial superclubs and licensed festivals (Creamfields, Gatecrasher, and later the vast EDM festival circuit in the United States and worldwide) absorbed much of the culture, while an underground free-party scene persisted in the UK, France (teknivals) and elsewhere.

Music and culture

Rave music centres on DJ-mixed electronic dance music: house, acid house, techno, trance, breakbeat hardcore, jungle and drum and bass, happy hardcore and gabber, among many other genres, typically at tempos from around 120 to 180 BPM. Fashion drew on baggy clothes, sportswear, fluorescent and cyber styles, smiley-face iconography (from acid house) and, in North American kandi culture, colourful beaded bracelets exchanged as tokens of friendship. The scene's stated ethos is summarised as PLUR — Peace, Love, Unity and Respect.

Drugs and controversy

Raves were controversial from the start because of their association with club drugs, especially MDMA (ecstasy), amphetamines and LSD, and because of the nuisance and safety issues around unlicensed events. Media panics followed drug-related deaths; the resulting policy responses ranged from criminalisation to harm-reduction approaches such as drug checking, free water and chill-out spaces. In the United States, the federal RAVE Act of 2003 (enacted as part of the Illicit Drug Anti-Proliferation Act) expanded liability for venue owners and promoters.
