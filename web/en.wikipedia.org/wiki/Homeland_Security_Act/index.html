The Homeland Security Act of 2002 (Public Law 107-296) is a United States federal law enacted in response to the September 11 attacks. It created the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), the largest reorganisation of the federal government since the creation of the Department of Defense in 1947, consolidating 22 federal agencies and programmes and some 170,000 employees into a single cabinet department.

Background

Following the attacks of September 11, 2001, President George W. Bush first created the Office of Homeland Security within the White House in October 2001, headed by Tom Ridge. In June 2002 the administration proposed a full department, and after debate in Congress — particularly over civil-service and labour provisions for department employees — the Act passed the House and Senate in November 2002 and was signed on November 25, 2002. The department formally began operations on March 1, 2003.

Provisions

The Act established DHS and transferred to it agencies including the Coast Guard, the Secret Service, the Customs Service, the Immigration and Naturalization Service's enforcement functions (reorganised into ICE and CBP), the Transportation Security Administration and the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). It created directorates for border and transportation security, emergency preparedness and response, information analysis and infrastructure protection, and science and technology. Other titles addressed information sharing between intelligence and law-enforcement agencies, cybersecurity, and liability protections for anti-terrorism technologies (the SAFETY Act provisions).

Criticism and later changes

Critics raised concerns about the department's size, the erosion of civil liberties, and the inclusion in the enacted text of unrelated provisions. FEMA's performance during Hurricane Katrina in 2005 led to the Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act of 2006, which strengthened the agency within DHS.
