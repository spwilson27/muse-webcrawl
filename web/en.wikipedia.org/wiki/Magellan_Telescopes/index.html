Magellan Telescopes

The Magellan Telescopes are a pair of 6.5-metre (21.3 ft) optical telescopes located at Las Campanas Observatory in the Atacama region of Chile. Operated by a consortium led by the Carnegie Institution for Science, they are named the Walter Baade Telescope and the Landon Clay Telescope, and are known for their exceptionally sharp natural seeing and high-quality optics.

History

The project grew out of the Magellan Consortium, a partnership of the Carnegie Institution of Washington, the University of Arizona, Harvard University, the University of Michigan and MIT. The Baade Telescope saw first light on 15 September 2000, and the Clay Telescope followed in 2002. The telescopes' borosilicate honeycomb mirrors were cast and polished by the University of Arizona's Steward Observatory Mirror Laboratory.

Instruments and science

Magellan instruments include the IMACS multiobject spectrograph and camera, the MagE echellette spectrograph, the FIRE infrared spectrograph, and the Planet Finding Spectrograph (PFS), a high-precision radial-velocity instrument used in exoplanet searches. The Clay Telescope hosts MagAO, an adaptive optics system with a deformable secondary mirror that has produced some of the sharpest visible-light images obtained from the ground, used for direct imaging of exoplanets and protoplanetary discs.

Site

Las Campanas is also the chosen site of the Giant Magellan Telescope, a 24.5-metre-equivalent telescope under construction by an international consortium, which will use seven 8.4-metre mirror segments.
