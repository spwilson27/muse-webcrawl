Constitution of the Fifth French Republic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Constitution of the Fifth French Republic was adopted by referendum on 28 September 1958 and promulgated on 4 October 1958. Drafted under Charles de Gaulle and Michel Debré after the collapse of the Fourth Republic amid the Algerian crisis, it created a strong executive: a president elected (since a 1962 referendum) by direct universal suffrage for a five-year term (seven until 2000), empowered to appoint the prime minister, dissolve the National Assembly, call referendums and, under Article 16, assume emergency powers; and a government responsible to parliament — the hybrid now classified as semi-presidential.

The text rebalanced parliamentarism: restricted legislative domain, government control of the parliamentary agenda, the blocked vote, and Article 49.3 allowing the government to pass bills by engaging its responsibility (used controversially for the 2023 pensions reform, among many earlier uses). The Constitutional Council, created as an arbiter, developed into a rights-reviewing court after its 1971 freedom-of-association decision incorporated the Declaration of 1789 and the 1946 preamble. The constitution has been amended some twenty-five times and proved durable through alternation in power and cohabitation.
