Intervention (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A courtroom, where third parties may seek to intervene in pending proceedings

In law, intervention is a procedure that allows a nonparty — an intervenor — to join ongoing litigation, either as a matter of right or at the discretion of the court, without the permission of the original litigants. The intervenor participates in order to protect an interest that may be affected by the outcome of the case.

United States federal practice

Rule 24 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides for two kinds of intervention. Intervention of right must be granted when a federal statute gives an unconditional right to intervene, or when the applicant claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and is so situated that disposing of the action may, as a practical matter, impair or impede the applicant's ability to protect that interest, unless existing parties adequately represent it. Permissive intervention may be granted where a statute gives a conditional right, or where the applicant's claim or defence shares a common question of law or fact with the main action, provided intervention does not unduly delay or prejudice the adjudication of the original parties' rights.

An intervenor ordinarily becomes a full party, able to present evidence and argument and to appeal. Intervention is common in constitutional and public law litigation, where states, organisations or affected groups seek to defend or challenge government action; for example, states frequently intervene to defend federal or state statutes when the executive declines to do so.

Other jurisdictions

Similar devices exist in other legal systems, sometimes under names such as "intervener" in United Kingdom appellate practice — the Supreme Court and the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council permit public bodies and interest groups to intervene, usually by written submissions — and in international courts, where states may apply to intervene in cases before the International Court of Justice under Article 62 of its Statute if they have an interest of a legal nature that may be affected.
