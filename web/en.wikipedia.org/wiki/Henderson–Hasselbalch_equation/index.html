Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

In chemistry and biochemistry, the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation relates the pH of a solution of a weak acid to the acid dissociation constant and the ratio of the concentrations of the conjugate base and the acid. It is widely used to estimate the pH of buffer solutions and the charge on biomolecules such as amino acids and proteins.

A buffer resists pH change most strongly near the pKa of its acid.

pH = pKa + log10( [A⁻] / [HA] )

History

Lawrence Joseph Henderson derived an equation relating hydrogen-ion concentration to the composition of a bicarbonate buffer in 1908, in work on acid–base balance in blood. Karl Albert Hasselbalch recast Henderson's relation in logarithmic terms in 1916–1917, in the course of studies associating it with the pH notation introduced by Søren Sørensen; the combined name commemorates both contributions.

Derivation and use

Starting from the definition of the acid dissociation constant, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA], taking negative logarithms and rearranging gives the equation above. A convenient consequence is that when the concentrations of acid and conjugate base are equal, the pH equals the pKa, which is also the point of maximum buffering capacity. In pharmacology the equation predicts the ionised fraction of a weak electrolyte drug, and hence its absorption across membranes; in physiology it underlies the bicarbonate buffer system of blood, linking pH, bicarbonate concentration and the partial pressure of carbon dioxide.

Limitations

The equation is an approximation. It assumes that the concentrations of acid and conjugate base at equilibrium are close to their formal (initial) concentrations — a good assumption for buffers but poor for very dilute solutions, strong acids or bases, or acids so weak that their dissociation is significant relative to the buffer concentration. More exact treatments solve the charge- and mass-balance equations directly or numerically, and activity coefficients may be needed at high ionic strength.
