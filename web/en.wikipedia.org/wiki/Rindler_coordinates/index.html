In relativistic physics, the Rindler coordinates are a coordinate system describing a uniformly accelerating frame of reference in flat (Minkowski) spacetime. Introduced in the form used today by Wolfgang Rindler, they are the relativistic analogue of a non-inertial frame and are the simplest setting in which an event horizon appears without gravity, making them a standard theoretical tool for studying black hole horizons and the Unruh effect.

Definition

For an observer with constant proper acceleration g, Rindler coordinates (η, ξ) are related to inertial Minkowski coordinates (t, x) by

t = ξ sinh(gη),   x = ξ cosh(gη)

which covers only the wedge x > |t| of Minkowski space — the region causally accessible to the accelerating observer. The boundary x = |t| acts as an event horizon (the Rindler horizon): light signals from beyond it can never reach the observer. A family of observers at fixed ξ maintains constant spacing without contradiction with relativity (a resolution related to Bell's spaceship paradox), with observers nearer the horizon requiring greater proper acceleration.

Physical significance

By the equivalence principle, Rindler coordinates locally mimic a uniform gravitational field, and the Rindler horizon behaves like a black hole horizon. Quantum field theory in this frame shows that the Minkowski vacuum appears to the accelerating observer as a thermal bath — the Unruh effect, with temperature proportional to the acceleration — providing key insight into Hawking radiation.
