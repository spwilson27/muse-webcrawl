Yamato (大和) was the lead ship of her class of battleships built for the Imperial Japanese Navy in the late 1930s. She and her sister ship Musashi were the heaviest and most powerfully armed battleships ever constructed, displacing nearly 72,000 tonnes at full load and armed with nine 46 cm (18.1 in) main guns, the largest naval artillery ever fitted to a warship.

Design and construction

Japan withdrew from the naval treaty system in the mid-1930s, freeing designers to build ships far beyond treaty limits. The Yamato class was designed to outgun any individual American battleship, offsetting Japan's numerical inferiority. Yamato was laid down at the Kure Naval Arsenal in 1937 under strict secrecy and commissioned a week after the attack on Pearl Harbor.

Service history

Yamato served as flagship of the Combined Fleet under Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto during 1942, but saw little surface action, as the Pacific War was dominated by aircraft carriers. She was torpedoed by the submarine USS Skate in December 1943 and repaired. In October 1944 she fired her main guns at enemy surface forces for the only time, during the Battle off Samar in the Philippines.

Sinking

On 6 April 1945, Yamato sailed on Operation Ten-Go, a one-way mission to attack the Allied fleet off Okinawa. Without air cover, she was overwhelmed by waves of American carrier aircraft on 7 April, capsizing and exploding after taking numerous bomb and torpedo hits. Of her crew of about 3,300, only around 280 survived.

Legacy

Yamato remains a powerful symbol in Japanese culture, lending her name — an ancient name for Japan itself — to films, the anime series Space Battleship Yamato, and the Yamato Museum in Kure, where a large-scale model is displayed.
