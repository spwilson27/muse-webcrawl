African-American culture

African-American culture, also known as Black American culture, refers to the contributions of African Americans to the culture of the United States, either as part of or distinct from mainstream American culture. The distinct identity of African-American culture is rooted in the history of slavery in the United States and in African traditions brought to North America during the transatlantic slave trade. It has had a profound influence on music, literature, cuisine, religion, and social life in the United States and beyond.

History

African-American culture began to take shape in the colonial era, when enslaved Africans combined elements of West and Central African traditions with European influences under the conditions of enslavement. Enslaved people preserved aspects of their heritage through oral storytelling, music, dance, and religious practice, often in secret. After emancipation in 1863–1865, newly freed communities built institutions—churches, schools, and businesses—that became the backbone of Black cultural life.

The Great Migration of the twentieth century carried millions of African Americans from the rural South to northern and western cities, transforming the culture and spreading it nationwide. The Harlem Renaissance of the 1920s was a flowering of Black literature, art, and music centered in New York City. The civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s further reshaped African-American cultural expression and its place in American life.

Music

African-American music is among the culture's most influential exports. Spirituals sung by enslaved people gave rise to gospel music, while work songs and field hollers contributed to the blues. Jazz emerged in New Orleans in the early twentieth century and became America's signature art form. Later genres created or shaped by African Americans include rhythm and blues, rock and roll, soul, funk, disco, and hip-hop, which originated in the Bronx in the 1970s and became a global phenomenon.

Literature and arts

African-American literature began with works such as the poetry of Phillis Wheatley and the slave narratives of Frederick Douglass and Harriet Jacobs. Twentieth-century writers including Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Richard Wright, Ralph Ellison, James Baldwin, Toni Morrison, and Maya Angelou produced works central to the American canon. In the visual arts, figures such as Jacob Lawrence, Romare Bearden, and Jean-Michel Basquiat reshaped modern art.

Cuisine

Soul food is the traditional cuisine of African Americans, developed from ingredients available to enslaved people and adapted from African, European, and Native American cooking. Signature dishes include fried chicken, collard greens, black-eyed peas, cornbread, macaroni and cheese, and sweet potato pie. Regional traditions such as Louisiana Creole and Gullah cooking are also important parts of the culture's culinary heritage.

Religion

The Black church—historically Black Protestant congregations and denominations such as the African Methodist Episcopal Church and the National Baptist Convention—has been a central institution in African-American life since the eighteenth century. Churches served as schools, social centers, and organizing hubs for the civil rights movement. A significant minority of African Americans are Muslim, Catholic, or members of other faiths.

Language

African American Vernacular English (AAVE) is a distinctive variety of English with its own grammatical patterns, studied extensively by linguists. It developed in the American South and spread nationally through the Great Migration, influencing mainstream American slang and popular speech.

Holidays and observances

Juneteenth (June 19) commemorates the emancipation of enslaved people in Texas in 1865 and became a U.S. federal holiday in 2021. Kwanzaa, created in 1966, is a week-long celebration of African-American culture and heritage held from December 26 to January 1. February is observed as Black History Month.
