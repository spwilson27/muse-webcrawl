Biomaterial

A total hip replacement prosthesis.

A biomaterial is a substance that has been engineered to interact with biological systems for a medical purpose — either a therapeutic purpose (to treat, augment, repair or replace a tissue function of the body) or a diagnostic one. The study of biomaterials is called biomaterials science or biomaterials engineering.

History

The use of natural materials in the body is ancient — Mayan peoples fashioned nacre tooth implants over a thousand years ago — but the modern field began after the Second World War, with pioneering work including Harold Ridley's intraocular lens (1949), vascular grafts of synthetic fabric, and the first successful artificial heart valves in the 1950s–60s. Early materials were chosen for inertness; later generations were designed to be bioactive (bonding to bone, as with hydroxyapatite coatings) or biodegradable, resorbing as tissue regenerates.

Materials and applications

Biomaterials include metals (titanium and its alloys, stainless steel, cobalt–chromium alloys) used in orthopaedic and dental implants; ceramics (alumina, zirconia, calcium phosphates) for bone repair and joint surfaces; polymers (silicone, polyethylene, polylactic acid) for sutures, catheters and drug delivery; and natural materials such as collagen and decellularised tissues used in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering scaffolds.

Biocompatibility

The central requirement is biocompatibility: the material must perform its function without eliciting an unacceptable local or systemic response, such as chronic inflammation, thrombosis or toxicity. Host response depends on surface chemistry, topography, degradation products and mechanical matching with tissue.
