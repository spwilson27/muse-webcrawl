Viscosity

The viscosity of a fluid is a measure of its resistance to deformation at a given rate — informally, its "thickness". Honey has a much higher viscosity than water. Viscosity results from internal friction between neighbouring layers of fluid moving at different velocities. For liquids, it corresponds to informal notions of thickness; for gases, it arises from molecular diffusion of momentum. The SI unit of dynamic viscosity is the pascal-second (Pa·s); the older poise (0.1 Pa·s) and centipoise remain common. Kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density, with SI unit m²/s.

Definition

For a simple shear flow between parallel plates, Newton's law of viscosity states that the shear stress τ is proportional to the velocity gradient (shear rate):

τ = μ (du/dy)

where μ is the (dynamic) viscosity. Fluids obeying this linear law are Newtonian (water, air, most oils). Fluids whose viscosity varies with shear rate are non-Newtonian: shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) fluids such as ketchup and blood, shear-thickening fluids such as cornstarch suspensions, and viscoelastic materials are studied in rheology.

Dependence on conditions

The viscosity of liquids generally decreases strongly with temperature (roughly exponentially, per the Andrade equation), whereas the viscosity of gases increases with temperature (approximately with the square root of absolute temperature, per kinetic theory and Sutherland's formula). Pressure has a modest effect on liquids but is significant in lubrication. Everyday reference points at 20 °C include water at about 1.0 mPa·s, olive oil around 56 mPa·s, honey on the order of 2,000–10,000 mPa·s, and pitch around 2.3 × 10⁸ Pa·s — pitch famously flows in the pitch drop experiment, with about one drop falling per decade.

Measurement and applications

Viscosity is measured with viscometers and rheometers: capillary (Ostwald), falling-ball, rotational, and vibrational instruments. It is central to fluid mechanics through the Reynolds number (the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, governing the laminar–turbulent transition), to lubrication engineering and motor-oil grades (SAE viscosity ratings), polymer processing, food science, and geophysics, where the mantle's viscosity (~10²¹ Pa·s) controls post-glacial rebound.
