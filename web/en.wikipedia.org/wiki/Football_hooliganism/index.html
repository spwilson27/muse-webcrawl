Football hooliganism

Football hooliganism, also known as soccer hooliganism in some regions, is disruptive and violent behaviour associated with the sport of association football. It typically involves conflicts between organised supporter groups — often called "firms" in the United Kingdom — usually occurring before or after matches, and away from stadiums as much as inside them.

History

Disorder at football matches has been recorded since the beginnings of the modern game in 19th-century England; matches were banned in some towns as early as the medieval period because of crowd violence. The modern phenomenon of organised hooligan firms emerged in England in the 1960s and 1970s, associated with working-class youth subcultures, terrace culture, and groups attached to clubs such as West Ham United's Inter City Firm, Millwall's Bushwackers, and Chelsea's Headhunters.

The 1980s saw a series of disasters and disturbances that forced reform. The Heysel Stadium disaster in Brussels in 1985, in which 39 spectators died before the European Cup Final between Liverpool and Juventus, led to a five-year ban on English clubs in European competition. The Hillsborough disaster of 1989 — though caused by crowd management failures rather than hooliganism — prompted the Taylor Report, all-seater stadiums, and intensive policing measures that greatly reduced organised violence inside English grounds.

Outside England

Hooliganism spread across Europe and beyond. In Italy, ultras groups developed a distinct culture; in Poland, the Netherlands, Germany, and Russia, firms and arranged fights ("ustawka") became common. In Latin America, Argentina's barra bravas have been linked to serious violence and organised crime, while in Turkey and the Balkans, intense derby rivalries have produced recurring disorder.

Responses

Measures against hooliganism include football banning orders, alcohol restrictions, segregated seating, CCTV, intelligence-led policing, and international cooperation at tournaments. Academic research, notably the "Leicester School" of sociologists, has examined the phenomenon in terms of class, masculinity, and identity.
