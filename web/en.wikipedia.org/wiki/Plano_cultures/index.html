Plano cultures

The Plano cultures (or Plano traditions) are the Paleo-Indian hunting cultures of the North American Great Plains and western interior that flourished after the Folsom culture, roughly 10,000 to 7,000 years ago. Named for their unfluted ("plano") lanceolate projectile points, the Plano peoples were specialised bison hunters who developed communal hunting techniques on a large scale as the climate warmed at the end of the last Ice Age.

Characteristics

Plano points — such as Agate Basin, Hell Gap, Alberta, Scottsbluff and Eden types — are long, parallel-flaked lanceolate points without the channel flake (flute) of Clovis and Folsom. The Cody complex (Alberta and Scottsbluff points, along with the distinctive Cody knife) is the best known. As mammoths and other megafauna disappeared, Plano groups focused overwhelmingly on ancient bison (Bison antiquus and later modern bison), hunting them communally.

Hunting methods and sites

Plano hunters drove herds into arroyos, over cliffs and into corrals, killing dozens or hundreds of animals at once. Famous kill sites include the Olsen-Chubbuck site in Colorado, where some 200 bison were trapped in an arroyo around 8,000 BC, and the Hudson-Meng site in Nebraska (Alberta points, about 9,800 years old). Butchery camps and bone beds show systematic processing of carcasses for meat and hides.
