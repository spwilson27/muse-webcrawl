Coat of arms of Finland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The coat of arms of Finland is a crowned lion on a red field, standing on a straight sabre and trampling a curved sabre with its hind foot, holding a raised sword in its right foreleg. The lion is surrounded by nine silver roses. In Finnish it is known as Suomen vaakuna.

Description

The blazon describes a golden lion rampant on a red (gules) shield, crowned, with its right foreleg replaced by an armoured human arm holding a straight sword, trampling upon a curved sabre, with nine silver roses scattered on the field. The design symbolises the position of Finland between East and West: the straight Western sword and the curved Eastern sabre.

History

The lion emblem originated in the sixteenth century, when Finland was part of the Kingdom of Sweden. A lion appeared in the arms granted to Duke John (later John III of Sweden) as Duke of Finland, and the arms were prominently carved on the tomb monument of Gustav I (Gustav Vasa) in Uppsala Cathedral, completed in the 1580s. During the period of the Grand Duchy of Finland within the Russian Empire, versions of the lion arms continued in use, sometimes with altered details.

After Finland declared independence in 1917, the lion coat of arms was adopted as the national arms. Its status was confirmed by law in 1978, which fixed the official description and protected its use. The arms appear on the presidential flag, official seals, coins and state insignia.
