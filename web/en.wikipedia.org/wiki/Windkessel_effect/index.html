Windkessel effect

The Windkessel effect (German: Windkessel, "air chamber") describes how the elasticity of the large arteries smooths the pulsatile output of the heart into a steadier flow of blood through the body's tissues. During systole, the aorta and other elastic arteries stretch to store part of the stroke volume; during diastole, their elastic recoil squeezes that stored blood onward, maintaining pressure and flow while the heart refills.

History and model

The concept was described qualitatively by Stephen Hales in the eighteenth century and formalized by the German physiologist Otto Frank in 1899. In the classic two-element Windkessel model, the arterial system is represented by a compliance (the elastic chamber) in parallel with a resistance (the peripheral vasculature), analogous to an electrical circuit. Later three- and four-element models add characteristic impedance and inertance to reproduce measured pressure waveforms more faithfully.

Clinical significance

With aging and disease, arteriosclerosis stiffens the large arteries and weakens the Windkessel effect. The result is higher systolic pressure, lower diastolic pressure, a widened pulse pressure, and increased workload on the heart — all associated with greater cardiovascular risk. Pulse pressure and pulse-wave velocity measurements exploit this physiology clinically.
