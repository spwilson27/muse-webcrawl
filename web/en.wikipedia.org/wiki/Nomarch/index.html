Nomarch

A nomarch (Ancient Greek: νομάρχης, from νόμος "district" and ἀρχή "rule") was the ruler of a nome, a territorial division of ancient Egypt. Each nome was governed by a nomarch responsible for administration, taxation, irrigation, and local religious cults. The office and the power of its holders changed greatly over the three thousand years of pharaonic history.

Old and Middle Kingdoms

In the Old Kingdom, nomarchs were royal appointees, but over time the office became hereditary in powerful provincial families. During the First Intermediate Period, when central authority collapsed, nomarchs became effectively independent rulers; their richly decorated provincial tombs — such as those at Beni Hasan, Asyut, and Aswan — are major sources for the period. The autobiography of Khnumhotep II at Beni Hasan describes a nomarch's career and privileges.

Pharaohs of the Middle Kingdom, particularly Senusret III, curtailed the power of the nomarchs, reducing the grandeur of provincial tombs and reasserting central control. In the New Kingdom, the office continued in diminished form, with civil administration reorganised under the vizier and local mayors.

Later uses

The title survived into the Ptolemaic period as an administrative rank. In modern Greece, a nomarch (νομάρχης) was the appointed prefect of a nomos (prefecture), an usage that lasted until local-government reforms replaced appointed prefects with elected regional governors in the 2010s.
