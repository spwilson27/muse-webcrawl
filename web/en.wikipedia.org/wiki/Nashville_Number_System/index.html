Nashville Number System

TypeMusical notation shorthand
OriginNashville recording studios, 1950s
Developed byNeal Matthews Jr.; popularised by Charlie McCoy
Based onScale degrees / figured bass

The Nashville Number System is a method of transcribing music by denoting the scale degree on which a chord is built. Developed in the recording studios of Nashville, Tennessee, in the 1950s, it allows session musicians to write a complete chord chart for a song as a compact sequence of numbers, which can then be played in any key.

How it works

In a major key, the chord built on the tonic is written "1", the chord on the supertonic "2", and so on through the scale. By default the numbers refer to the diatonic triads (1, 4 and 5 major; 2, 3 and 6 minor; 7 diminished), and symbols are added to alter chord quality, indicate inversions and bass notes, mark rhythm hits and accents, and show section repeats. Because the numbers describe harmonic function rather than fixed pitches, a singer can change key on the spot and the band can follow without rewriting the chart.

History and use

The system is credited chiefly to Neal Matthews Jr. of the Jordanaires, who adapted older figured-bass and solfège ideas in the late 1950s; harmonica player and session leader Charlie McCoy helped spread it through Nashville's studios. It remains the everyday working notation of Nashville session players and is widely used in country, gospel and worship music, where a band may need to learn an entire set quickly.
