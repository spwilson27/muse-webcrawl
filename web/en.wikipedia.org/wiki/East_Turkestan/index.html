East Turkestan (Uyghur: شەرقىي تۈركىستان) is a historical and political name for the region roughly corresponding to present-day Xinjiang in far western China. The term denotes the eastern part of the broader Turkestan region of Central Asia and is central to Uyghur national identity and the East Turkestan independence movement.

Geography

The region spans the Tarim Basin and the Taklamakan Desert in the south, ringed by oasis cities such as Kashgar, Turpan and Hotan, and the Dzungarian Basin in the north, separated by the Tian Shan mountains.

History

The oases of the Tarim Basin were waypoints on the Silk Road, governed successively by the Uyghur Khaganate, the Kara-Khanid Khanate and the Chagatai Khanate, and were brought under Qing rule in the 1750s during the conquest of Dzungaria. The name "Xinjiang" ("new frontier") was applied when the region became a province in 1884.

The republics of 1933 and 1944

Twice in the 20th century, independence movements proclaimed short-lived states: the First East Turkestan Republic at Kashgar (1933–34) and the Soviet-backed Second East Turkestan Republic at Ghulja (1944–49). Both were absorbed into the People's Republic of China in 1949, and Xinjiang was designated an autonomous region in 1955.

Modern politics

The World Uyghur Congress and the East Turkestan Government-in-Exile advocate self-determination, while Beijing treats East Turkestan terminology as separatist and links it to the banned East Turkestan Islamic Movement. See Human rights in China.
