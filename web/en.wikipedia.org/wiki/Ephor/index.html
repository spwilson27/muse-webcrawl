Ephor

The ephors (Greek: ἔφοροι éphoroi, "overseers") were a board of five annually elected magistrates in ancient Sparta who shared power with the two Spartan kings and constituted one of the principal checks within the Spartan constitution. The office existed from at least the eighth century BC until 227 BC, when it was abolished by Cleomenes III, and was briefly restored before disappearing after the Roman conquest.

Powers

Ephors were elected by the assembly of citizens (apella) from among all Spartiates over thirty, and served for one year. Their powers were extensive:

  • Oversight of the kings. Two ephors accompanied a king on campaign; the ephors exchanged monthly oaths with the kings — the king swearing to reign according to the laws, the ephors to preserve the kingship if he did. Every ninth year the ephors watched the sky on a clear night: a shooting star was taken as a sign that a king had offended the gods, and he could be suspended and tried.
  • Judicial authority. The ephors presided over major civil cases and, sitting with the gerousia, formed the high court that could try even kings.
  • Administration. They summoned the assembly, conducted foreign policy and received ambassadors, supervised the krypteia and the education of the young (agoge), and could fine, arrest and prosecute officials.
  • Control of the helots. On entering office the ephors annually declared war on the helots, so that killing them would incur no ritual pollution.

Historical evaluation

Ancient writers were divided on the office. Aristotle criticized the ephorate in the Politics: ephors were drawn from all the people, often poor men open to bribery, yet wielded power comparable to tyranny. Plato counted the ephorate among the restraints that saved Sparta from pure monarchy. Modern historians see it as evidence that Spartan government was a mixed constitution combining monarchy (the kings), oligarchy (the gerousia) and a democratic element (the ephorate and assembly).
