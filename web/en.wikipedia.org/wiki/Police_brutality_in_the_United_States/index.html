Police brutality in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Police brutality is the use of excessive force by law enforcement personnel while performing their duties. In the United States, police brutality has been a systemic issue since the 19th century, with documented incidents ranging from the violent suppression of labour strikes and civil rights demonstrations to individual killings of unarmed civilians. American police kill roughly 1,000 people per year — a rate far higher than in other developed democracies — and the burden falls disproportionately on Black Americans, who are killed at about three times the rate of white Americans relative to population, according to databases maintained by The Washington Post and Mapping Police Violence.

History

Early American policing was intertwined with slave patrols in the South and with strikebreaking in industrial cities. During the civil rights movement, images of police attacking peaceful marchers — notably in Birmingham, Alabama in 1963 and at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma in 1965 — galvanised national opinion. The 1991 beating of Rodney King in Los Angeles, captured on video, and the acquittal of the officers involved triggered the 1992 Los Angeles riots. The advent of smartphones and social media brought a new wave of documentation in the 2010s, including the killings of Eric Garner (2014), Michael Brown (2014), Tamir Rice (2014), Walter Scott (2015), Philando Castile (2016) and Breonna Taylor (2020).

George Floyd and the 2020 protests

On 25 May 2020, Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin murdered George Floyd by kneeling on his neck for over nine minutes. Bystander video sparked the largest protest movement in U.S. history, with demonstrations in all 50 states and many countries, centred on the Black Lives Matter movement and calls to reform, defund or abolish police departments. Chauvin was convicted of murder in 2021 — a rare outcome; historically only a small fraction of officers who kill civilians are charged, and fewer convicted.

Causes and accountability

Analysts cite contributing factors including the militarisation of police equipment and tactics, weak accountability structures, qualified immunity, union protections, poor training in de-escalation, and the legacy of racial segregation. No comprehensive federal database of police use of force existed for most of this period; the FBI's National Use-of-Force Data Collection, launched in 2019, relies on voluntary agency reporting.
