Black ice

Black ice on asphalt is nearly transparent and therefore hard to see

Black ice, sometimes called clear ice, is a thin coating of glaze ice on a surface, especially on roads. The ice itself is not actually black, but visually transparent, allowing the often black road below to be seen through it and thus giving it the term "black ice." The typically low levels of noticeable ice pellets, snow, or sleet surrounding black ice means that areas of the ice are often practically invisible to drivers and pedestrians, creating a significant hazard.

Formation

Black ice forms in several ways. On roads, it commonly forms when the air temperature is above freezing but the pavement — cooled below freezing overnight — causes moisture, dew, or light rain to freeze on contact. It also forms when water from melted snow refreezes after sunset, and when vehicle exhaust moisture condenses on cold pavement. Bridges and overpasses are especially susceptible because cold air circulates both above and below the road surface, cooling the deck faster than roads on solid ground; hence the common warning sign "Bridge ices before road." Water bodies freeze into black ice when calm water freezes slowly without trapped air bubbles, producing exceptionally clear, hard ice.

Hazards

Because black ice is nearly invisible, drivers often fail to slow down, and the thin layer provides almost no traction: vehicles may lose control with little warning, and braking distances increase dramatically. The lack of visual cues means weather similar to adjacent conditions can suddenly become dangerous, particularly in the early morning and evening. Black ice is a factor in numerous multiple-vehicle collisions each winter. Pedestrians and cyclists face similar risks on sidewalks and paths.

Detection and prevention

Road crews use pavement temperature sensors and road weather information systems to predict black ice formation and apply salt or other de-icing chemicals in advance. Studded tires and anti-lock brakes provide limited help; the primary advice to motorists is to slow down, increase following distance, and avoid sudden steering or braking when temperatures hover near freezing.

Other uses of the term

In nautical contexts, "black ice" may refer to ice forming on a ship's superstructure from sea spray, which can add dangerous weight and capsize small vessels. In ice skating and ice fishing, transparent lake ice is also called black ice and is prized for its smoothness and strength when thick enough.
