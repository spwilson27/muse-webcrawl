History of the Jews in Venice

The history of the Jews in Venice is closely tied to the Venetian Ghetto, established in 1516 — the first ghetto in Europe, from which the word itself derives, probably from the foundry (geto) on the site in Cannaregio.

The Ghetto

Venice permitted Jews to reside permanently in exchange for their services as moneylenders, merchants, and physicians. Jews were locked into the Ghetto at night, required to wear distinguishing signs, and crowded into some of the tallest buildings in the city. Despite restrictions, the community built five synagogues (scuole) and became a centre of Hebrew printing.

Community life

German, Italian, Spanish-Portuguese, and Levantine Jewish "nations" each maintained their own synagogue and customs. Figures such as the rabbi Leon Modena and the poet Sara Copia Sullam reflect a rich cultural life within confinement.

Emancipation and after

Napoleon's forces opened the Ghetto gates in 1797. Venetian Jews gained full equality with Italian unification. The community was persecuted under Fascist racial laws and deportations during the Second World War; a small community remains today. See also Venetian Ghetto.
