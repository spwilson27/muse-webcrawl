The Battle of Nagashino (長篠の戦い, Nagashino no tatakai) was fought on 28 June 1575 near Nagashino Castle in Mikawa Province, Japan, between the combined forces of Oda Nobunaga and Tokugawa Ieyasu and the army of Takeda Katsuyori. It is one of the most famous battles of the Sengoku period: Nobunaga's massed arquebusiers, firing from behind wooden stockades, shattered the formerly feared Takeda cavalry and permanently changed Japanese warfare.

Background

Tokugawa Ieyasu's outpost at Nagashino Castle was besieged by 15,000 Takeda troops, threatening his home province of Mikawa. A messenger, Torii Suneemon, slipped through the siege to summon help — and was crucified by the Takeda on his return after refusing to lie to the garrison, becoming a byword for loyal sacrifice. Nobunaga marched to the relief with some 30,000 men, joining Ieyasu's 8,000.

The battle

Nobunaga chose his ground on the plain of Shitaragahara, behind the Rengogawa stream, and protected his 3,000 arquebusiers with anti-cavalry wooden stockades. On the night before the battle, a Tokugawa raiding party destroyed the Takeda siege works behind Nagashino, freeing the garrison. Katsuyori, his supply line threatened and confident in his cavalry, attacked across the stream into the stockades, where successive waves of Takeda charges were met by disciplined volley fire and counterattacking spearmen. After some eight hours the Takeda army broke, having lost perhaps two-thirds of its strength, including many of its senior generals — the "Twenty-Four Generals" of the Takeda house never recovered.

Significance and debate

Tradition credits Nobunaga with rotating volleys by ranks of 1,000 gunners; modern historians debate the details, but agree that the large-scale, organised use of firearms by low-ranking ashigaru under professional command marked a turning point, ending the dominance of mounted samurai charges. The Takeda clan, crippled by the loss, was destroyed in 1582. The battle cleared Nobunaga's eastern flank and, with the later campaigns of Sekigahara, paved the road to the unification of Japan.
