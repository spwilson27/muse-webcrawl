Look-elsewhere effect
FieldStatistics, particle physics
Related conceptsMultiple comparisons problem, Bonferroni correction, trials factor
[Image: Look-elsewhere effect]

The look-elsewhere effect is a phenomenon in the statistical analysis of scientific experiments, particularly in particle physics: the more independent places one looks for an effect, the more likely a statistical fluctuation somewhere is to appear locally significant, even when no real effect exists. Formally, the probability of observing a given excess somewhere in a search range (the "global" p-value) is larger than the probability at any single specified point (the "local" p-value).

Explanation

Suppose a search examines a particle's possible mass across many bins. Even under the null hypothesis, random fluctuations make some bin unusually high; quoting only that bin's local significance would overstate the evidence. The correction factor between local and global significance is often called the trials factor. In the simplest model it approximates the number of effectively independent search regions, though correlations between nearby bins complicate the calculation (approaches include the method of Gross and Vitells, 2010).

Examples

The effect was widely discussed during the Higgs boson search: the ATLAS and CMS collaborations quoted global significances accounting for looking over the full mass range before claiming discovery at five standard deviations in 2012. Conversely, several transient "bumps" — such as the Θ+ pentaquark claims of the early 2000s and the 750 GeV diphoton excess of 2015 — disappeared with more data, consistent with look-elsewhere fluctuations.

Relation to other concepts

The look-elsewhere effect is a special case of the multiple comparisons problem, also relevant to data dredging and the Texas sharpshooter fallacy. Corrections such as Bonferroni's address analogous issues in other fields.
