Niche differentiation

Niche differentiation (also called niche partitioning or niche segregation) is the process by which competing species use the environment differently in ways that help them to coexist. It arises from the competitive exclusion principle, which holds that two species competing for exactly the same resources cannot stably coexist; natural selection therefore drives competitors to diverge in their resource use, habitat, or activity patterns.

Mechanisms

Differentiation can occur along several axes. In resource partitioning, species divide food or other resources — for example, warbler species foraging in different parts of the same tree, as documented in Robert MacArthur's classic studies. In spatial partitioning, species occupy different microhabitats, while in temporal partitioning they are active at different times of day or seasons. Character displacement is an evolutionary pattern in which traits of similar species diverge more where their ranges overlap — famously observed in the beak sizes of Darwin's finches on the Galápagos Islands.

Significance

Niche differentiation is a central explanation for the high biodiversity of ecosystems such as tropical rainforests and coral reefs, where many similar species coexist. Ecologists measure niche overlap to predict competition, coexistence, and the effects of invasive species. The concept complements niche construction and modern coexistence theory, which distinguish niche differences from fitness differences between competitors.
