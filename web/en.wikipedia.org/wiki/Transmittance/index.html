Transmittance

Transmittance compares transmitted with incident radiant flux.

In optics and spectroscopy, transmittance of the surface of a material is its effectiveness in transmitting radiant energy. It is the fraction of incident electromagnetic power that is transmitted through a sample, in contrast to the transmission coefficient, which is the ratio of the transmitted to incident electric field.

T = I / I0

where I0 is the intensity of radiation incident on the sample and I is the intensity leaving it. Transmittance is commonly expressed as a percentage and is related to absorbance by A = −log10 T.

Beer–Lambert law

For a homogeneous absorbing medium, transmittance falls exponentially with path length and concentration according to the Beer–Lambert law, the basis of quantitative spectrophotometry. Internal transmittance corrects for reflection losses at the sample surfaces, while hemispherical and diffuse transmittance distinguish between total and scattered transmitted light.

Applications

Transmittance measurements are used throughout analytical chemistry, atmospheric science (the transmission of sunlight through air), filter and lens specification, and oceanography, where the attenuation of light with depth governs photosynthesis in the surface ocean.
