Magnoliids (also magnoliidae) are a clade of flowering plants (angiosperms) comprising about 10,000 species in four orders: Canellales, Laurales, Magnoliales, and Piperales. The group includes many familiar plants — magnolias, tulip trees, laurels, cinnamon, avocados, black pepper, nutmeg, custard apples, and sassafras. Under the APG IV classification system, the magnoliids are one of the major lineages of the mesangiosperms, branching off early in angiosperm evolution, though after the basal ANA grade (Amborellales, Nymphaeales, Austrobaileyales).

Characteristics

Magnoliids are mostly woody plants, shrubs, and trees, often with aromatic tissues containing ethereal oil cells. Their flowers frequently have parts arranged spirally and numerous stamens and carpels, features once considered primitive. Many have vessel elements resembling those of other angiosperms, though some (such as Winteraceae in the Canellales) lack vessels entirely, relying on tracheids.

Classification and evolution

Older systems, such as Cronquist's, placed these plants in the subclass Magnoliidae within a broadly defined group of "primitive dicots". Molecular phylogenetics dissolved that grouping but upheld the magnoliids as a true clade, sister to the clade containing monocots and eudicots together. The fossil record and molecular clocks suggest the lineage diversified during the Cretaceous radiation of flowering plants.

Economic importance

The clade supplies major spices and foods: black pepper (Piperales), cinnamon, bay leaves and avocado (Laurales), and nutmeg (Magnoliales). Magnolias and tulip trees are widely planted ornamentals, and the wood of some Laurales is valued timber.
