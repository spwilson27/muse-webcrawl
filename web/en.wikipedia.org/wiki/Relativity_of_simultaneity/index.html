Relativity of simultaneity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The relativity of simultaneity is the concept that simultaneity — whether two events occur at the same time — is not absolute but depends on the observer's reference frame. In Albert Einstein's theory of special relativity, observers in relative motion can disagree about whether two spatially separated events happened simultaneously, because the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames.

Einstein's train thought experiment

Einstein illustrated the idea with a train struck by lightning at both ends. An observer on the platform, equidistant from the strikes, receives both flashes at once and judges them simultaneous. An observer at the train's midpoint, moving toward one flash and away from the other, receives them at different times — and, knowing light travels at the same speed relative to the train, correctly concludes the strikes were not simultaneous in the train's frame. Neither observer is wrong; simultaneity itself is frame-dependent.

Consequences

The effect follows mathematically from the Lorentz transformations: for two events separated by distance Δx and time Δt in one frame, a frame moving at speed v measures a time difference shifted by −vΔx/c². Only events at the same location, or causally connected events whose order is protected by causality, keep a frame-independent ordering. The relativity of simultaneity underlies the twin paradox, the ladder paradox, and the Andromeda paradox, which highlights how tiny relative velocities can shift distant "presents" by days.
