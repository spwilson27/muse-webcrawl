Einstein ring

An Einstein ring, also known as an Einstein–Chwolson ring, is the deformation of the light from a source (such as a galaxy or star) into a ring through gravitational lensing of the source's light by an object with an extremely large mass (such as another galaxy or a black hole). This occurs when the source, lens and observer are all in a perfect line — a configuration called syzygy. The phenomenon is a consequence of Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity, which predicts that massive objects curve spacetime and bend the path of light passing near them.

Introduction

Gravitational lensing was predicted by Einstein in 1936, though he considered observation unlikely. In 1924 Orest Chwolson had noted that a perfectly aligned lens would produce a halo-like image. When alignment is not perfect, the image typically appears as arcs or multiple images rather than a complete ring. The angular radius of an Einstein ring depends on the mass of the lens and the distances between source, lens and observer, and measuring it provides a way to weigh the lensing galaxy, including its dark matter.

Known Einstein rings

The first complete Einstein ring, designated B1938+666, was discovered in 1998 by astronomers at the University of Manchester using the MERLIN array and confirmed with the Hubble Space Telescope. The "Cosmic Horseshoe" (SDSS J090122.37+181432.3), discovered in 2007, is one of the largest known rings, produced by a massive luminous red galaxy about 4.6 billion light-years away lensing a more distant galaxy. Surveys such as the Sloan Lens ACS Survey have catalogued dozens of ring and arc systems, and the Hubble image of SDP.81 showed a striking near-complete ring formed by a submillimetre galaxy lensed by a foreground galaxy.

Use in astronomy

Einstein rings act as natural telescopes, magnifying distant background objects that would otherwise be too faint to study. They also allow precise measurements of the mass distribution of lensing galaxies and have been used to test general relativity and to probe the distribution of dark matter.
