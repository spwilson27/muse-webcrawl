Methemoglobinemia

Methemoglobinemia
SpecialtyHaematology, toxicology
SymptomsCyanosis, headache, fatigue, shortness of breath
CausesCongenital enzyme defects; oxidising drugs and chemicals
TreatmentRemoval of cause; methylene blue in severe cases

Methemoglobinemia is a condition in which an abnormal amount of methemoglobin — haemoglobin in which the iron at the centre of the heme group is in the ferric (Fe3+) rather than the usual ferrous (Fe2+) state — is present in the blood. Methemoglobin cannot bind oxygen effectively and also increases the oxygen affinity of the remaining normal heme groups, so tissue oxygen delivery is impaired. Normal levels are below about 1%; symptoms appear as levels rise.


Comparison of normal bright red blood with the chocolate-brown blood of methemoglobinemia

Causes

Acquired methemoglobinemia, the commoner form, follows exposure to oxidising agents: certain local anaesthetics (benzocaine, prilocaine), dapsone, nitrates and nitrites (including contaminated well water, a cause of "blue baby syndrome" in infants), aniline dyes, and some antimalarials. Congenital forms result from deficiency of the enzyme cytochrome b5 reductase, which normally keeps methemoglobin reduced, or from abnormal haemoglobin variants (haemoglobin M disease).

Signs and diagnosis

The hallmark is cyanosis out of proportion to respiratory findings, with blood that appears chocolate-brown and does not brighten on exposure to air. Pulse oximetry is misleading; diagnosis is by co-oximetry, which measures methemoglobin directly. Symptoms progress from headache, fatigue, and dizziness at moderate levels to dyspnoea, altered consciousness, seizures, and death at high levels.

Treatment

Mild acquired cases resolve once the offending agent is withdrawn. Symptomatic or severe cases are treated with intravenous methylene blue, which accelerates enzymatic reduction of methemoglobin; it is ineffective and potentially harmful in patients with G6PD deficiency, in whom alternatives such as high-dose ascorbic acid, riboflavin, or exchange transfusion may be needed.
