Laryngectomy

Laryngectomy is the surgical removal of the larynx, separating the airway from the mouth, nose and oesophagus. In a total laryngectomy the patient thereafter breathes through a stoma (tracheostomy opening) in the neck, and the normal voice is lost. Partial laryngectomy removes only part of the larynx and may preserve speech.

Indications and history

The operation is performed mainly for advanced laryngeal cancer, and occasionally for severe trauma or dysfunctional larynx after radiotherapy. The first successful total laryngectomy was performed by Theodor Billroth in Vienna on 31 December 1873, on a patient with laryngeal cancer. Modern practice increasingly favours organ-preservation treatment with chemotherapy and radiotherapy for many patients, reserving laryngectomy for advanced or recurrent disease.

Voice rehabilitation

After total laryngectomy, speech can be restored by three main methods: a tracheoesophageal voice prosthesis (a one-way valve fitted between trachea and oesophagus, now the most common method), oesophageal speech, and the electrolarynx, a vibrating device held against the neck. Patients also lose the ability to smell normally and must protect the stoma from water when swimming or showering.
