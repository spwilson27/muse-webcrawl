The Japanese dialects (方言, hōgen) are regional varieties of the Japanese language spoken across the Japanese archipelago. They differ in pitch accent, inflection, vocabulary, verb endings and sometimes phonology — in some cases enough to impede mutual intelligibility, a fact historically remarked in Japanese proverbs about travellers unable to understand distant provinces.

Classification

Linguists generally divide mainland Japanese into Eastern Japanese (including the Tōhoku, Kantō and eastern Chūbu dialects) and Western Japanese (Kansai, Chūgoku, Shikoku and Hokuriku), with Kyushu Japanese sometimes treated separately and the divergent Hachijō dialects of the Izu Islands forming their own branch. The Ryukyuan languages of Okinawa and the Amami islands are usually classified as separate languages rather than dialects, though they are traditionally called "dialects" in Japan.

Notable dialects

The Kansai dialect (Kansai-ben), spoken in Osaka, Kyoto and Kobe, is the best-known non-standard variety, famous for distinctive vocabulary (ōkini for "thank you"), the copula ya in place of da, and its association with comedy (manzai). The Tōhoku dialect (Tōhoku-ben, once stigmatised as zūzū-ben) is noted for merging certain vowel sounds. Hakata-ben in Fukuoka and Satsugū (Kagoshima) dialect, famously opaque even to other Japanese, are prominent Kyushu varieties.

Standardisation and decline

From the Meiji period, state education and later broadcasting promoted Standard Japanese (hyōjungo) based on Tokyo speech, and policies at times actively suppressed dialect use (as with the "dialect tag" punishments in Okinawa and Tōhoku schools). Since the late 20th century, dialects have undergone a revival as markers of regional identity and appear prominently in popular media, though dialect levelling continues in larger cities.
