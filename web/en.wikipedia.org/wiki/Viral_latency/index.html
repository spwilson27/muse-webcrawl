Viral latency

Viral latency is the ability of a pathogenic virus to lie dormant (latent) within a cell, as part of its lysogenic or persistent life cycle. A latent viral infection produces no infectious virions and typically causes no symptoms, yet the viral genome persists—either as an episome or integrated into the host DNA—and can reactivate later to enter the lytic cycle and produce disease or transmissible virus. Latency is a major obstacle to curing infections such as HIV and herpes.

The viral genome persisting silently within a host cell during latency.

Mechanisms

Herpesviruses are the classic example: after primary infection, herpes simplex virus retreats along nerves to sensory ganglia, where its genome persists in neurons with almost all viral genes silenced, expressing only latency-associated transcripts. Stress, illness, or immunosuppression can trigger reactivation, producing cold sores or genital lesions. Varicella-zoster virus similarly reactivates decades later as shingles. Retroviruses such as HIV integrate a DNA provirus into host chromosomes; latently infected resting memory T cells form a reservoir invisible to antiretroviral therapy, which is why HIV treatment must continue for life. Bacteriophage lambda provided the molecular model of lysogeny.

Types

  • Proviral latency — genome integrated into host DNA (retroviruses, phages).
  • Episomal latency — genome maintained as a separate circular DNA molecule in the nucleus (herpesviruses, papillomaviruses).

Clinical significance

Latency underlies recurrent and recrudescent viral diseases, complicates transfusion and transplantation safety, and drives research strategies such as "shock and kill" (forcing HIV out of latency so infected cells can be eliminated) and "block and lock" (deepening viral silencing).
