Sponge cake

Sponge cake
TypeCake
Main ingredientsFlour, sugar, eggs
VariationsGenoise, chiffon cake, angel food cake, Victoria sponge, castella

Sponge cake is a light cake made with eggs, flour, and sugar, sometimes leavened only by the air beaten into the eggs. Unlike butter cakes, a classic sponge contains little or no added fat, and its structure depends on a stable egg foam into which the dry ingredients are gently folded.

History

Foam cakes trace to Renaissance Italy, where cooks at the court of Savoy developed biscuits de Savoie. The Italian pan di Spagna ("bread of Spain") and the French génoise spread the technique across Europe; Portuguese traders later carried a version to Japan, where it became castella. In Britain, the filled and jam-layered Victoria sponge became a teatime standard in the nineteenth century.

Method

Whole eggs (or yolks and whites beaten separately) are whipped with sugar until the mixture reaches the "ribbon stage," falling in thick folds from the whisk. Sifted flour is folded in with minimal loss of air, and the batter is baked promptly. Because the foam is fragile, sponges are often cooled carefully, sometimes upside down, to prevent collapse.

Types

  • Genoise – whole eggs warmed and beaten with sugar; a small amount of melted butter may be folded in. The base for many French entremets and layered cakes.
  • Chiffon cake – incorporates vegetable oil and beaten egg whites in separate steps; moist and tender, invented in California in 1927.
  • Angel food cake – made only with egg whites, no yolk or fat; very white and light.
  • Victoria sponge – a richer British variant with butter, typically sandwiched with jam and cream.
  • Castella – Japanese sponge sweetened with honey or syrup, baked in rectangular moulds.

Uses

Sponge cakes are soaked with syrups and layered in desserts such as trifle, tiramisu (with ladyfinger biscuits, a related foam), Swiss rolls, and Bûche de Noël, and form the base of strawberry shortcake in several traditions.
