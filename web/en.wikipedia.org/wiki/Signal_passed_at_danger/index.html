Signal passed at danger

A signal passed at danger (SPAD) is an incident in railway operations in which a train passes a colour-light or semaphore signal showing a danger aspect (typically red) without authority. In North American terminology a similar event may be described as running a red signal. A SPAD is a serious safety event because the signal protects a section of line that may contain another train, conflicting movements, or an unsecured route; however, most SPADs result in no harm because safety margins and protection systems intervene.

Causes

Investigations of SPADs commonly identify human factors: distraction, fatigue, misreading a signal (especially where a less restrictive aspect at a nearby signal is mistaken for one's own — "reading through"), expectation bias, poor signal sighting, slippery rail conditions affecting braking, and communication failures between driver and signaller. A minority of SPADs are caused by technical failures or by signals returned to danger immediately in front of a train.

Risk and mitigation

The consequences of a SPAD depend on how far the train travels beyond the signal and what it encounters; the distance available is known as the overlap. Mitigation measures include defensive driving techniques, signal sighting committees, the Automatic Warning System (AWS), Train Protection & Warning System (TPWS), which applies the brakes automatically if a train approaches a red signal too fast or passes it at low speed, and automatic train protection and European Train Control System (ETCS) installations that supervise speed and movement authority continuously.

Statistics and investigation

In Great Britain, SPADs are recorded and risk-ranked by the rail industry and investigated by operators, Network Rail, and, for serious incidents, the Rail Accident Investigation Branch. SPAD rates are a key indicator of railway safety performance and have fallen with the introduction of TPWS and improved driver training.
