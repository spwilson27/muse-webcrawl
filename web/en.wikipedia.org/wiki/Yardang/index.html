A yardang is a streamlined, elongated hill carved from bedrock or consolidated sediment by the dual action of wind abrasion and deflation. Yardangs are typically three or more times longer than they are wide, and when viewed from above they resemble the hull of an inverted boat. Facing the wind is a steep, blunt face that gradually tapers to a lower, trailing edge.

The word "yardang" derives from the Turkic word yar, meaning "steep bank", and was introduced into English-language geology by the Swedish explorer Sven Hedin in 1903, after his travels in Central Asia.

Formation

Yardangs form in environments where water is scarce and the prevailing winds are strong and unidirectional. The wind picks up sand and silt, which act as an abrasive tool that sculpts exposed rock. Softer rock layers erode faster than harder ones, producing the characteristic streamlined profile. Most yardangs are carved in sandstone, siltstone, shale, or limestone, but they have also been observed in crystalline rocks such as granite.

Because the abrasive load of the wind is concentrated near the ground, yardangs often develop undercut bases, resembling pedestals or mushrooms in extreme cases. Fields of yardangs separated by flat, wind-scoured corridors (sometimes called "moats") can extend for tens of kilometres.

Distribution

Yardangs occur in many of the world's deserts. Famous examples include:

  • The White Desert of Egypt, where yardangs carved from Cretaceous chalk and limestone rise from the desert floor.
  • The Lut Desert of Iran, which contains some of the largest yardangs on Earth, in ridges up to 80 metres high and 150 kilometres long.
  • The Yardang National Geopark near Dunhuang in China's Gansu province.
  • The Danakil Depression and the deserts of the Arabian Peninsula.
  • The Atacama Desert of Chile and parts of the southwestern United States.

On other planets

Yardangs have been identified in orbital imagery of Mars, where they provide evidence of persistent wind regimes and help scientists estimate which Martian rocks are soft and easily eroded. Large yardang fields occur in the Medusae Fossae Formation near the Martian equator, and examples have also been photographed by rovers at Gale Crater. Features interpreted as yardangs have additionally been reported on Saturn's moon Titan, where they would have been carved in water-ice bedrock by winds blowing grains of organic sand.
