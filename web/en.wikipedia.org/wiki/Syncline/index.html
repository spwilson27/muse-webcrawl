In structural geology, a syncline is a fold with younger layers closer to the centre of the structure; consequently a syncline is typically a downward fold (synform), with the layers dipping toward the fold's axial surface. Its counterpart, the anticline, arches upward and has the oldest strata in its core.

Characteristics

On a geologic map, a syncline is recognised as a sequence of rock layers that dip toward a central axis, with the youngest strata exposed in the middle. Synclines form under compressive stress, as tectonic forces shorten the crust. Because erosion attacks the landscape over time, the shape of the ground surface need not match the fold: a syncline may underlie a ridge if its core is made of resistant rock — an example of inverted relief.

Economic significance

Unlike anticlines, which famously trap petroleum in their crests, synclines tend to channel fluids away from their cores; however, groundwater and some mineral deposits accumulate in synclinal basins, and the troughs of synclines often preserve thick sequences of sedimentary rock that record basin history.

Examples

The Powder River Basin in Wyoming and the synclinal valleys of the Appalachian fold belt of Pennsylvania are well-known examples. Large downwarped regions are sometimes called geosynclines in older literature, a term largely superseded by plate-tectonic models of sedimentary basins.
