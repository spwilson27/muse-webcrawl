Gyros

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Pork gyros meat on a vertical spit beside a wrapped pita sandwich]

Gyros is a Greek dish of meat — traditionally pork, also chicken — seasoned, stacked on a vertical rotisserie, roasted and shaved, then wrapped in soft pita with tomato, onion, tzatziki and, in Greece, a handful of fries inside the wrap. The name (γύρος, "turn") mirrors its Turkish cousin döner and its Levantine sibling shawarma, from which it descends: Greek refugees from Asia Minor brought spit-roasted meat to Athens after 1922, and by the postwar decades the gyro had become Greece's defining fast food, and then the Greek diaspora's gift to the world.

From Athens to Chicago

In Athens, gyros meat is usually individual slices of pork stacked by hand, yielding irregular, crispy-edged shavings. In the United States the format industrialised: Chicago's Greek community developed the compressed cone of seasoned ground beef and lamb, sliced from the spit and griddled — the Parthenon restaurant's owners are widely credited with popularising, and factories with mass-producing, the American gyro cone in the 1970s. New York's Greek diners and food carts made it a street-food standard. Pronunciation follows the Greek: "yee-ros", though English speakers say it every possible way.

Anatomy of a wrap

The classic Athenian wrap is a study in contrasts: hot fatty meat, cool garlicky tzatziki, raw onion bite, tomato juice, salty fries, all inside a pillowy, oil-kissed pita. Chicken gyros with mustard sauce (a Thessaloniki habit), vegetarian versions with grilled halloumi, and "gyros plates" over rice or salad extend the idea. However it is served, the rules are freshness off the spit and generosity with the shavings.
