Geometrical optics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Geometrical optics, or ray optics, is a model of optics that describes light propagation in terms of rays. The ray in geometrical optics is an abstraction useful for approximating the paths along which light travels under certain circumstances. In this model, a ray is a line or curve perpendicular to the light's wavefronts, directed along the flow of energy.

Geometrical optics is founded on three empirical laws: the law of rectilinear propagation (light travels in straight lines in a homogeneous medium), the law of reflection, and Snell's law of refraction. It works well when the wavelength of light is small compared with the sizes of structures it encounters, but it cannot explain diffraction and interference, which require wave optics.

Basic principles

Reflection

When a ray strikes a smooth surface, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal to the surface. Specular reflection in plane and curved mirrors is treated by tracing rays through focal points.

Refraction

At the interface between two media of refractive indices n1 and n2, a ray bends according to Snell's law, n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2. This underlies the design of lenses, which form images by refracting rays according to the lensmaker's equation.

Fermat's principle

The laws of geometrical optics can be derived from Fermat's principle: light follows the path that makes the optical path length stationary — usually, though not always, the path of least time.

Key concepts

  • Focal length and power of a lens, governing image formation.
  • Optical aberrations — spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism, chromatic aberration — deviations from ideal imaging caused by the approximations of the paraxial regime.
  • Ray tracing, the systematic computation of ray paths through an optical system, now done computationally for lens design.
  • Caustics, envelopes of reflected or refracted rays, such as the bright curves seen on a table under a glass of water.

History

The earliest surviving treatise on optics, attributed to Euclid, already treats light as travelling in straight rays. Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) systematised the subject in his Book of Optics (1021). Kepler, Descartes, and Snell established the refraction law in the 1620s–30s, and Fermat gave the unifying principle of least time in 1662. Geometrical optics remained the working theory of instrument design through the development of telescopes, microscopes, and photographic lenses.
