Glam rock

Glam rock is a style of rock music that developed in the United Kingdom in the early 1970s, performed by musicians who wore outrageous costumes, makeup and hairstyles — glitter, platform boots and science-fiction or androgynous personas. Musically it drew on 1950s rock and roll, stomping rhythms and anthemic choruses, paired with a knowing, camp theatricality. Its leading figures included T. Rex, David Bowie, Roxy Music, Slade, Mott the Hoople, the Sweet and Gary Glitter.

Characteristics

Glam combined a back-to-basics rock and roll drive with elaborate visual presentation. Performers cultivated flamboyant, often sexually ambiguous images; songs frequently dealt with fame, youth, decadence and science fiction. The movement split roughly into a pop-oriented "glitter" wing (Slade, Sweet, Gary Glitter) producing chart singles, and a more art-school, experimental wing (Bowie, Roxy Music, Brian Eno) whose influence extended into punk and new wave.

History

Marc Bolan of T. Rex is generally credited with launching glam in 1970–71: after the hit single "Ride a White Swan", he adopted glitter makeup and satin costumes, and the albums Electric Warrior (1971) and The Slider (1972) made T. Rex the biggest act in Britain. David Bowie, who had scored a hit with "Space Oddity" (1969), created the persona of Ziggy Stardust for The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars (1972), bringing alien androgyny and concepts of stardom to the centre of the genre before theatrically "retiring" Ziggy on stage in July 1973. Roxy Music, Alice Cooper and Lou Reed (whose Bowie-produced Transformer appeared in 1972) carried glam aesthetics into the United States, where related acts included the New York Dolls and Kiss.

By 1975–76 glam had faded as its stars moved on — Bowie into the "plastic soul" of Young Americans, Bolan's career declining before his death in 1977 — but its fingerprints remained on punk's DIY theatrics, the New Romantics, glam metal in the 1980s, and Britpop in the 1990s.

Legacy

Critics regard glam as a pivotal moment when pop stars became self-conscious constructions and gender presentation in rock was openly questioned. The genre has been repeatedly revived and referenced, in films such as Velvet Goldmine (1998) and Hedwig and the Angry Inch, and in the work of artists from Prince and Lady Gaga onward.
