2001: A Space Odyssey

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The stewardess walking the rotating centrifuge of the space station, in the film's serene vision of Pan Am-era orbital travel]

2001: A Space Odyssey is a 1968 epic science fiction film produced and directed by Stanley Kubrick, co-written with Arthur C. Clarke and partly inspired by Clarke's story 'The Sentinel'. From ape-men's first tool to a mission to Jupiter shadowed by the computer HAL 9000, and through the Star Gate to the Star Child, it is the most ambitious and arguably the greatest science-fiction film ever made — a work of visual and musical grandeur (Strauss, Khachaturian, Ligeti) with almost no dialogue.

Premiering to baffled walkouts and mixed reviews, it became a phenomenon among young audiences and the definitive big-screen experience of the space age — made, astonishingly, before Apollo 11 landed. Its production design (NASA consultants, Harry Lange and Frederick Ordway) set the look of the future for half a century.

Structure

Four movements: The Dawn of Man (a monolith teaches apes the bone-weapon; the match-cut from bone to orbiting satellite is cinema's boldest ellipsis); a near-silent ballet of docking spacecraft to 'The Blue Danube'; the Jupiter mission, where HAL's calm murder of the crew is film's definitive machine intelligence; and the psychedelic Star Gate sequence ending in a neoclassical bedroom and rebirth. Kubrick refused all explanation, and the film's meanings have multiplied accordingly.

Reception and influence

It won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects (Kubrick's only Oscar). Every space film since — Star Wars, Alien, Interstellar — works in its shadow; its 50th-anniversary re-release in 70mm and 'unrestored' prints toured as a sacred event. The British Film Institute and Sight and Sound polls keep it in the highest tier of the canon.
