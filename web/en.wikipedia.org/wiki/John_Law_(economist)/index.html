John Law (economist)

John Law (pronounced "Laws" in French period usage; baptised 21 April 1671 – 21 March 1729) was a Scottish economist and financier who founded France's first central bank and presided over the Mississippi Bubble of 1720, one of history's most famous financial manias.

Early life

Born in Edinburgh to a goldsmith-banker, Law became a London dandy ("Beau Law") and fled to the continent after killing a rival, Beau Wilson, in a duel in 1694. His 1705 treatise Money and Trade Considered proposed a paper currency backed by land — ideas Scotland rejected but which he later put into practice in France.

The System in France

After the death of Louis XIV left France burdened with debt, the Regent Philippe d'Orléans allowed Law to found the Banque Générale (1716), which became the Banque Royale, issuing paper notes, and the Compagnie d'Occident (the Mississippi Company), which took over Louisiana trade and the national debt. A frenzy of speculation in company shares made Law, briefly Controller-General of Finances and one of Europe's most powerful men, immensely unpopular when the bubble burst in 1720 and the notes collapsed.

Exile and death

Law fled France in December 1720, losing his fortune; he died in poverty in Venice in 1729. Economists have since reassessed him as a pioneer of monetary theory and central banking whose scheme failed chiefly through over-issue of notes and shares.
