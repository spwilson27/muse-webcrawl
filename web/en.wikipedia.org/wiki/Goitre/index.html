A goitre (also spelled goiter) is a swelling in the neck resulting from an enlarged thyroid gland. A goitre can be associated with a thyroid that is not functioning properly, although most goitres are caused by a deficiency of iodine in the diet and are not associated with inflammation or cancer.

More than 200 million people worldwide are affected by goitre, the great majority of cases being due to iodine deficiency. The term is from the Latin gutturia, meaning throat.

Signs and symptoms

A goitre may present as a palpable or visible enlargement of the thyroid gland at the base of the neck. When large, it can compress the trachea or oesophagus, causing difficulty breathing or swallowing. Many goitres are asymptomatic and are detected only on physical examination.

Causes

The most common cause worldwide is iodine deficiency, which impairs the thyroid's ability to produce thyroid hormones. Other causes include autoimmune diseases such as Hashimoto's thyroiditis and Graves' disease, thyroid nodules, thyroiditis, and, rarely, thyroid cancer. Certain substances known as goitrogens can also interfere with iodine uptake.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically based on physical examination, thyroid function tests measuring TSH and free T4, and ultrasound imaging of the thyroid. Fine-needle aspiration may be used to evaluate suspicious nodules.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause and size. Small, asymptomatic goitres may simply be monitored. Iodine supplementation treats deficiency-related goitre; thyroid hormone replacement can shrink goitre in hypothyroid patients. Radioactive iodine or surgery may be required for large or compressive goitres.

Epidemiology and history

Goitre has been recognised since ancient times; Chinese physicians used burnt sponge and seaweed — rich in iodine — to treat it. The introduction of iodised salt in the 20th century dramatically reduced its prevalence in many countries, though it remains common in parts of Africa and Asia where iodine intake is low.
