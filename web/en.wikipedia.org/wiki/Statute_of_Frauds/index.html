Statute of Frauds

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Statute of Frauds (29 Cha. 2 c. 3) was an Act of the Parliament of England, enacted in 1677 under the formal title "An Act for Prevention of Frauds and Perjuries". It required certain types of contracts to be in writing and signed in order to be enforceable, in an effort to prevent fraud and perjury in oral agreements.

The statute's concept has been transmitted to the legal systems of many common-law jurisdictions, including every U.S. state, where a "statute of frauds" generally requires written evidence for specified categories of contracts.

Categories covered

The original Act covered, among other things:

  • Promises by an executor to answer for damages out of his own estate
  • Promises to answer for the debt, default or miscarriage of another (suretyship)
  • Agreements made in consideration of marriage
  • Contracts for the sale of land or any interest in land
  • Agreements not to be performed within one year of their making
  • Contracts for the sale of goods above a threshold value (section 17)

These categories are remembered in American law teaching by the mnemonic "MY LEGS" (Marriage, Year, Land, Executor, Goods, Suretyship).

Subsequent history

In England and Wales most of the original Act was repealed by the Law Reform (Enforcement of Contracts) Act 1954, leaving the guarantee provision; contracts for the disposition of interests in land are now governed by section 2 of the Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989. In the United States, the Uniform Commercial Code §2-201 requires a writing for sales of goods of $500 or more, and exceptions—including part performance, admission and specially manufactured goods—soften the rule.

Defences and exceptions

Equity historically responded with doctrines such as part performance and proprietary estoppel to prevent the statute itself being used as an instrument of fraud. Non-compliance generally makes a contract unenforceable rather than void.
