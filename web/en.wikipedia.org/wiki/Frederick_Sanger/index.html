Frederick Sanger (13 August 1918 – 19 November 2013) was an English biochemist who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry twice. He is one of only three people to have won the chemistry prize twice and one of five to have won any two Nobel Prizes.

His 1958 prize was for determining the complete amino-acid sequence of insulin (1949–55) — the first protein ever sequenced, proving proteins have definite structures. His 1980 prize (shared with Walter Gilbert and Paul Berg) was for the dideoxy 'chain-termination' method of DNA sequencing (1977), which made genome projects possible and was used to sequence the first complete genomes, including bacteriophage φX174 (1977) and, later, most of the Human Genome Project. The Wellcome Sanger Institute near Cambridge is named for him.

Sequencing methods

Sanger's career was a sequence of methods for sequences: paper chromatography and 2D 'fingerprinting' for proteins, then, for DNA, the plus-and-minus method and finally dideoxy sequencing, in which chain-terminating nucleotides produce a ladder of fragments read as the sequence. For three decades 'Sanger sequencing' was genomics' workhorse; even after next-generation methods displaced it, its accuracy keeps it in use for validation. Colleagues remembered Sanger as a quiet, bench-working craftsman who avoided administration and publicity.
