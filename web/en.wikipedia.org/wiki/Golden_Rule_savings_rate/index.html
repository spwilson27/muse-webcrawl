Golden Rule savings rate

In economics, the Golden Rule savings rate is the rate of saving that maximises the level of steady-state consumption in the Solow–Swan model of economic growth. It was introduced by Edmund Phelps in 1961, who named it after the biblical Golden Rule — "do unto others as you would have them do unto you" — interpreting it as each generation saving for future generations the proportion it wishes past generations had saved for it.

The model

In the Solow model, a higher saving rate raises the steady-state capital stock and output per worker, but capital must also be maintained: depreciation and a growing workforce require ever more investment merely to keep capital per worker constant. Consumption per worker is output minus this break-even investment. It is maximised where the marginal product of capital equals the sum of the depreciation rate and the growth rate of effective labour (MPK = δ + n + g).

Implications

An economy saving above the Golden Rule rate is dynamically inefficient: it could consume more at every date by saving less, because it has accumulated so much capital that maintaining it consumes more than the extra output it yields. Saving below the Golden Rule rate means steady-state consumption could be raised, but only at the cost of lower consumption during the transition. The concept underlies debates about capital taxation, social security, and whether real economies over- or under-accumulate capital.
