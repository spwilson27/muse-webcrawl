John Burroughs

John Burroughs (April 3, 1837 – March 29, 1921) was an American naturalist and nature essayist, active in the conservation movement in the United States. His bestselling nature essays made him one of the most popular American writers of his time and earned him the nickname "John o' Birds."

Life

Born on a farm in Roxbury, New York, in the Catskill Mountains, Burroughs worked as a teacher, journalist, and Treasury Department clerk in Washington, D.C., where he befriended Walt Whitman. In 1874 he bought a farm at West Park, New York, on the Hudson River, naming it Riverby; he later built the rustic cabin Slabsides nearby, which became a retreat for visitors including Theodore Roosevelt, John Muir, Henry Ford, and Thomas Edison.

Writing

Beginning with Wake-Robin (1871), Burroughs published more than two dozen volumes of essays, including Birds and Poets, Locusts and Wild Honey, and Signs and Seasons. In 1903 he sparked the "nature fakers" controversy by denouncing sentimental, inaccurate animal stories in an Atlantic Monthly article, a campaign later taken up by President Roosevelt.

Legacy

The John Burroughs Medal, awarded for distinguished nature writing, was established in his honour in 1926. John Burroughs High School in Burbank, California, and numerous schools and parks bear his name.
