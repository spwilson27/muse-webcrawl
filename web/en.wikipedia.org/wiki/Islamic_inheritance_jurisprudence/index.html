Islamic inheritance jurisprudence

Islamic inheritance jurisprudence is the branch of Islamic law (fiqh) known as ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ ("the science of the ordained shares") that governs the distribution of a deceased Muslim's estate. Its rules are derived principally from the Qur'an — especially verses 4:11, 4:12, and 4:176 of Surah An-Nisa — supplemented by hadith and juristic reasoning. It is one of the most mathematically intricate branches of classical Islamic law.

Principles

After payment of funeral expenses, debts, and bequests (waṣiyya) — which may dispose of at most one-third of the estate, and generally not in favor of an existing heir — the remaining estate is distributed among fixed-share heirs (aṣḥāb al-furūḍ) and residuaries (ʿaṣaba).

Fixed shares

Twelve categories of heirs receive fixed Qur'anic shares: fractions such as 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 2/3, 1/3, and 1/6. For example:

  • A husband receives 1/2 if the deceased wife left no children, 1/4 if she did.
  • A wife receives 1/4 if there are no children, 1/8 if there are.
  • Parents each receive 1/6 when the deceased left children.
  • Daughters: one daughter receives 1/2; two or more share 2/3 — unless sons are present, in which case children inherit as residuaries with a son receiving twice the share of a daughter.

Adjustments

If fixed shares total more than the whole estate, shares are proportionally reduced (ʿawl); if less, the surplus goes to residuaries, or, in their absence, may be returned to the fixed heirs (radd). Certain relatives block others from inheriting entirely (ḥajb).

Differences among schools

Sunni schools largely agree on the core scheme. Twelver Shia law differs significantly: it classifies heirs into classes, allows daughters to exclude more distant male relatives, and does not apply ʿawl. Modern Muslim-majority states have codified inheritance law with various reforms, including in some countries the "obligatory bequest" for orphaned grandchildren.
