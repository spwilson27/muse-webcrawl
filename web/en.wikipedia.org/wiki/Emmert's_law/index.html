Emmert's law

Emmert's law states that objects that generate retinal images of the same size will look different in physical size if they appear to be located at different distances. Specifically, the perceived size of an object whose retinal image is constant (such as an afterimage) varies in proportion to its apparent distance. The law is named after the German ophthalmologist Emil Emmert (1844–1911), who described the phenomenon in 1881.

Description

An afterimage, for example, can be produced by briefly looking at a bright light and then redirecting one's gaze to various surfaces at different distances. The afterimage appears to change in size depending on where it is projected: when viewed against a nearby surface it appears small, and when viewed against a distant surface it appears large, even though the size of the image on the retina remains unchanged.

The effect can be expressed mathematically. If the retinal image size is constant, the perceived size S of the projected afterimage is directly proportional to its apparent distance D: S ∝ D. This is closely related to the phenomenon of size constancy and to the operation of the visual system in combining retinal image size with distance information to compute object size.

Relation to other illusions

Emmert's law helps explain several optical illusions, including the Moon illusion, in which the Moon appears larger near the horizon than high in the sky, and the Ponzo illusion. In the Moon illusion, distance cues associated with the horizon make the brain interpret the same retinal image as a larger object.
