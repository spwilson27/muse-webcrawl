Glass ionomer cement

Glass ionomer cement (GIC) is a dental restorative material formed by the acid–base reaction between a fluoroaluminosilicate glass powder and a polyalkenoic acid liquid. Developed in the late 1960s by Alan Wilson and Brian Kent, it bonds chemically to tooth structure and releases fluoride.

Properties

Glass ionomer sets by an acid–base reaction in which the acid attacks the glass particles, releasing calcium and aluminium ions that crosslink the polyacid chains. Its coefficient of thermal expansion is close to that of dentine, and it adheres to enamel and dentine without a separate bonding agent. Fluoride release can help protect against secondary caries.

Uses

GICs are used for fillings in low-stress areas, as cavity liners and bases, for luting crowns and orthodontic brackets, and in the atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) technique. Resin-modified glass ionomers add light-cured resin components to improve strength and handling, though conventional glass ionomer remains weaker and less wear-resistant than composite resin.
