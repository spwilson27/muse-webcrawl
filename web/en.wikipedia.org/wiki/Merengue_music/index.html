Merengue music

Merengue
Origin: Dominican Republic
Typical instruments: accordion, tambora, güira, saxophone
Time signature: 2/4

Merengue is a style of Dominican music and dance. It is the national dance and music of the Dominican Republic and an important part of Dominican cultural identity. Partners hold each other in a closed position and move to fast-paced music in 2/4 time, typically at 110 to 190 beats per minute.

Instrumentation

The traditional form, merengue típico, features the diatonic accordion, the two-headed tambora drum (played with one stick and one bare hand), and the güira, a metal scraper. Modern orchestral merengue adds saxophones—often playing rapid "jaleo" figures—trumpets, piano, bass, and congas. Other instruments have included the marimba and the marímbula.

History

Merengue emerged in the nineteenth century in the Cibao region of the Dominican Republic, blending European dance forms with African rhythms. Initially associated with the rural poor and denounced by elites, it was promoted as national music under the dictatorship of Rafael Trujillo (1930–1961), who made it the country's official dance music. After Trujillo's fall, artists such as Johnny Ventura modernized the genre, and in the 1980s–90s merengue achieved wide international popularity. In 2016, UNESCO inscribed the music and dance of merengue on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Structure and styles

A merengue song typically alternates verses with a call-and-response chorus (jaleo) over a fast, driving rhythm. Variants include the slower pambiche, merengue típico, orchestral merengue, and later fusions such as merenhouse and merengue urbano (mambo).
