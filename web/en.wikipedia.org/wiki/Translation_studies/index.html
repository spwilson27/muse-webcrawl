Translation studies

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Translation studies is an academic interdiscipline dealing with the systematic study of the theory, description, and application of translation, interpreting, and localisation. As an interdiscipline, it borrows from linguistics, comparative literature, history, cultural studies, philosophy, computer science, and sociology.

Development

Although translation has been discussed since Cicero, Jerome, and Luther, translation studies emerged as a named discipline in the second half of the twentieth century. James S. Holmes's paper "The Name and Nature of Translation Studies" (presented 1972) is widely regarded as its founding statement, mapping the field into pure and applied branches. Through the 1980s and 1990s the field underwent a "cultural turn", associated with Susan Bassnett and André Lefevere, shifting attention from linguistic equivalence to translation as rewriting within target cultures.

Key concepts

Central debates include the nature of equivalence, the tension between foreignising and domesticating strategies (Friedrich Schleiermacher; Lawrence Venuti's "translator's invisibility"), functionalist approaches such as skopos theory (Hans Vermeer), descriptive translation studies and Gideon Toury's concept of translation norms, polysystem theory (Itamar Even-Zohar), and, since the 2000s, sociological, ethical, and technological approaches including corpus studies and machine translation.
