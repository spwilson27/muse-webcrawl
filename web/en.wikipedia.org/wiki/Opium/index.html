Opium (from Greek ópion, "poppy juice") is the dried latex obtained from the seed pods of the opium poppy (Papaver somniferum). It contains a mixture of alkaloids, principally morphine (typically around 10 percent), along with codeine, thebaine, papaverine and noscapine. Opium has been used since antiquity as an analgesic and sedative, and it remains the raw material for the production of morphine, codeine and semi-synthetic opioids such as heroin.

History

Cultivation of the opium poppy spread from the ancient Near East through the Mediterranean world; opium was described by Greek and Roman physicians, used in Islamic medicine, and traded widely along the Silk Road. In the early modern period it became a major commodity of European colonial trade. The British East India Company's opium exports to China, and China's attempts to suppress the trade, led to the First and Second Opium Wars (1839–1842 and 1856–1860). Opium dens and tinctures such as laudanum were widespread in the nineteenth century before twentieth-century drug-control treaties progressively restricted non-medical use.

Production

Opium is harvested by scoring the unripe seed capsules with a blade; the milky latex that exudes darkens and thickens in air and is scraped off the following day. The leading source of illicit opium for decades has been Afghanistan, with production also in Myanmar and Laos (the "Golden Triangle") and Mexico. Licit cultivation for pharmaceutical alkaloids takes place under licence in countries including India, Turkey and Australia.

Effects and regulation

Opium is smoked, eaten or drunk; its opioids act on receptors in the brain and gut, producing analgesia, euphoria, sedation and constipation, with risks of dependence, tolerance and fatal respiratory depression in overdose. Production and trade are governed by the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961) and monitored by the International Narcotics Control Board.
