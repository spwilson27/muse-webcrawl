Ebola

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ebola is a severe, often fatal viral haemorrhagic fever of humans and other primates, caused by ebolaviruses (family Filoviridae). It was first recognised in 1976 in outbreaks near the Ebola River in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo and in Sudan. Outbreaks occur sporadically in sub-Saharan Africa; the largest, in West Africa in 2014–2016, caused more than 28,000 cases and about 11,000 deaths.

Transmission and illness

The virus spreads through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of infected people or animals, and through contaminated materials; it is not airborne over distance. Fruit bats are considered the likely natural reservoir. Illness begins with fever, fatigue and muscle pain, progressing to vomiting, diarrhoea, rash and, in many cases, internal and external bleeding and organ failure. Case fatality rates have averaged around 50%, varying by species and outbreak.

Treatment and prevention

Care is mainly supportive — fluids and treatment of symptoms — but monoclonal antibody treatments and a vaccine (rVSV-ZEBOV, licensed from 2019) have improved outcomes for Zaire ebolavirus, the deadliest species. Rapid isolation, contact tracing, safe burials and protective equipment for health workers are central to ending outbreaks.
