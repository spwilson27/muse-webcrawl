Crown Jewels of the United Kingdom

The Crown Jewels of the United Kingdom are a collection of royal ceremonial objects kept at the Tower of London, including the regalia and vestments worn by British monarchs at their coronations. They comprise St Edward's Crown, the Imperial State Crown, orb, sceptres, swords, ampulla and anointing spoon, and numerous other crowns, tiaras and pieces of plate — some 140 royal ceremonial objects in total, set with more than 23,000 gemstones.

History

England's medieval regalia was destroyed or sold after the execution of Charles I in 1649, when Oliver Cromwell ordered the crowns broken up and the gold melted down as symbols of the "detestable rule of kings". Almost all the present regalia dates from the Restoration: for the coronation of Charles II in 1661, new pieces were made by the goldsmith Sir Robert Vyner, including St Edward's Crown and the Sovereign's Orb. The medieval anointing spoon and ampulla survived — the spoon because it was bought back, tradition says, by a royalist.

In 1671 the Irish adventurer Colonel Thomas Blood came close to stealing the jewels, flattening St Edward's Crown with a mallet before being caught; he was unexpectedly pardoned by Charles II. The jewels have been displayed to the public since the 17th century and are held in the Jewel House at the Tower, guarded by the Yeomen Warders.

Principal pieces

  • St Edward's Crown — solid gold, used only to crown the monarch, who then exchanges it for the lighter Imperial State Crown.
  • Imperial State Crown — set with the Black Prince's Ruby (a spinel), the Stuart Sapphire, St Edward's Sapphire, and the Cullinan II diamond.
  • Sovereign's Sceptre with the Cross — set with Cullinan I, the "Great Star of Africa", the largest clear-cut diamond in the world at 530.2 carats.
  • Koh-i-Nūr diamond — the famous 105.6-carat diamond set in the crown of Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother; its ownership is disputed by India and others.
