The Clavicipitaceae are a family of fungi within the order Hypocreales. Members of the family are notable for their ecological relationships with plants, insects, and other fungi: many are parasites or symbionts of grasses, sedges, and rushes, while others infect scale insects and cicadas or parasitize the fruiting bodies of other fungi. The family is best known for Claviceps purpurea, the ergot fungus of rye and other cereals.

Ecology and significance

Many clavicipitaceous fungi live within grass tissues as endophytes. Genera such as Epichloë grow systemically in cool-season grasses and produce alkaloids that deter insect and mammalian herbivores — a mutualism for the plant but a source of livestock toxicoses such as fescue toxicosis and ryegrass staggers. Claviceps species infect the ovaries of grasses and cereals, replacing the grain with a dark sclerotium (ergot) rich in ergot alkaloids; consumption of contaminated grain historically caused ergotism ("St. Anthony's fire"), while purified alkaloids became important pharmaceuticals, including ergometrine and the migraine drug ergotamine.

Genera

The family includes, among others:

  • Claviceps — ergot fungi of cereals and grasses
  • Epichloë (including former Neotyphodium asexual forms) — grass endophytes
  • Balansia — tropical grass symbionts
  • Metarhizium — insect-pathogenic fungi widely used in biological pest control
  • Hypocrella and Aschersonia — parasites of scale insects and whiteflies
  • Moelleriella and related genera

Molecular phylogenetic studies in the 2000s revised the family's boundaries, splitting off the Cordycipitaceae and Ophiocordycipitaceae (which now contain most of the famous "zombie-ant" and caterpillar fungi) and confirming Clavicipitaceae in a narrower sense centered on plant-associated and some insect-associated lineages.
