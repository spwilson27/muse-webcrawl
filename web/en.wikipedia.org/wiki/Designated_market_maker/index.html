A designated market maker (DMM) is a market maker responsible for maintaining fair and orderly markets in an assigned set of listed securities. On the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), DMMs — formerly known as "specialists" — are firms assigned to particular stocks, in which they have obligations to quote at the national best bid and offer a substantial share of the time, to facilitate price discovery, to dampen volatility, and to open and close the stock each trading day.

Role and obligations

Unlike ordinary market makers, who may trade any security and may withdraw at will, a DMM has affirmative obligations to its assigned securities: maintaining continuous two-sided quotes, supplying liquidity at times of imbalance or stress, and overseeing the opening and closing auctions that set benchmark prices. In return, DMMs receive informational and economic advantages, including access to order-flow information and exchange rebates. Since the NYSE's Hybrid Market reforms of the 2000s, most DMM activity is electronic, though human DMMs still operate on the trading floor and intervene manually in volatile conditions.

Other exchanges

Similar systems exist elsewhere: many European exchanges appoint official market makers or "sponsors" for less liquid shares, and options exchanges assign lead market makers to particular option classes. The function contrasts with purely order-driven markets, where no participant bears an obligation to quote.
