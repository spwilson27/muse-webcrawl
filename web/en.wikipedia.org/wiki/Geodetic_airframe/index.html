Geodetic airframe

A geodetic airframe is a type of construction for the airframes of aircraft developed by British aeronautical engineer Barnes Wallis in the 1930s, based on his earlier work on airship structures. It makes use of a space frame formed from a spirally crossing basket-weave of load-bearing members, in which each member follows a geodetic curve — the shortest path between two points on the curved surface of the structure.

Characteristics

Because any single member can be destroyed without compromising the whole structure, geodetic airframes were noted for their exceptional strength, lightness and damage tolerance. The design distributes loads throughout the entire skin of the aircraft, so damage to one part does not lead to catastrophic failure of the whole.

Applications

The best-known applications were the Vickers aircraft of the Second World War era, including the Wellesley, Wellington and Warwick bombers, and later the Windsor. The Wellington in particular became famous for its ability to return home despite heavy battle damage. The method was labour-intensive, however, and fell out of favour as stressed-skin monocoque construction became standard.
