Architectural acoustics

Architectural acoustics (also known as room acoustics and building acoustics) is the science and engineering of achieving good sound within a building. It deals with the production, transmission, and reception of sound in enclosed spaces, and with the control of noise and vibration between spaces and from the outside environment.

History

The discipline is generally dated to Wallace Clement Sabine, who, beginning in 1895, investigated the poor acoustics of a lecture hall at Harvard University and derived the first quantitative theory of reverberation. Sabine's formula relates reverberation time to room volume and total absorption, and he went on to advise on Boston Symphony Hall (1900), widely regarded as one of the finest concert halls in the world. Earlier builders, from Greek theatre designers to the makers of Gothic cathedrals, had solved acoustic problems empirically.

Key concepts

The central quantity in room acoustics is the reverberation time (RT60), the time taken for sound to decay by 60 decibels after the source stops. Target values depend on use: roughly 1.8–2.2 seconds for symphonic concert halls, less for chamber music and opera, and under a second for lecture rooms, where speech intelligibility — often measured by the Speech Transmission Index or clarity metrics such as C50 — is paramount. Other concerns include early reflections, lateral energy, diffusion, the control of echoes and focusing, and background-noise ratings such as Noise Criteria (NC) curves.

Sound insulation and noise control

The second branch of building acoustics concerns keeping unwanted sound out: airborne sound insulation of walls and floors (measured by quantities such as the Sound Transmission Class), impact sound from footsteps, and noise from building services, roads, railways, and aircraft. Mass, separation, airtightness, and absorption within cavities are the main tools. Building regulations in most countries set minimum insulation standards for dwellings, schools, and hospitals.

Practice

Modern acoustic consultants use ray-tracing and wave-based computer models, auralisation, and scale-model testing to predict the sound of halls before construction. Landmark twentieth-century halls whose acoustics were shaped by consultants include the Philharmonie in Berlin and the Walt Disney Concert Hall in Los Angeles.
