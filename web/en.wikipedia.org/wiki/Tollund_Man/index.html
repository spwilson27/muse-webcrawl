Tollund Man

Tollund Man
Died: c. 405–380 BC
Found: 1950, Bjældskovdal, Denmark
Housed: Silkeborg Museum

The Tollund Man (died c. 405–380 BC) is a naturally mummified corpse of a man who lived during the Pre-Roman Iron Age. He was found in 1950, preserved as a bog body, near Silkeborg in the Jutland peninsula of Denmark.

Discovery and examination

Peat cutters discovered the body in the Bjældskovdal bog on 8 May 1950. The man was extraordinarily well preserved—his face so lifelike that the finders initially believed him a recent murder victim. He wore a pointed leather cap fastened under his chin and a belt, and around his neck was the braided leather noose with which he had been hanged.

Interpretation

Analysis of his stomach contents revealed a last meal of a porridge of barley, flax, and wild seeds. Most scholars interpret the Tollund Man, like other Iron Age bog bodies such as the Grauballe Man, as a human sacrifice—perhaps an offering to fertility deities, consistent with Tacitus's account of ritual hangings among Germanic peoples.

The body is displayed at the Silkeborg Museum; only the head is original, as preservation techniques of the 1950s led the rest of the body to be reconstructed.
