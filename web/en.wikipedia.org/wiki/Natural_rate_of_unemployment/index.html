Natural rate of unemployment

The natural rate of unemployment is the level of unemployment consistent with a stable rate of inflation. It represents the hypothetical unemployment rate consistent with aggregate production being at a "long-run" level, in which the economy is at full employment in the sense that actual inflation equals expected inflation.

The natural rate is determined by the supply side of the labour market.

FieldMacroeconomics, labour economics
Key proponentsMilton Friedman, Edmund Phelps

History

The concept was developed independently by Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps in the late 1960s, in critiques of the idea of a permanent trade-off between inflation and unemployment implied by the original Phillips curve. Friedman argued in his 1967 presidential address to the American Economic Association that monetary policy could not permanently hold unemployment below its natural rate; attempting to do so would produce accelerating inflation.

Components

The natural rate is generally understood to comprise frictional unemployment (workers between jobs or searching for better matches) and structural unemployment (mismatch between workers' skills or locations and available jobs). It excludes cyclical unemployment caused by recessions. Its level depends on labour-market institutions, unemployment benefits, minimum wages, unionisation, demographics and technology.

Relation to NAIRU

The natural rate is closely related to the NAIRU (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment). The terms are often used synonymously, though NAIRU is an estimated empirical threshold above which inflation tends to fall and below which it tends to rise, whereas the natural rate is a theoretical construct tied to labour-market equilibrium.

Criticism

Critics note that the natural rate is unobservable and must be estimated with wide uncertainty; that it may shift over time; and that prolonged high unemployment may itself raise the natural rate through skill loss and discouragement — a phenomenon known as hysteresis.
