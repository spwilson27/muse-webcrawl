Lodestone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lodestone is a naturally magnetized piece of the mineral magnetite. Lodestones are magnets found in nature that can attract iron, and they were the first magnets known to humanity. The word lodestone comes from Middle English meaning "course stone" or "leading stone", because a suspended lodestone points the way north–south; the property was exploited in the earliest magnetic compasses.

Origin

Only some magnetite deposits form lodestones. The prevailing explanation is that lodestones are magnetized by the intense magnetic fields of lightning strikes: magnetite is a ferrimagnetic mineral with a suitable crystal structure, and a nearby lightning bolt can align its magnetic domains, converting an ordinary piece of magnetite into a permanent magnet. This accounts for the rarity of lodestones and their frequent occurrence near the surface, where lightning can reach them.

History

Magnetite's attractive power was known in ancient Greece—Plato and others referred to the "Magnesian stone", named for Magnesia in Thessaly—and in China, where texts from the Han dynasty describe lodestone "south-pointers" used in divination. By the 11th–12th centuries the Chinese and then Europeans were using lodestone compasses for navigation; sailors "charged" compass needles by stroking them with a lodestone, and carrying a lodestone aboard ship was standard practice for centuries. The first scientific treatise on magnetism, Peter Peregrinus's Epistola de magnete (1269), was based on experiments with lodestones, and William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600) still used them extensively. With the invention of artificial magnets in the 18th century, lodestones became a curiosity rather than a tool.
