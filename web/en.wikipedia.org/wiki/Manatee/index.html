Manatees (family Trichechidae, genus Trichechus) are large, fully aquatic, mostly herbivorous marine mammals sometimes known as sea cows. There are three accepted living species: the West Indian manatee (Trichechus manatus), the Amazonian manatee (T. inunguis) and the African manatee (T. senegalensis). They measure up to about 4 metres in length, weigh as much as 590 kilograms, and have paddle-like flippers. Together with the dugong they make up the order Sirenia.

Description

Manatees have heavy, fusiform bodies with wrinkled grey skin, a rounded paddle-shaped tail (distinguishing them from the dugong's fluked tail), and flexible forelimb flippers used for steering and manipulating food. They have only six cervical vertebrae, fewer than almost any other mammal, and their teeth are continually replaced horizontally throughout life, an adaptation to abrasive plant diets. Dense bones act as ballast to counteract buoyancy from gas produced during digestion.

Habitat and behaviour

Manatees inhabit shallow, warm coastal waters, rivers and estuaries of the Caribbean, the Amazon Basin, Florida and West Africa. They are slow-moving grazers, spending much of the day feeding on seagrasses and other aquatic vegetation and resting near the surface. Florida manatees gather in winter at warm-water refuges such as natural springs and power-plant outflows.

Conservation

All three species are listed as vulnerable. Threats include collisions with watercraft — a leading cause of death in Florida — habitat loss, entanglement, and harmful algal blooms. Manatees are protected under laws such as the US Marine Mammal Protection Act and the Florida Manatee Sanctuary Act.
