Tendril

In botany, a tendril is a specialised slender stem, leaf or petiole with a threadlike shape used by climbing plants for support and attachment, generally by twining around suitable hosts. Tendrils are thigmotropic: contact with a solid object triggers differential growth that coils the tendril around it.

Origin and anatomy

Tendrils may be modified stems (as in grape, Vitis), modified leaves or leaflets (as in the garden pea, Pisum sativum, where the terminal leaflets become tendrils), or modified inflorescences. Charles Darwin's On the Movements and Habits of Climbing Plants (1865) described their circumnutation — slow revolving movements that sweep the tip in search of support.

Coiling mechanism

Touch-sensitive cells on the tendril's surface respond to mechanical stimulation with coiling within minutes. Once wrapped, many tendrils form springs: the coil reverses direction at its midpoint (a "tendril perversion"), producing a flexible spring that absorbs wind loads without detaching. Lignification later stiffens the coil into a strong permanent attachment.

Examples

Well-known tendril-bearing plants include peas, grapes, passionflowers, cucumbers and other cucurbits, Virginia creeper (whose tendrils end in adhesive discs), and sweet peas.
