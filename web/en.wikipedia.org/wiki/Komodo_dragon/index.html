Komodo dragon

A Komodo dragon on Rinca Island

The Komodo dragon (Varanus komodoensis) is a species of monitor lizard found in the Indonesian islands of Komodo, Rinca, Flores, and Gili Motang. It is the largest extant species of lizard, growing to a maximum length of about 3 metres (10 ft) and weighing up to roughly 70 kilograms (150 lb). A member of the family Varanidae, it is the apex predator of its island ecosystems.

Evolution and description

Fossil evidence indicates the lineage arose in Australia around 3.8 million years ago and spread westward to Indonesia as sea levels fell. The dragon's size is a classic case of island gigantism — or, in a competing view, the retention of the large size of its Australian ancestors. Its rough, armoured skin is reinforced with osteoderms; its long forked tongue samples the air for carrion scent, which it can detect kilometres away. Serrated teeth, similar to those of sharks, are replaced throughout life.

Feeding and venom

Komodo dragons hunt deer, wild boar, and water buffalo, and also scavenge carrion. Their bite delivers venom from glands in the lower jaw — anticoagulant compounds that promote bleeding and shock — while a loosely hinged skull and expandable stomach let the lizard swallow prey in huge pieces, consuming up to 80% of its body weight in one meal. Prey that escapes a bite often succumbs later to blood loss and infection, and dragons track wounded animals patiently. Wild dragons have occasionally attacked humans.

Reproduction

Mating occurs between May and August; females lay about 20 eggs in burrows or abandoned megapode nests, guarding them for a time before the young hatch some seven to eight months later. Hatchlings live in trees to avoid cannibalistic adults. Remarkably, females are capable of parthenogenesis: isolated females in zoos have produced viable all-male offspring through terminal fusion automixis.

Conservation

Komodo National Park was established in 1980 to protect the species. Numbers are estimated in the low thousands, and in 2021 the IUCN moved the species from Vulnerable to Endangered, citing habitat loss and projected sea-level rise on its low-lying island homes.
