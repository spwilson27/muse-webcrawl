Cultural assimilation

Cultural assimilation is the process by which a minority group, or individual members of it, gradually adopt the customs, language, values, and behaviours of a dominant culture, so that the group's distinctiveness diminishes over time. The concept is central to the sociology of immigration and ethnic relations, where it is contrasted with integration, cultural pluralism, and multiculturalism.

Classical theory

Chicago School sociologists Robert Park and Ernest Burgess described a "race relations cycle" of contact, competition, accommodation, and eventual assimilation. Milton Gordon's influential Assimilation in American Life (1964) distinguished seven stages, from cultural (acculturation) and structural assimilation (entry into majority institutions and social circles) to marital amalgamation and the disappearance of prejudice — arguing that structural assimilation was the key that unlocked all the others. Later research qualified the picture: assimilation is not always straight-line. "Segmented assimilation" theory (Alejandro Portes and Min Zhou) holds that immigrants' children may assimilate downward into marginalised native populations, upward into the middle class, or preserve community ties as a resource for mobility.

Policy and controversy

Historically, states often pursued forced assimilation of minorities and Indigenous peoples — through boarding schools that punished children for speaking their languages, bans on minority languages and dress, and settlement policies. Such coercion is now widely condemned, and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples protects against forced assimilation. Debate continues, however, over how much cultural convergence liberal democracies may legitimately expect of newcomers — in language acquisition, civic norms, and law — and where expectation becomes coercion. Critics of the term argue it smuggles in the majority's norms as the neutral endpoint; defenders reply that some shared culture is a precondition of common citizenship.
