Institutional fact
A fact that exists only within a system of human institutions.

An institutional fact is a fact whose existence depends on human institutions — systems of collectively accepted rules — in contrast to a brute fact, which exists independently of any institution. The distinction is central to John Searle's philosophy of social reality, developed from Speech Acts (1969) through The Construction of Social Reality (1995) and Making the Social World (2010).

Definition and examples

That a given piece of engraved paper is a twenty-dollar bill, that two people are married, that a player scored a touchdown, or that a corporation owns a building are institutional facts: they hold only because a community collectively recognizes constitutive rules of the form "X counts as Y in context C", assigning a status function to an object or person. The physical object (the brute fact) gains deontic powers — rights, duties, obligations, permissions — that exist only through collective acceptance. Money, property, marriage, governments, and language itself are built from layered institutional facts.

Constitutive rules and declarations

Unlike regulative rules, which govern antecedently existing activities (such as rules of the road), constitutive rules create the very possibility of the activity: the rules of chess do not regulate pushing pieces of wood, they make chess exist. Searle argues that institutional facts are maintained by speech acts called declarations and require language — or at least symbolic representation — to exist, distinguishing human institutions from the collective behaviors of other animals.

Related work

The philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe earlier distinguished brute facts relative to descriptions (1958), and the economist and theorist of "institutional facts" in law, legal scholars such as Neil MacCormick developed the concept for legal theory. Critics debate whether collective intentionality can be reduced to individual attitudes and whether money in digital form alters the analysis.
