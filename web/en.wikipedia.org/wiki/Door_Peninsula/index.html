Door Peninsula

The Door Peninsula is a peninsula in eastern Wisconsin that separates Green Bay from Lake Michigan. It is known for Door County, which occupies most of the peninsula, and is often called the "Cape Cod of the Midwest" for its shoreline resorts, state parks, lighthouses and orchards.

Geography

The peninsula is about 70 miles (110 km) long and is formed by the Niagara Escarpment, a dolomitic limestone ridge that runs beneath the region and reappears at Niagara Falls. Its rocky shoreline includes cliffs, caves and sandy beaches. The Sturgeon Bay Ship Canal, completed in 1881, cut the peninsula in two, allowing ships to avoid the dangerous passage at its tip.

History

The passage between the peninsula's tip and Washington Island was known to French voyageurs as Porte des Morts — "Death's Door" — for the many shipwrecks there; Door County and the peninsula take their name from this passage. Native peoples including the Ho-Chunk, Menominee and Potawatomi lived in the region long before French explorers arrived in the 17th century.

Tourism

Door County's villages — including Fish Creek, Sister Bay, Ephraim and Baileys Harbor — draw visitors for boating, fishing, cherry and apple orchards, art galleries and five state parks, notably Peninsula State Park. The Cana Island Lighthouse and other historic lights remain popular landmarks.
