Culture of fear
ConceptSociology / political science
Key figuresFrank Furedi, Barry Glassner, Noam Chomsky

The culture of fear (or climate of fear) is the concept that people may incite fear in the general public to achieve political or workplace goals through emotional bias. It is also a term used by some scholars and commentators to describe a perceived pervasive atmosphere of anxiety in contemporary society, in which risks are exaggerated and public discourse is dominated by alarm about threats such as crime, terrorism, disease, immigration, and environmental catastrophe.

Origins of the term

The phrase was popularised by the British sociologist Frank Furedi, whose book Culture of Fear: Risk-taking and the Morality of Low Expectation (1997) argued that modern Western societies have become preoccupied with safety and risk-averse to a degree disproportionate to actual dangers. The American sociologist Barry Glassner developed a related argument in The Culture of Fear: Why Americans Are Afraid of the Wrong Things (1999), examining how media coverage and advocacy groups amplify rare dangers while neglecting common ones.

Mechanisms

Commentators identify several mechanisms by which fear is cultivated: sensationalist news media that reward alarming stories; politicians who frame elections around security threats; commercial interests that market protective products; and advocacy campaigns that use worst-case scenarios to mobilise support. The result, critics argue, is a public that overestimates risks such as stranger abduction or terrorism while underestimating everyday hazards such as road accidents.

Related concepts

The culture of fear is closely related to the concept of moral panic, in which a person or group is portrayed as a threat to social values, and to George Gerbner's "mean world syndrome", the finding that heavy television viewers perceive the world as more dangerous than it is. It has also been linked to the precautionary principle and debates about the "risk society" described by Ulrich Beck.

Criticism

Critics of the concept argue that some fears dismissed as irrational — such as concern over climate change or pandemics — are grounded in real evidence, and that labelling public anxiety a "culture of fear" can itself be a political move used to dismiss legitimate grievances.
