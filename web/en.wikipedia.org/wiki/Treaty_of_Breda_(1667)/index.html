The Treaty of Breda, often called the Peace of Breda, was signed on 31 July 1667 at Breda in the Dutch Republic, ending the Second Anglo-Dutch War (1665–1667) between England and the Dutch Republic and their respective allies. Negotiations were hastened by the humiliating Dutch Raid on the Medway in June 1667 and by a French invasion of the Spanish Netherlands, which made both sides anxious for peace.

Terms

The treaty was based largely on uti possidetis — each side kept what it held. England retained New Netherland in North America, including New Amsterdam (renamed New York), which it had seized in 1664. The Dutch retained Suriname, captured from the English in 1667, and the tiny spice island of Run in the East Indies — a settlement of the long dispute over the nutmeg trade, in which the English effectively traded their claim to Run for Manhattan. The English Navigation Acts were relaxed to allow Dutch ships to carry German goods to England, and Acadia was restored to France.

Legacy

The exchange of a South American sugar colony and an Indonesian nutmeg island for the future site of New York City is often cited as one of history's most consequential bargains, though contemporaries valued the sugar and spice possessions at least as highly.
