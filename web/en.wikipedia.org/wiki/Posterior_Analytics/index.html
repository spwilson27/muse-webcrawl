Posterior Analytics

The Posterior Analytics forms part of Aristotle's Organon, his collected works on logic

AuthorAristotle
SubjectLogic, epistemology, philosophy of science

Greek titleἈναλυτικὰ Ὕστερα

The Posterior Analytics (Greek: Ἀναλυτικὰ Ὕστερα; Latin: Analytica Posteriora) is a text from Aristotle's Organon that deals with demonstration, definition, and scientific knowledge (Greek: epistēmē). It follows the Prior Analytics, which sets out the theory of the syllogism, and asks what further conditions a syllogism must satisfy to constitute genuine scientific understanding rather than mere true belief.

Content

Aristotle argues that scientific knowledge is knowledge of causes: to know something scientifically is to know why it must be so, through a demonstration — a deductive argument whose premises are true, primary, immediate, better known than and prior to the conclusion, and explanatory of it. Demonstrations must ultimately rest on first principles that cannot themselves be demonstrated, on pain of infinite regress or circularity. These principles include definitions and axioms grasped by nous, an intuitive intellectual faculty developed through perception, memory, and experience (epagōgē, induction).

The work's second book turns to definition, the four questions one can ask about a thing (whether it is, what it is, that it is, and why it is), and the role of division and causal inquiry in discovering essences. Famous examples include Aristotle's treatments of thunder, eclipses, and the deciduousness of broad-leaved trees, where the middle term of the syllogism identifies the cause.

Influence

The Posterior Analytics shaped ideals of science for two millennia. Translated into Latin by James of Venice in the twelfth century, commented on by figures including Robert Grosseteste and Thomas Aquinas, and studied intensively in the medieval universities and the Islamic philosophical tradition, it supplied the standard model of rigorous demonstration against which later thinkers — including early modern critics such as Galileo's contemporaries — defined their own methods.
