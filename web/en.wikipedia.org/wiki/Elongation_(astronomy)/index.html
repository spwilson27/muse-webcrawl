Elongation (astronomy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In astronomy, a planet's elongation is the angular separation between the Sun and the planet, as seen from Earth, measured along the planet's ecliptic longitude. Elongation determines when and how well a planet can be observed: a planet at small elongation is lost in the Sun's glare, while one at large elongation is visible for much of the night.

Inferior and superior planets

The inferior planets, Mercury and Venus, never stray far from the Sun: Mercury reaches a maximum elongation of about 18°–28°, and Venus about 45°–47°. An eastern elongation places the planet east of the Sun, so it appears in the evening sky after sunset; a western elongation places it in the morning sky before sunrise. Greatest elongation is the moment of maximum angular separation and the most favourable time to observe these planets.

The superior planets — Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune — can reach any elongation up to 180°. At 180° the planet is in opposition, opposite the Sun in the sky, rising at sunset and visible all night; at 0° it is in conjunction with the Sun. At elongations of roughly 90° the planet is said to be at quadrature.

Elongation of the Moon

The term is also applied to the Moon and to other bodies: the Moon's elongation from the Sun governs its phase, growing from 0° at new moon to 180° at full moon. Elongation is likewise used for comets, asteroids, and the moons of other planets relative to their parent body as seen from Earth.
