Mezzanine capital

Mezzanine capital is a hybrid of debt and equity financing that gives the lender the right to convert to an equity interest in the company in case of default, generally after venture capital companies and other senior lenders are paid. In terms of risk, it exists between senior debt and equity. Mezzanine debt tends to be a call option form of debt that typically incorporates equity-based options, such as warrants, with a lower-priority debt.

Structure

Mezzanine financing combines elements of both debt and equity. Lenders usually require a fixed interest rate, which may be partly "payment-in-kind" (PIK), and may also take warrants or other equity participation. Because it is subordinated to senior debt but senior to common equity, mezzanine is more expensive than bank debt but cheaper than raising equity, and it is typically unsecured and repayable over a medium term (often five to seven years) with a bullet repayment at maturity.

Uses

Mezzanine capital is frequently used in leveraged buyouts (LBOs) to fill the gap between senior debt capacity and the equity contribution, in growth financing for companies that have exhausted conventional lending, and in real estate development and acquisition financing as a junior layer behind the first mortgage.
