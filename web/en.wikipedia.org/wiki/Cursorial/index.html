Cursorial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cursorial locomotion
DefinitionAdaptation for running on land
ExamplesCheetah, pronghorn, horses, ostriches, wolves
Contrasts withFossorial (digging), arboreal (trees), saltatorial (jumping)

A cursorial organism is one that is adapted specifically to running. An animal can be considered cursorial if it has the ability to run fast (e.g. the cheetah) or if it can travel long distances at a modest speed (endurance running, as in wolves and horses).

Adaptations

Cursorial animals typically show elongated limbs, especially in the lower segments; reduced or lost digits and clavicles; movement restricted to a single plane of flexion at the joints; and digitigrade or unguligrade foot posture, in which the animal runs on its toes or hooves. Tendons act as springs to store elastic energy at each stride. In birds, flightless runners such as ostriches, rheas, and emus show the same trends: long legs, reduced toes, and large thigh muscles.

Evolution

Cursorial adaptations evolved independently in many lineages, often in open grassland habitats, driven by predator–prey arms races. Extinct examples include the South American terror birds (phorusrhacids), running crocodilians, and cursorial dinosaurs such as ornithomimids. Among mammals, ungulates and carnivorans are the dominant modern cursorial groups.
