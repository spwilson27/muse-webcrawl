The Mistick Krewe of Comus is the oldest continuously active New Orleans Mardi Gras krewe (carnival organisation). Founded in 1856 by six Anglo-American businessmen, the Krewe first paraded on Mardi Gras night, 24 February 1857, and is credited with transforming New Orleans Carnival — introducing organised themed parades with flambeau-lit floats, masked and costumed riders, and an invitation-only tableau ball — establishing the template that later krewes, including Rex and the Krewe of Proteus, would follow.

History

Before 1857, New Orleans Mardi Gras celebrations were loosely organised, and the city authorities had threatened to suppress the increasingly rowdy festivities. In December 1856, six men who had previously participated in the Cowbellion de Rakin Society parades in Mobile, Alabama, gathered at the Gem Restaurant on Royal Street and formed a new secret society. They took their name from Comus, the Greek god of revelry (via John Milton's masque Comus), and adopted the deliberately archaic spelling "Mistick Krewe".

The Krewe's first parade, on the night of Shrove Tuesday 1857, featured two floats — one carrying Comus himself, the other depicting scenes from Milton's Paradise Lost — drawn through gas-lit streets accompanied by torchbearers (flambeaux). Membership was secret, invitations to the ball were coveted, and Comus himself traditionally appeared masked, his identity unknown. After the parade, the Krewe held a tableau ball at the Gaiety Theatre, at which mythological scenes were presented as living pictures.

Influence and traditions

Comus established many conventions of modern Mardi Gras: the themed parade with coordinated floats, the captain who leads the procession, masked riders who toss trinkets (throws) to spectators, the flambeau tradition, and the debutante ball with its court of maids and queen. The Krewe traditionally closed the Mardi Gras season by parading on the night of Fat Tuesday, and after 1873 Comus and Rex met ceremonially at the "Meeting of the Courts". Its invitation lists and iconography reflected the racial and social exclusions of the era's old-line krewes — a criticism that came to a head in the early 1990s.

Withdrawal from parading

In 1991 the New Orleans City Council passed an ordinance requiring parading organisations to certify that they did not discriminate in membership on the basis of race and other factors. Comus, along with Momus and Proteus, chose to cease parading rather than open its membership process; Proteus later returned to the parade schedule in 2000, but Comus has not paraded since 1991. The Krewe continues to hold its private ball on Mardi Gras night, including the traditional meeting of the courts with Rex.
