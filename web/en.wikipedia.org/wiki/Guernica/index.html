Guernica

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Guernica (1937), Picasso's monumental black, white and grey mural: a bull, a screaming horse and a mother with a dead child amid the bombing's chaos

Guernica is a large 1937 oil painting by Pablo Picasso, his best-known work and one of the most powerful anti-war statements in art. In black, white and grey, across a canvas 3.49 × 7.77 metres, it depicts the suffering inflicted by the bombing of the Basque town of Guernica on 26 April 1937 by Nazi Germany's Condor Legion, allies of Franco's Nationalists in the Spanish Civil War. It is housed in the Museo Reina Sofía, Madrid.

Commissioned by the Spanish Republican government for the Spanish pavilion at the 1937 Paris International Exhibition, the painting toured the world to raise funds and awareness, then spent decades at the Museum of Modern Art in New York until, in accordance with Picasso's wish, it returned to a democratic Spain in 1981.

Imagery

A bull stands impassive at left; at centre a horse screams, pierced by a spear, beneath an electric eye-lamp; a mother wails over her dead child in a pose recalling the Pietà; fallen and fleeing figures, a severed arm gripping a broken sword, and flames complete the scene. Picasso refused fixed interpretations of the bull and horse — 'the bull is a bull and the horse is a horse' — but the mural's meaning as universal protest was fixed by its history: a tapestry copy at the United Nations was famously covered during a 2003 press briefing on the Iraq War.

Reception

Early reviews were divided, but the painting's world tour and the war that followed made it an icon; the 1970s saw it adopted by anti-war movements everywhere. Its return to Spain in 1981, guarded amid Basque separatist demands that it go to the Basque Country instead, marked Spain's transition to democracy. It moved from the Prado to the Reina Sofía in 1992 and, too fragile to travel, no longer leaves Madrid.
