Harsha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Ruins and stupa remains at Kannauj, Harsha's imperial capital]

Harsha (c. 590 – 647 AD), also Harshavardhana, was an Indian emperor of the Pushyabhuti (Vardhana) dynasty who ruled much of northern India from Kannauj (606–647) — the last great Hindu emperor to unite the north before the early medieval regional kingdoms. His reign is unusually well lit: the court poet Banabhatta's Harshacharita biography, Harsha's own three Sanskrit plays, inscriptions, and the eyewitness pilgrim Xuanzang, whom Harsha honoured at the great Kannauj assembly (643) and the quinquennial Prayaga almsgiving. Checked in the south at the Narmada by the Chalukya Pulakeshin II, Harsha's empire dissolved at his death without an heir, opening the tripartite age of Palas, Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas.

The empire of Kannauj

Harsha came to the throne at sixteen amid catastrophe — his father dead, his brother Rajyavardhana murdered by the Gauda king Shashanka's treachery (the sources' version), his sister Rajyashri widowed and nearly self-immolated. Uniting Thanesar and widowed Kannauj's crowns, he spent six years (by Bana's account) conquering 'the five Indies' — Punjab, Kannauj, Bengal's fringe, Gujarat, Malwa — and the next thirty-five ruling them through a peripatetic court, land-grant feudatories and a vast army, forgiving Shashanka's realm only at its master's death. His administration, Xuanzang reports, was mild: light taxes, few capital punishments, rest-houses and hospitals endowed along the roads.

Patron and playwright

Harsha wrote (or sponsored under his name) the plays Ratnavali, Priyadarshika and the Buddhist Nagananda; his court sheltered Bana and the Chinese pilgrim alike. A Shaivite by family who leaned toward Mahayana Buddhism in later life, he embodied the ecumenical kingship Xuanzang praised. Assassinated attempts and an heirless death undid his work at once (647): a usurper briefly seized Kannauj until a Tang–Tibet expedition restored order — a measure of the vanished empire's prestige, and of its fragility.
