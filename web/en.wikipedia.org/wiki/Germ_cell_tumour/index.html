Germ cell tumour

A germ cell tumour (GCT) is a neoplasm derived from germ cells. Germ cells normally occur inside the gonads (the ovary and testis), but germ cell tumours can also arise in other parts of the body — a legacy of embryonic development, during which germ cells travel from the yolk sac to the gonads and may come to rest in extragonadal sites such as the mediastinum, retroperitoneum, and brain.

Classification

Germ cell tumours are broadly divided into germinomatous and non-germinomatous types. In the testis the germinomatous form is called seminoma; its ovarian counterpart is dysgerminoma. Non-germinomatous tumours include embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma, and teratoma. Mixed germ cell tumours contain more than one component.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Testicular germ cell tumours typically present as a painless lump. Ovarian tumours may cause abdominal pain or distension. Tumour markers — alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) — are important in diagnosis, staging, and monitoring. Diagnosis is confirmed by histology following surgical removal.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment usually begins with surgery: radical orchiectomy for testicular tumours. Platinum-based chemotherapy, classically the BEP regimen (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin), has made germ cell tumours among the most curable of cancers, with five-year survival exceeding 90% even for many patients with metastatic disease.
