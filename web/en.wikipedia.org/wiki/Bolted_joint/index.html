Bolted joint

A bolted joint is one of the most common elements in construction and machine design. It consists of a captor — a bolt or screw — that captures and joins other parts, secured with a matching female nut thread. Bolted joints are a type of mechanical joint used to connect two or more parts together to form a complete assembly, and they are designed so that the assembly can be taken apart and reassembled without damaging the components.

Types

Bolted joints may be loaded in tension, in shear, or in a combination of the two. In a tension joint, the applied load acts along the axis of the bolt, tending to separate the joint members. In a shear joint, the load acts perpendicular to the bolt axis, and the joint members are prevented from slipping either by friction between the clamped surfaces (a friction-type or slip-critical joint) or by bearing of the bolt shank against the sides of the holes (a bearing-type joint). Double shear occurs when a bolt passes through three plates and is loaded across two cross-sections.

Mechanics

When a nut is tightened, the bolt elongates slightly and the joint members are compressed, producing a clamping force known as the preload. The relationship between applied torque and preload is often approximated by T = K·F·d, where T is torque, F is preload, d is the nominal bolt diameter, and K is a dimensionless "nut factor" that accounts for friction in the threads and under the nut face. Because friction varies widely, torque-controlled tightening typically achieves preload accurate only to about ±25–35%.

So long as the external tensile load does not exceed the clamping force, the joint members remain in contact and the bolt experiences only a fraction of the applied load, determined by the relative stiffnesses of the bolt and the members. This behaviour is commonly analysed with a joint diagram plotting force against deflection for the bolt and the members.

Failure modes

Bolted joints can fail by tensile rupture of the bolt, stripping of the internal or external threads, fatigue cracking under cyclic loading, crushing or tearing of the joint members, and self-loosening under transverse vibration — a phenomenon studied in the classic Junker test. Proper preload, adequate engagement length, and locking devices such as lock nuts, thread-locking fluid, or safety wire are used to mitigate these failures.
