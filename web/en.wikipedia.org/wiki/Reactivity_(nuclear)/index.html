Reactivity (nuclear)

In nuclear reactor physics, reactivity is a dimensionless quantity that measures the departure of a reactor from criticality. It is defined in terms of the effective neutron multiplication factor keff as ρ = (keff − 1) / keff.

Interpretation

When keff = 1, reactivity is zero and the reactor is exactly critical: the neutron population and power remain steady. Positive reactivity (keff > 1) makes the reactor supercritical and power rises; negative reactivity (keff < 1) makes it subcritical and power falls.

Units

Reactivity is commonly expressed in pcm (per cent mille, 10−5) or in dollars and cents, where one dollar equals the effective delayed neutron fraction βeff — the threshold at which the reactor becomes prompt critical. A reactivity of one dollar means the reactor can sustain a chain reaction on prompt neutrons alone, leading to very rapid power excursions.

Reactivity feedback

Changes in temperature, moderator density, void fraction, and fuel burnup all feed back on reactivity. Well-designed reactors have a negative temperature coefficient of reactivity, so that a power increase heats the core and inserts negative reactivity, providing inherent stability. Control rods of neutron-absorbing material such as boron or cadmium are used to adjust reactivity deliberately.
