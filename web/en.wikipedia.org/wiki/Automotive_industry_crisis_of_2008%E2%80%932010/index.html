Automotive industry crisis of 2008–2010

The automotive industry crisis of 2008–2010 was part of the 2008 financial crisis and the resulting Great Recession. The crisis mainly affected the American automobile industry, though it also hit manufacturers in Europe and elsewhere. A collapse in consumer demand, tight credit and high fuel prices left General Motors and Chrysler facing bankruptcy.

Background

United States vehicle sales fell from more than 16 million a year in the mid-2000s to under 10 million in 2009. The Detroit Three were burdened by legacy labour and pension costs and by a product mix weighted towards trucks and SUVs whose sales collapsed when fuel prices spiked in 2008. Credit markets froze at the same time, removing both consumer car loans and the makers' working capital.

In late 2008 the United States government extended bridge loans to GM and Chrysler under the Troubled Asset Relief Program. Chrysler filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 30 April 2009 and emerged under the control of Italy's Fiat; General Motors filed on 1 June 2009 and emerged 40 days later with the US Treasury as majority owner. Ford, which had mortgaged its assets for credit before the crash, survived without a bailout or bankruptcy.

Governments elsewhere introduced scrappage schemes paying buyers to trade in old cars, which cushioned demand in Germany, France and other markets. The crisis accelerated restructuring of the industry, plant closures across the American Midwest, and a longer-term shift toward smaller, more fuel-efficient vehicles.
