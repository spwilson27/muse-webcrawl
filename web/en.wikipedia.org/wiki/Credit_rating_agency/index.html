A credit rating agency (CRA) is a company that assigns credit ratings, which rate a debtor's ability to pay back debt by making timely principal and interest payments and the likelihood of default. An agency may rate the creditworthiness of issuers of debt obligations, of debt instruments, and in some cases of the servicers of the underlying debt — including governments, corporations, non-profit organisations and structured-finance vehicles.

The Big Three

The industry is dominated by three firms — Moody's Investors Service, S&P Global Ratings and Fitch Ratings — which together account for the overwhelming majority of ratings worldwide. Their letter grades range from the highest quality (Aaa/AAA) down through investment grade to speculative ("junk") grades and default. Ratings influence the interest rates borrowers pay and are embedded in many regulations and investment mandates.

Business model and criticism

Agencies historically charged investors (subscriber-pays) but shifted in the 1970s to an issuer-pays model, in which the issuer of a bond pays for its rating — a structure widely criticised as a conflict of interest. Agencies were heavily criticised after the 2007–2008 financial crisis for assigning high ratings to mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations that later defaulted in large numbers. Reforms included the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and the EU's CRA Regulations, increasing oversight, transparency and liability.

History

Credit reporting in the United States began with mercantile agencies in the 19th century. Henry Varnum Poor published railway bond information from 1860; John Moody founded Moody's in 1909 to rate railroad bonds; Poor's and Standard Statistics merged in 1941 to form Standard & Poor's. Credit ratings became integral to bond markets as they expanded over the 20th century.
