Earth system

The Earth system is the integrated physical, chemical, biological, and human system of planet Earth. It is the subject of Earth system science, which studies the interactions among the atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, geosphere (lithosphere), and biosphere, together with the flows of energy and matter (such as the carbon and water cycles) that connect them.


The interacting spheres of the Earth system

Viewing Earth as a single system emphasises feedbacks and couplings: for example, changes in atmospheric composition affect climate, which alters ocean circulation, ice cover, vegetation, and weathering rates, which in turn feed back on the atmosphere. The concept underlies modern approaches to climate change, biogeochemical cycles, and global change research, including the planetary boundaries framework, which identifies key Earth-system processes — such as climate, biosphere integrity, and nutrient flows — within which humanity can operate safely.

History of the concept

Precursors include Alexander von Humboldt's holistic view of nature, Vladimir Vernadsky's concept of the biosphere, and James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis. The modern Earth-system framing was consolidated in the 1980s, notably through NASA's Earth System Science Committee and the Bretherton diagram (1986), which charted the coupled processes linking the planet's components. International research programs such as the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme institutionalised the approach.

Human role

Human societies are now treated as an integral component of the Earth system rather than an external forcing, a recognition reflected in the concept of the Anthropocene. Earth system models couple representations of the physical climate system with biogeochemistry and, increasingly, human systems.
