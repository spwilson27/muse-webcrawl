ComposerHeinrich Ignaz Franz Biber

InstrumentationViolin and basso continuo

The Rosary Sonatas (also known as the Mystery Sonatas) are a collection of fifteen sonatas for violin and basso continuo, plus a concluding solo passacaglia, composed around 1676 by the Bohemian-Austrian composer Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber. Each sonata corresponds to one of the fifteen Mysteries of the Rosary in Catholic devotion, and the work is celebrated for its extensive use of scordatura — the deliberate mistuning of the violin's strings — with a different tuning prescribed for nearly every sonata.

Structure

The fifteen sonatas follow the traditional grouping of the Rosary: the five Joyful Mysteries (from the Annunciation to the Finding in the Temple), the five Sorrowful Mysteries (from the Agony in the Garden to the Crucifixion), and the five Glorious Mysteries (from the Resurrection to the Coronation of the Virgin). The collection closes with a Passacaglia in G minor for solo violin, known as the "Guardian Angel", one of the most substantial works for unaccompanied violin before J. S. Bach. In the manuscript, preserved in the Bavarian State Library in Munich, each sonata is prefaced by a small copper engraving of its Mystery.

Scordatura

Only the first sonata and the Passacaglia use the standard tuning (g–d′–a′–e″). The other sonatas retune the strings to produce special resonances, sympathetic vibrations and otherwise impossible chords and double-stops. For the Resurrection sonata, the two middle strings are crossed over one another at the bridge and nut so that the instrument itself displays a cross — a piece of musical symbolism printed in the manuscript. The notation is written to be read as if in normal tuning, making the sounding result a famous example of Baroque musical cryptography.

History

Biber, chapel master at the court of the Prince-Archbishop of Salzburg, dedicated the collection to Archbishop Maximilian Gandolph von Kuenburg, a promoter of Rosary devotion. The sonatas were not published in Biber's lifetime and became widely known only after the manuscript's modern edition in the early 20th century; they are now cornerstones of the Baroque violin repertoire.
