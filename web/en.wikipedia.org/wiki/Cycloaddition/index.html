Cycloaddition

A cycloaddition is a chemical reaction in which two or more unsaturated molecules (or parts of the same molecule) combine to form a cyclic product in which there is a net reduction of bond multiplicity. The resulting new ring is built by forming two new sigma bonds at the ends of the pi systems of the reactants.

Notation and types

Cycloadditions are classified by the number of atoms each component contributes to the new ring, written [m + n]. The most famous is the [4 + 2] Diels–Alder reaction, in which a diene reacts with a dienophile to give a six-membered ring. Other major classes include [2 + 2] cycloadditions (often photochemical), 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions such as the Huisgen reaction producing triazoles — the archetypal "click" reaction — and [4 + 3] and higher-order variants.

Mechanism and selection rules

Many cycloadditions are pericyclic reactions, proceeding in a concerted fashion through a cyclic transition state. The Woodward–Hoffmann rules, based on orbital symmetry, predict which combinations are thermally or photochemically allowed: for example, thermal [4 + 2] cycloaddition is allowed while thermal [2 + 2] is symmetry-forbidden and generally requires light or a stepwise mechanism. Stereochemistry at the reacting centres is retained in concerted reactions, making cycloadditions powerful tools for building complex ring systems with control of multiple stereocentres.

Applications

Cycloadditions are workhorses of organic synthesis, used to construct carbocycles and heterocycles in natural product synthesis, pharmaceuticals, and polymer chemistry. The Diels–Alder reaction in particular is prized for its reliability and atom economy, and reversible cycloadditions underlie self-healing materials.
