Middle English (abbreviated ME) was the stage of the English language spoken and written from roughly the mid-12th century to about 1500, between Old English and Early Modern English. It was the language of Geoffrey Chaucer, of the Gawain poet, and of the first complete English Bible translations.

From Old to Middle English

The Norman Conquest of 1066 displaced English from court and chancery for two centuries; when English writing resumed — the final Peterborough continuation of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle is a landmark — the language had changed profoundly. Unstressed syllables levelled, the rich Old English inflectional system largely collapsed (grammatical gender disappeared; case endings shrank), and word order became the main bearer of grammar. Contact with Old Norse in the Danelaw contributed everyday words and even the pronouns they/them/their, while French supplied thousands of words for government, law, fashion, food and art.

Dialects and standardisation

Middle English was strikingly regional — Chaucer's London verse differs visibly from the Northwest Midlands language of Sir Gawain — with no national standard until the 15th-century Chancery usage and, decisively, Caxton's printing press (1476). Around 1400 began the Great Vowel Shift, the long chain of pronunciation changes that separates Chaucer's vowels from Shakespeare's.

Literature

The period produced the alliterative revival (Piers Plowman), the romances, the mystery plays, and Chaucer's Canterbury Tales and Troilus and Criseyde — works still read today with only modest glossing, a testament to how much of modern English was already in place.
