Eye movement is the voluntary or involuntary movement of the eyes, helping in acquiring, fixating, and tracking visual stimuli. The movements of the eyes are performed by six extraocular muscles under the control of brainstem and cortical networks, and are typically conjugate — both eyes move together — so that an image of interest is centred on the high-acuity fovea of each retina.

Types

Saccades are rapid, ballistic jumps of the eyes between fixations, among the fastest movements the body produces (up to about 900°/s). During reading and scene viewing, vision is mostly gathered during the intervening fixations, and perception is partially suppressed during the saccade itself. Smooth pursuit movements track a moving target. Vergence movements rotate the eyes in opposite directions to fixate near or far objects. The vestibulo-ocular reflex counter-rotates the eyes to stabilize the image during head movement, and the optokinetic response stabilizes gaze during sustained motion of the visual field.

Control

Saccades are generated by burst neurons in the brainstem (paramedian pontine reticular formation for horizontal movements, midbrain for vertical), coordinated under the superior colliculus and cortical eye fields. The cerebellum calibrates accuracy. Dysfunction produces characteristic signs: nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia, and gaze palsies.

Research and applications

Patterns of fixation and saccades are used to study reading, attention, and visual search; recording methods include electro-oculography, scleral search coils, and video-based eye tracking. Abnormal eye movements assist in the diagnosis of neurological and psychiatric disorders.
