Pelvic girdle

The bony pelvis, formed by the pelvic girdle and sacrum.

The pelvic girdle, also called the bony pelvis or pelvic skeleton, is the ring of bones that connects the trunk to the lower limbs. In adult humans it consists of the two hip bones (coxal bones), each formed by the fusion of the ilium, ischium, and pubis, together with the sacrum and coccyx at the back.

Structure

The two hip bones meet at the front at the pubic symphysis and articulate with the sacrum at the sacroiliac joints behind, forming a basin-shaped ring. Each hip bone carries the acetabulum, the cup-shaped socket that receives the head of the femur at the hip joint. The pelvic cavity enclosed by the girdle is divided by the pelvic brim into the greater (false) pelvis above and the lesser (true) pelvis below.

Functions

The pelvic girdle transfers the weight of the upper body from the axial skeleton to the legs in standing and walking, provides attachment for powerful muscles of the trunk and lower limb, and protects the pelvic viscera, including the bladder, reproductive organs, and rectum. In females the pelvis is broader and its outlet wider, adaptations for childbirth; pelvic shape differences are a principal example of sexual dimorphism in the human skeleton.

Clinical significance

Pelvic fractures, often from high-energy trauma, can be life-threatening because of associated bleeding and organ injury. The shape and dimensions of the female pelvis (pelvimetry) are relevant to labour and delivery. Conditions affecting the girdle include sacroiliac joint dysfunction and developmental dysplasia of the hip.
