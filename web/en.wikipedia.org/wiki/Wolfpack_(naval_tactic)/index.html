Wolfpack (naval tactic)

A wolfpack was a convoy attack tactic employed in the Second World War by German U-boats against Allied shipping — and later used by United States Navy submarines against Japanese shipping in the Pacific. The idea was to concentrate a group of submarines against a convoy so that the escorts could not cover every attacker, allowing the pack to inflict heavy losses over successive nights.

German usage

The tactic, called Rudeltaktik ("pack tactic"), was developed before the war by Karl Dönitz, commander of the German U-boat arm. U-boats were positioned in long patrol lines across expected convoy routes. When one boat sighted a convoy, it shadowed it and reported its position while headquarters (BdU) directed other boats to converge. The pack then attacked on the surface at night, when the low-profile U-boats were hard to spot and their speed exceeded that of the convoy. Wolfpacks were most effective from 1940 to early 1943; their largest successes included the battles around convoys SC 7 and HX 79 in October 1940.

Defeat of the tactic

Allied countermeasures — radar, high-frequency direction finding ("Huff-Duff"), dedicated escort groups, escort carriers, very-long-range aircraft closing the mid-Atlantic air gap, and the breaking of the naval Enigma — turned the tables in the spring of 1943, the crisis known to the U-boat arm as "Black May". Losses became unsustainable and Dönitz withdrew his packs from the North Atlantic convoy routes.

American usage

The US Navy adopted coordinated attack groups ("coordinated attack groups", also called wolfpacks) from 1943, typically of three boats under a single commander, against Japanese merchant shipping, with considerable success.
