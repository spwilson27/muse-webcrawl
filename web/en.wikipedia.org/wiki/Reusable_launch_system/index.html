Reusable launch vehicle

A reusable launch vehicle has parts that can be recovered and reflown, unlike an expendable launch system, which discards its components during flight. Reusability aims to reduce the cost of access to space by avoiding the need to build an entirely new vehicle for every launch, in analogy to commercial aviation.

History

The Space Shuttle (1981–2011) was the first partially reusable orbital launch system: its orbiters and solid rocket boosters were recovered and refurbished, though at high cost and with a low flight rate. Its Soviet counterpart Buran flew once, uncrewed, in 1988. After decades in which orbital rockets remained expendable, SpaceX landed a Falcon 9 first stage in December 2015 and first refl ew one in March 2017; by the early 2020s recovered boosters had flown more than twenty times each, dramatically lowering launch prices. SpaceX's Starship, which first reached space in 2023, is designed for full and rapid reusability of both stages. Other partially reusable systems in development include Blue Origin's New Glenn, Rocket Lab's Neutron and several Chinese vehicles, while spaceplanes such as Dream Chaser continue the winged approach.

Recovery methods

Recovered components may return by parachute and splashdown (as with the Shuttle boosters and Rocket Lab's Electron), by horizontal runway landing (winged vehicles), or by propulsive vertical landing, the approach used by Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy and Starship boosters, which descend under engine power to a landing pad or a drone ship at sea.
