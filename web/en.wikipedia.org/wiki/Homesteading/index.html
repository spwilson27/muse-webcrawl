Homesteading is a lifestyle of self-sufficiency or, historically, a legal regime under which settlers could acquire ownership of public land by residing on it and improving it. The term is most strongly associated with the United States Homestead Acts, beginning with the Homestead Act of 1862, which granted adult citizens (or intended citizens) who had never borne arms against the US government up to 160 acres (65 hectares) of unappropriated public land, provided they lived on it, built a dwelling, and cultivated it for five years ("proving up").

History

The Homestead Act of 1862, signed by President Abraham Lincoln, took effect on 1 January 1863. Over the following decades, similar legislation — including the Southern Homestead Act of 1866 and the Enlarged Homestead Act of 1909 — distributed land across the American West. In total, about 270 million acres, roughly 10 percent of the land area of the United States, were transferred to private ownership under the homestead laws. The programme ended in the contiguous states with the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976, though homesteading continued in Alaska until 1986.

Canada's Dominion Lands Act of 1872 offered a comparable system, granting 160 acres for a small registration fee and duties of residence and cultivation, to encourage settlement of the Prairies.

Claimants and difficulties

Homesteaders — often called "sodbusters" for the sod houses built on the treeless plains — faced drought, blizzards, grasshopper plagues, and isolation. Fraud was widespread: speculators and corporations used dummy claimants and legal loopholes (such as the Timber Culture and Desert Land Acts) to accumulate large holdings. Historians also emphasize that homesteading rested on the dispossession of Native American peoples from their lands.

Modern homesteading

In contemporary usage, "homesteading" often denotes a self-reliant rural or semi-rural lifestyle: growing food, keeping livestock, preserving food, generating off-grid power, and practising traditional crafts. The modern movement draws on back-to-the-land traditions of the 1930s and 1970s and has grown with online communities, though it no longer involves obtaining free public land in most cases.
