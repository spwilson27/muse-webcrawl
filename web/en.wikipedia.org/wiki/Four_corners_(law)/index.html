Four corners (law)

In law, the four corners is the doctrine holding that the meaning of a legal document — such as a contract, will, or statute — should be determined from the text contained within its "four corners" alone, without reference to extrinsic evidence. A court applying the four corners rule interprets the document solely on the basis of what appears in the instrument itself, provided the language is unambiguous.

Contract interpretation

The four corners doctrine is closely related to the parol evidence rule, which generally bars evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements that would vary the terms of a fully integrated written contract. If a contract's terms are clear and unambiguous on their face, courts applying the doctrine will not consider outside evidence — such as negotiations or course of dealing — to alter their meaning.

Insurance law

In insurance law, the "four corners" rule (sometimes part of an "eight corners" rule when the complaint is compared with the policy) is used in some jurisdictions, notably Texas, to decide whether an insurer has a duty to defend its insured: the court compares the allegations in the plaintiff's petition with the terms of the insurance policy, and only if a claim potentially covered by the policy is alleged does the duty to defend arise.

Criticism

Critics argue that strict application of the four corners rule can produce results contrary to the parties' actual intentions, and many jurisdictions permit extrinsic evidence where language is ambiguous or to resolve latent ambiguities.
