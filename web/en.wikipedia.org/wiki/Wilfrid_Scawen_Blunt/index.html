Wilfrid Scawen Blunt (17 August 1840 – 10 September 1922) was an English poet, writer, traveller, and anti-imperialist campaigner. After a decade in the diplomatic service, he travelled extensively in the Middle East, married Lady Anne King-Noel, a granddaughter of Lord Byron, and with her studied Arabic, rode into the Arabian deserts, and founded the Crabbet Arabian Stud, one of the most influential Arabian horse breeding studs in the world.

Travels and Arab causes

Blunt's journeys among the Bedouin of Mesopotamia and northern Arabia, described in works such as The Future of Islam (1881) and his wife's Bedouin Tribes of the Euphrates and A Pilgrimage to Nejd, made him a leading British advocate of Arab independence from the Ottoman Empire. He championed the Egyptian nationalist Ahmed Urabi, befriended the exiled Sheikh Jamal-ud-Din al-Afghani, and attacked British imperial policy in Egypt and India in pamphlets and poetry.

Irish nationalism and imprisonment

Blunt also supported Irish Home Rule; in 1888, while campaigning against evictions in Ireland, he was arrested under the Coercion Act and imprisoned for two months in Galway and Kilmainham Gaols — an experience he recorded in In Vinculis (1889). He stood unsuccessfully for Parliament several times.

Poetry and diaries

His poetry includes the sonnet sequence The Love Sonnets of Proteus (1881). His extensive personal diaries, published as My Diaries (1919–1920), are a celebrated and caustic record of late Victorian and Edwardian political society, drawing on his friendships with figures from Lord Randolph Churchill to W. B. Yeats.
