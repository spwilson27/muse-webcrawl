Medical cannabis in the United States

Medical cannabis in the United States is legal under state law in most states, but remains illegal under federal law. This conflict between state and federal regimes has shaped the history, regulation, and research of medical cannabis in the country.

History

Cannabis was widely used in American medicine in the 19th century and appeared in the United States Pharmacopeia until 1942. Federal restrictions tightened through the 20th century, culminating in the Controlled Substances Act of 1970, which placed marijuana in Schedule I — defined as having no accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse.

The modern medical cannabis movement began with the AIDS epidemic of the 1980s and 1990s, when patients and activists argued cannabis relieved wasting, nausea, and pain. In 1996, California voters approved Proposition 215, the Compassionate Use Act, becoming the first state to legalize medical cannabis. Dozens of states followed over the next two decades, and by the 2020s a majority of states had comprehensive medical programs.

Federal law and enforcement

Under federal law, cannabis remains a Schedule I substance, and its production and distribution remain federal offenses. Federal enforcement against state-legal programs has varied by administration: the 2009 Ogden memo and the 2013 Cole memo deprioritized enforcement against state-compliant actors, while later administrations shifted policy. Congress has repeatedly attached riders (the Rohrabacher–Farr amendment) barring the Department of Justice from spending funds to interfere with state medical cannabis programs.

Pharmaceutical cannabinoids approved by the FDA — dronabinol, nabilone, and cannabidiol (Epidiolex) — are legal and scheduled separately from plant cannabis.

State programs

State programs differ greatly. Most require a physician recommendation and a state-issued patient card; qualifying conditions commonly include chronic pain, cancer, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, HIV/AIDS, and PTSD, though lists vary. Some states permit home cultivation; others restrict supply to licensed dispensaries. Many states also recognize out-of-state patient cards through reciprocity.

Research barriers

Schedule I status has long complicated research: for decades, federally approved research cannabis could come only from a single source at the University of Mississippi, and studies faced extensive approval hurdles. Regulatory changes in the 2020s expanded licensed research suppliers, but research still lags behind the scale of patient use.
