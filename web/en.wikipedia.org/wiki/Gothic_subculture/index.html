Goth subculture

The goth subculture is a subculture that began in the United Kingdom during the early 1980s, within the gothic rock scene, an offshoot of the British post-punk music scene. The names goth and gothic subculture derive from the music genre. Notable gothic rock artists who helped develop and shape the subculture include Siouxsie and the Banshees, Bauhaus, The Cure, and Joy Division. The goth subculture has survived much longer than others of the same era, and has diversified and spread internationally; it is associated with a distinctive fashion, aesthetics drawing on Gothic literature and horror films, and a taste for the dark, melancholic and macabre.

History

Origins (1979–1984)

The term "gothic" was applied to a strand of moody, atmospheric post-punk around 1979–1981; Bauhaus's 1979 single "Bela Lugosi's Dead" is often cited as the genre's founding record. London's Batcave club, opened in 1982, became a focal point for the emerging scene, mixing gothic rock with glam, horror imagery and cabaret. Early goth was closely intertwined with punk and the new romantic movement, but developed its own identity through music papers such as NME and Melody Maker and through regional scenes in Leeds (Sisters of Mercy) and elsewhere.

Development and diversification (1985–2000)

In the later 1980s and 1990s the subculture spread through Europe, North America and beyond. Subgenres and affiliated styles — dark wave, ethereal wave (e.g., Cocteau Twins, Dead Can Dance), deathrock in the United States, and later industrial and EBM crossovers — broadened the scene. Germany developed an especially large "schwarze Szene" ("black scene") with major festivals; the Wave-Gotik-Treffen in Leipzig, founded in 1992, grew into the largest goth gathering in the world. In Japan, goth aesthetics intertwined with visual kei and gothic Lolita fashion.

21st century

The internet enabled a global, loosely networked scene, with mail-order and later online retail sustaining goth fashion independently of local clubs. Periodic moral panics — most notably after the 1999 Columbine High School massacre in the United States, whose perpetrators were inaccurately associated with goth culture — brought unwanted attention, while academic study of the subculture increased. The subculture persists through club nights, festivals, alternative markets and online communities, and has influenced fashion and popular culture well beyond its own boundaries.

Characteristics

Goth identity centers on music but encompasses fashion, literature, film and an aesthetic sensibility favoring darkness, melancholy, romanticism and the macabre. Influences frequently cited include Gothic fiction (from Horace Walpole and Mary Shelley to Anne Rice's vampire novels), horror cinema (especially classic Universal and Hammer films), and Victorian mourning customs. Despite popular stereotypes, the subculture has no unified ideology or religion; members hold diverse beliefs, and scholars have noted the scene's general ethos of tolerance and individualism.

Fashion

Gothic fashion is characterized by predominantly black clothing, often combining elements of punk, Victorian dress, fetish wear and vintage styles, with pale makeup, dark eyeliner and elaborate hair. Recognized substyles include trad goth, romantic goth, cyber goth and gothic Lolita.

Media representation and controversies

Goth characters and aesthetics appear widely in film and television, from The Craft and South Park's goth kids to Tim Burton's work, which is especially beloved within the scene. The subculture has periodically been stigmatized in news coverage of youth crime and violence, though research has generally found no association between goth identification and criminality; some studies have explored correlations with self-harm and depression, findings that remain debated. In 2013, Greater Manchester Police in England began recording attacks on goths and other alternative subcultures as hate crimes, following the 2007 murder of Sophie Lancaster.
