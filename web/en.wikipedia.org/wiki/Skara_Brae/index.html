Skara Brae (/ˈskærə ˈbreɪ/) is a stone-built Neolithic settlement, located on the Bay of Skaill on the west coast of Mainland, the largest island in the Orkney archipelago of Scotland. Consisting of ten clustered houses, it was occupied from about 3180 BCE to about 2500 BCE and is Europe's most complete Neolithic village. Skara Brae gained UNESCO World Heritage Site status as part of the Heart of Neolithic Orkney; it is sometimes called "Scotland's Pompeii" because of its remarkable preservation.

Discovery and excavation

The site was buried under sand dunes for millennia until a great storm in 1850 stripped the grass from an overlooking knoll and revealed stone buildings. Excavations by William Watt, and later by Vere Gordon Childe in the 1920s–30s, uncovered a village of interconnected houses built of dry stone and covered passages.

The houses

Each house is a single room of roughly 40 square metres with stone furniture: a central hearth, a dresser, box beds and storage cells, all built in stone because timber was scarce. The settlement had a sophisticated drainage system and, in House 8 — interpreted as a workshop — evidence of heat-treated stone tools. The inhabitants farmed cattle and sheep, grew barley, fished and gathered shellfish, and made grooved ware pottery.

Abandonment

Occupation ended around 2500 BCE for reasons still debated; climate change and shifting sands are leading explanations. The site is now threatened by coastal erosion and is protected by a sea wall.
