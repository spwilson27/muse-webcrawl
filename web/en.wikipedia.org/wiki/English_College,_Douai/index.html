The English College (French: Collège des Anglais) was a Catholic seminary in Douai, in the Spanish Netherlands (now northern France), founded in 1568 by William Allen, later a cardinal. It was created to train priests for the English mission at a time when Catholic worship was suppressed in Elizabethan England, and it became associated with the production of the Douay–Rheims Bible, the first full English translation of the Bible by Catholics.

History

Allen, an Oxford academic in exile, established the college shortly after the University of Douai itself was founded. Students combined theological study with preparation for clandestine ministry in England, where many of the college's alumni — later remembered among the English Martyrs — were executed under anti-Catholic penal laws. Between 1578 and 1585, political difficulties forced the college to relocate temporarily to Rheims, during which period the New Testament of the Douay–Rheims Bible was published (1582); the Old Testament followed in 1609–10.

The college flourished for over two centuries, spawning sister institutions and serving English, Welsh and Irish Catholic communities — an Irish College and a Scots College were also established at Douai. It was suppressed during the French Revolution: the community was expelled in 1793 and its property confiscated. Staff and students returning to England in 1794 helped found new seminaries, notably St Edmund's College, Ware, and Ushaw College, which inherited Douai's traditions and archives.
