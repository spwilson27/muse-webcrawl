Luce's choice axiom, formulated by R. Duncan Luce in his 1959 book Individual Choice Behavior, is a principle of probabilistic choice stating that the probability of selecting one item over others is unaffected by the presence or absence of other items in the choice set — a formal independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives condition for random choice.

Statement

For a finite set of alternatives, let PS(x) denote the probability of choosing x from set S. The axiom requires that for x in a subset T of S, PS(x) = PT(x) × PS(choice from T). It follows that there exist positive scale values v(x) such that the probability of choosing x from S equals v(x) divided by the sum of v over all members of S. This is the multinomial logit or Bradley–Terry–Luce form.

Applications and criticism

The model underpins discrete-choice analysis in econometrics and marketing, ranking models in statistics, and softmax action selection in machine learning. Behavioural experiments, however, reveal systematic violations: the similarity effect, attraction effect, and compromise effect show that real choice probabilities depend on the composition of the choice set, contrary to the axiom.
