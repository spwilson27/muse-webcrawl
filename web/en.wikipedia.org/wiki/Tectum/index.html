Tectum

[Image: The tectum forms the roof of the midbrain]

The tectum (Latin for "roof") is the dorsal part of the midbrain (mesencephalon), lying behind the cerebral aqueduct. It is responsible for auditory and visual reflexes and is best known for its two paired structures: the superior colliculi, concerned with vision, and the inferior colliculi, concerned with hearing, together called the corpora quadrigemina.

Anatomy and function

The superior colliculus receives retinal input and maps visual space; it coordinates rapid orienting movements of the eyes and head (saccades) toward stimuli. In non-mammalian vertebrates the homologous optic tectum is the dominant visual processing centre, and classic experiments on its regenerating retinal connections by Roger Sperry founded the chemoaffinity hypothesis of neural development. The inferior colliculus is a major relay of the ascending auditory pathway, integrating signals from brainstem nuclei before passing them to the medial geniculate body of the thalamus.

Development and clinical notes

The tectum develops from the alar plate of the embryonic midbrain, patterned by signalling centres such as the midbrain–hindbrain (isthmic) organiser. Tectal tumours, usually slow-growing gliomas, are a rare cause of obstructive hydrocephalus and Parinaud's syndrome, in which compression of the dorsal midbrain impairs upward gaze.
