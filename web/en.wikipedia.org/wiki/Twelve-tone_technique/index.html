Twelve-tone technique

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The twelve-tone technique — also known as dodecaphony, twelve-tone serialism, and (in British usage) twelve-note composition — is a method of musical composition first devised by the Austrian composer Josef Matthias Hauer in 1919/1920 and subsequently developed by Arnold Schoenberg in the early 1920s.

The technique is a means of ensuring that all 12 notes of the chromatic scale are sounded as often as one another in a piece of music while preventing the emphasis of any one note, thereby avoiding tonality. Music composed using the technique is therefore a form of atonal music.

The tone row

The basis of the technique is the tone row (German: Grundgestalt or Reihe), an ordering of the twelve pitch classes of the chromatic scale. The composer works with the row and its transformations: the prime form, its inversion (the row turned upside down), its retrograde (the row played backwards), and the retrograde inversion. Each of these forms can be transposed to begin on any of the twelve pitches, giving up to 48 forms of the row from which the piece is constructed. Repetitions of a note are generally avoided until the row has been completed.

History

Schoenberg developed the method after years of writing freely atonal music, seeking a structural principle to replace tonal harmony. His first fully twelve-tone works include the Suite for Piano, Op. 25 (1921–23) and the Wind Quintet, Op. 26. His pupils Anton Webern and Alban Berg adopted and developed the method, and the three became known as the Second Viennese School.

After the Second World War, a younger generation — including Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Luigi Nono and Milton Babbitt — extended the serial principle from pitch to other parameters such as rhythm, dynamics and timbre, a development known as total serialism.

Criticism

The technique has been controversial. Detractors have found twelve-tone music cerebral and inaccessible, while defenders credit it with opening new expressive and structural possibilities. Schoenberg himself predicted that the method would ensure the supremacy of German music for a hundred years.
