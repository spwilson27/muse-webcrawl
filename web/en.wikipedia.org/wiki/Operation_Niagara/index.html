Operation Niagara was a US aerial bombardment campaign conducted from January to March 1968 in support of the Marine garrison at Khe Sanh Combat Base in north-western South Vietnam during the Vietnam War. Named for the torrent of fire it was meant to unleash, it was one of the most concentrated applications of air power of the war.

Background

In early 1968 two North Vietnamese Army (PAVN) divisions surrounded the reinforced Marine regiment at Khe Sanh, near the Laotian border. Fearing a repetition of the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu, President Lyndon B. Johnson demanded that the base be held. General William Westmoreland ordered a massive air effort, coordinated by the Seventh Air Force under General William Momyer, to break the siege without committing large ground reinforcements.

The bombardment

Between 22 January and 31 March 1968, American aircraft flew more than 24,000 tactical sorties and 2,700 B-52 sorties, dropping approximately 110,000 tons of bombs — more than 1,500 tons a day — on PAVN positions around the base. B-52 Arc Light strikes were brought to within 1,000 metres of the Marine perimeter, guided by the Combat Skyspot radar system. Sensor fields (Operation Igloo White), defoliation and the aerial resupply of the garrison, including LAPES parachute extractions, formed part of the overall effort.

Outcome

The siege was broken in April 1968 by the ground relief effort Operation Pegasus, and North Vietnamese forces withdrew towards Laos having suffered very heavy losses; US estimates claimed over 10,000 PAVN casualties, though the true figure is disputed. Whether Khe Sanh was ever intended as a main attack or a diversion for the Tet Offensive remains debated. The base was abandoned in July 1968, fuelling criticism that the battle's strategic value had been exaggerated.
