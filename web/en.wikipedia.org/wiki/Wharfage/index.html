Wharfage

Wharfage is the fee charged by a wharf owner or port authority for the use of a wharf in loading, unloading or handling cargo and for the berthing of a vessel alongside it. The term also refers more broadly to the use of a wharf itself, and in older usage to wharves collectively.

Nature of the charge

Wharfage is typically assessed on the cargo passing over the wharf, calculated by weight or measurement — for example, per ton, per cubic metre or per container (TEU) — rather than on the vessel itself; the corresponding charge on the ship is usually called dockage, berthage or port dues. In container terminals wharfage is commonly levied by the terminal operator or port authority and is distinct from stevedoring charges for the labour of loading and unloading, terminal handling charges, and storage or demurrage for cargo left on the wharf beyond a free period.

Contract and liability context

Whether wharfage is payable by the shipowner, charterer, shipper or consignee depends on the contract of carriage and the terms of the charterparty; clauses such as "free in and out" allocate cargo-handling costs accordingly. In United States maritime law, wharfage charges by public port authorities are regulated under federal shipping law, and a wharfinger providing facilities owes duties of reasonable care, including warning vessels of known hazards at the berth — a principle established in admiralty case law concerning the wharfinger's liability.

Historical notes

Wharfage was an important source of municipal revenue in medieval and early modern ports, where the right to levy it was often granted by charter. In London, legal quays were designated from 1559 for the landing of goods subject to customs, and wharfingers — the owners and operators of wharves — formed a recognised trade in the great entrepôt ports of the Thames, Liverpool and Bristol.
