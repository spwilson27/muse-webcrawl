Don Juan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Don Juan (Spanish: [doŋ ˈxwan]) is a legendary, fictional libertine and seducer who first appeared in the Spanish Golden Age play El burlador de Sevilla y convidado de piedra ("The Trickster of Seville and the Stone Guest", c. 1630), attributed to Tirso de Molina. In the story, the nobleman Don Juan Tenorio seduces and dishonours a series of women, kills the father (the Commander) of one of them, and mockingly invites the dead man's statue to dinner; the statue drags the unrepentant Don Juan down to hell.

Later versions

The legend spread across Europe and was reshaped by many major writers and composers: Molière's Dom Juan (1665), Mozart and Lorenzo Da Ponte's opera Don Giovanni (1787) — whose Leporello catalogues 1,003 conquests in Spain alone — Lord Byron's satirical epic poem Don Juan (1819–1824), which made its hero more a victim of women than a predator, and José Zorrilla's Don Juan Tenorio (1844), still performed in Spain around All Saints' Day, in which Don Juan is redeemed by love.

Legacy

"Don Juan" has entered common usage as a synonym for a promiscuous seducer of women, and the figure has been read variously as a cautionary sinner, a rebel against social and religious authority, and a case study in psychology.
