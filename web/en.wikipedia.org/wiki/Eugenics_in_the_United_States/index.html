Eugenics in the United States

Eugenics displays at American state fairs promoted selective breeding ideas in the 1920s.

Eugenics in the United States was a widespread social and scientific movement in the first half of the twentieth century that sought to "improve" the hereditary qualities of the population through selective reproduction. Inspired by Francis Galton's ideas, American eugenicists promoted immigration restriction, anti-miscegenation laws and the compulsory sterilisation of people deemed "unfit", policies later cited as models—and then exceeded—by Nazi Germany.

Origins and institutions

The movement gained institutional form with the Eugenics Record Office (1910) at Cold Spring Harbor, directed by Charles Davenport and superintendent Harry Laughlin, funded by the Carnegie Institution and Harriman money. Researchers compiled pedigree studies of families labelled feeble-minded or criminal, while "Fitter Families" contests at state fairs normalised eugenic ideals.

Sterilisation laws

Indiana enacted the first compulsory sterilisation law in 1907; more than thirty states followed. In Buck v. Bell (1927), the Supreme Court upheld Virginia's statute, Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. declaring "three generations of imbeciles are enough." Over 60,000 people—disproportionately poor, disabled, institutionalised and members of racial minorities—were sterilised under such laws into the 1970s. California performed roughly a third of the total.

Immigration and race

Eugenic testimony, especially Laughlin's, helped secure the Immigration Act of 1924, which imposed national-origins quotas excluding most Asian immigration and restricting southern and eastern Europeans. Eugenic arguments also supported state anti-miscegenation statutes, struck down only in Loving v. Virginia (1967).

Decline and legacy

Scientific criticism, the Carnegie Institution's withdrawal of support (1939), and revulsion at Nazi crimes discredited the movement after 1945, though sterilisation programmes persisted in some states into the 1970s and eugenics boards operated in North Carolina until 1977. Several states have since apologised and created compensation schemes for surviving victims.
