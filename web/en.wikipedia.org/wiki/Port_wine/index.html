Port wine

Port wine (Portuguese: vinho do Porto) is a Portuguese fortified wine produced in the Douro Valley of northern Portugal. It is typically a sweet red wine, often served with dessert, although it also comes in dry, semi-dry, and white varieties.

Production

Port is made by adding grape spirit (aguardente) to wine during fermentation, halting the process and preserving residual sugars while raising the alcohol content to around 19–20%. The wine is then aged in barrels or bottles in cellars in Vila Nova de Gaia, across the river from Porto, from which it takes its name.

Styles

Styles include ruby, tawny (aged in wood, acquiring a nutty, caramel character), vintage port (from a single declared year, aged in bottle), late bottled vintage (LBV), colheita, and white port. The Douro demarcated region, established in 1756, is one of the oldest protected appellations in the world.

History

The trade grew in the late 17th century when British merchants, at war with France, turned to Portuguese wines; the Methuen Treaty of 1703 favoured imports of Portuguese wine into England.
