A safety lamp is a type of lamp designed for use in flammable atmospheres, principally coal mines where firedamp (mostly methane) could be ignited by a naked flame. The classic designs of the early nineteenth century enclosed the flame within a metal gauze or glass chamber so that it could not ignite gas outside the lamp, while changes in the flame also warned miners of dangerous gas.

History

Explosions in British coal mines claimed many lives in the early 1800s, including the Felling mine disaster of 1812, which killed 92 men and boys. Dr William Reid Clanny produced an early safety lamp in 1813. In 1815 Sir Humphry Davy was asked to address the problem and developed the Davy lamp, in which the flame is surrounded by a fine wire gauze: air can pass through to feed the flame, but the gauze conducts heat away so effectively that the flame cannot pass back through and ignite the surrounding gas. Independently, the engineer George Stephenson developed the Geordie lamp at Killingworth Colliery, using a glass tube and restricted air inlets. A priority dispute followed, but both designs entered widespread use from 1816.

Gas detection

Beyond giving light, safety lamps served as primitive gas detectors. In the presence of methane the flame burns taller and develops a pale blue "cap"; in oxygen-deficient air (blackdamp) the flame dims and goes out. Trained officials ("firemen") would test for gas by carefully lowering the flame and observing its behaviour. Later flame lamps were refined with locked cases, internal igniters and bonnets, and specialised testing lamps were developed.

Later developments

Although safety lamps saved many lives, critics noted that they gave poor light and may have encouraged owners to work gassier, deeper seams. Flame lamps were gradually superseded in the twentieth century by electric cap lamps, and electronic methanometers replaced them for gas measurement. Flame safety lamps nevertheless remained in statutory use in British pits for gas testing well into the modern era.
