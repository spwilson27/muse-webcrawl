Positional notation

Positional notation (or place-value notation, or positional numeral system) usually denotes the extension to any base of the Hindu–Arabic numeral system (or decimal system). More generally, a positional system is a numeral system in which the contribution of a digit to the value of a number is the value of the digit multiplied by a factor determined by the position of the digit.

Principle

In ordinary base-ten notation, the number 939 is understood to represent 9 hundreds, 3 tens, and 9 units, i.e. 9×102 + 3×101 + 9×100. The same principle works for any base: in binary (base 2) only the digits 0 and 1 are used, and each position corresponds to a power of two. Positions to the right of the radix point represent negative powers of the base, giving fractions.

History

The Babylonians used a positional system with base 60 (sexagesimal), though it initially lacked a true zero. The Maya independently developed a vigesimal (base-20) positional system with a zero symbol. The fully developed decimal positional system, including zero as a number, arose in India and was transmitted to Europe via Arabic mathematical works, notably those of al-Khwarizmi and Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202). Positional notation displaced earlier additive systems such as Roman numerals because it makes arithmetic algorithms — addition, multiplication, long division — mechanical and efficient.

Applications

Positional systems underlie modern computing (binary, octal, hexadecimal), measurement, and finance. The concept extends to polynomials, where the coefficients play the role of digits and the indeterminate plays the role of the base.
