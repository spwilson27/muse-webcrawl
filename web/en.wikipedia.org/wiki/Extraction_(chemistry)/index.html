Extraction (chemistry)

Image placeholder: Liquid–liquid extraction in a separatory funnel

Extraction in chemistry is a separation process consisting of the separation of a substance from a matrix. The term also refers to the removal of one or more compounds of interest from a sample or source material using a solvent. Common examples include the extraction of a compound from a solid or liquid mixture, such as brewing tea or coffee, decaffeinating coffee beans, or separating a product from a reaction mixture.

Types

Liquid–liquid extraction (solvent extraction) partitions compounds between two immiscible liquid phases, typically water and an organic solvent, according to their relative solubilities; it is commonly performed with a separatory funnel. Solid–liquid extraction leaches soluble material from a solid, as in the Soxhlet extraction used for continuous extraction of compounds from solid samples. Other methods include solid-phase extraction, supercritical fluid extraction (often with carbon dioxide), steam distillation, and microwave- and ultrasound-assisted extraction.

Applications

Extraction is fundamental to analytical chemistry sample preparation, the purification of pharmaceuticals and natural products, the processing of ores and petroleum, and the nuclear fuel cycle, where the PUREX process extracts uranium and plutonium from spent fuel.
