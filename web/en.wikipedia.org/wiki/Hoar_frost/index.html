Hoar frost

Hoar frost (also hoarfrost, radiation frost, or pruina) is a deposit of white ice crystals formed on surfaces when water vapour in the air comes into contact with a surface that is below freezing and below the frost point. The vapour passes directly into the solid phase by deposition, without first condensing as liquid dew.

Formation

Hoar frost typically forms on clear, calm nights when exposed surfaces cool by radiation to below the frost point of the surrounding air. Because the crystals grow by direct deposition, they often form delicate, feathery or needle-like structures on objects such as grass, fences, branches and window panes. The name derives from the Old English hār, "white-haired" or "grey with age", describing the white, hairy appearance of the deposit.

Types

  • Surface hoar — crystals formed on snow, ice or frozen ground; an important weak layer in avalanche formation.
  • Air hoar — crystals forming in free air on objects, requiring the surface to be colder than the air.
  • Depth hoar — faceted crystals growing within a snowpack under a strong temperature gradient.
  • Crevasse hoar — crystals formed inside glacial crevasses.

Distinction from other frosts

Hoar frost differs from rime ice, which forms when supercooled water droplets in fog freeze on contact, producing a milky, opaque coating. It also differs from black frost, in which vegetation is damaged by freezing without a visible white deposit. Heavy hoar frost can weigh down branches, and surface hoar on snow is a persistent avalanche hazard monitored by forecasters.
