DNG stores unprocessed sensor data plus standard metadata, intended as a long-term archival "digital negative".

Digital Negative (DNG) is an open, lossless raw image file format developed by Adobe and used for digital photography. It was launched on September 27, 2004. The launch was accompanied by the first version of the DNG specification, plus various products, including a free-of-charge DNG converter utility.

Rationale

Before DNG, camera manufacturers each used their own proprietary and often undocumented raw formats, raising fears that photographs would become unreadable as formats were abandoned. Adobe proposed DNG as a single, publicly documented archival format based on the TIFF/EP standard, into which proprietary raw files could be converted without losing data, with standardised metadata and optional embedded previews and checksums for file validation.

Adoption and criticism

Some manufacturers — including Leica, Ricoh, Pentax (Hoya), Hasselblad and Samsung at various times — have written DNG natively in cameras, and DNG is mandatory in the CinemaDNG standard. Larger manufacturers such as Canon and Nikon have not adopted it in-camera, preferring their own formats, though Adobe's software supports DNG conversion universally. Critics have questioned converter fidelity and the wisdom of discarding native files; supporters regard DNG as the safest long-term archive.
