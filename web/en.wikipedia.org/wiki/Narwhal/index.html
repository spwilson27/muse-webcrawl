Narwhal

Narwhal (Monodon monoceros)
Family: Monodontidae
Range: Arctic waters of Canada, Greenland, Norway, Russia
Length: 3.95–5.5 m (excluding tusk)
Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)

The narwhal (Monodon monoceros) is a species of toothed whale native to the Arctic. It is the only member of the genus Monodon and one of two living species in the family Monodontidae, along with the beluga whale. Males are distinguished by a long, straight, spiralled tusk, which is in fact an elongated upper-left canine tooth that can grow up to about 3 metres; females mostly lack a visible tusk.

Biology and behaviour

Narwhals inhabit the Arctic waters around Greenland, Canada, Norway and Russia, favouring deep offshore waters and coastal areas in summer. They dive to great depths — over 1,500 metres — feeding on Greenland halibut, shrimp, squid and Arctic cod. The tusk is richly innervated and is thought to serve sensory functions as well as playing a role in male social hierarchy and mating displays; groups of males may engage in "tusking", rubbing tusks at the surface.

Conservation and culture

Narwhals are hunted by Inuit communities under quota in Canada and Greenland for meat, skin (muktuk) and tusks. Medieval Europeans prized narwhal tusks as unicorn horns, believed to neutralise poison. The IUCN lists the species as Least Concern, though some populations are small and the species is considered vulnerable to climate-driven loss of sea ice.
