Seal hunting

Seal hunting, or sealing, is the personal or commercial hunting of seals. It has been practised for thousands of years by Indigenous peoples of the Arctic, for whom seals remain a vital source of food, skins, oil and materials. Commercial sealing developed from the 16th century onwards, supplying fur, leather and oil, and driving some species — such as the Caribbean monk seal — to extinction and others to near-extinction before protective legislation in the 20th century.

Modern hunts

The largest contemporary commercial hunt is Canada's Atlantic harp seal hunt, conducted mainly off Newfoundland and Labrador and in the Gulf of St Lawrence, regulated by quotas and licensing. Namibia conducts a Cape fur seal hunt, and regulated hunts also occur in Greenland, Russia, Norway and other northern countries. Indigenous subsistence hunting continues throughout the Arctic, including in Alaska, Canada, Greenland and Chukotka.

Controversy

Seal hunting is among the most contentious wildlife issues in the world. Animal welfare organisations campaign against the commercial killing of seals, particularly of young harp seals ("whitecoats", whose commercial hunting Canada banned in 1987), citing cruelty and sustainability. The European Union banned trade in most seal products in 2009, with exemptions for Indigenous hunts, a measure upheld at the WTO but criticised for harming Inuit communities. Defenders of the hunts maintain they are humane, sustainable, and economically and culturally essential.
