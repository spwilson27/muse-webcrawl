Gloster E.28/39


The Gloster E.28/39, Britain's first jet aircraft.

The Gloster E.28/39, also referred to as the Gloster Whittle, Gloster Pioneer, or Gloster G.40, was the first British jet-engined aircraft to fly. Developed by the Gloster Aircraft Company for the Royal Air Force, it served as an experimental testbed for the turbojet engine developed by Frank Whittle's Power Jets company.

Development

In 1939 Power Jets, the company formed by Frank Whittle to develop his jet propulsion concept, approached Gloster's chief designer George Carter with a proposal for a small experimental aircraft built around Whittle's engine. The Air Ministry issued Specification E.28/39 — from which the aircraft took its name — for an aircraft to test the new engine, and Gloster received a contract in early 1940 for two prototypes, serials W4041 and W4046.

The E.28/39 was a small low-wing monoplane of all-metal construction with a tricycle undercarriage and the engine mounted amidships, fed by air intakes on either side of the nose and exhausting through a jet pipe beneath the tail. The first prototype was powered by a Power Jets W.1 engine producing about 860 lbf (3.8 kN) of thrust.

First flight

After taxiing trials in April 1941 — during which the aircraft made a few brief "hops" into the air — the E.28/39 made its first true flight on 15 May 1941 from RAF Cranwell, piloted by Gloster's chief test pilot, Gerry Sayer. The flight lasted about 17 minutes and was entirely successful. Testing continued over the following two years with progressively more powerful engines, including the W.1A and the W.2/500, and the type reached speeds of around 466 mph (750 km/h).

Surviving aircraft

The second prototype, W4046, was lost in an accident in 1943 when its ailerons jammed at altitude; the pilot bailed out safely. The first prototype, W4041, survived and was presented to the Science Museum in London in 1946, where it remains on display today. Although the E.28/39 was never intended as a combat aircraft, it proved the practicality of jet propulsion and led directly to the twin-engined Gloster Meteor, the only Allied jet fighter to see service in the Second World War.
