Still Bay

The Still Bay or Stillbay industry is a term given by archaeologists A. J. H. Goodwin and C. van Riet Lowe in 1929 to a Middle Stone Age stone tool manufacturing assemblage in South Africa. It is named after the town of Still Bay (Stilbaai) in the Western Cape, where the characteristic tools were first described. The industry is dated to roughly 72,000–71,000 years ago, during Marine Isotope Stage 4.

Characteristics

Still Bay assemblages are distinguished by finely made, thin bifacial points — leaf- or laurel-shaped foliate points produced by pressure flaking, often on heat-treated silcrete. The points were probably hafted as spearheads or knives. Associated finds include engraved ochre, perforated shell beads, and worked bone. Key sites include Blombos Cave, Sibudu Cave, Diepkloof Rock Shelter, and Hollow Rock Shelter. At Blombos, Still Bay layers overlie and interleave with some of the earliest evidence of symbolic behaviour in the archaeological record.

Heat treatment

A hallmark of the Still Bay is the deliberate heat treatment of silcrete stone before knapping, which improves its flaking properties. Controlled experiments have shown that heating transforms the rock microstructure, allowing the production of the thin, regular bifacial points characteristic of the industry. This is among the earliest well-documented uses of pyrotechnology in tool manufacture.

Significance

The Still Bay, together with the roughly contemporary Howieson's Poort industry, is central to debates about the origins of behavioural modernity. Its sophisticated technology, use of pigments, and apparent symbolic artefacts have been taken as evidence that cognitively modern behaviour emerged in Africa tens of thousands of years before the "Upper Palaeolithic revolution" in Europe. The industry's relatively brief duration and its appearance during a period of climatic instability have prompted hypotheses linking it to population bottlenecks and genetic drift among early human populations.
