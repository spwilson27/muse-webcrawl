Geometric albedo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The geometric albedo of an astronomical body is the ratio of its actual brightness at zero phase angle (that is, as seen from the light source, fully illuminated) to that of an idealized flat, fully reflecting, diffusively scattering (Lambertian) disk with the same cross-sectional area. It is a dimensionless quantity used to measure how much of the incident sunlight a body reflects back toward the Sun and observer when fully lit.

Relation to other albedos

Geometric albedo differs from Bond albedo, which measures the fraction of total incident energy scattered in all directions; the two are connected through the phase integral. Because of phenomena such as the opposition surge, geometric albedo can exceed 1: the icy Saturnian moon Enceladus, resurfaced by bright water-ice geysers, has a geometric albedo of about 1.38, the highest known for any Solar System body. By contrast the Moon's geometric albedo is only about 0.12, similar to worn asphalt, despite its bright appearance in the night sky.

Typical values

Approximate visual geometric albedos include: Mercury 0.14, Venus 0.69 (its clouds are highly reflective), Earth 0.37, Mars 0.17, Jupiter 0.54, Saturn 0.50, Uranus 0.49, and Neptune 0.44. Among asteroids, dark carbonaceous bodies may fall below 0.05, while the dwarf planet Ceres is about 0.09.

Use in astronomy

For asteroids and trans-Neptunian objects, geometric albedo combined with absolute magnitude yields an estimate of diameter, a relationship exploited in surveys where size cannot be measured directly. Combined with thermal infrared observations, albedo also helps classify surface composition.
