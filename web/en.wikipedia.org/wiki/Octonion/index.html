Octonion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The Fano plane encodes the multiplication rules of the imaginary octonion units.

In mathematics, the octonions are a normed division algebra over the real numbers, a kind of hypercomplex number system. They are eight-dimensional: an octonion is a linear combination of the basis elements 1, e1, …, e7 with real coefficients. The octonions extend the quaternions, just as the quaternions extend the complex numbers and the complex numbers extend the reals. They were described in 1843 by John T. Graves, in a letter to William Rowan Hamilton, and independently published by Arthur Cayley in 1845; they are sometimes called Cayley numbers.

Properties

Octonion multiplication is neither commutative nor associative, but it is alternative: the subalgebra generated by any two elements is associative. The octonions possess a norm satisfying N(xy) = N(x)N(y), which makes them a division algebra — every non-zero octonion has a multiplicative inverse. By Hurwitz's theorem, the reals, complex numbers, quaternions and octonions are the only normed division algebras over the real numbers. The multiplication of the seven imaginary units can be summarised by the Fano plane, a finite projective plane with seven points and seven lines.

The automorphism group of the octonions is the exceptional Lie group G2, the smallest of the exceptional groups, and the octonions play a role in the construction of the other exceptional Lie groups and in parts of theoretical physics, including string theory and special relativity in certain formulations.

Construction

The octonions can be built from the quaternions by the Cayley–Dickson construction, which doubles a *-algebra at each step: reals → complex numbers → quaternions → octonions. Each doubling loses a property — ordering, then commutativity, then associativity. Applying the construction once more yields the 16-dimensional sedenions, which are no longer a division algebra, having zero divisors.
