Density matrix

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of a density matrix as a grid: populations on the diagonal, coherences off-diagonal fading as decoherence acts]
Decoherence kills the off-diagonal elements of the density matrix.

In quantum mechanics, a density matrix (or density operator) is a matrix that describes the quantum state of a physical system when that state is a statistical mixture — or when the system is entangled with an environment and considered on its own. Introduced by John von Neumann (1927) and, independently, by Lev Landau, it generalises the Wave function (which suffices only for pure states).

The density matrix ρ has expectation values given by the trace rule ⟨A⟩ = Tr(ρA); pure states satisfy Tr(ρ²) = 1, mixtures give less; its diagonal entries are populations and its off-diagonal entries are coherences. Quantum decoherence is, in this language, the decay of off-diagonal elements in the pointer basis — the formalism in which decoherence, open quantum systems, quantum statistical mechanics and quantum information (where ρ is the fundamental object) are all written. Tracing out an environment from an entangled whole — the partial trace — is how the formalism handles subsystems, and the von Neumann entropy of ρ quantifies both mixture and entanglement.

Why it matters

No real quantum system is isolated: the density matrix is the honest description of every experiment ever performed, from NMR (where it was first heavily used) to quantum computing with noisy qubits. Master equations (Lindblad form) govern its evolution in open systems — the working theory of every quantum technology.
