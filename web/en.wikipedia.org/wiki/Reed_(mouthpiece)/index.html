Reed (mouthpiece)

Reed
MaterialTraditionally cane (Arundo donax); also synthetic polymers
TypesSingle reed; double reed; free reed
Used inClarinet, saxophone (single); oboe, bassoon (double); harmonica, accordion (free)
Graded byStrength (stiffness), typically numbered 1–5

A reed is a thin strip of material that vibrates to produce sound in certain musical instruments. In wind instruments the reed beats against, or vibrates with, a mouthpiece or another reed as air passes, chopping the airflow into pulses that set the air column resonating. Traditionally reeds are cut from the cane Arundo donax, though synthetic reeds are increasingly common for their durability and consistency.

Single reeds

A single reed is a flat, tapered cane vane fixed by a ligature over a table on the mouthpiece, leaving a narrow gap (the tip opening) through which air passes. It is used by the clarinet and saxophone families. Single reeds are graded by strength — a measure of stiffness — usually on a scale from about 1 (soft) to 5 (hard); softer reeds respond easily and suit beginners, while harder reeds give advanced players greater control and projection at higher resistance.

Double reeds

A double reed consists of two shaped pieces of cane bound together on a metal staple, which vibrate against each other. Players of the oboe and cor anglais use a relatively narrow reed; the bassoon reed is broader. Double-reed players traditionally make or heavily adjust their own reeds, scraping the cane to tune response and intonation — a time-consuming craft central to the instruments' technique. Shawms, crumhorns and the chanters of many bagpipes also use double reeds.

Free reeds

In free-reed instruments — the harmonica, accordion, concertina, reed organ and harmonium — a metal tongue mounted over a slot vibrates freely through it under air pressure from bellows or the player's breath, rather than beating against a surface.

Care

Cane reeds are hygroscopic and delicate: players rotate several reeds, store them flat in cases, and expect each to last only hours to weeks of playing as the cane softens and the tip chips or warps. Synthetic reeds sacrifice some tonal nuance for longevity and immunity to moisture.
