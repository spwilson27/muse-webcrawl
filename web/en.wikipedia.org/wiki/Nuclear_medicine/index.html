Nuclear medicine

Nuclear medicine is a medical specialty that uses small amounts of radioactive substances (radiopharmaceuticals) to diagnose and treat disease. Unlike most radiology, which images anatomy, nuclear medicine images physiology — how organs function — because the radiotracer accumulates according to metabolic or biochemical processes in the body.

Techniques

A radiopharmaceutical combines a radionuclide with a targeting molecule and is usually injected intravenously. Emitted gamma rays are detected by a gamma camera to produce planar images or, by rotating the camera, tomographic SPECT (single-photon emission computed tomography) scans. Positron-emitting tracers are imaged with PET (positron emission tomography), most commonly using fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG, labelled with fluorine-18) to map glucose metabolism — widely used in oncology, cardiology and neurology. Hybrid scanners combine PET or SPECT with CT or MRI for anatomical localisation.

Common isotopes and studies

The workhorse isotope is technetium-99m, obtained from molybdenum-99 generators, used for bone scans, myocardial perfusion imaging, renal and thyroid studies. Others include iodine-131 (thyroid), thallium-201 and gallium-67 (historically), xenon-133 (lung ventilation) and fluorine-18. Typical studies include bone scintigraphy for metastases, V/Q scans for pulmonary embolism, thyroid uptake scans, gastric emptying and hepatobiliary (HIDA) scans.

Therapy

Nuclear medicine also delivers targeted radiotherapy: iodine-131 for hyperthyroidism and thyroid cancer; radium-223 and lutetium-177 (e.g. Lu-177 DOTATATE for neuroendocrine tumours, and Lu-177 PSMA for prostate cancer) for metastatic disease; yttrium-90 microspheres for liver tumours; and radioimmunotherapy for lymphoma.

Safety and training

Radiation doses are generally comparable to those of CT examinations and are governed by the ALARA principle and licensing of radioactive materials. Nuclear medicine physicians typically train in radiology or a dedicated nuclear medicine residency, working with technologists, radiopharmacists and medical physicists. The field's history runs from the first clinical uses of radioiodine in the 1940s and Benedict Cassen's rectilinear scanner (1951) to Hal Anger's gamma camera (1958) and modern PET/CT.
