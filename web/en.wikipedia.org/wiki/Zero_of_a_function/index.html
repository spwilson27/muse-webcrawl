Zero of a function

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a zero of a real-, complex-, or generally vector-valued function f is a member x of the domain of f such that f(x) vanishes at x, that is, f(x) = 0. A zero is thus an input value that produces an output of zero. A root of a polynomial is a zero of the corresponding polynomial function.

Solution of equations

Every equation of the form f(x) = g(x) can be rewritten by setting h(x) = f(x) − g(x), so solving an equation is equivalent to finding the zeros of a function. Graphically, a real zero of a real-valued function is the x-coordinate of a point where its graph meets the x-axis (an x-intercept).

Properties and methods

By the intermediate value theorem, a continuous real function with values of opposite sign at the ends of an interval has at least one zero inside it; this underlies the bisection method. Other numerical techniques for locating zeros include Newton's method and secant methods. A zero is said to have multiplicity m if the function can be written as (x − x₀)m·g(x) with g(x₀) ≠ 0 near the zero. For polynomials, the fundamental theorem of algebra states that a polynomial of degree n with complex coefficients has exactly n complex zeros, counted with multiplicity.

In general contexts

The term is used throughout mathematics: zeros of analytic functions are isolated unless the function is identically zero; in algebraic geometry the zero set of a collection of polynomials is an algebraic variety; and in control theory the zeros of a transfer function, together with its poles, determine system behaviour.
