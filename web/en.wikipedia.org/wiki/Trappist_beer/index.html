Trappist beer is beer brewed by Trappist monks — members of the Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance — within the walls of their monasteries. Since 1997 the name has been protected: a beer may only carry the "Authentic Trappist Product" label if it is brewed within a Trappist monastery, under the supervision of the monks, and if the proceeds support the monastic community and charitable works rather than commercial profit.

Breweries

Historically most Trappist breweries are in Belgium — Westmalle, Chimay, Rochefort, Orval, Achel and Westvleteren — with others in the Netherlands (La Trappe, Zundert), Austria (Engelszell), the United States (Spencer), Italy (Tre Fontane), England (Tynt Meadow), Spain (Cardena) and France (Mont des Cats, whose beer is brewed at another abbey and does not carry the full label). The number of recognised breweries changes over time as monasteries begin or cease brewing.

Styles

Trappist beers are typically top-fermented, bottle-conditioned ales of considerable strength. Traditional designations by strength include enkel (single), dubbel (a brown ale around 6–7% ABV), tripel (a pale golden ale around 9%, popularised by Westmalle) and quadrupel (dark ales above 10%). Westvleteren 12, a quadrupel from the Saint-Sixtus Abbey, is frequently rated among the world's best beers and is famously sold only at the abbey in limited quantities.

Abbey beer

"Abbey beer" (bière d'abbaye) is a broader, unprotected term for beers brewed commercially in a monastic style or under licence from an abbey; unlike Trappist beer it need not be made by monks.
