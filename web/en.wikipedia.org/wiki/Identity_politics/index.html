Identity politics

Identity politics is political activity and theorising founded in the shared experiences of injustice of members of a particular social group — such as a race, ethnicity, gender, sexuality, religion, or class — who organise to assert that group's distinct perspective and interests. The term came into wide use in the later twentieth century, and is often credited to the Combahee River Collective, a Black feminist organisation whose 1977 statement declared that "the most profound and potentially most radical politics come directly out of our own identity".

History

Identity-based organising has roots in earlier movements — abolitionism, suffrage, and national liberation — but the characteristic form emerged from the "new social movements" of the 1960s and 1970s: the civil rights movement, second-wave feminism, gay liberation, Black Power, and the American Indian Movement. These movements argued that formal legal equality was insufficient because dominant norms themselves embodied the perspectives of dominant groups, and they developed concepts such as standpoint, consciousness-raising, and later intersectionality (Kimberlé Crenshaw, 1989), which analyses how identities overlap to produce distinct experiences of disadvantage.

Debates

Identity politics is among the most contested terms in contemporary politics. Supporters argue that it names real structures of marginalisation invisible to a falsely universal standpoint, and that recognition — in Charles Taylor's phrase, "the politics of recognition" — is a human need whose denial inflicts harm. Critics, from Marxists such as Adolph Reed to liberals such as Francis Fukuyama (Identity, 2018), argue that it fragments universal coalitions, essentialises fluid identities, and shifts attention from material redistribution; Nancy Fraser has framed the tension as one between recognition and redistribution. The term is also used pejoratively in popular debate, where its precise referent is often unclear.
