Operationalism

In philosophy of science, operationalism (or operationism) is a pragmatic philosophy that holds that concepts have meaning only in terms of the operations used to measure or define them. The term was coined by the physicist Percy Williams Bridgman, who proposed the idea in his 1927 book The Logic of Modern Physics.

Overview

Bridgman argued that a concept is synonymous with the corresponding set of operations used to determine it: for example, the concept of length is fixed by the operations used to measure length, and different operations (such as measuring with a ruler versus with radar) strictly define different concepts. The doctrine was influenced by Einstein's operational analysis of simultaneity in special relativity.

Application and criticism

Operationalism strongly influenced logical positivism and the behavioural sciences: in psychology, B. F. Skinner and others applied operational definitions to concepts such as intelligence, and S. S. Stevens formalised operational definitions of measurement. Critics have objected that the approach splinters concepts (each measurement procedure yielding a distinct concept), that many theoretical terms cannot be fully reduced to operations, and that operational definitions alone cannot capture the meaning of theoretical concepts in science.
