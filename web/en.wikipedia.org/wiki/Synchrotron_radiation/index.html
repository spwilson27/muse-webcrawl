Synchrotron radiation

Synchrotron radiation (also known as magnetobremsstrahlung) is the electromagnetic radiation emitted when relativistic charged particles are subject to an acceleration perpendicular to their velocity, as occurs when they move through a magnetic field. It is named after its discovery in a synchrotron particle accelerator.

Properties

Synchrotron light is emitted in a narrow cone in the direction of the particle's motion, is highly collimated, intensely bright, and spans a broad spectrum from infrared through visible light to hard X-rays. It is also highly polarised and emitted in short pulses reflecting the structure of the particle bunches in the ring.

Applications

Dedicated synchrotron light sources are used for X-ray crystallography of proteins, materials science, imaging, and spectroscopy. In astrophysics, synchrotron emission from electrons spiralling in cosmic magnetic fields is observed in supernova remnants, pulsar wind nebulae such as the Crab Nebula, and the jets of active galactic nuclei.

History

The radiation was first observed in 1947 at the General Electric synchrotron in Schenectady, New York, and its theory had been developed by Dmitry Ivanenko, Isaak Pomeranchuk, Julian Schwinger, and others in the 1940s.
