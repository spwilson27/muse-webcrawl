Kukri

The kukri (Nepali: खुकुरी, romanized: khukurī) is a type of short sword with a distinct recurve in its blade that originated in the Indian subcontinent. It serves multiple purposes as a melee weapon and as a regular cutting tool throughout most of Nepal. The kukri, along with the karda and chakmak, is the national weapon of Nepal, traditionally used by the Nepali people and famously by the Gurkhas.

Design

The kukri's blade is typically about 30 cm (12 in) long with a pronounced forward curve: the blade widens and bends inward toward the tip, concentrating weight toward the point and producing a chopping action comparable to a machete or axe. A notch, the cho or kaudi, is often cut near the handle, surrounded by varied interpretations of its purpose, from a religious symbol to a means of preventing blood from dripping onto the handle. The sheath (dap) usually houses two small accessory knives: the blunt chakmak, used for sharpening and striking sparks, and the small sharp karda, a general-purpose utility blade.

History and use

The kukri's origins are ancient, with curved blades of the type depicted in South Asian art for millennia. The weapon became iconic through the Gurkha regiments recruited by the British and Indian armies from the early 19th century, beginning with the Anglo-Nepalese War (1814–1816), in which British forces encountered it in combat. Gurkha soldiers continue to carry the kukri on active service, and it features in their regimental insignia.

Manufacture

Traditional kukris are hand-forged in Nepal by kami (blacksmith) castes, often from high-carbon steel such as recycled vehicle leaf springs, with handles of wood, horn, or bone. Ceremonial and presentation versions may be richly decorated with silver fittings and carved sheaths.
