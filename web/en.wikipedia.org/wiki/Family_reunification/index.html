Family reunification

Family reunification
FieldImmigration law, refugee law
Legal basisNational immigration acts; EU Directive 2003/86/EC; Refugee Convention principles

Family reunification (also called family reunion or family migration) is the process by which family members separated by migration — for example, where one member has emigrated, sought asylum, or been displaced — are enabled to join that person in the destination country. It is a major category of legal immigration in many countries and is widely recognised as connected to the right to family life protected by instruments such as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the European Convention on Human Rights.

Eligibility

Rules vary by jurisdiction but generally allow citizens and permanent residents (and, in many systems, recognised refugees) to sponsor close relatives: spouses and partners, minor children, and in some cases dependent parents or adult children. Sponsors typically must demonstrate adequate income and housing, and applicants undergo identity, security and health checks. Waiting times and quotas — especially for lower-preference categories in the United States, where U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents petition under the family-sponsored preference system — can extend to many years.

Refugees and subsidiary protection

For refugees, family reunification is often the only safe and legal route for relatives to join them, because family members may be unable to travel or to apply for visas from conflict zones. The 1951 Refugee Convention does not itself contain an express right, but UNHCR and the treaty's Final Act recommend that states facilitate the reunion of refugee families. The European Union's Family Reunification Directive (2003/86/EC) sets minimum standards for member states, with more favourable provisions for refugees.

Debates

Family reunification is politically contested. Supporters argue it promotes integration, stability and child welfare; critics argue that high volumes strain public services or that eligibility is abused, and policy debates often use the contested term "chain migration". Restrictions tend to be tightened or loosened with changes of government in major destination countries.
