Buddhist holidays

Buddhist holidays are festivals and observance days celebrated by Buddhists around the world, most of them based on the traditional lunisolar calendars of Asian countries, so their dates in the Gregorian calendar vary from year to year. The most important festivals commemorate events in the life of Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha): his birth, enlightenment and death (parinirvana).

Major festivals

  • Vesak (also called Buddha Purnima or Buddha Jayanti) — celebrates the birth, enlightenment and death of the Buddha, which according to tradition all occurred on the same full-moon day of the month of Vesakha. It is a public holiday in many Asian countries and was recognised by the United Nations in 1999.
  • Magha Puja — commemorates a spontaneous gathering of 1,250 enlightened monks before the Buddha, held on the full moon of the third lunar month in Thailand, Cambodia, Laos and Myanmar.
  • Asalha Puja (Dhamma Day) — commemorates the Buddha's first sermon, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, delivered at Sarnath.
  • Uposatha — observance days held on the full moon, new moon and quarter moons, when laypeople may take the eight precepts and visit temples.
  • Vassa (rains retreat) — a three-month monastic retreat during the rainy season, beginning with Vassa entry day and ending with Pavarana and the Kathina robe-offering ceremony.
  • Ullambana (Obon in Japan, Vu Lan in Vietnam) — a Mahayana festival honouring ancestors and relieving the suffering of hungry ghosts, based on the story of Maudgalyayana rescuing his mother.
  • Bodhi Day — celebrated in Japan (Rohatsu, 8 December) commemorating the Buddha's enlightenment.
  • Parinirvana Day — observed by Mahayana Buddhists to commemorate the Buddha's death.
  • Saga Dawa — the Tibetan festival month centred on the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and parinirvana.
  • Losar — the Tibetan New Year, also celebrated in Bhutan and by Himalayan communities.

Observance

On major holidays, lay Buddhists typically visit temples to offer food to monks, listen to sermons, meditate, release captive animals, and take part in processions and candlelit circumambulation (wian tian in Thailand). The timing and customs vary considerably between Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions and between countries.
