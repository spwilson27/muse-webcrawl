Achievement gap in the United States

The achievement gap in the United States refers to persistent disparities in educational performance between groups of students, most prominently between racial and ethnic groups and between students of different socioeconomic backgrounds. It is measured through standardised test scores, graduation rates, grade point averages and college enrolment and completion.

Patterns

On the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), white and Asian students have on average scored substantially higher than Black, Hispanic and Native American students in reading and mathematics, and students from higher-income families outperform those from low-income families. Gaps are already present at school entry and widen for some groups during schooling. The gaps are not fixed: they narrowed substantially in the 1970s and 1980s before stalling, and vary markedly between states and districts.

Causes debated

Research attributes the gap to combinations of family resources, early childhood conditions, school funding and quality, teacher experience and expectations, segregation, summer learning differences and broader structural inequality. Policy responses have included early-childhood programmes such as Head Start, standards-and-accountability reforms such as the No Child Left Behind Act (2001) and its successor the Every Student Succeeds Act (2015), which require disaggregated reporting of results by student subgroup.
