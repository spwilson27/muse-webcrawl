Indigenous land claims in Canada

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Indigenous land claims in Canada are legal and political claims by Indigenous peoples—First Nations, Inuit and Métis—to land and resources in Canada, based on long-standing use and occupancy and on rights recognised by the Royal Proclamation of 1763, treaties, and section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, which recognises and affirms the existing Aboriginal and treaty rights of the Indigenous peoples of Canada.

Types of claims

Comprehensive claims arise in areas where Indigenous title has not been extinguished by treaty or other legal means—most of British Columbia, the North, and parts of Quebec and Atlantic Canada. They are negotiated as modern treaties or land-claims agreements, such as the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement (1975), the Inuvialuit Final Agreement (1984), the Nunavut Agreement (1993), which led to the creation of Nunavut in 1999, and the Nisga'a Final Agreement (2000), the first modern treaty in British Columbia.

Specific claims allege that the Crown failed to fulfil its obligations under historic treaties or the Indian Act, for example by mismanaging reserve lands or trust funds. Since 2008 many specific claims may be brought before the Specific Claims Tribunal Canada.

Key jurisprudence

Land-claims law was transformed by Supreme Court decisions: Calder v. British Columbia (1973), which recognised that Aboriginal title exists in Canadian law; R v. Sparrow (1990) on the justification of infringements; Delgamuukw v. British Columbia (1997), which defined title and admitted oral history evidence; Haida Nation v. British Columbia (2004), which established the duty to consult; and Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia (2014), the first declaration of Aboriginal title, over roughly 1,750 km² in British Columbia.

Process and criticism

Comprehensive claims negotiations have been criticised for taking decades, costing claimant groups large sums borrowed against eventual settlements, and historically requiring the extinguishment or modification of title. Since 2015 the federal government has pursued reconciliation frameworks and rights-recognition tables, and in 2021 Canada legislated the implementation of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP).
