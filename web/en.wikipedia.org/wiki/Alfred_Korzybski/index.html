Alfred Habdank Skarbek Korzybski (3 July 1879 – 1 March 1950) was a Polish-American philosopher, engineer and independent scholar who developed the discipline of general semantics. His best-known work, Science and Sanity (1933), presented a system he called a non-Aristotelian logic, intended to improve human thinking and behaviour by making people aware of how language shapes their perceptions.

Life

Korzybski was born in Warsaw, then part of the Russian Empire, into an aristocratic Polish family of engineers and scholars. He studied chemical engineering and spoke several languages. During the First World War he served as an intelligence and artillery officer; after being wounded, he was sent to North America to coordinate the shipment of artillery to Russia, and he remained in the United States, becoming a citizen in 1940. His first book, Manhood of Humanity (1921), introduced the concept of humans as a "time-binding" class of life.

General semantics

General semantics emphasises that our knowledge of the world is limited by the structure of our nervous systems and our languages; we never deal directly with reality but only with abstractions of it. Korzybski's most famous dictum is "the map is not the territory" — a word is not the thing it represents. He devised practical aids such as the structural differential and techniques of "extensional devices" (indexing, dating, quotation marks) to train what he called consciousness of abstracting.

In 1938 he founded the Institute of General Semantics in Chicago. His ideas influenced fields as diverse as cognitive therapy, science fiction, and media studies, and attracted followers including William S. Burroughs and Robert A. Heinlein, though general semantics remained outside mainstream academic philosophy and psychology.
