Peisistratus (Greek: Πεισίστρατος; c. 600–527 BC) was a ruler of ancient Athens during most of the period between 561 and 527 BC. A brilliant military commander and shrewd politician, he seized power as tyrant three times—being twice exiled—before establishing a stable rule after the Battle of Pallene in 546 BC. Although a tyrant, he governed moderately, maintained the Solonian constitution in form, and is often judged to have laid foundations for later Athenian greatness.

Rule and policies

Peisistratus supported the poor farmers of Attica with loans, travelling judges and a modest land or produce tax, encouraging olive cultivation and pottery exports. He promoted Athens as a cultural centre, developing the Panathenaic festival, patronising the recitation of Homer's epics, and beginning major building projects including an early temple on the Acropolis and the Enneakrounos fountain house. He also secured Athenian interests abroad, keeping control of Sigeum and the Thracian Chersonese and purifying Delos.

Legacy

On his death in 527 BC he was succeeded by his sons Hippias and Hipparchus, whose harsher rule ended with Hipparchus's assassination in 514 BC and Hippias's expulsion in 510 BC—events that opened the way to Cleisthenes' democratic reforms.
