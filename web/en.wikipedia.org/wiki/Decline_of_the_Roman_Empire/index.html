Decline of the Roman Empire

Decline of the Roman Empire
Periodc. 3rd–5th centuries AD (West)
Key eventsCrisis of the Third Century; division of the Empire; sack of Rome 410 and 455; deposition of Romulus Augustulus, 476
Famous accountEdward Gibbon, The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire (1776–1789)

The decline of the Roman Empire refers to the long process by which the Roman state lost control of its western provinces during late antiquity, culminating in the collapse of imperial authority in the West in the fifth century AD, while the Eastern Empire — later called Byzantine — survived for nearly another thousand years. Historians have proposed hundreds of explanations, ranging from military pressure and economic crisis to disease, climate change, and internal political instability.

Chronology

The Crisis of the Third Century (235–284) saw civil war, plague, inflation, and invasion nearly destroy the Empire before Diocletian's reforms stabilised it as the Dominate. Constantine founded Constantinople in 330. The Empire was lastingly divided in 395 between the sons of Theodosius I. Rome was sacked by the Visigoths in 410 and by the Vandals in 455. In 476 the Germanic leader Odoacer deposed the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus — a date conventionally marking the Empire's fall in the West.

Explanations

  • Gibbon blamed the erosion of civic virtue and the rise of Christianity.
  • Military explanations stress barbarian migration and invasion, the cost of defending long frontiers, and reliance on foederati troops.
  • Economic explanations cite heavy taxation, currency debasement, slavery's decline, and the loss of tax-rich provinces such as North Africa to the Vandals.
  • Recent scholarship emphasises epidemic disease (the Antonine and Cyprian plagues), the Late Antique Little Ice Age, and the sheer contingency of civil wars and assassinations.

Transformation versus fall

Since Peter Brown's work on late antiquity, many historians prefer to speak of a "transformation of the Roman world" rather than a catastrophic fall, pointing to continuities in law, religion, and local life. Others, such as Bryan Ward-Perkins, argue from archaeological evidence that the collapse in material living standards was real and severe.
