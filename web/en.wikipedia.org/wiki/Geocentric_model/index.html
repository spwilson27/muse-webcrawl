In astronomy, the geocentric model (also known as geocentrism) is a superseded description of the universe with Earth at the centre, around which the Sun, Moon, planets, and stars revolve. It was the predominant cosmological system in the ancient and medieval worlds, reaching its most sophisticated form in the work of Claudius Ptolemy in the 2nd century AD.

The Ptolemaic system, from a 16th-century cosmological diagram.

The Ptolemaic system

Building on Aristotle's physics of nested celestial spheres and the geometry of Apollonius and Hipparchus, Ptolemy's Almagest modelled each planet as moving on a small circle (an epicycle) whose centre moved along a larger circle (the deferent) around a point near Earth. Devices such as the eccentric and the equant allowed the model to predict planetary positions — including retrograde motion — with impressive accuracy, and it served astronomy, astrology, and calendar-making for some 1,400 years in both the Islamic world and Christian Europe.

Decline

Nicolaus Copernicus published a heliocentric alternative in 1543 (De revolutionibus). Initially no more accurate, the Copernican system gained ground through Kepler's elliptical orbits (1609), Galileo's telescopic observations such as the phases of Venus (1610), and finally Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687), which explained the dynamics. Stellar aberration (1729) and stellar parallax (1838) provided direct observational evidence of Earth's motion.
