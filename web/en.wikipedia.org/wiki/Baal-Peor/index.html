Baal-Peor

Baal-Peor (Hebrew: בַּעַל פְּעוֹר, Baʿal Pəʿōr) is a term used in the Hebrew Bible to refer to a god worshipped at Mount Peor in Moab, identified as a local form of the Canaanite storm and fertility god Baal. The name means "Lord of Peor" or "Lord of the Opening".

Biblical account

The principal narrative concerning Baal-Peor appears in the Book of Numbers (chapter 25). While the Israelites were encamped at Shittim during their journey through the wilderness, Israelite men began to consort with Moabite women and to participate in sacrifices to their gods, bowing down to Baal of Peor. In response, a plague was sent among the Israelites, killing 24,000 people. The episode ends when Phinehas, grandson of Aaron, kills an Israelite man and a Midianite woman with a spear, after which the plague is halted.

Later references

The incident is recalled elsewhere in scripture, including Deuteronomy 4:3, Joshua 22:17, Hosea 9:10, and Psalm 106:28, where it serves as a warning against idolatry and intermarriage with foreign peoples. In later tradition, Baal-Peor was sometimes associated with Belphegor, one of the demons of medieval demonology, who came to be linked with sloth and licentiousness.

Interpretation

Scholars generally interpret Baal-Peor as a local manifestation of Baal tied to the mountain Peor in the land of Moab, east of the Jordan River. The narrative is often read as a polemic against foreign cultic practices and as part of the broader biblical theme of Israel's exclusive covenant loyalty.
