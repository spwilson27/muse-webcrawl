Nuremberg rallies

LocationNuremberg, Germany
Years active1923, 1925, 1927, 1929, 1933–1938
OrganiserNazi Party (NSDAP)
PurposePropaganda, party unity, militarism

The Nuremberg rallies (German: Reichsparteitage, literally "Reich Party Congresses") were a series of celebratory gatherings held by the Nazi Party in Germany. The first Nazi Party rallies took place in 1923 and 1925 in Munich and in 1926 in Weimar, but from 1927 they were held in Nuremberg, which became the permanent venue. The rallies were large Nazi propaganda events, especially after Adolf Hitler's rise to power in 1933, and typically attracted hundreds of thousands of party members, supporters and spectators. They were held each September for about a week and were intended as demonstrations of German national unity, loyalty to Hitler, and the growing strength of the Nazi movement.

History and purpose

Nuremberg was chosen because of its central location within Germany, its good rail connections, its large open spaces, and the strong local Nazi organisation under Julius Streicher. The rallies of 1933 to 1938 were staged on a monumental scale at the Nazi Party Rally Grounds, a vast complex of some 11 square kilometres in the south-east of the city designed largely by Albert Speer. Features included the Zeppelinfeld grandstand, the Congress Hall, the Luitpold Arena and the Great Road. Speer's famous "cathedral of light" (Lichtdom), formed by 152 anti-aircraft searchlights aimed skyward, became an enduring symbol of the rallies.

Each rally was given a programmatic name reflecting regime propaganda: the 1933 rally was the "Rally of Victory", the 1934 rally was officially the "Rally of Unity and Strength", the 1935 rally the "Rally of Freedom" (marking the reintroduction of conscription), and the 1938 rally, held shortly before the Munich Agreement, the "Rally of Greater Germany". The 1939 rally, named the "Rally of Peace", was cancelled at short notice when Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, beginning the Second World War.

Propaganda films

The rallies were extensively documented in Nazi propaganda films. Leni Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will (1935), filmed at the 1934 rally, is the most famous example and is widely regarded as a landmark of propaganda filmmaking. Other films include Der Sieg des Glaubens (1933) and Tag der Freiheit: Unsere Wehrmacht (1935). The 1935 rally was also the occasion for the announcement of the Nuremberg Laws, which stripped German Jews of citizenship and prohibited marriage and sexual relations between Jews and non-Jewish Germans.

Legacy

After the war, Nuremberg hosted the Nuremberg trials of major Nazi war criminals. The rally grounds survive in partial form and now include a documentation centre addressing the history of the rallies and the Nazi regime.
