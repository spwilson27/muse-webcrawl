The 73rd United States Congress was a meeting of the legislative branch of the United States federal government, composed of the Senate and the House of Representatives. It met in Washington, D.C., from March 4, 1933, to January 3, 1935, during the first two years of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency. Democrats held large majorities in both chambers following the 1932 elections held in the depths of the Great Depression.

The Hundred Days

Roosevelt called Congress into special session beginning March 9, 1933, launching the famous "First Hundred Days" of the New Deal. In that period Congress passed an extraordinary volume of legislation, including the Emergency Banking Act, the Economy Act, the Agricultural Adjustment Act, the Tennessee Valley Authority Act, the National Industrial Recovery Act, the Glass–Steagall Banking Act and the act creating the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. The session also sent the Twenty-first Amendment, repealing Prohibition, to the states.

Second session

The second session, in 1934, produced the Securities Exchange Act creating the SEC, the Communications Act creating the FCC, the National Housing Act and the Gold Reserve Act. The 73rd Congress is generally regarded as one of the most consequential in American history, fundamentally expanding the role of the federal government in the economy.
