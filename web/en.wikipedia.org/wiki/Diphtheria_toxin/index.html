Diphtheria toxin

Diphtheria toxin is an exotoxin secreted mainly by Corynebacterium diphtheriae, the bacterium that causes diphtheria. The toxin gene is carried on a prophage (a virus integrated into the bacterial chromosome), so only lysogenised strains produce it. It is a single polypeptide chain of 535 amino acids consisting of two subunits linked by disulfide bridges, known as an A-B toxin.

Mechanism

The B subunit binds to the heparin-binding epidermal growth factor precursor on susceptible cells, and the toxin is internalised by endocytosis. Acidification of the endosome allows the A subunit to enter the cytosol, where it catalyses the ADP-ribosylation of elongation factor EF-2 using NAD+. This inactivates EF-2 and halts protein synthesis, killing the cell. A single toxin molecule in the cytosol can be sufficient to kill a cell.

Clinical significance

The toxin is responsible for most of the systemic complications of diphtheria, including myocarditis and neuropathy, and for the formation of the characteristic pseudomembrane in the throat. Diphtheria toxoid — the toxin inactivated with formaldehyde — is the basis of diphtheria vaccines, widely given in combination preparations such as DTaP and Td. Diphtheria antitoxin, raised in horses, remains the specific treatment for severe disease.
