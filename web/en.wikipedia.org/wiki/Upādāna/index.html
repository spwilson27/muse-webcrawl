Upādāna is a Buddhist concept meaning "clinging" or "attachment" — literally, the taking up of fuel, as a fire takes up fuel to keep burning. It is the ninth link in the twelvefold chain of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda): conditioned by craving (taṇhā), clinging arises, and clinging in turn conditions becoming (bhava), birth, aging and death. Overcoming upādāna is central to liberation from saṃsāra.

Four kinds of clinging

The early texts analyse upādāna into four kinds: clinging to sensual pleasures (kāmupādāna), clinging to views (diṭṭhupādāna), clinging to rites and rituals as sufficient in themselves (sīlabbatupādāna), and clinging to a doctrine of self (attavādupādāna). Each represents a progressively deeper fixation by which beings sustain the illusion of a permanent self and perpetuate rebirth.

The fuel metaphor

In the Pali canon the word also means the material that sustains a fire. The Buddha used the image to explain how consciousness and rebirth are sustained without a transmigrating soul: just as a fire burns in dependence on fuel, a being continues in dependence on clinging; when the fuel of attachment is exhausted, as in the case of an arahant, there is "extinguishment" — nibbāna, itself a term drawn from the blowing-out of a flame.

Relation to the aggregates

The five aggregates of form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness are called the "aggregates subject to clinging" (pañcupādānakkhandhā), and the Buddha's summary of suffering in the First Noble Truth is that these five aggregates, when clung to, are dukkha.
