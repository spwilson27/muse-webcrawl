The Companions occupy a central place in Islamic memory and historiography.

The Sahabah (Arabic: الصحابة, "the companions") were the disciples and followers of the Islamic prophet Muhammad who "saw or met the Prophet during his lifetime, physically or heard the Prophet's speech during his lifetime and were believers in his message and died as Muslims". They number, by later estimates, well over 100,000, and include the first converts in Mecca, the Emigrants (Muhajirun) who joined the Hijrah to Medina in 622, and the Helpers (Ansar) of Medina who received them.

Role in Islam

The Companions are the primary transmitters of Muhammad's words and deeds: the hadith literature reaches back through chains of narration (isnad) that begin with a Companion eyewitness. The first four caliphs — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali — are drawn from their ranks, as are the ten promised Paradise (al-ʿashara al-mubashshara). Under the caliphs Umar and Uthman the Companions led the early Muslim conquests and the compilation of the Quran.

Views

Sunni Islam holds the Companions to be upright (ʿudūl) as a class and models of faith, citing Quranic praise of the "first forerunners" among the Muhajirun and Ansar. Shia Islam venerates those Companions who remained loyal to Ali and the Prophet's family, while regarding others critically for the succession dispute after Muhammad's death in 632 and the ensuing First Fitna, which divided the Companions themselves.
