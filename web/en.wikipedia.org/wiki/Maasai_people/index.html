The Maasai (Swahili: Wamasai) are an Eastern Nilotic ethnic group inhabiting northern, central and southern Kenya and northern Tanzania, near the African Great Lakes region. Their native language is Maasai (Maa), a member of the Nilotic language family related to the Dinka, Kalenjin and Nuer languages. Except for some elders living in rural areas, most Maasai people speak the official languages of Kenya and Tanzania: Swahili and English. They are famous internationally for their distinctive customs and dress, semi-nomadic pastoralism, and residence near many of East Africa's game parks.

History

Maasai oral tradition holds that they originated north of Lake Turkana in the lower Nile valley and migrated south during the 15th–18th centuries, expanding at the expense of other peoples and reaching their greatest extent in the 19th century, from northern Kenya to central Tanzania. Epidemics of rinderpest and smallpox, drought, and internal conflicts in the 1890s — the period Maasai call Emutai, "the disaster" — devastated their herds and population. Under British colonial rule, treaties of 1904 and 1911 moved the Kenyan Maasai from the central Rift Valley into southern reserves, a grievance that persists in modern land claims.

Culture and society

Traditional Maasai society is organised by an age-set system (moran being the celebrated warrior grade), with circumcision ceremonies marking the passage of boys to manhood and elders making communal decisions. Cattle are central to livelihood, diet (traditionally milk, meat and blood), wealth and ritual; the Maasai believe their god Enkai entrusted all cattle to them. Settlements (enkang) consist of houses built by women from mud, sticks, grass and dung, arranged around a thorn-fenced kraal. Distinctive dress includes red-checked shuka cloth, elaborate beadwork whose colours carry symbolic meanings, and the adumu "jumping dance" performed by moran.

Modern challenges

Government policies in Kenya and Tanzania have long pressed the Maasai toward sedentary agriculture and formal education, while land privatisation, subdivision of group ranches, wildlife conservation boundaries and tourism development have reduced their grazing land; disputes around the Maasai Mara conservancies and Tanzania's Loliondo and Ngorongoro areas have drawn international attention. The Maasai nonetheless remain active pastoralists who also participate in politics, business, and cultural tourism, and their imagery has become an emblem of Kenya and Tanzania worldwide.
