Gia Long

Gia Long (Vietnamese: [zaː lawŋ]; 8 February 1762 – 3 February 1820), born Nguyễn Phúc Ánh, often referred to simply as Nguyễn Ánh, was the founding emperor of the Nguyễn dynasty of Vietnam. After decades of civil war against the Tây Sơn dynasty, he unified the country in 1802 and founded the dynasty that ruled Vietnam until 1945. He established his capital at Huế, adopted the era name Gia Long — combining Gia Định (Saigon) and Thăng Long (Hanoi) to symbolise unity — and, with Chinese recognition, took the name Việt Nam for the state.

Struggle against the Tây Sơn

Nguyễn Ánh was the nephew of the last Nguyễn lord of the south. When the Tây Sơn rebellion swept away Nguyễn rule in 1777, his family was massacred and he survived as a fugitive teenager, repeatedly rallying forces in the Mekong Delta and losing them. He sought help from Siam and, through the French missionary bishop Pigneau de Béhaine, from France; the 1787 Treaty of Versailles promised French aid that never officially arrived, though French volunteers such as Olivier de Puymanel and Jean-Baptiste Chaigneau helped organise his army, navy and fortresses. After years of fighting, Nguyễn Ánh retook Saigon (1788), then advanced north: Qui Nhơn fell in 1799, Phú Xuân (Huế) in 1801, and Hanoi in 1802, completing the unification of Vietnam for the first time in centuries.

Reign

As emperor, Gia Long built the great Citadel of Huế with French-influenced Vauban-style fortifications, reorganised administration, embarked on road-building (the Mandarin Road), and promulgated the Gia Long Code (1815), a legal code modelled on that of Qing China. He rewarded his French advisers with mandarin rank but resisted granting France trade or territorial concessions, declined to persecute Catholics while discouraging the religion, and maintained tributary relations with the Qing while campaigning to extend influence over Cambodia and Laos.

Death and legacy

Gia Long died in 1820 and was buried in the Thiên Thọ mausoleum complex near Huế. He was succeeded by his fourth son, Minh Mạng. Assessments of Gia Long remain contested in Vietnam: officially long condemned as a reactionary who invited foreign intervention, he is also credited as the unifier of the modern Vietnamese state and the founder of its last dynasty.
