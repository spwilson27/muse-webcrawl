Leslie Alvin White (January 19, 1900 – March 31, 1975) was an American anthropologist known for his advocacy of the theory of cultural evolution, his studies of the Pueblo peoples of the American Southwest, and his efforts to establish anthropology as a science of culture, which he called culturology. White spent most of his academic career at the University of Michigan, where he built one of the most influential anthropology programs in the United States.

Early life and education

White was born in Salida, Colorado, and spent part of his early life on a ranch in Kansas. He served in the United States Navy during the final months of World War I. He studied at Louisiana State University and Columbia University before completing his doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1927, where he came under the influence of the Boasian tradition in anthropology—though he later became one of its sharpest critics.

Career

White taught at the University at Buffalo before joining the faculty of the University of Michigan in 1930, where he remained until his retirement in 1970. At Michigan he trained a generation of anthropologists and used his lecture platform to promote a materialist, evolutionary view of culture that ran against the dominant particularism of Franz Boas and his students.

Cultural evolution and culturology

In works such as The Science of Culture (1949), The Evolution of Culture (1959), and The Concept of Cultural Systems (1975), White argued that culture is a system in its own right and that its development is governed by the amount of energy a society harnesses per capita. He expressed this in his famous formula E × T → C, where an increase in energy (E) or in the efficiency of the technological means of putting energy to work (T) produces cultural development (C). White's revival of nineteenth-century evolutionism, stripped of its racial assumptions, helped inspire the later schools of cultural ecology and cultural materialism.
