Naram-Sin (Akkadian: Narām-Sîn, "beloved of Sin") was the fourth king of the Akkadian Empire, reigning in the 23rd century BC, traditionally dated c. 2254–2218 BC. The grandson of Sargon of Akkad, he presided over the empire at its greatest extent and was the first Mesopotamian king to claim divinity during his lifetime, styling himself "god of Akkad" and "king of the four quarters of the world".

Reign

Naram-Sin campaigned widely: he defeated the Lullubi of the Zagros, subdued Magan (Oman), fought in Syria and Anatolia, and crushed a great revolt of the Sumerian cities of the south led by Kish and Uruk. His victory over the Lullubi king Satuni is commemorated on the famous Victory Stele of Naram-Sin, a masterpiece of Akkadian art (now in the Louvre) showing the deified king, wearing a horned helmet, ascending a mountain above his defeated enemies.

Deification and legend

Naram-Sin's self-deification marked a turning point in Mesopotamian kingship; his name was thereafter written with the divine determinative. Later Babylonian tradition, however, remembered him ambivalently: the poem known as the Curse of Agade blamed the fall of Akkad on Naram-Sin's sacrilegious attack on the Ekur temple of Enlil at Nippur, which supposedly brought down the devastating invasion of the Gutians.

Legacy

Administrative reforms under Naram-Sin standardised weights, measures and accounting across the empire. His reign represents the apex of Akkadian power; after his death the empire weakened under his son Shar-Kali-Sharri and collapsed within decades.
