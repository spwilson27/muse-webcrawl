Ginzburg–Landau theory

Ginzburg–Landau theory is a mathematical theory of superconductivity proposed in 1950 by Vitaly Ginzburg and Lev Landau. Based on Landau's general theory of second-order phase transitions, it describes a superconductor near its critical temperature using a complex order parameter representing the density of superconducting electrons.

The theory

The theory expands the free energy in powers of the order parameter and its gradient. Minimizing this free energy yields the Ginzburg–Landau equations, which govern how the order parameter and magnetic field vary in space. Two characteristic lengths emerge: the coherence length and the penetration depth; their ratio, the Ginzburg–Landau parameter κ, distinguishes type-I from type-II superconductors.

Applications

Alexei Abrikosov used the theory to predict the vortex lattice state in type-II superconductors, work that later contributed to a Nobel Prize. Although phenomenological, the theory was later derived from the microscopic BCS theory by Lev Gor'kov, and it remains widely used in the study of superconductors and superfluids.
