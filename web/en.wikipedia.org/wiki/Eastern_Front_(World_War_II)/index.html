Eastern Front (World War II)

The Eastern Front was a theatre of World War II fought between the European Axis powers and co-belligerent Finland against the Soviet Union and its allies. It encompassed the largest military confrontation in history, running from Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union — Operation Barbarossa, launched on 22 June 1941 — to the Soviet capture of Berlin and the German surrender in May 1945. Some 8–10 million Soviet soldiers and perhaps 14–17 million Soviet civilians died (total Soviet losses are usually estimated at around 27 million), alongside some 5 million Axis military dead; the great majority of the Wehrmacht's combat losses of the war fell in the East. The front was also the principal arena of the Nazi war of annihilation: the Holocaust by bullets in the occupied territories, the siege starvation of Leningrad, the deliberate starvation and death of millions of Soviet prisoners of war, and scorched-earth devastation on a continental scale.

Operation Barbarossa (1941)

Germany invaded on 22 June 1941 with some three million Axis troops — the largest invasion force in history — striking for Leningrad, Moscow and Ukraine. The surprise was near-total: frontier armies were encircled in vast pockets at Białystok–Minsk, Smolensk, Kyiv (where over 600,000 Soviet soldiers were captured) and Vyazma; by December the Wehrmacht had taken millions of prisoners and stood at the gates of Moscow and around besieged Leningrad. But logistics, mud and then winter outran German planning, the Soviet government refused to crack, fresh Siberian divisions arrived after Stalin learned Japan would not attack, and on 5–6 December 1941 the Red Army counter-attacked before Moscow and drove the Germans back — the first great defeat of the Wehrmacht.

1942: Stalingrad

In 1942 Hitler struck south for the Caucasus oilfields and the Volga (Case Blue). The Sixth Army reached Stalingrad in August; the city became a byword for attritional horror as the armies fought street by street through the autumn. On 19 November 1942 the Red Army launched Operation Uranus, smashing the Romanian armies on the flanks and encircling some 300,000 Axis troops in the pocket. Field Marshal Paulus surrendered the remnants on 31 January–2 February 1943. Stalingrad — with perhaps two million casualties in the campaign — was the war's great turning point in the East, and Germany never regained the strategic initiative except briefly.

1943: Kursk

After Manstein's virtuoso recapture of Kharkov in March 1943, the front stabilised around a huge Soviet salient at Kursk. Hitler's Operation Citadel (July 1943) — the largest tank battle in history, including the clash at Prokhorovka — was ground down by prepared defences in depth; the Soviet counter-offensives that followed retook Oryol, Belgorod and Kharkov, crossed the Dnieper, and liberated Kyiv (6 November). By the end of 1943 the Red Army had recovered two-thirds of the occupied territory, and at the Tehran Conference Stalin could negotiate as a victor in the making.

1944: Bagration and the advance west

The siege of Leningrad was finally lifted in January 1944 after some 872 days. In June, three years to the day after Barbarossa, the Red Army launched Operation Bagration in Belarus: Army Group Centre — some 28 divisions — was annihilated in five weeks, and Soviet forces advanced to the Vistula and the borders of East Prussia, the greatest German defeat of the war. The Warsaw Uprising (August–October 1944) by the Polish Home Army was crushed by the Germans while the Red Army remained across the river — an episode still bitterly debated. In the Balkans, Romania and Bulgaria changed sides, Finland left the war, and by the end of 1944 Hungary was being fought over and Soviet troops stood in Yugoslavia and at Budapest.

1945: Berlin

The final offensives of January–April 1945 carried the Red Army across Poland (liberating Auschwitz on 27 January), through East Prussia and Silesia, and to the Oder. On 16 April the Berlin operation began with the fronts of Zhukov and Konev converging on the capital; the city fell on 2 May after savage fighting, Hitler having killed himself on 30 April. Soviet and American troops had met on the Elbe at Torgau on 25 April. Germany surrendered unconditionally at Reims on 7 May and again, at Soviet insistence, at Berlin-Karlshorst on 8/9 May — marked in Russia as Victory Day, 9 May. The political settlement of the front's outcome had meanwhile been argued out at Yalta and would be finished at Potsdam: an Eastern Europe within the Soviet sphere, the fact on which the Cold War was built.
