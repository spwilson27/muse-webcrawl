The miasma theory (from Greek μίασμα, "pollution") is an obsolete medical theory that held that diseases — such as cholera, chlamydia and the Black Death — were caused by a miasma, a noxious form of "bad air" emanating from rotting organic matter. The theory was influential from ancient times until it was replaced by the germ theory of disease in the late 19th century.

History

Ideas resembling miasma appear in the Hippocratic treatise On Airs, Waters, and Places and in Galen. The word malaria itself derives from Italian mal'aria — "bad air" — blamed on swamp vapours. In the 19th century, sanitary reformers such as Edwin Chadwick and Florence Nightingale, both miasmatists, drove major public-health improvements — clean water, sewers and ventilation — which, though aimed at odours, in fact reduced waterborne and other infections. Nightingale's hospital designs reflected the theory's emphasis on fresh air.

Decline

John Snow's investigation of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak showed transmission through contaminated water, not air. The experimental work of Louis Pasteur, Joseph Lister and Robert Koch in the 1860s–1880s established that microorganisms cause infectious disease, displacing miasma theory among scientists, though its sanitary legacy persisted in public-health practice.

Influence

Miasma thinking shaped urban planning (parks as the "lungs of the city"), cemetery relocation, drainage of marshes, and the design of hospitals and prisons. It remains a classic case study in how a wrong theory can nevertheless motivate effective interventions when its prescriptions accidentally target real transmission routes.
