Tintern Abbey (Welsh: Abaty Tyndyrn) is a ruined Cistercian abbey beside the village of Tintern in Monmouthshire, Wales, on the Welsh bank of the River Wye. Founded in 1131 by Walter de Clare, it was the first Cistercian house in Wales and only the second in Britain (after Waverley Abbey). Its surviving remains, dominated by the great Gothic church built between 1275 and 1301, are among the most celebrated monastic ruins in Britain.

History

The monks came from L'Aumône in France, a daughter house of Cîteaux. The community prospered on its estates in the Wye Valley and beyond, building first in simple Romanesque style. The present church was begun under the patronage of Roger Bigod III, Earl of Norfolk, and is a fine example of Decorated Gothic, with a seven-light west window and elevated stone vaulting over the choir and transepts. In 1402 the abbey suffered in the revolt of Owain Glyndŵr. At the Dissolution of the Monasteries, Abbot Wiche surrendered the abbey to Henry VIII's commissioners on 3 September 1536; the lead was stripped from the roofs and the buildings fell into ruin.

The picturesque ruin

From the 18th century, Tintern became a celebrated destination of picturesque tourism on the Wye Tour, admired by artists and poets. J. M. W. Turner painted it, and William Wordsworth, visiting in July 1798, composed "Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey" — one of his most famous poems, reflecting on memory and nature. The site passed into state care in 1914 and is now managed by Cadw, the Welsh historic environment service. The roofless church, cloister, chapter house, refectory, and monks' infirmary survive in a remarkably complete plan.
