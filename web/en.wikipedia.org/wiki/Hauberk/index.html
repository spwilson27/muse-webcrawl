Hauberk

A hauberk or byrnie is a shirt of mail (chainmail) armour, generally extending to the mid-thigh or knees and including sleeves. The word derives from the Old French hauberc, from a Frankish word meaning "neck protection" (halsberg). The hauberk was the defining body defence of the early and high medieval knight, worn from the early Middle Ages until plate armour superseded it in the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Construction

A hauberk was constructed from thousands of interlinked metal rings — a full garment could contain 20,000 to 30,000 rings and weigh 11 to 14 kilograms (25–30 lb), with the weight carried largely on the hips by a belt. Rings were usually riveted closed for strength. The garment was put on over a padded under-layer (a gambeson or aketon) that absorbed blows and prevented chafing. Many hauberks included an integral hood (coif) and mittens, and the skirt was often split front and back to allow riding.

History

Mail armour predates the hauberk by many centuries — the Celts are credited with inventing mail, and Roman soldiers wore the lorica hamata. After Rome's fall, mail remained the premier armour of early medieval elites; the Frankish and Carolingian worlds prized the long mail shirt known in Old English as the byrnie. The Norman knights depicted on the Bayeux Tapestry wear knee-length hauberks with elbow-length sleeves. Over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries sleeves lengthened to the wrist and mail leggings (chausses) were added. As plate defences developed, the hauberk shrank to the shorter haubergeon, worn beneath plate, before mail retreated to protecting joints and gaps.

Variants

The shorter, lighter version was called a haubergeon ("little hauberk"), reaching only to the waist or hips. In Japan, an analogous mail shirt was the kusari katabira.
