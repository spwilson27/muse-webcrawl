Lagrange's theorem (group theory)


A group partitioned into cosets of a subgroup

In group theory, Lagrange's theorem states that for any finite group G, the order (number of elements) of every subgroup H of G divides the order of G. The theorem is named after Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who encountered a special case of the result in his work on permutation groups and polynomial equations in 1770–71, though the general statement and proof came later with the development of abstract group theory.

Proof

The proof uses the left cosets of H in G. Each coset aH has exactly the same number of elements as H, because multiplication by a is a bijection. The cosets partition G, so the order of G equals the number of cosets (the index [G : H]) multiplied by the order of H; hence the order of H divides the order of G.

Corollaries

Important consequences include: the order of any element of a finite group divides the order of the group; every group of prime order is cyclic; and Fermat's little theorem follows by applying the result to the multiplicative group of integers modulo a prime. The converse of Lagrange's theorem is false in general — a group may lack subgroups of some order dividing its own — though partial converses hold, such as the Sylow theorems and Cauchy's theorem.
