In German orthography, the letter ß, called Eszett or scharfes S ("sharp S"), represents the /s/ phoneme in Standard German when following long vowels and diphthongs. It originated as a ligature of the long s (ſ) and the s or z of blackletter typefaces. The character's Unicode names are "LATIN SMALL LETTER SHARP S" and, for its capital form, "LATIN CAPITAL LETTER SHARP S".

Origin

The ß emerged in medieval manuscripts and early printing from ligatures combining ſ with s or z in blackletter (Fraktur) type. By the early modern period it had become a standard character of German typesetting, distinct from the ss combination used in roman (Antiqua) type.

Usage

ß is used after long vowels and diphthongs: Straße ("street"), groß ("large"), weiß ("white"). It never begins a word. Swiss Standard German abolished the letter entirely in the 20th century, replacing it everywhere with ss, while Germany and Austria retain it.

The 1996 spelling reform

Before the German spelling reform of 1996, ß was also used after short vowels in some positions (for example at the end of a syllable, as in daß). The reform restricted ß to use after long vowels and diphthongs, so daß became dass, while Straße remained unchanged.

Capital form

Because ß never starts a word, historically it had no capital; in all-caps text it was written SS (or SZ). A capital ẞ existed in some typefaces and was included in Unicode in 2008; in 2017 the Council for German Orthography officially admitted ẞ as an alternative to SS for capitalised writing.
