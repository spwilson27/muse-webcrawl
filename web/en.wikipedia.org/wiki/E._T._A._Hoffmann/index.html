Ernst Theodor Amadeus Hoffmann (born Ernst Theodor Wilhelm Hoffmann; 24 January 1776 – 25 June 1822) was a German Romantic author of fantasy and Gothic horror, a jurist, composer, music critic and artist. He changed his third name from Wilhelm to Amadeus in homage to Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. His stories form the basis of Jacques Offenbach's opera The Tales of Hoffmann, in which Hoffmann appears as the protagonist.

Hoffmann's tales, which mingle the everyday world with the supernatural and uncanny, profoundly influenced later literature. "The Sandman" (1816) became central to Freud's essay on the uncanny, and "The Nutcracker and the Mouse King" (1816) inspired Tchaikovsky's ballet The Nutcracker. His novel The Devil's Elixirs (1815–16) and the satirical Life and Opinions of the Tomcat Murr (1819–21) are regarded as masterpieces of German Romantic fiction.

Life

Born in Königsberg (now Kaliningrad), Hoffmann studied law at the University of Königsberg and worked as a Prussian civil servant and judge in Posen, Warsaw and Berlin. Alongside his legal career he pursued music intensely, composing operas, symphonies and chamber music and writing influential music criticism, including a celebrated review of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony. His opera Undine premiered in Berlin in 1816. Financial insecurity, heavy drinking and ill health marked his later years; he died in Berlin at the age of 46.

Works and influence

Hoffmann's fiction pioneered the modern fantastic and horror genres and influenced Edgar Allan Poe, Nikolai Gogol, Charles Dickens, Fyodor Dostoevsky and Franz Kafka, among many others. His Kreisleriana essays introduced the tortured Kapellmeister Johannes Kreisler, a figure later used by Robert Schumann. The operetta and opera adaptations of his stories — including Delibes' Coppélia, based on "The Sandman" — kept his work before wide audiences throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
