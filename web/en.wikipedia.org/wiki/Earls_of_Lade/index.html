The Earls of Lade (Old Norse: Hlaðajarlar; Norwegian: Ladejarlene) were a dynasty of Norse rulers of the Trøndelag region of Norway from their seat at Lade, near modern Trondheim, during the late ninth to the eleventh centuries. For much of that period they were the principal rivals — and at times the de facto rulers on behalf of foreign kings — of the Fairhair dynasty of Norwegian kings.

Origins

The dynasty's founder, Hákon Grjótgarðsson, allied himself with Harald Fairhair in the unification of Norway and was made earl of the Trøndelag. His son Sigurðr Hákonarson was a friend and adviser of King Hákon the Good but was killed by the sons of Eiríkr Bloodaxe. Sigurðr's son, Hákon Sigurðarson (Hákon jarl), became the effective ruler of Norway from about 975 to 995, governing as a vassal of the Danish king Harald Bluetooth while resisting Christianisation and defending the old Norse religion. He was killed by his own thrall, Kark, during the rising that brought Óláfr Tryggvason to power.

Later earls

Hákon's sons, Eiríkr Hákonarson and Sveinn Hákonarson, recovered power after the Battle of Svolder (1000), ruling Norway as earls under Danish and Swedish overlordship. Eiríkr later governed Northumbria for Cnut the Great in England. The last of the line, Hákon Eiríksson, died at sea around 1029–1030, and with his death the power of the Lade earls ended; Trøndelag passed under direct royal control as Norway was consolidated under Saint Óláfr and his successors.

Legacy

The earls are major figures in the Icelandic sagas, particularly Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla, which portrays them as defenders of the old order in Trøndelag against the Christianising kings. Their farm at Lade, at the mouth of the Nidelva river, gave the dynasty its name — Hlaðir means "loading places" or "quays".
