In astrophysics, the silicon-burning process is the last in a sequence of stages of nuclear fusion that occur in the cores of massive stars (at least 8–11 solar masses). It follows the carbon-, neon-, and oxygen-burning processes and precedes the collapse of the star's core and its explosion as a core-collapse supernova. Silicon burning creates the iron-peak elements — chiefly nickel-56 — that make up the innermost core of the star.

The layered "onion skin" structure of an evolved massive star, with the silicon-burning shell surrounding the iron core.

Process

Silicon burning begins when the core of the star contracts and heats to roughly 2.7 billion kelvin. At these temperatures, photons are energetic enough to photodisintegrate silicon-28 nuclei into lighter particles — protons, neutrons, and alpha particles. These particles are then recaptured by other nuclei, gradually building up heavier elements in a state of quasi-statistical equilibrium.

Unlike the earlier burning stages, silicon burning is not a simple two-body fusion reaction. Instead, a network of photodisintegration and particle-capture reactions rearranges the material toward the most tightly bound nuclei near iron and nickel. The dominant product is nickel-56, which later decays through cobalt-56 to iron-56.

Timescale and core collapse

Silicon burning is extremely brief compared with earlier stages: a star may burn hydrogen for millions of years but consumes its silicon in roughly a single day. Once an inert iron-nickel core has formed and grown to approach the Chandrasekhar limit (about 1.4 solar masses), fusion can no longer support the star, because fusing iron-peak nuclei absorbs rather than releases energy. The core then collapses in less than a second, producing a core-collapse supernova and leaving behind a neutron star or black hole.
