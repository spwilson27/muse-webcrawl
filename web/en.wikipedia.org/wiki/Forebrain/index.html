Forebrain

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The forebrain or prosencephalon is the rostral (forward-most) division of the vertebrate brain. In humans it includes the cerebrum — by far the largest part of the brain — together with the thalamus, hypothalamus and related structures. It is responsible for most higher functions: perception, cognition, voluntary movement, emotion and homeostatic regulation.

Development

The embryonic brain begins as three swellings of the neural tube: forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. The forebrain divides into the telencephalon, which becomes the cerebral hemispheres (cortex, white matter and basal ganglia), and the diencephalon, which becomes the thalamus, hypothalamus, epithalamus and subthalamus. In mammals the telencephalon expands enormously, folding into gyri and sulci to fit the skull.

Structure and function

The cerebral cortex processes sensory information, plans and executes voluntary movement, and supports language, memory and consciousness. The basal ganglia help select and initiate actions and are implicated in habit learning; the limbic structures of the medial forebrain (including the amygdala and hippocampus) underlie emotion and memory formation. The thalamus relays nearly all sensory input to the cortex, while the hypothalamus regulates hunger, thirst, temperature, circadian rhythms and — via the pituitary gland — the endocrine system, linking the forebrain to hormone systems such as the release of follicle-stimulating hormone.
