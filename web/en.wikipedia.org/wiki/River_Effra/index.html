River Effra

River Effra
LocationSouth London, England
SourceUpper Norwood
MouthRiver Thames at Vauxhall
LengthApprox. 3 mi (4.8 km)
StatusLargely subterranean (culverted)

The River Effra is a former above-ground river in south London, England, now largely flowing underground as part of the city's storm-relief sewer system. It rises in Upper Norwood and flows roughly northwards through Dulwich, Herne Hill, and Brixton, before entering the River Thames at Vauxhall. Although almost entirely hidden, its valley still shapes the local street pattern, and its name survives in Effra Road, Brixton.

Course

The Effra's sources were streams on the high ground around Crystal Palace and Upper Norwood. From there the river descended through West Dulwich and Herne Hill, then ran along the line of present-day Brixton, where it was joined by tributary streams from Tulse Hill and Streatham. North of Brixton it passed Kennington and Oval, reaching the Thames at Vauxhall, near the modern Vauxhall Bridge. The river's mouth was altered in the 19th century when it was diverted to feed the culvert known as the Effra sewer; storm discharges still enter the Thames beside Vauxhall in heavy rain.

History

In the Middle Ages the Effra flowed through marshy farmland and powered at least one mill. As London expanded southwards in the 19th century, the river—open, polluted, and increasingly a health hazard—was progressively culverted and incorporated into Joseph Bazalgette's metropolitan sewer network. A well-known local tale claims that a coffin was once floated down the Effra from West Norwood Cemetery to the Thames after the burial plot was flooded; the story is generally regarded as folklore. Another tradition holds that Queen Elizabeth I sailed a barge up the Effra to visit Sir Walter Raleigh in Brixton, which historians also treat as legend.

The Effra's valley still floods occasionally, notably in Herne Hill and Dulwich in 2004 and 2013, and campaigners have periodically proposed "daylighting" stretches of the river. A small above-ground remnant and commemorative plaques mark its route, and the river-grained local topography—visible in the dip of Herne Hill and the slope of Brixton Hill—records its presence.

Etymology

The name Effra is uncertain in origin. It was once thought to derive from a Celtic root meaning "torrent", but modern scholarship suggests it may come from a manorial name, appearing in documents as the "Heffra" and in connection with Brixton's history; the name is recorded only from the later medieval period.
