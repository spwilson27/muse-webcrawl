Liberated Africans in Sierra Leone

Liberated Africans in Sierra Leone were enslaved Africans freed by the British Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron and by colonial courts after the British abolition of the slave trade in 1807. Between 1808 and the 1860s, roughly 85,000–100,000 Africans were landed at Freetown, Sierra Leone, after being taken from captured slave ships. They are sometimes known as "Recaptives". Together with earlier settler groups — the Nova Scotian Settlers and the Jamaican Maroons — the Liberated Africans and their descendants formed the Sierra Leone Creole (Krio) people.

Background

Following the Slave Trade Act 1807, British cruisers patrolled the West African coast to suppress the trade. Captured slave ships were taken to Freetown, where a Vice-Admiralty Court — and later a Court of Mixed Commission — condemned the vessels and formally emancipated the Africans found aboard.

Settlement and society

On arrival, Liberated Africans were registered, given access to medical care, and settled in villages around the Sierra Leone peninsula, such as Regent, Gloucester, Leicester, Wilberforce, and Waterloo. Many were apprenticed to settlers or enlisted in the Royal African Corps and the West India Regiments. The largest ethnic groups among them were Yoruba (known locally as Aku), Igbo, Efik, and Akan speakers, drawn from across West and Central Africa. Under the governorship of Charles MacCarthy, parish organisation and missionary activity — particularly by the Church Missionary Society — promoted Christian education; Fourah Bay College, founded in 1827, served this population.

Legacy

The Liberated Africans profoundly shaped Sierra Leonean society. The mixing of peoples and their exposure to Western education produced the Krio language and culture, and recaptives and their descendants became prominent as traders, teachers, clergy, and professionals throughout British West Africa. Some Liberated Africans, notably Yoruba speakers, later returned to their homelands in present-day Nigeria, where returnees known as Saro formed influential communities in Lagos and Abeokuta.
