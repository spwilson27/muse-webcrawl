Monoamine transporter

Monoamine transporters (MATs) are protein structures that function as integral plasma-membrane transporters to regulate concentrations of extracellular monoamine neurotransmitters. The three major classes are the serotonin transporter (SERT), the dopamine transporter (DAT), and the norepinephrine transporter (NET). They are responsible for the reuptake of their associated amine neurotransmitters — serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine, respectively — from the synaptic cleft back into the presynaptic neuron, terminating synaptic transmission.

Function and mechanism

Monoamine transporters belong to the solute carrier 6 (SLC6) family of Na⁺/Cl⁻-dependent transporters. They exploit sodium and chloride ion gradients to move neurotransmitter molecules against their concentration gradient, in a transport cycle alternating between outward-facing and inward-facing conformations. A related protein, the vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT, SLC18 family), packages monoamines into synaptic vesicles inside the neuron and uses a proton gradient instead.

Clinical significance

Monoamine transporters are the principal targets of many psychiatric and recreational drugs. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) block SERT and are widely prescribed for depression and anxiety disorders; serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) block both SERT and NET. Tricyclic antidepressants, the ADHD drug atomoxetine (a NET inhibitor), and stimulants such as methylphenidate (DAT and NET) also act at these proteins. Cocaine blocks all three transporters, while amphetamines are transporter substrates that reverse transport and deplete vesicular stores, producing powerful stimulant effects.

Structure

The transporters share a common fold of twelve transmembrane helices, first revealed by the bacterial leucine transporter LeuT and later by crystal and cryo-EM structures of Drosophila and human transporters, which have guided drug design.
