Judaea (Latin: Iudaea; Greek: Ἰουδαία) was a Roman province in the Levant, formed in 6 CE when the former Herodian tetrarchy of Judea was annexed directly under Roman rule after the deposition of Herod Archelaus. It encompassed the regions of Judea, Samaria, and Idumea, and was administered first by prefects and later by procurators subordinate to the governor of Syria.

History

After the death of Herod the Great in 4 BCE, Augustus divided his kingdom among his sons. Archelaus ruled Judea as ethnarch but was deposed in 6 CE following complaints from his subjects. Judaea then became a province of equestrian rank, governed from Caesarea Maritima, with a census conducted by Quirinius that provoked unrest led by Judas the Galilean.

The province was the setting of the ministry of Jesus and the early Christian movement, as well as growing tensions between Jewish inhabitants and Roman authorities. These culminated in the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 CE), ending with the destruction of the Second Temple in Jerusalem in 70 CE and the fall of Masada. A second revolt under Bar Kokhba (132–135 CE) led Hadrian to merge Judaea into the larger province of Syria Palaestina and to rebuild Jerusalem as the pagan colony Aelia Capitolina.

Administration

Governors held the title praefectus before 41 CE — Pontius Pilate (26–36 CE) is the best known — then procurator. Legionary forces were stationed after 70 CE, notably Legio X Fretensis in Jerusalem. The province paid tribute to Rome and used Roman law alongside Jewish law administered by the Sanhedrin for internal matters, though capital punishment was reserved.

Legacy

Judaea gives its name to Judaism and to the historical region of Judea. Its history is a primary source for understanding Roman provincial governance, Second Temple Judaism, and the origins of Christianity and rabbinic Judaism.
