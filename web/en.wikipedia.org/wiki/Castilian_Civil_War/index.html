The Castilian Civil War was a war of succession over the Crown of Castile fought between 1351 and 1369, or in its decisive phase between 1366 and 1369. It pitted supporters of King Peter I — known to his enemies as Peter the Cruel and to his defenders as Peter the Just — against those of his illegitimate half-brother, Henry of Trastámara (the future Henry II). The war drew in foreign powers and became a theatre of the Hundred Years' War between England and France.

Background

Peter I's favouritism toward his mistress María de Padilla and her family, his conflicts with the high nobility, and tensions over taxation and royal authority fuelled noble opposition. Henry of Trastámara, an illegitimate son of Alfonso XI, gathered support from discontented nobles and from France, whose king sought to place a friendly, pro-French ruler on the Castilian throne and to win Castile's powerful fleet for the French cause.

Course of the war

In 1366 Henry invaded with a mercenary army — the "Free Companies" led in part by Bertrand du Guesclin — and was proclaimed king at Burgos, forcing Peter to flee. Peter secured English support through the Treaty of Libourne with Edward, the Black Prince. In April 1367 the Anglo-Castilian army won the Battle of Nájera, capturing du Guesclin and restoring Peter, but Peter's failure to pay his allies and the Black Prince's illness and withdrawal reversed his position. Henry returned with renewed French backing and defeated and killed Peter in March 1369 at the Battle of Montiel, traditionally said to have stabbed his half-brother personally with du Guesclin's help.

Aftermath

Henry II founded the House of Trastámara, rewarding his supporters with extensive grants (the mercedes enriqueñas). The war shifted Castile firmly into the French alliance, and Castilian fleets later harried English shipping, contributing to the naval Battle of La Rochelle (1372). The conflict also witnessed violent attacks on Jewish communities amid the chaos of the war.
