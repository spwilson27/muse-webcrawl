Theatre of the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The theatre of the United States encompasses stage entertainment in the country from colonial times to the present. American theatre began in the colonial era with performances by professional companies imported from Britain; the first permanent theatre in the colonies opened in Williamsburg, Virginia, in 1716. After independence, American companies and playwrights gradually developed a national repertoire alongside touring stars, melodrama, minstrel shows, vaudeville, and spectacular productions.

Early theatre

The Hallam family's London Company of Comedians arrived in Virginia in 1752 and became the leading professional troupe of the colonies. Anti-theatrical prejudice, especially in Puritan New England, slowed development, and Congress discouraged theatre during the Revolutionary War, but playhouses multiplied in the 1790s in Philadelphia, New York, Boston, and Charleston. Royall Tyler's The Contrast (1787) was an early successful American comedy, and William Dunlap, actor-manager and author of more than 60 plays, is often called the "father of American drama".

Nineteenth century

The 19th century was dominated by actor-managers and touring stars such as Edwin Forrest, Charlotte Cushman, and Edwin Booth, by melodrama and adaptations of novels such as Uncle Tom's Cabin, and by popular entertainments including blackface minstrelsy — a hugely popular but racist form that shaped American popular culture. Long-running companies gave way to touring "combination" companies organized from New York, and by 1896 the Theatrical Syndicate controlled most major bookings.

Twentieth century and Broadway

The Little Theatre movement, the Provincetown Players (who launched Eugene O'Neill), and the Group Theatre of the 1930s raised American drama's artistic ambitions. O'Neill, Tennessee Williams, Arthur Miller, and Edward Albee achieved international stature, while Broadway became the commercial center of American theatre, particularly for the musical, developed by figures such as Rodgers and Hammerstein and Stephen Sondheim. From the 1960s, the regional theatre movement created major non-profit companies across the country, and Off-Broadway and Off-Off-Broadway nurtured experimental work by playwrights including Sam Shepard, August Wilson, and Tony Kushner.
