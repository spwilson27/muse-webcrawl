Reasonable suspicion

Reasonable suspicion is a legal standard of proof in United States law that is less demanding than probable cause, the standard for arrests and search warrants, but more demanding than an inchoate "hunch". It requires that an officer have specific and articulable facts, together with rational inferences from those facts, that reasonably suggest criminal activity has occurred or is imminent. The standard is an objective one, assessed under the totality of the circumstances.

Origin: Terry v. Ohio

The doctrine derives from Terry v. Ohio (1968), in which Chief Justice Earl Warren's opinion upheld a police officer's brief investigatory stop of men he reasonably suspected of casing a store for a robbery, and a protective pat-down ("frisk") of their outer clothing for weapons, justified by reasonable suspicion that they were armed. The resulting encounters are known as Terry stops. Because a Terry stop is a "seizure" and a frisk a "search" within the Fourth Amendment, both must be justified at inception and reasonably related in scope to the justification.

Development

Later decisions elaborated the standard. In United States v. Sokolow (1989) the Court confirmed that reasonable suspicion is a considerably lower threshold than probable cause and may rest on a combination of factors each innocent in itself. In Alabama v. White (1990) an anonymous tip corroborated as to future behaviour supplied reasonable suspicion for a vehicle stop, applying the flexible approach of Illinois v. Gates. Illinois v. Wardlow (2000) held that headlong flight in a high-crime area may contribute to reasonable suspicion. Navarette v. California (2014) sustained a stop based on a 911 call reporting dangerous driving. Traffic stops require at least reasonable suspicion of a violation (Whren v. United States upheld stops supported by probable cause regardless of motive), and their duration may not exceed the time reasonably required to complete the stop's mission (Rodriguez v. United States, 2015).

Related contexts

Reasonable suspicion also figures in other settings: vehicle stops at the international border (United States v. Brignoni-Ponce), searches by public school officials under the related "reasonable grounds" standard of New Jersey v. T.L.O. (1985), and stops by probation and parole officers. It contrasts with the preponderance standard used in civil trials and with the probable-cause standard required for an arrest.
