Public policy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public policy is an institutionalised proposal or a decided set of elements, such as laws, regulations, guidelines and actions, to solve or address relevant and real-world problems, guided by a conception and often implemented by programs. It is made by governments on behalf of the public, shaped by political parties, civil servants, interest groups, experts and citizens, and studied in political science, economics and dedicated policy schools.

The policy process

Classical models describe stages — agenda setting, formulation, adoption, implementation, evaluation and feedback — though real policy-making is messier, as theories of bounded rationality, incrementalism (Lindblom's "muddling through"), punctuated equilibrium and policy networks describe. Policy instruments include law and regulation, taxation and spending, information and nudges, public provision and markets; choice among them is ideological as well as technical. Policy analysis evaluates options and outcomes (cost–benefit, cost-effectiveness, distributional analysis), feeding "evidence-based policy" — itself subject to debate about evidence hierarchies and the politics of expertise.

Fields

The field subdivides by domain: health, education, environment and climate, social security, housing, transport, foreign and defence policy — each with its own institutions and literatures. Implementation research since Pressman and Wildavsky (1973) stresses the gap between policy as written and policy as delivered by street-level bureaucrats.
