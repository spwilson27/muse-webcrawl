Treaty 7

Signed22 September 1877
LocationBlackfoot Crossing, present-day Alberta
PartiesThe Crown; Blackfoot Confederacy, Tsuut'ina, Stoney Nakoda and others
TypeNumbered Treaty

Treaty 7 is an agreement between the Crown and several First Nations peoples in what is now southern Alberta, signed on 22 September 1877 at Blackfoot Crossing on the Bow River. It is one of the numbered treaties negotiated by the Canadian government with Indigenous peoples of the Prairies between 1871 and 1921.

Parties and terms

The First Nations signatories included the peoples of the Blackfoot Confederacy — the Siksika, Kainai (Blood) and Piikani — together with the Tsuut'ina (Sarcee) and the Stoney Nakoda. Under the written terms, the First Nations ceded a large territory, roughly 130,000 square kilometres between the Red Deer River and the United States border, in exchange for reserve lands calculated at one square mile for each family of five, annual payments, ammunition and twine, cattle, and agricultural assistance. A further adhesion was signed by additional chiefs at Fort Macleod in December 1877.

Interpretation and legacy

The treaty's meaning has long been disputed. Indigenous oral traditions maintain that the nations understood the agreement as one of peace and land-sharing, not as an outright surrender of the land, and differences in translation and in the parties' understanding of the negotiations — conducted through interpreters in a setting of declining buffalo herds and smallpox — remain central to modern scholarship and litigation. The Treaty 7 region is home to five First Nations today, and the treaty continues to ground their rights and their relationship with the Crown; its anniversary is commemorated annually at Blackfoot Crossing Historical Park.
