Organic Rankine cycle

The organic Rankine cycle (ORC) is a thermodynamic cycle in which a high-molecular-mass organic fluid with a liquid–vapour phase change at lower temperature than water is used instead of steam as the working fluid. The fluid allows Rankine cycle heat recovery from lower-temperature sources such as biomass combustion, industrial waste heat, geothermal heat, and solar ponds.

Working principle

The ORC follows the same four steps as the steam Rankine cycle: the working fluid is pumped to high pressure, evaporated (and often superheated) in a boiler heated by the external source, expanded through a turbine driving a generator, and condensed back to liquid. Because organic fluids boil at lower temperatures, useful power can be extracted from heat sources of 70–300 °C that could not efficiently raise steam. Many organic fluids are "dry" fluids whose saturated vapour curve has a positive slope, so the vapour remains superheated during expansion and no droplet erosion of turbine blades occurs.

Fluid selection

Choosing the working fluid is central to ORC design: the fluid's critical temperature should suit the heat-source temperature, and its thermal stability, toxicity, flammability, environmental impact (ozone depletion and global warming potential), and cost all matter. Common choices include hydrocarbons such as n-pentane and isopentane, aromatic toluene, siloxanes such as octamethyltrisiloxane (MDM), and fluorinated refrigerants.

Applications

ORC plants are widespread in geothermal power (where they are often called binary cycle plants), biomass combined heat and power, industrial waste-heat recovery in cement, steel and glass plants, and engine exhaust recovery. Commercial suppliers include Turboden, Ormat, and Exergy, with unit sizes from a few kilowatts to tens of megawatts.
