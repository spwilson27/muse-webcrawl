Alfred Marshall (26 July 1842 – 13 July 1924) was an English economist, founder of the Cambridge school and a central figure in neoclassical economics. His Principles of Economics (1890) was the dominant textbook of the English-speaking world for decades.

Contributions

Marshall systematised the Supply and demand analysis of price — his famous analogy compares the two curves to the blades of a scissors — and introduced or formalised elasticity of demand, consumer surplus, the distinction between short and long runs, quasi-rent, and the representative firm. His partial-equilibrium method analysed one market at a time, holding other things equal (ceteris paribus), and he insisted on the continuity of economic doctrine — natura non facit saltum ("nature does not leap") was the motto on his title page. Through his teaching he shaped John Maynard Keynes (his student) and A. C. Pigou, and professionalised economics as a university subject at Cambridge.
