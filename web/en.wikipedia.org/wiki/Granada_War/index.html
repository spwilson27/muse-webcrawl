Granada War

The Granada War (Spanish: Guerra de Granada) was a series of campaigns between 1482 and 1491 by the Catholic Monarchs, Isabella I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon, against the Emirate of Granada, the last Muslim state in the Iberian Peninsula. It ended with the capitulation of Granada on 2 January 1492, completing the Christian Reconquista and, in the same year as Columbus's first voyage and the expulsion of the Jews, marking a defining moment in the formation of Spain.

Course of the war

War began after the Granadan seizure of Zahara (1481) and the Castilian capture of Alhama (1482). Civil war within the Nasrid dynasty — Sultan Abu'l-Hasan against his son Boabdil and his brother el Zagal — fatally divided the emirate; Boabdil was captured at Lucena (1483) and released as a Castilian client. Ferdinand then methodically reduced the western strongholds with heavy artillery trains: Ronda fell in 1485, Loja in 1486, Málaga after a bitter siege in 1487 (its population was enslaved), and Baza and Almería in 1489 as el Zagal capitulated.

The siege of Granada

In 1491 the Catholic Monarchs built the fortified camp-city of Santa Fe before Granada and blockaded the capital, now the emirate's last remnant under Boabdil. Negotiations produced the Treaty of Granada (25 November 1491), guaranteeing the Muslims their property, law and religion; on 2 January 1492 Boabdil handed over the keys of the Alhambra — the scene later romanticised as "the Moor's last sigh" — and went into exile, ultimately to Morocco.

Consequences

Annexation ended nearly eight centuries of Muslim rule in Iberia. The treaty's religious guarantees were progressively abandoned: forced conversions after 1499 provoked revolts and led, a century later, to the expulsion of the Moriscos. The war's costs were met with crusading taxes and Genoese finance, and its artillery-and-logistics methods previewed the Italian Wars.
