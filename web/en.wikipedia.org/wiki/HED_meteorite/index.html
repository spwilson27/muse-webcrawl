HED meteorites are a clan of stony (achondrite) meteorites named from the initials of their three subgroups: the howardites, eucrites, and diogenites. Together they make up roughly five percent of all meteorite falls and are of exceptional scientific interest because their mineralogy, chemistry, and oxygen isotope signatures link them to a single differentiated parent body — the asteroid 4 Vesta, the second-most-massive object in the asteroid belt.

Subgroups

Eucrites are basaltic lava rocks, composed mainly of pyroxene and plagioclase, interpreted as solidified surface flows or shallow intrusions of the parent body's crust. Diogenites are coarse-grained orthopyroxene cumulates that crystallized in deeper magma chambers or the upper mantle of the body. Howardites are polymict breccias — mechanical mixtures of eucrite and diogenite fragments, thought to be regolith material blasted together by impacts on the parent surface.

Origin on Vesta

Spectroscopic matches between the HEDs and Vesta's surface, together with the discovery of a dynamical family of small "Vestoid" asteroids capable of delivering fragments to Earth-crossing orbits, established the Vesta connection. NASA's Dawn spacecraft, which orbited Vesta in 2011–2012, confirmed it: the giant south-polar Rheasilvia basin exposes deep crustal and mantle material matching diogenite and howardite compositions, and is the probable excavation site of most HED meteorites.
