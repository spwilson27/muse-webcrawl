Combined Operations Headquarters was a department of the British War Office set up in 1940 during the Second World War to harass the Germans on the European continent by means of raids carried out by use of combined naval and land forces. Its emblem combined an eagle (RAF), a Thompson submachine gun (army commandos) and an anchor (Royal Navy), symbolizing joint operations.

History

The Directorate of Combined Operations was created in June 1940 after the Dunkirk evacuation, with Lieutenant General Alan Bourne as its first head, succeeded within weeks by Admiral of the Fleet Sir Roger Keyes. In October 1941 Keyes was replaced by Lord Louis Mountbatten, who was given the title Chief of Combined Operations and a seat on the Chiefs of Staff Committee. Mountbatten greatly expanded the organization; when he left in 1943 to become Supreme Allied Commander in South East Asia, Major General Robert Laycock, former commander of Layforce and the Special Service Brigade, took over and served until the headquarters was wound down in 1947.

Operations and legacy

Combined Operations planned or supported many famous raids, including the Lofoten Islands raid (Operation Claymore, 1941), the St Nazaire Raid (Operation Chariot, 1942) and the Dieppe Raid (Operation Jubilee, 1942). It developed landing craft, amphibious doctrine, and specialist units — the British Commandos grew up under its umbrella — and amassed the experience that made the Normandy landings of 1944 possible. Its experimental establishments tested equipment from floating "Mulberry" harbours to PLUTO fuel pipelines.
