The Battle of Indus took place on 24 November 1221 on the banks of the Indus River, between the army of Genghis Khan and the remnants of the Khwarezmian Empire under its last shah, Jalal al-Din Mangburni. It was the final battle of the Mongol conquest of Khwarezmia: Jalal al-Din's army was annihilated, though he himself escaped by a famous plunge into the river.

Background

After the Mongols destroyed the Khwarezmian Empire's great cities — Bukhara, Samarkand, Merv and Nishapur — Shah Muhammad II died a fugitive on a Caspian island, leaving his son Jalal al-Din to rally what resistance remained. Jalal al-Din won a striking victory over a Mongol detachment at Parwan near Ghazni, but quarrels over spoils split his coalition, and Genghis Khan pursued him with the main Mongol army. Jalal al-Din retreated toward India, hoping to cross the Indus and seek refuge in the Delhi Sultanate; Genghis caught him with the river at his back.

The battle

Trapped against the Indus, Jalal al-Din drew up his smaller army with the river guarding — and sealing — his rear. After fierce fighting in which the Khwarezmian centre held for a time, Genghis sent a detachment across the hills to fall on Jalal al-Din's flank and rear, collapsing his line. Surrounded, Jalal al-Din cut his way to the riverbank, and, having dispatched his family to prevent their capture, rode his horse off a high bank into the Indus and swam to the far shore under a hail of arrows. Genghis, watching, is said to have forbidden his men to shoot and to have remarked to his sons that such a man was worthy of admiration.

Aftermath

Jalal al-Din spent three years in exile in India before returning to Persia, where he waged a brilliant but ultimately futile decade-long resistance until his murder in 1231. The battle completed the destruction of the Khwarezmian Empire and brought Mongol power to the borders of India, though Genghis turned back west rather than invading the subcontinent.
