Tortilla

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A stack of warm corn tortillas wrapped in a cloth]

A tortilla is a thin, round flatbread of Mesoamerican origin. In Mexico and Central America the original and still central form is the corn tortilla, made from nixtamalized maize dough (masa); the northern-Mexican and Tex-Mex flour tortilla is made from wheat flour with lard or shortening. (In Spain, confusingly, tortilla means an omelette.) Tortillas are the foundation of tacos, enchiladas, quesadillas, burritos (flour) and many other dishes, and a daily staple for millions.

Corn tortillas and nixtamalization

Dried maize kernels are simmered and steeped in an alkaline solution (water with slaked lime or wood ash), a process called nixtamalization, practised for millennia. It loosens the hulls, softens the grain for grinding into masa, and — crucially — makes niacin and calcium available, preventing deficiency diseases. The masa is pressed between plates or patted by hand and cooked on a hot flat griddle, the comal, until it puffs and browns in spots.

Flour tortillas

Wheat arrived with the Spanish; in northern Mexico, where wheat grew better than maize, flour tortillas developed, enriched with lard and leavened slightly or not at all. They are larger, softer and more pliable — suited to the burrito — and dominate in the borderlands and the United States, where factory tortillas now outsell many bread categories.

Uses

Beyond wraps, tortillas are fried into tostadas and chips (totopos, served with guacamole and salsa), layered in chilaquiles and enchiladas, and used as an eating utensil in themselves. The word and the food travelled worldwide with Mexican cuisine's global popularity.
