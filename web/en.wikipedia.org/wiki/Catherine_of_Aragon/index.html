Catherine of Aragon (Spanish: Catalina de Aragón; 16 December 1485 – 7 January 1536) was Queen of England as the first wife of King Henry VIII from their marriage on 11 June 1509 until their marriage was annulled on 23 May 1533. The youngest surviving child of Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile, she had previously been Princess of Wales by her brief marriage to Henry's elder brother Arthur.

Early life and first marriage

Catherine was raised at the victorious court of her parents, the Catholic Monarchs, and received a humanist education that made her one of the most learned princesses in Europe. Betrothed in childhood to cement an Anglo-Spanish alliance, she married Arthur, Prince of Wales, at St Paul's Cathedral in November 1501. Arthur died five months later, probably of the sweating sickness, leaving the sixteen-year-old Catherine a widow in a foreign court. After years of financial and diplomatic limbo, a papal dispensation was obtained permitting her to marry Arthur's brother, on the basis that her first marriage had never been consummated.

Queen of England

Catherine married Henry VIII shortly after his accession in 1509. She was a popular and capable queen: in 1513, while Henry campaigned in France, she served as regent and governor of the realm during the Scottish invasion defeated at Flodden, riding to address the troops in armour — an event she proudly reported to her husband. She was a patron of humanist learning, a friend of Erasmus and Thomas More, and a vigorous defender of education for women, including her daughter. Of her pregnancies, however, only one child survived infancy: the Princess Mary, born in 1516, who would reign as Mary I. Henry's longing for a male heir turned him against the marriage.

The King's Great Matter

From about 1527 Henry sought an annulment, arguing that his marriage to his brother's widow violated Leviticus and that the papal dispensation had been invalid. Catherine refused to yield, maintaining that her marriage to Arthur had never been consummated and her marriage to Henry was lawful. At the legatine trial at Blackfriars in 1529 she made a celebrated appeal, kneeling before Henry to plead her case, then walked out and refused to recognise the court's authority. When the Pope would not annul the marriage, Henry broke with Rome, setting England on the road to the Reformation; in 1533 Archbishop Cranmer declared the marriage void and Henry married Anne Boleyn. Catherine was styled Dowager Princess of Wales and denied the title of queen to her death.

Final years and death

Separated from her daughter and moved between increasingly modest houses, Catherine lived under pressure to accept the new settlement, which she steadfastly refused, signing her letters "Katherine the Queen". She died at Kimbolton Castle on 7 January 1536, most likely of cancer of the heart, and was buried in Peterborough Cathedral as a Princess of Wales rather than a queen. Popular devotion to her memory in England was deep and lasting, and Shakespeare later gave her a dignified portrayal in Henry VIII.
