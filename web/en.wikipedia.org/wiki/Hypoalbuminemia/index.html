Hypoalbuminemia

Hypoalbuminemia (or hypoalbuminaemia) is a medical sign defined by a low level of albumin in the blood, conventionally below 3.5 g/dL. It occurs in many illnesses, including cirrhosis of the liver, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition, protein-losing enteropathy and the systemic inflammatory response, and it is a strong predictor of poor outcomes in hospitalised patients.

Albumin is synthesised by the liver; low levels reflect loss, dilution or reduced synthesis.

Causes

Mechanisms include decreased synthesis (liver disease, malnutrition), increased loss (nephrotic syndrome through the kidneys, protein-losing enteropathy through the gut, burns through the skin), increased catabolism in inflammation and sepsis, and dilution or redistribution in critical illness and fluid overload. Albumin is a negative acute-phase protein, so its level falls during inflammation independently of nutritional state.

Effects

Reduced plasma oncotic pressure contributes to oedema, ascites and pleural effusions. Because albumin binds calcium, bilirubin, fatty acids and many drugs, hypoalbuminemia alters total calcium measurements and the pharmacokinetics of highly protein-bound drugs such as phenytoin.

Management

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. Intravenous albumin has specific roles — for example after large-volume paracentesis, in spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and in hepatorenal syndrome — but routine correction of the number alone does not improve outcomes in most settings.
