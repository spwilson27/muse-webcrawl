Duchy

A duchy, also called a dukedom, is a country, territory, fief, or domain ruled by a duke or duchess, a ruler hierarchically second to the king or queen in Western European tradition. Typically, during the European Middle Ages, duchies were independent sovereign states; only in the era of modern empires and kingdoms were they absorbed as provinces of a larger state.

History

The office of duke derives from the late Roman dux, a provincial military commander. In the Frankish kingdoms, dukes governed large border territories, and the German "stem duchies" (Stammesherzogtümer) — Saxony, Franconia, Bavaria, Swabia, and Lotharingia — formed the building blocks of the East Frankish kingdom. In France, the duchies of Normandy, Brittany, Burgundy, and Aquitaine were near-sovereign principalities whose dukes were peers of the realm. After the Norman Conquest, England's duchies (Lancaster, York, Cornwall) became appanages of the royal family rather than territorial governments.

Grand duchy

A grand duchy is ruled by a grand duke or grand duchess and ranks above an ordinary duchy. Historically these included Tuscany, Baden, Hesse, Mecklenburg-Schwerin, and Luxembourg. The Grand Duchy of Luxembourg is today the world's only sovereign grand duchy.

Surviving duchies

In the United Kingdom, two royal duchies survive as private estates: the Duchy of Lancaster, held by the monarch, and the Duchy of Cornwall, held by the heir to the throne, both originating in the fourteenth century. Elsewhere, former duchies survive as regions and cultural identities, such as Brittany and Normandy in France and Schleswig-Holstein in Germany.
