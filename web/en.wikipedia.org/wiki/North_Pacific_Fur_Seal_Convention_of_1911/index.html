North Pacific Fur Seal Convention of 1911

Northern fur seals on the Pribilof Islands

The North Pacific Fur Seal Convention of 1911, formally the Convention between the United States and Other Powers Providing for the Preservation and Protection of Fur Seals, was a treaty signed on 7 July 1911 between the United States, the United Kingdom (for Canada), Japan and Russia. It is often described as the first international treaty for the conservation of wildlife.

Background

During the 19th century, commercial pelagic sealing — the hunting of northern fur seals at sea — had devastated herds breeding on the Pribilof Islands (United States), the Commander Islands (Russia) and Robben Island (Japan). Because most seals killed at sea were females, each skin taken could mean a pup starving ashore; the Pribilof herd fell from millions to a few hundred thousand animals. Disputes over pelagic sealing in the Bering Sea had earlier led to the Bering Sea Arbitration of 1893, which failed to halt the decline.

Terms

The convention prohibited pelagic sealing in the North Pacific north of 30° latitude, with exceptions for Indigenous peoples using traditional methods. In exchange, the United States and Russia, which controlled the main rookeries, shared a percentage of their regulated land-based harvests with Canada and Japan. Japan received payments in lieu of sealing around Robben Island, which it agreed to close.

Legacy

The treaty succeeded: fur seal herds recovered substantially within decades. Japan withdrew from the convention in 1940, and it effectively lapsed during the Second World War. A successor agreement, the Interim Convention on Conservation of North Pacific Fur Seals, was concluded in 1957 and remained in force, with protocols, until 1984.
