Reform Edict of 1856

The Reform Edict of 1856, in Ottoman Turkish the Islahat Hatt-ı Hümâyûnu ("Imperial Reform Edict", often called the Hatt-ı Hümayun), was a decree issued by Sultan Abdülmecid I on 18 February 1856. A centrepiece of the Tanzimat reforms, it promised equality in education, government appointments and justice to all subjects of the Ottoman Empire regardless of religion.

Background

The edict followed the Hatt-ı Şerif of Gülhane (1839), which had begun the Tanzimat modernisation. It was issued during the Crimean War, partly under pressure from Britain, France and Austria, whose governments sought guarantees for Ottoman Christians ahead of the Congress of Paris (1856) that would end the war and admit the Ottoman Empire to the Concert of Europe.

Provisions

  • Legal equality of Muslim and non-Muslim subjects; abolition of degrading forms of address and of the cizye poll tax on non-Muslims (replaced, in practice, by a military exemption tax).
  • Non-Muslims became eligible for government service and military service, and for admission to state schools.
  • Freedom of religion and of church building/repair; recognition of the privileges of religious communities, with reformed mixed councils.
  • Prohibition of torture and of unequal testimony; reforms of prisons, taxation and provincial administration; foreigners permitted to own property.

Reception and effects

The edict was welcomed by Ottoman Christians and European diplomats but resented by many Muslims, who saw it as eroding their privileged status, and by some clergy who feared state intrusion. Implementation was uneven, but the edict provided the legal framework for the secularising reforms, mixed courts and provincial councils that culminated in the Ottoman constitution of 1876.
