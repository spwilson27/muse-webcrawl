Alphonse Bertillon

Born22 April 1853, Paris, France
Died13 February 1914 (aged 60), Paris
OccupationPolice officer, forensic scientist

Alphonse Bertillon (22 April 1853 – 13 February 1914) was a French police officer and biometrics researcher who developed anthropometry, the first scientific system of criminal identification, and standardised modern forensic practices including the mug shot and the systematic photography of crime scenes.

The Bertillon system

Working as a records clerk at the Paris Prefecture of Police, Bertillon became frustrated with the inability to identify repeat offenders who gave false names. His system, adopted in 1882–83, recorded eleven bodily measurements (standing height, arm span, head length and breadth, foot length, and others) together with standardised front and profile photographs — the origin of the mug shot — and a precise verbal description (the portrait parlé). Because adult bone dimensions vary between individuals and stay fixed, the filed cards could identify recidivists; the system scored an early success identifying a repeat offender in 1883 and spread worldwide.

Later career and controversy

Anthropometry was superseded after 1900 by fingerprinting, which was simpler and more reliable — a transition hastened by cases such as Will West (1903), in which two men had nearly identical Bertillon measurements. Bertillon also contributed to forensic document examination, ballistics and crime-scene photography, but his reputation suffered from his role as a handwriting expert for the prosecution in the Dreyfus affair, where his convoluted "expertise" helped convict an innocent man.
