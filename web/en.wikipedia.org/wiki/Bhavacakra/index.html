The bhavacakra (Sanskrit: भवचक्र; Tibetan: སྲིད་པའི་འཁོར་ལོ་) is a symbolic representation of saṃsāra (the cycle of birth, death and rebirth) found on the outside walls of Tibetan Buddhist temples and monasteries, and in the Indian state of Sikkim and Bhutan. It is often translated as the wheel of life or wheel of existence. In the Mahayana tradition, it is believed the drawing was designed by the Buddha himself, in order to help ordinary people understand Buddhist teachings.

Origins

According to tradition, the Buddha commissioned the first wheel of life as a gift for the king Bimbisara, painted by artists under his direction. Versions of the image survive in painting at the Ajanta Caves in India, and it remains a standard feature at the entrance of Tibetan monasteries.

Description

The wheel is held in the claws of Yama, the fearsome lord of death, symbolising that all beings in the cycle are subject to impermanence and mortality. At the hub, three animals — a pig, a snake and a bird (rooster) — bite one another's tails, representing the three poisons of ignorance, attachment and aversion, which keep the wheel turning.

The main disc depicts the six realms of rebirth: the realms of the gods, the asuras (demigods), humans, animals, hungry ghosts (pretas) and hells. A smaller ring around them shows beings rising and falling between realms. The rim of the wheel is divided into twelve sections illustrating the twelve nidānas, the links of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda).

Outside the wheel, in the top corners, stand the Buddha — pointing the way out of the cycle, often toward a white circle representing nirvana — and a moon symbolising liberation.
