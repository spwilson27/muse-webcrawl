Helvetica, also known by its original name Neue Haas Grotesk, is a widely used sans-serif typeface developed in 1957 by Swiss typeface designer Max Miedinger and Eduard Hoffmann. It is one of the most popular typefaces of the 20th century and a hallmark of the International Typographic Style (also known as the Swiss Style).

Helvetica is a neo-grotesque design, one influenced by the famous 19th-century (1800s) typeface Akzidenz-Grotesk and other German and Swiss designs. Its use became a hallmark of the International Typographic Style that emerged from the work of Swiss designers in the 1950s and 1960s, becoming one of the most popular typefaces of the mid-20th century. Over the years, a wide range of variants have been released in different weights, widths, and sizes, as well as matching designs for a range of non-Latin alphabets.

Notable features of Helvetica as originally designed include a high x-height, the termination of strokes on horizontal or vertical lines, and an unusually tight spacing between letters, which gave it a dense, solid appearance. Developed by the Haas'sche Schriftgiesserei (Haas Type Foundry) of Münchenstein (Basel), Switzerland, its release was planned to match the success of Ludwig & Mayer's similar Franklin Gothic and Monotype's grotesque designs.

History

The original typeface was designed in 1957 by Max Miedinger with input from Eduard Hoffmann, managing director of the Haas type foundry in Münchenstein, Switzerland. Miedinger, a freelance graphic designer who had formerly worked as a Haas salesman and designer, took up the challenge of designing a new sans-serif typeface. He worked closely with Hoffmann, who had the final say on many design decisions. Haas set out to design a new sans-serif typeface that could compete with the successful Akzidenz-Grotesk in the Swiss market.

Originally called Neue Haas Grotesk (New Haas Grotesque), it was rapidly licensed by Linotype and renamed Helvetica in 1960, derived from Confoederatio Helvetica, the Latin name for Switzerland. The change of name was proposed by Heinz Eul, a Haas parent company executive.

Characteristics

Helvetica is a neo-grotesque typeface; its letterforms have a mechanical, neutral quality intended to give no additional meaning. Its key features include:

  • Tall x-height, which improves readability at small point sizes
  • Terminated strokes that end on horizontal or vertical lines
  • Tight spacing between letters, giving a dense appearance
  • A relatively narrow width that allows for economical typesetting
  • Uniform stroke widths throughout

Usage

Helvetica is among the most widely used sans-serif typefaces. Versions exist for a wide variety of alphabets, including Latin, Cyrillic, Hebrew, Japanese, Korean, Greek, Arabic, Armenian, and Vietnamese. It has been used extensively in signage, corporate identity, and publishing. The American government uses Helvetica for federal forms and helped establish the typeface; it was the default typeface of New York City Subway signage in its latter days, and appears in the logos of many multinational corporations, including American Airlines, BMW, Lufthansa, Microsoft, Panasonic, and Target.

Helvetica's clean lines and neutral appearance have made it a staple of modern graphic design. The typeface has been the subject of exhibitions and the 2007 documentary film Helvetica, directed by Gary Hustwit, made to mark its 50th anniversary.

Variants

A wide range of variants have been released over the years, including Helvetica Light, Helvetica Condensed, Helvetica Rounded, and the 1983 redesign known as Neue Helvetica (Helvetica Now in its 2019 refresh), which offered improved spacing, punctuation suited to different text sizes, and a wider range of weights.
