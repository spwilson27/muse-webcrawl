Brocken spectre

Brocken spectre
TypeAtmospheric optical phenomenon
CauseObserver's shadow projected onto cloud or mist, with a glory around the shadow's head
Named forThe Brocken, highest peak of the Harz mountains, Germany

A Brocken spectre (German: Brockengespenst), also called a Brocken bow or mountain spectre, is the magnified (and apparently enormous) shadow of an observer cast in mid-air upon any type of cloud opposite a strong light source. The phenomenon can appear on any misty mountainside, cloud bank, or from an aeroplane, but the frequent fogs and low-altitude accessibility of the Brocken, the highest peak in the Harz mountain range in Germany, have created a local legend from which the phenomenon draws its name. The Brocken spectre was observed and described by Johann Silberschlag in 1780, and has since been recorded in literature about the region.

Occurrence

The "spectre" appears when the sun shines from behind the observer, who is looking down into mist or fog. The light projects the observer's shadow through the mist, often in an odd triangular shape due to perspective distortion. The apparent magnification of the size of the shadow is an optical illusion that occurs because the shadow is projected onto water droplets at varying distances in the mist, defeating the observer's judgement of distance; the shadow is judged to be at the same distance as nearby landmarks in the mist, making it seem far larger than it is.

The spectre may appear to move (sometimes quite suddenly) because of the movement of the cloud layer and variations in density within the cloud. The head of the figure is often surrounded by the glowing halo-like rings of a glory, rainbow-coloured rings that appear around the point directly opposite the sun (antisolar point) when sunlight is reflected by a cloud of uniformly sized water droplets.

In culture

The Brocken spectre is a prominent feature of the Walpurgis Night scene in Goethe's Faust, and appears in works by Samuel Taylor Coleridge, Thomas De Quincey and Charles Dickens. The phenomenon also gave its name, in part, to the meteorological term Brockenbock and is commemorated on the summit of the Brocken, where fog occurs on average on some 300 days per year.
