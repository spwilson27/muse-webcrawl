Deficiency judgment

A deficiency judgment is a judgment obtained by a lender against a borrower for the amount still owed on a secured debt after the collateral has been sold and the proceeds applied to the debt. It arises most commonly in mortgage foreclosure: if a foreclosed property sells for less than the outstanding loan balance, the difference is the "deficiency", and the lender may seek a personal judgment against the borrower for that sum, together with foreclosure costs and interest.

Availability

Whether a deficiency judgment is allowed depends on the jurisdiction and the type of loan. Some U.S. states, notably California, restrict or prohibit deficiency judgments on purchase-money mortgages through "anti-deficiency" statutes, a policy developed during the Great Depression. Other states permit them but limit recovery to the difference between the debt and the property's fair market value rather than its (often lower) foreclosure-sale price, under "fair value" offset rules. Deficiency judgments are generally not available on nonrecourse loans, where the lender's remedy is limited to the collateral by agreement.

Consequences

A deficiency judgment is an ordinary money judgment: it may be collected through garnishment or liens, can appear on credit reports, and may be discharged if the borrower files for bankruptcy, subject to exceptions. Strategic default and short sales are often negotiated with deficiency waivers in mind, since a borrower who agrees to a short sale may ask the lender to forgive the remaining balance.
