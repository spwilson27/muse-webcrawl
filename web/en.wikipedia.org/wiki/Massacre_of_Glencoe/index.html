Massacre of Glencoe

The Massacre of Glencoe took place in Glen Coe in the Highlands of Scotland on 13 February 1692. An estimated 30 members and associates of Clan MacDonald of Glencoe were killed by government soldiers — companies of the Earl of Argyll's Regiment of Foot, commanded by Captain Robert Campbell of Glenlyon — who had been billeted with the MacDonalds for about twelve days. Others died of exposure after their homes were burned. The killings were carried out because the MacDonald chief, Alasdair MacIain, had been late in pledging allegiance to the new monarchs, William III and Mary II.

Background

After the Glorious Revolution of 1688, the Scottish Highlands remained a stronghold of Jacobitism, supporting the exiled James VII. Following the Jacobite defeat in 1690–91, the government offered indemnity to chiefs who swore allegiance to William and Mary before 1 January 1692. Alasdair MacIain, chief of the small Glencoe branch of Clan Donald, set out late, was delayed by a snowstorm and a bureaucratic error — he first went to Fort William rather than Inveraray — and swore the oath on 6 January, six days late. Officials in Edinburgh, including the Secretary of State for Scotland, John Dalrymple, Master of Stair, seized on the delay as a pretext to make an example of the clan.

The massacre

In late January 1692, about 120 soldiers of Argyll's regiment under Captain Robert Campbell of Glenlyon arrived in Glencoe asking for billets, as was customary; the MacDonalds housed and fed them for nearly two weeks. On the evening of 12 February Campbell received written orders to fall upon the MacDonalds at five the next morning, to kill all under seventy years of age, and to take special care that the chief and his sons not escape. At dawn on 13 February the soldiers turned on their hosts. MacIain was shot as he rose from bed; killings took place at settlements along the glen, houses were torched, and many inhabitants fled into a blizzard, where women, children and old men died of exposure. Two of the chief's sons escaped.

Aftermath

Public revulsion grew when details emerged, particularly the breach of Highland hospitality — the crime of "murder under trust," considered especially heinous in Scots law. A parliamentary inquiry in 1695 condemned the affair as murder and laid blame on the Master of Stair, who resigned, but no one was prosecuted. The massacre has remained a potent symbol in Scottish memory, often (though somewhat inaccurately given the mixed clan composition of the government force) retold as a Campbell–MacDonald feud.
