Soviet montage theory

The Odessa steps sequence in Eisenstein's Battleship Potemkin (1925)

Soviet montage theory is an approach to understanding and creating cinema that was developed by Soviet filmmakers in the 1920s — among them Lev Kuleshov, Dziga Vertov, Vsevolod Pudovkin, and Sergei Eisenstein. It holds that the editing (montage) of shots, rather than the content of any single shot, is the essence of cinema: the collision or linkage of images produces meanings and emotional effects that the individual shots do not contain.

The Kuleshov effect

Lev Kuleshov's workshop experiments in the early 1920s demonstrated the phenomenon now called the Kuleshov effect: audiences shown the same neutral shot of an actor's face intercut with a bowl of soup, a dead child, or a reclining woman attributed different emotions — hunger, grief, desire — to the actor. Meaning, Kuleshov argued, arises from juxtaposition, not from the shot itself.

Eisenstein's montage

Eisenstein developed montage into a dialectical theory. Drawing on Marxist dialectics and the structure of Japanese ideograms, he argued that two shots placed in collision produce a third, qualitatively new concept in the viewer's mind. In writings such as "The Cinematographic Principle and the Ideogram" (1929) he distinguished five methods of montage: metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal, and intellectual. Strike (1925), Battleship Potemkin (1925), and October (1928) exemplify the method; the Odessa steps massacre in Potemkin remains cinema's most imitated sequence.

Pudovkin and Vertov

Pudovkin's approach was gentler: editing as "linkage" rather than collision, building scenes from carefully observed details, as in Mother (1926). Vertov's Kino-Eye theory and Man with a Movie Camera (1929) treated montage as the organisation of documentary truth, rejecting staged fiction altogether.

Decline and legacy

By the mid-1930s the imposition of Socialist Realism curtailed formal experimentation, and Eisenstein's later work was attacked for "formalism". Montage theory nevertheless became foundational for film studies worldwide, influencing documentary, advertising, music video, and the editing grammar of mainstream cinema.
