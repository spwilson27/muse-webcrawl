Estrogen receptor

Estrogen receptors (ERs) are a group of proteins found inside cells. They are receptors that are activated by the hormone estrogen (17β-estradiol). Two classes of estrogen receptor exist: nuclear estrogen receptors (ERα and ERβ), which are members of the nuclear receptor family of intracellular receptors, and membrane estrogen receptors (mERs) such as GPER, which are mostly G protein-coupled receptors.

Types

Once activated by estrogen, the nuclear estrogen receptor can translocate into the nucleus and bind to DNA to regulate the activity of many genes — hundreds in humans. ERα is encoded by the gene ESR1 on chromosome 6 and is the dominant receptor in the uterus, liver, and breast epithelium. ERβ, encoded by ESR2 on chromosome 14, was discovered in 1996 and predominates in the ovary, prostate, lung, bladder and central nervous system. The two subtypes often have opposing effects on proliferation, and their ratio in a tissue helps determine its response to estrogens.

Signalling

In the classical genomic pathway, estrogen diffuses across the cell membrane and binds the receptor in the nucleus; the hormone–receptor complex dimerises and binds estrogen response elements (EREs) in the promoters of target genes, recruiting coactivators or corepressors. ERs can also regulate genes without direct DNA binding by tethering to other transcription factors such as AP-1 and Sp1. Membrane-initiated signalling produces rapid, non-genomic effects through kinase cascades.

Clinical significance

About 70% of breast cancers are ERα-positive, and their growth can be blocked by antiestrogens. Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) such as tamoxifen and raloxifene act as antagonists in breast but agonists in other tissues, while selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs) such as fulvestrant destroy the receptor protein. ER status testing by immunohistochemistry is a routine part of breast cancer diagnosis and guides endocrine therapy.
