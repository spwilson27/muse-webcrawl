Edward Teller (1908–2003)

Edward Teller (Hungarian: Teller Ede; January 15, 1908 – September 9, 2003) was a Hungarian-American theoretical physicist known colloquially as "the father of the hydrogen bomb." He made contributions to nuclear and molecular physics, spectroscopy, and surface physics, and was a central — and controversial — figure in the United States weapons program.

Early life

Teller was born in Budapest into a Jewish family. He left Hungary amid political turmoil and studied chemical engineering in Karlsruhe and physics in Munich and Leipzig, earning his doctorate in 1930 under Werner Heisenberg. He worked with Niels Bohr in Copenhagen before emigrating to the United States in 1935, becoming a professor at George Washington University.

Manhattan Project

After becoming a U.S. citizen in 1941, Teller joined the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, working in Hans Bethe's theoretical division. Even during the war he pressed for work on the "Super," an early hydrogen bomb concept.

Hydrogen bomb

After the first Soviet atomic test in 1949, Teller lobbied strongly for a crash program to build a hydrogen bomb. In 1951, with mathematician Stanislaw Ulam, he arrived at the Teller–Ulam design, the breakthrough that made multi-megaton thermonuclear weapons practical. The first full-scale test, Ivy Mike, took place in 1952.

Controversies and later life

Teller's 1954 testimony in the security hearing of J. Robert Oppenheimer alienated much of the physics community. He co-founded Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, advocateded for the Strategic Defense Initiative, and in later years supported controversial proposals ranging from nuclear excavation to asteroid defense. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2003, shortly before his death.
