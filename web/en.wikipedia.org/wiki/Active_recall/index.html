Active recall

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Active recall is a learning technique based on retrieving information from memory rather than passively reviewing it. Instead of rereading notes or highlighting a text, the learner poses questions and attempts to answer them from memory — using flashcards, practice tests, or simply closing the book and writing down everything remembered — before checking the correct answer. The improvement in later retention caused by the act of retrieval itself is known in cognitive psychology as the testing effect or retrieval practice effect.

Evidence

The testing effect is among the most robust findings in the study of learning. Experiments from the early 20th century onward, and prominently the work of Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (2006), show that students who study and then take recall tests retain substantially more after a delay than students who spend the same time restudying, even though restudying can feel more productive and can give better results on an immediate test. A widely cited review of study strategies (Dunlosky et al., 2013) rated practice testing among the highest-utility techniques, ahead of rereading and highlighting, which were rated low.

Why it works

Retrieval is thought to strengthen the memory trace and the routes to it, elaborate the retrieved knowledge by connecting it to the cues used, and make forgetting itself more informative: a failed retrieval identifies gaps and directs further study (metacognition). Effortful but successful retrieval produces the largest gains, an example of what Robert Bjork calls a "desirable difficulty".

Practice

Active recall is frequently combined with spaced repetition, in which reviews are scheduled at expanding intervals; software such as Anki and SuperMemo automates the scheduling and presents flashcards just as they are about to be forgotten. Other applications include the Feynman technique (explaining a topic in plain language from memory), practice examinations in professional training, and question banks in medical education.
