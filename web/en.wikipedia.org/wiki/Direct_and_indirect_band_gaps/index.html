Direct and indirect band gaps

In semiconductor physics, the band gap of a material is classified as direct or indirect according to whether the maximum of the valence band and the minimum of the conduction band occur at the same value of crystal momentum (k-vector) in the Brillouin zone. The distinction has major consequences for the optical properties of the material — in particular for whether it can emit or absorb light efficiently.

Direct band gaps

In a direct-gap material, an electron at the bottom of the conduction band can recombine with a hole at the top of the valence band without any change in momentum. Since a photon carries almost negligible momentum, the transition can proceed by photon emission alone, so direct-gap semiconductors absorb and emit light strongly. This is why materials such as gallium arsenide (GaAs) and gallium nitride (GaN) are used for LEDs, semiconductor lasers and efficient thin-film solar cells.

Indirect band gaps

In an indirect-gap material the conduction band minimum and valence band maximum lie at different momenta. A transition between them must conserve momentum, so it requires the simultaneous participation of a phonon (a quantum of lattice vibration) to supply or absorb the momentum difference. This three-particle process is much less probable, so absorption near the band edge is weak and radiative recombination is slow and inefficient. Silicon and germanium, the dominant electronic materials, are indirect-gap semiconductors, which is why they are poor light emitters despite their excellent electronic properties; much research aims at making silicon emit light through nanostructuring, strain, or combination with direct-gap materials.

Measurement and significance

The type of gap can be inferred from the shape of the optical absorption edge: absorption rises steeply with photon energy for direct gaps and more gradually, with a phonon-assisted threshold behaviour, for indirect gaps. The direct/indirect distinction governs the design of photodetectors, solar cells, light emitters and thermophotovoltaic devices.
