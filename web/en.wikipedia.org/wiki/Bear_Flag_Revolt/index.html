Date14 June – 9 July 1846
LocationSonoma, Alta California, Mexico (present-day California, U.S.)
ResultCalifornia Republic proclaimed, then absorbed into U.S. occupation

The Bear Flag Revolt was a brief rebellion by American settlers in Mexican Alta California in June 1846, during the Mexican–American War. The rebels seized the town of Sonoma, captured the Californio commander Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo, and proclaimed an independent "California Republic" under a flag bearing a grizzly bear — the origin of the modern flag of the U.S. state of California.

Background

By the 1840s several hundred American immigrants had settled in California, and tensions rose over Mexican officials' threats to expel non-citizens. The explorer John C. Frémont, in the region with a U.S. Army topographical party, encouraged settler resistance without formally committing the United States. Unaware that war had already been declared between the United States and Mexico, the settlers decided to act on their own.

The revolt

On 14 June 1846 about thirty settlers rode into Sonoma and took Vallejo prisoner without a shot; he was held at Sutter's Fort. The rebels elected William B. Ide as commander and raised their homemade flag, featuring a star, a grizzly bear and the words "California Republic". Skirmishes followed with Californio forces under José de la Rosa and Joaquín de la Torre, including the Battle of Olómpali on 24 June. On 7 July the U.S. Navy under Commodore John D. Sloat occupied Monterey and raised the American flag; two days later the Bear Flag at Sonoma was lowered and replaced by the Stars and Stripes, ending the twenty-five-day republic. Most Bear Flaggers enlisted in Frémont's California Battalion.

Legacy

The Bear Flag, redesigned in 1911, became the official state flag of California, and the revolt is commemorated by a monument in the plaza at Sonoma, now part of Sonoma State Historic Park.
