Noon bell


Labelled placeholder: church bell

The noon bell (Hungarian: déli harangszó) is a tradition associated with the Siege of Belgrade (then Nándorfehérvár) in 1456. Pope Callixtus III ordered the bells of every European church to be rung every day at noon, as a call for believers to pray for the defenders of the city. The practice, rooted in the papal bull of 29 June 1456, has continued in many churches to this day, and in Hungary and surrounding regions the noon bell retains strong national significance.

History

In 1456 the Ottoman Empire under Sultan Mehmed II besieged Belgrade, defended by the forces of John Hunyadi and the crusader reinforcements of Giovanni da Capistrano. On 29 June 1456, before news of the victory reached Rome, Pope Callixtus III issued a bull ordering daily noon prayers and bell-ringing across Christendom for the success of the defence. The siege was lifted on 22 July 1456, one of the notable Christian victories over the Ottomans in the 15th century.

Because news travelled slowly, the order became permanently associated with the victory itself, and the noon ringing continued as a commemoration even after its original intercessory purpose was fulfilled. Pope Alexander VI confirmed the daily practice in 1500 in connection with prayers against the plague and the Ottoman threat.

Legacy

In Hungary the noon bell is regarded as a symbol of the Belgrade victory and of John Hunyadi's defence of Europe; it is commemorated in literature, monuments, and broadcast media — Hungarian radio traditionally marks midday with bell chimes. The tradition is also kept in many Croatian, Slovak, Polish and other Central European churches.
