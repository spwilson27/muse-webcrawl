English law is the common law legal system of England and Wales, comprising mainly criminal law and civil law, each branch having its own courts and procedures. It has established a wide influence internationally, and variations of its principles — in particular its emphasis on precedent, or stare decisis — form the basis of the legal systems of many Commonwealth countries and of the United States.

English law has no single formal code; it is principally a system of judge-made law developed through decisions of the superior courts, supplemented by Acts of Parliament and, historically, by a body of law derived from the European Union. The doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty means that legislation enacted by Parliament overrides inconsistent common law.

History

The common law developed after the Norman Conquest of 1066 through the centralised royal courts at Westminster, gradually displacing local customary law. The separate jurisdiction of equity, administered by the Lord Chancellor, mitigated the rigidity of the common law; the two were fused administratively by the Judicature Acts of 1873–75. During the nineteenth century Parliament increasingly legislated, and statute became the dominant source of new law.

Sources of law

The principal sources are: (1) case law, in which the ratio decidendi of a superior court's decision binds lower courts; (2) legislation, including primary Acts and delegated legislation; (3) retained EU law preserved after Brexit; and (4) customary law, of marginal importance today. Authoritative commentary by institutional writers such as Blackstone also retains persuasive weight.

Courts and judiciary

The Supreme Court of the United Kingdom, created in 2009, is the final court of appeal for civil cases and for criminal cases from England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Below it sit the Court of Appeal, the High Court (King's Bench, Chancery and Family Divisions), the Crown Court, the County Court and magistrates' courts, together with a system of tribunals. Judges are appointed by the Judicial Appointments Commission on the basis of merit.

The legal profession

The profession is divided: solicitors generally advise clients and prepare cases, while barristers specialise in advocacy and are instructed through solicitors, though the boundary has blurred since the Legal Services Act 2007. Judges were historically drawn from the ranks of experienced barristers.
