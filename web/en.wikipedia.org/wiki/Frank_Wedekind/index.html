Frank Wedekind

Benjamin Franklin Wedekind (24 July 1864 – 9 March 1918), usually known as Frank Wedekind, was a German playwright. His work, often critical of bourgeois attitudes — particularly toward sex — is now regarded as a precursor of expressionism and of the epic theatre of Bertolt Brecht. His most famous plays are Spring Awakening (1891) and the two "Lulu" plays, Earth Spirit (1895) and Pandora's Box (1904).

Born: 24 July 1864, Hanover, Kingdom of Hanover
Died: 9 March 1918, Munich, Germany
Occupation: Playwright, actor, journalist
Notable works: Spring Awakening; Earth Spirit; Pandora's Box

Life

Wedekind was born in Hanover and grew up largely at Lenzburg Castle in Switzerland, which his father, a retired physician and political emigré, had bought. He studied in Munich and Zurich, worked in advertising and journalism, and from 1896 wrote for the satirical magazine Simplicissimus. A poem mocking Kaiser Wilhelm II forced him to flee to Switzerland in 1898; on returning to Germany he served a prison sentence for lèse-majesté at Königstein Fortress.

He performed his own satirical songs on the cabaret stage — notably at Munich's Die Elf Scharfrichter — accompanying himself on guitar, and acted in his own plays. In 1906 he married the actress Tilly Newes, who played many of his heroines. He died in Munich in 1918 following complications after an appendectomy.

Works

Frühlings Erwachen (Spring Awakening, written 1891, first performed 1906) depicts schoolchildren crushed by sexual repression and authoritarian education; banned or censored for decades, it was later adapted into a successful Broadway rock musical (2006). The two Lulu plays trace the rise and fall of a femme fatale through marriages, murder and prostitution to her death at the hands of Jack the Ripper; they became the basis of Alban Berg's opera Lulu and of G. W. Pabst's film Pandora's Box (1929) starring Louise Brooks. Other works include Marquis of Keith (1901), King Nicolo (1902) and Franziska (1912).

Influence

Wedekind's episodic structures, grotesque satire and direct assaults on bourgeois morality anticipated expressionist drama and strongly influenced the young Brecht, who called him one of the great educators of the modern theatre.
