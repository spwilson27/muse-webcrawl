A cook is a professional who prepares food for consumption in the food industry, in settings such as restaurants. A cook is sometimes referred to as a chef, although in the professional kitchen the terms are not interchangeable: chefs generally have more training, responsibility and creative authority, while cooks typically work under a chef's direction preparing dishes to order.

Roles and ranks

In large professional kitchens, cooks may be organised according to the brigade de cuisine system developed by Auguste Escoffier. Line cooks staff individual stations — such as grill, sauté, fry or pantry (garde manger) — while prep cooks handle chopping, portioning and other preliminary work. Entry-level positions include kitchen porter and commis cook, and advancement leads through demi-chef and chef de partie roles.

Training

Cooks may train through culinary school programmes, apprenticeships, or on-the-job experience. Formal qualifications include degrees and diplomas in culinary arts and certificates in food safety and hygiene, which are legally required in many jurisdictions before a person may handle food commercially. Many regions also require food handler permits.

Working conditions

Cooking professionally involves long hours, often including evenings, weekends and holidays, in hot, fast-paced environments. The work carries risks of burns, cuts and slips, and requires the ability to perform under time pressure during service. Uniforms traditionally include a jacket, apron, neckerchief and hat (toque), and non-slip shoes are standard safety equipment.

History

Professional cooking is ancient: cooks were employed in the households of rulers in Egypt, Greece and Rome, and specialised cookshops existed in imperial China. In medieval Europe, cooks were organised into guilds, and French haute cuisine later codified professional standards that spread worldwide. The modern restaurant industry, emerging in late 18th-century Paris, created the professional cook as a public-facing trade.
