Uvea

The uvea (from Latin uva, "grape"), also called the uveal layer, uveal coat, uveal tract, or vascular tunic, is the pigmented middle layer of the eye, lying between the outer sclera and the inner retina. It is named for its resemblance to a peeled grape when dissected from the eyeball. The uvea is heavily vascularised and pigmented with melanin, which absorbs scattered light inside the eye.

Structure

The uvea is divided into three connected parts, from front to back:

  • The iris, the coloured diaphragm that controls the size of the pupil and hence the amount of light entering the eye.
  • The ciliary body, which produces aqueous humour and, through the ciliary muscle and zonular fibres, changes the shape of the lens during accommodation.
  • The choroid, a dark, blood-rich layer that supplies oxygen and nourishment to the outer retina and absorbs excess light.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the uvea is called uveitis, and is classified anatomically as anterior (iritis), intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis. Uveitis can be associated with autoimmune and infectious diseases and may threaten vision if untreated. The uvea can also be the site of tumours, most notably uveal melanoma, the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults, which arises from the melanocytes of the choroid, ciliary body, or iris.
