The Fon people are a Gbe ethnic group of West Africa, the largest group in the Republic of Benin, where they make up roughly two-fifths of the population. Fon communities are also found in southwestern Nigeria and Togo. They speak the Fon language, a member of the Gbe language family, and are historically associated with the Kingdom of Dahomey.

History

Fon tradition traces their origins to Tado, in present-day Togo, from which migrants moved east and founded Allada and, later, Abomey. Around the early seventeenth century the Fon kingdom of Dahomey was established on the Abomey plateau and expanded into a powerful, highly centralised state. Dahomey became a major participant in the Atlantic slave trade through the port of Whydah (Ouidah), while also building a formidable military tradition that included the celebrated all-female regiments known to Europeans as the "Dahomey Amazons". French forces conquered the kingdom in 1892–1894, and it was absorbed into French West Africa.

Culture and religion

The Fon are the principal originators of Vodun (Voodoo), an organised religion centred on spirits (vodun) such as Mawu-Lisa, the creator pair, and Legba, messenger and guardian of crossroads. Vodun was carried across the Atlantic by enslaved Africans and gave rise to related traditions in Haiti, Louisiana, Cuba and Brazil. January 10 is celebrated in Benin as National Vodun Day. Fon art is renowned for its appliqué cloths, brass and iron figures, and the carved wooden bocio statues used as protective charms.

Society

Traditional Fon society is organised in patrilineal clans and was historically headed by kings at Abomey, whose palaces — now a UNESCO World Heritage Site — are decorated with bas-reliefs recording royal history. Agriculture, especially of maize, cassava, yams and oil palm, remains the main occupation, alongside trade and crafts.
