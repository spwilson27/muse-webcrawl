Cancer screening

Cancer screening aims to identify cancer before symptoms appear, or to detect precancerous changes that can be treated to prevent cancer from developing. Screening involves the use of tests — such as blood tests, imaging, or tissue sampling — in people who appear healthy. When abnormal tissue or cancer is found early, it may be easier to treat, and several screening programmes have been shown to reduce deaths from specific cancers.

Principles

Not every cancer is suitable for screening. A screening test should be accurate, acceptable to the population, and applied where early treatment improves outcomes. Screening carries potential harms, including false-positive results (which cause anxiety and further invasive testing), false negatives, and overdiagnosis — the detection of cancers that would never have caused symptoms or death. Decisions about screening therefore balance benefits and harms, usually targeted by age and risk factors.

Common screening programmes

  • Breast cancer — mammography is widely used for women in defined age groups.
  • Cervical cancer — Pap smear (cytology) and HPV testing detect precancerous cell changes.
  • Colorectal cancer — faecal occult blood / immunochemical tests, sigmoidoscopy and colonoscopy; colonoscopy also allows removal of precancerous polyps.
  • Prostate cancer — prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing, whose use remains debated because of overdiagnosis.
  • Lung cancer — low-dose computed tomography for people at high risk, mainly heavy smokers.

History

The Pap smear, developed from the work of Georgios Papanikolaou in the 1920s–1940s, became the model for population screening after organised programmes dramatically reduced cervical cancer incidence. Mammography screening expanded from the 1960s onwards, and faecal testing for colorectal cancer followed. National bodies such as the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force periodically review the evidence and issue graded recommendations.
