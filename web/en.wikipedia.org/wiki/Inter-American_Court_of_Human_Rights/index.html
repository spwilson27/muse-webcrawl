Inter-American Court of Human Rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Inter-American Court of Human Rights is an international court based in San José, Costa Rica, established under the American Convention on Human Rights (1969, in force 1978) as part of the inter-American human rights system of the Organization of American States. Together with the Inter-American Commission (Washington, D.C.), which filters cases and can bring them before the Court, it supervises the rights obligations of the roughly two dozen states that have accepted its contentious jurisdiction.

Jurisprudence

The Court's landmark first judgment, Velásquez Rodríguez v. Honduras (1988), established states' duty to prevent, investigate and repair violations — the due-diligence doctrine — in response to forced disappearances. Its reparations practice (transformative, community-level remedies), its control de convencionalidad doctrine obliging domestic judges to review national law against the Convention, its advisory opinions (on migrants' rights, the environment and human rights (2017), and gender identity), and its case law on amnesties (Barrios Altos, voiding Peru's self-amnesty laws) have made it the boldest of the regional courts. Compliance is patchy and politics hostile episodically (Venezuela and Trinidad and Tobago denounced the Convention; other states have threatened withdrawal after adverse rulings) — the standard fragility of supranational rights courts, in an acute regional form.
