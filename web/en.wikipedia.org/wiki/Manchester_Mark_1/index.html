Manchester Mark 1

The Manchester Mark 1 was one of the earliest stored-program computers, developed at the University of Manchester, England. Work began in August 1948, following the successful run of its prototype, the Manchester "Baby" (Small-Scale Experimental Machine), which on 21 June 1948 executed the first program stored in a computer's electronic memory. The Mark 1 was operational by April 1949 and was used for research including work on Mersenne primes and optics calculations.

Design

Designed principally by F. C. Williams, Tom Kilburn and Geoff Tootill, the machine used Williams–Kilburn cathode-ray tubes as its main store, holding 40-bit words. The final version (October 1949) had 128 words of main memory, a magnetic drum backing store of 1,024 words, and introduced index registers (called B-lines), an innovation that made modifying instruction addresses practical and that became standard in computer design. Alan Turing, who joined Manchester in 1948, wrote the machine's programming manual and later used it for work on morphogenesis.

Legacy

The Mark 1 provided the basis for the Ferranti Mark 1, the world's first commercially available general-purpose electronic computer, delivered in February 1951. A famous early application was Christopher Strachey's draughts program and, in 1952, a computer-generated love-letter program written by Strachey — an early example of artificial creativity.
