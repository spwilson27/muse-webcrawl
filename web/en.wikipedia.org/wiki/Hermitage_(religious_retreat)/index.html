Hermitage (religious retreat)

A hermitage is a dwelling or retreat where a hermit lives in seclusion from the world, typically for religious devotion, prayer and contemplation. The word derives from the Greek eremos ("desert"), recalling the Desert Fathers of early Christianity who withdrew to the Egyptian wilderness. In a wider sense the term also covers small religious retreat houses and, in later usage, secluded rural dwellings and country houses built in a deliberately rustic style.

History

Hermitages appear in nearly every contemplative tradition: Hindu ascetics withdrew to forest hermitages (ashrams), Buddhist and Daoist recluses built mountain huts, and Christian anchorites from the 3rd century onward sought solitude in deserts, forests and islands. In medieval Europe hermitages ranged from natural caves and cells attached to churches to organised communities — the Carthusian charterhouse, where monks lived as hermits in individual cells around a common church, and the Camaldolese hermitages of Italy. Some hermits, like Julian of Norwich, lived as anchoresses walled into cells in towns, giving spiritual counsel through a window.

Forms

A hermitage might be a single hut, cottage or cave with an oratory, garden and well; a cluster of such cells could form a laura, in which solitaries met only for common worship. In the Roman Catholic Church, canon law still recognises diocesan hermits, who profess the evangelical counsels and live "in the silence of solitude" under their bishop.

Ornamental hermitages

In 18th-century Britain and France, aristocratic estates featured ornamental hermitages — rustic summer houses of rough timber, roots and thatch in landscape gardens — sometimes staffed by a hired "garden hermit" engaged to live picturesquely and not cut his hair or nails. Such follies survive at estates such as Painshill in Surrey. The most famous Russian "hermitage" is the Hermitage Museum in Saint Petersburg, which began as Catherine the Great's private retreat adjoining the Winter Palace ("hermitage" in the courtly French sense of a private pavilion) before growing into one of the world's great museums.
