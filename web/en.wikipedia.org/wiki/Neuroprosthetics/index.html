Neuroprosthetics
Brain–computer interface controlling a prosthetic arm
FieldBiomedical engineering, neuroscience
ExamplesCochlear implant, retinal implant, deep brain stimulation, BCI motor prostheses

Neuroprosthetics (also called neural prosthetics) is a discipline related to neuroscience and biomedical engineering concerned with developing prostheses that interface with the nervous system to restore or augment sensory, motor or cognitive function lost through injury or disease. Neuroprostheses either record neural signals to control external devices or stimulate nervous tissue to produce artificial sensation or movement.

History

The first widely successful neuroprosthesis is the cochlear implant, developed from the 1950s–1970s and approved for clinical use in the 1980s; it converts sound into electrical stimulation of the auditory nerve and has restored hearing to hundreds of thousands of people with severe-to-profound deafness. Other established devices include deep brain stimulation (DBS) systems for Parkinson's disease and essential tremor, spinal cord stimulators for chronic pain, sacral nerve stimulators, and phrenic pacers.

Motor and sensory prostheses

Research-grade motor neuroprostheses use implanted electrode arrays — such as the Utah array in the BrainGate trials — to record from motor cortex, decoding intended movement to control computer cursors, robotic arms or functional electrical stimulation of the user's own muscles. Retinal implants (e.g., Argus II) and cortical visual prostheses aim to restore vision; bidirectional prosthetic limbs with sensory feedback are an active area of study.

Challenges

Longevity of the electrode–tissue interface, scarring, signal stability, wireless power and data, and ethical questions about cognition, identity and access are major themes in the field.
