Wiman Joseon (194–108 BC) was a dynasty of Gojoseon, founded when the Yan refugee Wiman seized the throne of King Jun of Gojoseon in 194 BC. It was the final dynasty of Gojoseon before its conquest by the Han dynasty of China, and its fall led to the establishment of the Four Commanderies of Han in the northern Korean peninsula.

Founding

Wiman was a military leader from the Chinese state of Yan who, according to the Records of the Grand Historian and the Book of Han, crossed the Pae River (possibly the Yalu) with about a thousand followers after the fall of Yan to the Han. He initially entered the service of King Jun of Gojoseon, who entrusted him with guarding the western frontier. In 194 BC Wiman revolted, drove Jun south — where Jun is said to have founded a state called Han — and installed himself as king, ruling from the capital Wanggeom-seong, generally identified with modern Pyongyang.

Expansion and prosperity

Under Wiman and his descendants, the kingdom strengthened its military, attracted large numbers of Han refugees, and prospered by controlling trade between the Chinese commanderies and the peoples of Manchuria and the peninsula. It subjugated neighbouring chiefdoms such as Jinbeon and Imdun and blocked direct contact between Han China and local states such as Jin, which the Han court came to regard as an obstacle.

Fall

Relations with Han deteriorated over trade and the sheltering of fugitives. In 109 BC Emperor Wu of Han sent envoys, and after the murder of the envoy She He, launched a two-pronged invasion by land and sea. The initial campaigns stalled, but in 108 BC, after internal division led to the assassination of King Ugeo, Wiman's grandson, Wanggeom-seong fell. The Han established four commanderies — Lelang, Xuantu, Lintun and Zhenfan — to administer the conquered territory.

Historical debate

The ethnicity of Wiman and the nature of his state remain debated in Korean and Chinese historiography. Korean historians generally view Wiman Joseon as a continuation of Gojoseon with a change of ruling house, whereas some Chinese accounts emphasize Wiman's origin in Yan.
