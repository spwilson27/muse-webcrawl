Qigong (simplified Chinese: 气功; traditional Chinese: 氣功; pinyin: qìgōng) is a system of coordinated body posture and movement, breathing, and meditation originating in China and used for purposes of health, spirituality, and martial-arts training. The term combines qi (氣), the vital life-force of traditional Chinese philosophy, with gong (功), meaning cultivation or skill acquired through steady practice.

Qigong practices typically involve meditative movement, slow flowing coordinated movement, deep rhythmic breathing, and a calm meditative state of mind. It is practised for recreation, exercise, relaxation, preventive medicine, self-healing, alternative medicine, meditation, self-cultivation, and training for martial arts.

History

The roots of qigong lie in ancient Chinese practices of self-cultivation, including Daoist inner alchemy, Buddhist meditation, and the health-preserving exercises known historically as daoyin ("guiding and pulling"). Illustrated daoyin charts dating to the Han dynasty were excavated at Mawangdui in the 1970s. The modern term "qigong" was popularised in the 1950s in the People's Republic of China, when it was promoted as a secular form of health exercise and integrated into state-sanctioned medical and rehabilitation programmes. A mass "qigong fever" swept China in the 1980s and early 1990s, during which charismatic masters claimed extraordinary healing powers; the movement was curtailed by a government crackdown in the late 1990s, particularly after the suppression of Falun Gong, itself a qigong-derived practice.

Forms and practice

There are thousands of documented qigong forms. Well-known sets include the Eight Pieces of Brocade (Baduanjin), the Five Animal Frolics (Wuqinxi, attributed to the physician Hua Tuo), the Muscle/Tendon Changing Classic (Yijin Jing), and the Six Healing Sounds (Liuzijue). Practice may emphasise static postures (such as standing meditation, zhan zhuang), dynamic movements, or seated breathing exercises. Qigong is commonly taught as a gentler, more meditative complement to tai chi.

Health claims and research

Clinical research has examined qigong for balance, blood pressure, chronic pain, and quality of life in chronic disease. Systematic reviews generally conclude that the evidence is limited by small sample sizes and methodological weaknesses, though gentle qigong exercise appears safe for most people and may offer modest benefits comparable to other low-intensity exercise.
