Two-line element set

A two-line element set (TLE, or more generally an element set) is a data format encoding a list of orbital elements of an Earth-orbiting object for a given point in time, the epoch. Using suitable prediction models (most importantly the SGP4/SDP4 family), the state (position and velocity) of the object at any point in the past or future can be estimated. TLEs are produced by the United States Space Force's 18th Space Defense Squadron and were historically distributed by NORAD; they are the standard means by which satellite catalogues, amateur satellite trackers, and software libraries describe the orbits of artificial satellites and space debris.

Format

Despite the name — a survival from the era of 80-column punched cards — a TLE now usually consists of three lines of exactly 69 characters: a title line with the satellite's name (line 0), followed by lines 1 and 2, which begin with the digits "1" and "2". Line 1 gives the satellite catalogue number, classification, international designator, epoch, the first derivative of mean motion, the ballistic drag term, ephemeris type, element set number and a checksum. Line 2 gives the catalogue number, inclination, right ascension of the ascending node, eccentricity (with an implied leading decimal point), argument of perigee, mean anomaly, mean motion, and revolution number at epoch, plus a final checksum digit. All angles are in degrees and mean motion in revolutions per day.

Accuracy and use

TLEs are fitted to observed data and are moderately accurate — typically within about a kilometre at epoch, degrading with time as perturbations and drag accumulate, so they must be refreshed regularly. They are consumed by satellite-tracking programs such as those maintained by CelesTrak and by amateur radio operators predicting passes, and are routinely used in conjunction with the SGP4 propagation model. Because of their limited precision, TLEs are not suitable for precise orbit determination or close-approach manoeuvres, and replacement formats (such as the Orbit Mean-elements Message, OMM) have been introduced in professional practice, though the TLE remains ubiquitous.
