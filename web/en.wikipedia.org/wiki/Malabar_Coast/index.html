The Malabar Coast is the long, narrow southwestern shoreline of the Indian subcontinent, running from the Konkan down to Kanyakumari (Cape Comorin), roughly corresponding to modern Kerala and coastal Karnataka. Hemmed in by the Western Ghats to the east and washed by the Arabian Sea, it is a land of lagoons and backwaters, heavy monsoon rains — and, for two thousand years, the world's chief source of black pepper.

Trade and history

Roman gold poured into ports such as Muziris for pepper, as Pliny complained; the coast appears in the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea and traded with Arabia, China and the Mediterranean. Its harbours hosted ancient Jewish, Christian (traditionally founded by St Thomas) and Muslim communities. Calicut under the Zamorin was the great medieval emporium; Vasco da Gama's landfall there in 1498 opened the European age of the spice trade, followed by Portuguese, Dutch, French and British contests for the coast.

Geography and culture

The monsoon arrives first on this coast, sustaining rice paddies, coconut groves and spice gardens. Malayalam is the principal language; society historically organised itself in distinctive matrilineal and caste forms, and the coast produced the martial art kalaripayattu and the Kathakali dance-drama.
