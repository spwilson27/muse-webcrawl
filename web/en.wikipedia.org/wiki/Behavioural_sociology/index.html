Behavioural sociology

Behavioural sociology (American spelling: behavioral sociology) is a branch of sociology that applies the principles of behaviourism and reinforcement to the explanation of social behaviour. Rather than treating social structures or norms as irreducible, behavioural sociologists attempt to show how macro-level social patterns emerge from sequences of individual behaviour shaped by reward, punishment and exchange.

Origins and development

The perspective descends from B. F. Skinner's radical behaviourism and from George C. Homans's exchange theory, set out in Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms (1961), in which Homans proposed that social behaviour is an exchange of goods — material and non-material, such as approval and prestige — governed by propositions derived from operant psychology. Peter M. Blau extended exchange analysis to larger social structures, while later theorists such as Richard Emerson developed exchange-network theory, analysing how dependence and power arise from the structure of exchange relations.

Key ideas

Central concepts include reinforcement schedules in social settings, the matching law, and the emergence of norms and status orders from repeated exchanges. Behavioural sociologists argue that even phenomena like altruism, trust and institutional authority can be modelled, at least in part, as consequences of reinforcement histories and exchange networks. The approach has been criticised for reductionism — for downplaying cognition, culture and meaning — but it remains an influential micro-foundation for parts of analytical sociology and social network analysis.
