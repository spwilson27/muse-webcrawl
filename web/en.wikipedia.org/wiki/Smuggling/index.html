Smuggling

Smuggling is the illegal transportation of objects, substances, information or people, such as out of a building, into a prison, or across an international border, in violation of applicable laws or other regulations. Smuggling is most commonly motivated by profit — to evade customs duties or taxes, or to move goods whose trade is banned — and has existed since the first taxes and prohibitions.

Motivations and types

There are various motivations to smuggle. These include the participation in illegal trade, such as the drug trade, illegal immigration and human smuggling, tax evasion, getting contraband to a prisoner in a prison, or the theft of the items being smuggled. Examples include:

  • Contraband goods: narcotics, weapons, endangered-species products (ivory, pangolin scales), counterfeit goods, cigarettes and alcohol where duties are high.
  • People: human smuggling (consensual illegal border crossing for a fee), distinct from human trafficking which involves coercion.
  • Tax and duty evasion: undervaluation, mislabelling, and routing goods through remote beaches, tunnels, drones, ultralight aircraft, semi-submersibles or concealed compartments in vehicles and shipping containers.
  • Sanctions evasion: moving embargoed goods such as oil or technology to or from sanctioned states.

History

Smuggling flourished wherever tariffs were high: in 18th-century Britain, tea, brandy, gin and lace were landed by organised gangs on the Kent and Sussex coasts, fought by Revenue officers and inspiring Kipling's poem "A Smuggler's Song". During American Prohibition (1920–1933), "rum-runners" landed Canadian and Caribbean liquor offshore while bootleggers distributed it inland. Wartime rationing, post-colonial trade controls, and modern drug prohibition have each generated their own smuggling economies.

Enforcement

Customs and border agencies counter smuggling with inspection, intelligence, scanners, sniffer dogs and penalties including fines, seizure of goods and vehicles, and imprisonment. Definitions and penalties vary: many jurisdictions distinguish commercial smuggling from trivial personal evasion, and some goods (such as undeclared currency above a threshold) are contraband only because they were not declared.
