Scottish Chaucerians

The Scottish Chaucerians were a group of Scottish poets of the 15th and early 16th centuries who wrote under the influence of Geoffrey Chaucer, often acknowledging him explicitly as their master. Writing in Middle Scots, they adapted Chaucerian forms — especially rhyme royal dream visions and courtly narrative — to Scottish subjects and language. They form a central part of the group known as the Makars.

Older Scots verse manuscripts preserve the Chaucerians' work.

Principal figures

  • Robert Henryson (c. 1425–c. 1500), whose Testament of Cresseid continues Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde, and whose Morall Fabillis rework Aesop.
  • William Dunbar (c. 1460–c. 1520), court poet to James IV, whose works range from the allegorical The Thrissil and the Rois to the comic Dance of the Sevin Deidly Synnis.
  • Gavin Douglas (c. 1474–1522), whose Eneados (1513) was the first complete translation of Virgil's Aeneid into English or Scots.
  • King James I of Scotland, author of The Kingis Quair, a Chaucerian dream vision written during his captivity in England.

Characteristics

The Scots Chaucerians combined imitation of Chaucer with a distinctive aureate diction and a mixture of high courtly style and earthy realism. Literary historians debate how derivative the label "Chaucerian" is: the Scots poets were also in dialogue with Lydgate and with continental traditions, and their work is now valued as original literature in its own right.
