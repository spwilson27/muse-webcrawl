Polarimetry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polarimetry is the measurement and interpretation of the polarization of transverse waves, most notably electromagnetic waves, such as radio or light waves. Typically polarimetry is done on electromagnetic waves that have traveled through or have been reflected, refracted or diffracted by some material in order to characterize that object. In chemistry, polarimetry most often refers to measuring the optical rotation of plane-polarised light by a solution of chiral molecules, using an instrument called a polarimeter.

Optical rotation

Chiral substances rotate the plane of linearly polarised light. The observed rotation depends on the concentration of the sample, the path length, the temperature, and the wavelength of light (classically the sodium D line at 589 nm). The specific rotation is a standardised physical property used to identify and assess the purity of compounds such as sugars, amino acids and pharmaceuticals. The sugar industry historically relied on polarimetry (saccharimetry) to determine sucrose content, and the International Sugar Scale is defined in polarimetric terms.

Techniques and instruments

A classical polarimeter consists of a light source, a polariser, a sample tube, a rotatable analyser and an eyepiece or detector. Modern automatic polarimeters use photoelectric detection and Faraday modulation to achieve high precision. Related techniques include circular dichroism spectroscopy and the measurement of Stokes parameters for fully characterising a light beam's polarisation state, employed in astronomy, remote sensing and materials science.

Applications

  • Chemistry and pharmacy — enantiomeric purity and identity of chiral compounds.
  • Sugar and food industries — sucrose and invert sugar determination.
  • Astronomy — studying magnetic fields, scattering atmospheres and interstellar dust through the polarisation of starlight.
  • Remote sensing — polarimetric radar and lidar for surface classification.
