Critical chain

Critical chain scheduling protects the project with an aggregated buffer at the end.

The critical chain is the central concept of critical chain project management (CCPM), a method of planning and managing projects developed by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and presented in his 1997 business novel Critical Chain. Derived from the Theory of Constraints, the critical chain is defined as the longest chain of dependent tasks in a project once both precedence dependencies and resource dependencies are taken into account — distinguishing it from the traditional critical path, which considers only task precedence.

Method

In CCPM, the safety margin habitually padded into individual task estimates is stripped out (tasks are scheduled at their aggressive, roughly 50%-confidence durations) and the removed safety is aggregated into explicit buffers: a project buffer at the end of the critical chain, feeding buffers where non-critical chains join it, and resource buffers that warn resources of upcoming critical work. Execution is monitored through "buffer management" — tracking how much of the buffer has been consumed relative to how much of the chain is complete, often displayed as a fever chart.

Rationale and reception

The method targets common behaviours Goldratt blamed for project delay: student syndrome (starting work at the last moment), Parkinson's law (work expanding to fill available time), and multitasking by shared resources. CCPM has been adopted in construction, defence, software and product development; academic evaluations report improved on-time completion in case studies, while critics argue that evidence is largely anecdotal and that aggressive estimates may not always be feasible.
