Generative art

Generative art is art that in whole or in part has been created with the use of an autonomous system — most commonly a computer program whose rules, randomness and parameters produce the work, rather than the artist drawing each element directly. The artist designs the system, sets constraints, and selects or curates outputs; the system generates compositions, images, sounds or texts that could not be fully predicted in advance.

History

Precursors include the chance procedures of Dada, John Cage's aleatoric music, and Sol Le Witt's instruction-based wall drawings. Computer generative art began in the 1960s: Georg Nees exhibited computer graphics generated on a Zuse Z22 in Stuttgart in 1965, and Frieder Nake, A. Michael Noll and Vera Molnár — who worked first with a "machine imaginaire" of hand-executed rules, then with plotters — established algorithmic plotter drawing as an art form. Harold Cohen's AARON program, begun in the 1970s, autonomously produced drawings and paintings for decades.

The 2000s saw generative methods spread through creative-coding tools such as Processing (2001) and its web successor p5.js, and through "creative AI": generative adversarial networks (2014) and diffusion models enabled image synthesis from text prompts, moving generative art into mainstream debate about authorship and originality. From 2021, platforms such as Art Blocks sold on-chain generative works minted as NFTs, with artists like Tyler Hobbs (Fidenza) reaching wide audiences.

Techniques

Common techniques include pseudo-randomness with curated seeds, L-systems and fractals, flow fields and particle systems, cellular automata such as Conway's Game of Life, genetic algorithms in which audiences or artists select "fittest" outputs, and machine-learning models. Works may be static images, plotter drawings, animations, installations responding to live data, or generative music and literature.
