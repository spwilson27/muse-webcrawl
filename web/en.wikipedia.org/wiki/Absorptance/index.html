Absorptance

Absorptance is a measure of the effectiveness of a surface or material in absorbing electromagnetic radiation. It is defined as the ratio of the radiant flux absorbed by the surface to the radiant flux incident upon it, and is a dimensionless quantity between 0 and 1. Absorptance must be distinguished from absorbance, a logarithmic quantity used in spectroscopy for the attenuation of light passing through a material.

Definitions

For radiation incident on a body, conservation of energy gives α + ρ + τ = 1, where ρ is reflectance and τ is transmittance. The spectral absorptance α(λ) is evaluated at a single wavelength, while total absorptance integrates over a spectrum, weighted by the source distribution — so a surface's absorptance for sunlight differs from its absorptance for room-temperature infrared. For an opaque surface τ = 0 and α = 1 − ρ.

Kirchhoff's law

By Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation, at thermal equilibrium the absorptance of a surface at a given wavelength and direction equals its emissivity under the same conditions (α = ε). This is why good absorbers are good emitters: a black surface that absorbs nearly all incident radiation also radiates maximally, and a perfect absorber (α = 1 at all wavelengths) is the idealised black body.

Applications

Absorptance is a key parameter in solar energy engineering, where absorber coatings are designed for high solar absorptance but low infrared emittance (selective surfaces), in radiative heat transfer and building physics, and in remote sensing, where spectral absorptance features identify materials. It differs from the spectroscopist's absorbance A = −log₁₀(transmittance), which describes light passing through a sample rather than the fraction absorbed at a surface.
