The castles of Great Britain and Ireland number in the thousands, built mainly between the Norman Conquest of 1066 and the 17th century. They served as fortresses, administrative centres and aristocratic residences, and they remain among the most visible monuments of medieval Britain and Ireland.

Development

The Normans introduced the motte-and-bailey castle, rapidly erected in earth and timber, and then permanent stone keeps such as the White Tower at the Tower of London. Edward I's conquest of Wales produced the great concentric castles of Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech and Beaumaris. In Scotland, tower houses proliferated; in Ireland, Norman and later Gaelic lords built tower houses across the countryside. Design evolved with gunpowder artillery toward lower, thicker fortifications, while many later "castles" — such as Culzean or Inveraray — were country houses in romantic castellated style.

Fate and preservation

Many castles were destroyed in the Wars of the Roses, the Wars of the Three Kingdoms (when Oliver Cromwell "slighted" numerous strongholds) and Irish conflicts. Others survived as prisons, barracks or residences. Today, bodies such as English Heritage, Historic Environment Scotland, Cadw and the Office of Public Works care for the major ruins and inhabited castles, and castle tourism is a significant industry.
