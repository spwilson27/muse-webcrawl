Fraud

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, fraud is intentional deception intended to result in financial or personal gain for the deceiver or damage to another individual. The specific legal definition varies by jurisdiction; fraud is a crime in most and also a civil wrong (a tort) that grounds damages and rescission of transactions procured by it. Core elements in common law are a false representation of material fact, made knowingly or recklessly, intended to be relied upon, actually and justifiably relied upon, causing loss.

Forms and scale

The family is vast: consumer and telemarketing fraud; financial frauds (Ponzi and pyramid schemes, accounting fraud, securities fraud); public-procurement and benefits fraud; insurance fraud; identity theft and payment fraud — increasingly industrialised online (phishing, business-email compromise, romance and investment "pig-butchering" scams) and attributed by investigators substantially to organised criminal networks. Official statistics undercount it badly (victims' shame, corporate non-reporting), yet even so fraud is, in countries that measure it, the most common crime category experienced by individuals (roughly two in five crimes in England and Wales by the Crime Survey's recent measures, a figure that transformed British policing debates). Prevention doctrine emphasises the fraud triangle (pressure, opportunity, rationalisation) and situational controls over after-the-fact prosecution, which recovers little.
