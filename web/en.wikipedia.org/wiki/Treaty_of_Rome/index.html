Treaty of Rome

The Treaty of Rome, officially the Treaty establishing the European Economic Community (TEEC), is an international agreement that led to the founding of the European Economic Community (EEC). It was signed on 25 March 1957 by Belgium, France, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and West Germany, and entered into force on 1 January 1958. Together with the Euratom Treaty signed the same day, it forms the legal foundation of what became the European Union.

Provisions

The treaty established a common market with the free movement of goods, persons, services, and capital, a customs union with a common external tariff, and common policies for agriculture and transport. It created the institutions of the Community: a Commission, a Council of Ministers, a European Parliamentary Assembly, and a Court of Justice. The EEC's stated aim was to promote "harmonious development of economic activities, a continuous and balanced expansion, an increase in stability, an accelerated raising of the standard of living and closer relations between the States belonging to it."

Development

The EEC merged its institutions with the other European Communities in 1967, and the treaty was substantially amended by the Single European Act, the Maastricht Treaty — which renamed it the Treaty establishing the European Community — and the Treaty of Lisbon, under which it became the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU). The 50th and 60th anniversaries of the signing were marked by commemorative summits in Berlin (2007) and Rome (2017). See also European Economic Community and Treaties of the European Union.
