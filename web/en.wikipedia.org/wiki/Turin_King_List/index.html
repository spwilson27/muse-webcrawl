The Turin King List, also known as the Turin Royal Canon, is an ancient Egyptian hieratic papyrus thought to date from the reign of Pharaoh Ramesses II during the New Kingdom. It is held in the Museo Egizio (Egyptian Museum) in Turin, Italy, and is the most extensive surviving list of kings compiled by the ancient Egyptians.

Content

The papyrus lists the names of rulers of Egypt from the earliest times down to the time of its writing, giving the length of each reign in years, months and days. Unusually, it begins with gods and mythical kings before passing to mortal rulers, and it includes rulers omitted from other, more selective lists, such as the Hyksos kings and rulers of the intermediate periods — though it pointedly marks the Hyksos as foreign kings. The list is grouped into dynastic clusters that broadly correspond to the dynasties later described by Manetho.

History of the document

The papyrus was found in fragments at Deir el-Medina, near Thebes, by the Italian traveller Bernardino Drovetti around 1820 and was acquired by the museum in Turin in 1824. It had been badly damaged in transport, and scholars including Champollion, Gustav Seyffarth and later Sir Alan Gardiner worked to reconstruct the order of its fragments. Because parts remain lost or unplaced, the list's reconstruction — and hence Egyptian chronology — is still debated.

Significance

Together with the Abydos King List, the Karnak King List, the Saqqara Tablet and Manetho's history, the Turin Canon is a primary source for the sequence of Egyptian rulers. Its reign lengths are crucial for constructing the chronology of ancient Egypt, particularly for the First and Second Intermediate Periods.
