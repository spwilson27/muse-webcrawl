Empire of Trebizond

Empire of Trebizond

Years: 1204 – 1461

Capital: Trebizond (Trabzon)

Languages: Greek (Pontic), Laz

Religion: Eastern Orthodox Christianity

First emperor: Alexios I Megas Komnenos

Last emperor: David Megas Komnenos

The Empire of Trebizond, or Trapezuntine Empire, was one of the three Byzantine Greek successor states formed after the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople in 1204. Centred on the city of Trebizond (modern Trabzon) on the south-eastern shore of the Black Sea, it survived for more than 250 years — outliving the restored Byzantine Empire itself by eight years — until its conquest by the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II in 1461.

Foundation

The empire was founded by the brothers Alexios and David Komnenos, grandsons of the deposed Byzantine emperor Andronikos I, who had taken refuge in Georgia. With the support of their aunt, Queen Tamar of Georgia, they seized Trebizond in April 1204, just before Constantinople fell to the Crusaders. Alexios was proclaimed emperor, claiming the Roman imperial title in rivalry with the Empire of Nicaea and the Latin Empire. David expanded westward along the Black Sea coast before being pushed back by the Nicaeans under Theodore I Laskaris.

History

At its height under Alexios I, the empire controlled the southern Black Sea coast from Sinope to the Georgian frontier, plus outposts in the Crimea (the Perateia). The loss of Sinope to the Seljuk Turks in 1214 confined it to the eastern Pontos. Trebizond grew rich as the western terminus of a branch of the Silk Road: trade with Persia and Central Asia flowed through its port, and Genoese and Venetian merchants maintained colonies there. The court of the "Grand Komnenoi" (Megas Komnenos), as its rulers styled themselves, was famous for scholarship and for dynastic marriages — Trapezuntine princesses were sought after by Muslim and Christian rulers alike.

The empire survived the Mongol invasions by accepting tributary status, endured civil wars in the 14th century, and navigated between the rising Ottoman Empire and its neighbours for a century. After the fall of Constantinople in 1453 its days were numbered: in 1461 Mehmed II marched on Trebizond, and the last emperor, David, surrendered the city on 15 August 1461. David was later executed in Constantinople in 1463.

Legacy

Trebizond preserved a distinctive Pontic Greek culture, visible in monuments such as the church of Hagia Sophia of Trebizond with its celebrated frescoes. Its romantic image in later literature — a remote, fabulously wealthy Greek empire on the edge of the known world — far exceeds its modest territorial size.
