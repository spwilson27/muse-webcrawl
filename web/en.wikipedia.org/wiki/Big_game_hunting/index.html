Big-game hunting

Big-game hunting is the hunting of large animals — such as deer, elk, moose, bear, and in Africa the so-called "Big Five" (lion, elephant, buffalo, leopard and rhinoceros) — for trophies, sport, meat or commerce. It is regulated in most jurisdictions through licensing, seasons, bag limits and restrictions on methods, and it remains the subject of ethical and conservation debate.

Big-game hunting is closely regulated in most countries.

History

Hunting large animals for subsistence is as old as humanity, but recreational trophy hunting developed in the nineteenth century among colonial elites; the safaris of figures such as Theodore Roosevelt, who collected specimens for the Smithsonian in 1909–1910, epitomised the era. The Boone and Crockett Club, founded in 1887, promoted "fair chase" ethics and record keeping that helped seed the North American conservation movement.

Conservation role

Supporters argue that licence fees and trophy fees fund habitat protection and anti-poaching work, and that regulated hunting in North America — financed through excise taxes such as the Pittman–Robertson Act of 1937 — underwrote the recovery of deer, elk and waterfowl populations. Critics contend that trophy hunting can harm small populations, disrupt social structures in species such as lions and elephants, and that photographic tourism offers a better alternative.

Methods

Depending on the quarry and the law, methods include spot-and-stalk rifle hunting, hunting with hounds, baiting (where legal), calling, and bowhunting. Dangerous-game hunting in Africa typically requires a professional hunter and large-bore rifles.
