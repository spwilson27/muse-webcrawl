The Northern Wei (386–535), also known as the Tuoba Wei, was an imperial dynasty of China founded by the Tuoba clan of the Xianbei. It ruled northern China during the Northern and Southern dynasties period, unifying the north in 439 and surviving until it split into Eastern and Western Wei in 534–535.

History

Tuoba Gui, posthumously Emperor Daowu, founded the state in 386 and proclaimed himself emperor in 398, establishing his capital at Pingcheng (modern Datong). His successors conquered the rival northern states, and by 439 Emperor Taiwu had unified northern China; he also patronised and then, briefly, persecuted Buddhism. Later rulers, notably Emperor Xiaowen (r. 471–499), pursued deep sinicisation: in 494 Xiaowen moved the capital south to Luoyang, banned Xianbei dress and language at court, adopted Chinese surnames (the imperial clan took the name Yuan), and promoted intermarriage. These reforms integrated the Xianbei into Chinese civilisation but alienated the northern garrisons, whose revolts (the Rebellion of the Six Garrisons, 523) fatally weakened the dynasty. In 528 the warlord Erzhu Rong massacred the court, and in 534–535 the empire split between the Gao and Yuwen factions into Eastern Wei and Western Wei.

Culture and legacy

Northern Wei patronage produced the great cave-temple complexes of Yungang (near Pingcheng) and Longmen (near Luoyang), masterpieces of Buddhist sculpture. The dynasty's equal-field land system and its administrative models influenced the later Sui and Tang dynasties, and its sinicisation laid groundwork for China's reunification in 589.
