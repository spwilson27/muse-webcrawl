Anglo-Norman language

The Anglo-Norman language is a dialect of Old Norman that was used in England and to a lesser extent elsewhere in Great Britain during the Anglo-Norman period. It developed from the Norman French brought to England by William the Conqueror and his followers after the Norman Conquest of 1066, and was the language of the English court, administration, and culture for several centuries.

History

After the Conquest, Norman became the prestige language of the aristocracy, while Latin remained the language of record and English continued to be spoken by the majority of the population. Over time, the insular variety of Norman diverged from the continental dialects spoken in Normandy and France, developing distinctive phonological and orthographic features. By the thirteenth century, Anglo-Norman was increasingly a learned, acquired language rather than a mother tongue, taught through grammars and treatises such as Walter of Bibbesworth's Tretiz.

Anglo-Norman remained in use in law (as "Law French"), Parliament, and administration into the fifteenth century, and it profoundly influenced the vocabulary of Middle English: thousands of words relating to government, law, art, literature, and cuisine entered English from Anglo-Norman.

Characteristics

Notable features distinguishing Anglo-Norman from continental Old French include the preservation of the Germanic /w/ sound where Central French developed /gw/ and later /g/ (hence English doublets such as ward/guard and warranty/guarantee), the retention of the /k/ sound before /a/ where Parisian French palatalised it (compare cattle and chattel), and characteristic spellings with ei for continental oi.

Literature

A rich body of Anglo-Norman literature survives, including romances, saints' lives, chronicles, and didactic works. Authors include Marie de France, who wrote her Lais at the English court, the chroniclers Geffrei Gaimar and Jordan Fantosme, and the hagiographer Matthew Paris wrote partly in the language as well. Legal and administrative texts, including the Year Books, continued in Law French long after the spoken language had died out.
