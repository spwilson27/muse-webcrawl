Mele (Hawaiian)

Mele
Cultural originNative Hawaiian
FormChanted or sung poetry
AccompanimentHula; ipu, pahu and other instruments

Mele are Hawaiian songs and chants. The word mele in the Hawaiian language denotes sung or chanted poetic texts, and mele form the verbal core of Hawaiian musical and dance tradition, providing the words to which hula is danced. Historically, mele preserved genealogies, histories, myths, prayers, and praises of chiefs and places, functioning as an oral literature of great sophistication.

Types of mele

  • Mele oli — chanted without dance, including prayers (mele pule) and genealogies (mele koihonua), such as the great creation chant Kumulipo.
  • Mele hula — sung or chanted with dance, accompanied by instruments such as the ipu (gourd) and pahu (sharkskin drum).
  • Mele inoa — name songs composed in honor of a person, especially chiefs.
  • Mele ma'i, mele kanikau (dirges) and others composed for particular occasions.

Composition and structure

Mele employ rich figurative language, including kaona — layered, concealed meanings. Composers (haku mele) were highly esteemed, and mele were transmitted orally with exacting standards before being committed to writing in the 19th century. Today mele are studied and performed by hula halau and cultural practitioners as part of the Hawaiian cultural renaissance.
