Leviticus

The Book of Leviticus (from Greek Λευιτικόν, Leuitikón; Hebrew: וַיִּקְרָא, Vayikra, "And He called") is the third book of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) and of the Old Testament. It is also the third book of the Torah (the Pentateuch). Many of its laws concern the Levites and the Aaronide priesthood — hence its Greek name — and it lays out the sacrificial system, the distinction between clean and unclean, and the Holiness Code, which scholars conventionally date to the Priestly source. Rather than narrating events, Leviticus consists almost entirely of legal and ritual instruction given, in the text's presentation, by God to Moses at Mount Sinai.

Contents

Leviticus can be divided into several large blocks:

  • Chapters 1–7: the sacrifices — burnt offerings, grain offerings, peace (well-being) offerings, sin (purification) offerings and guilt (reparation) offerings — prescribed first for the people and then for the priests.
  • Chapters 8–10: the ordination of Aaron and his sons as priests, the first sacrifices at the tabernacle, and the death of Nadab and Abihu, who offered "strange fire".
  • Chapters 11–15: the laws of ritual purity — clean and unclean animals (the dietary laws of kashrut), purification after childbirth, skin diseases (tzaraath, traditionally rendered "leprosy") and mildew in houses, and bodily discharges.
  • Chapter 16: the ritual of Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, including the high priest's entrance into the Holy of Holies and the sending of the scapegoat to Azazel.
  • Chapters 17–26 (the Holiness Code): laws of holiness for all Israel — "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" — covering sacrifice, sexual conduct, ethical commands (including "love your neighbour as yourself", 19:18), festivals, sabbatical and jubilee years, and blessings and curses.
  • Chapter 27: an appendix on vows and valuations.

Themes and interpretation

The book's central concern is the presence of a holy God dwelling among Israel in the tabernacle: holiness must be guarded by boundaries between holy and common, clean and unclean, and atonement must repair breaches. The anthropologist Mary Douglas influentially read the purity laws as a symbolic system mirroring the order of creation. Within Judaism, the sacrificial cult ceased with the destruction of the Second Temple in AD 70, and prayer, repentance and acts of loving-kindness took the place of sacrifice, though the book is still read in full in the annual Torah cycle and stands at the centre of the sequence of weekly portions. In Christian interpretation, the book is read typologically — the Letter to the Hebrews presents Christ's death as the fulfilment of the Day of Atonement ritual — while its ethical commands, especially the love command of 19:18, are treated as enduring. Some of its prohibitions (e.g. 18:22 and 20:13) remain the subject of vigorous contemporary debate.

Composition

Traditional Jewish and Christian belief attributes the book to Moses. Mainstream biblical scholarship, following the documentary hypothesis and its successors, assigns most of Leviticus to the Priestly source (P), composed in the exilic or post-exilic period (6th–5th centuries BC), with the Holiness Code (H) sometimes treated as a distinguishable layer that edited the whole Pentateuch; older material may well be embedded in both.
