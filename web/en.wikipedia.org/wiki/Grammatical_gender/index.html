Grammatical gender

Articles and adjectives change form to agree with a noun's grammatical gender.

In linguistics, grammatical gender is a specific form of noun classification system in which the gender of a noun determines the forms of associated words, such as adjectives, articles and verbs, through agreement. Common systems are masculine/feminine, masculine/feminine/neuter, animate/inanimate, and extensive noun-class systems such as those of the Bantu languages. Grammatical gender need not correspond to natural (biological) gender: German das Mädchen ("the girl") is neuter, while French la table ("the table") is feminine.

Overview

Gender is an inherent property of nouns in gendered languages. A noun's gender is visible chiefly through agreement targets — determiners, adjectives, pronouns and sometimes verbs take different forms according to the gender of the noun they relate to. Assignment of nouns to genders may be partly semantic (words for males typically masculine, words for females feminine) and partly formal (based on phonology or morphology, e.g. Spanish nouns ending in -o mostly masculine, in -a mostly feminine), with a residue assigned arbitrarily.

Proto-Indo-European is reconstructed with three genders — masculine, feminine and neuter — preserved in languages such as German, Russian, Greek, Latin, Sanskrit and Icelandic. Many descendants have simplified: Romance languages generally merged neuter into masculine (Romanian partially excepted), Dutch and the mainland Scandinavian languages merged masculine and feminine into a "common" gender opposed to neuter, and English lost grammatical gender entirely during the Middle English period, retaining natural gender in pronouns only.

Number of genders

  • Two — masculine/feminine (French, Spanish, Italian, Arabic, Hebrew); common/neuter (Dutch, Danish, Swedish).
  • Three — masculine/feminine/neuter (German, Russian, Latin, Greek, most Slavic and Baltic languages, Sanskrit).
  • Animate/inanimate — as in Algonquian languages and, in a layered form, in the historical development of Indo-European.
  • Many noun classes — Bantu languages such as Swahili have ten or more classes, often counted as genders, grouping nouns by semantics (people, plants, objects, abstracts, etc.).
  • None — Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, Persian, Mandarin Chinese and Japanese have no grammatical gender.

Gender and society

Grammatical gender has been debated in language reform: advocates of gender-neutral or gender-inclusive language in French, German, Spanish and other languages have proposed new pronouns, endings and forms (such as the German gender star or Spanish -e), prompting academic and political controversy. Experimental psycholinguistic studies have suggested that grammatical gender can subtly influence speakers' associations with inanimate objects, though the size and robustness of such effects are disputed.
