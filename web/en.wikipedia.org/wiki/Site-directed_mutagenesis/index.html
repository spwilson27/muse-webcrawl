Site-directed mutagenesis

Site-directed mutagenesis is a molecular biology technique used to make specific, intentional changes to the DNA sequence of a gene and its gene products. Unlike random mutagenesis, it allows a researcher to substitute, insert, or delete chosen nucleotides at a defined position, making it a fundamental tool for studying protein structure and function, enzyme mechanisms, and gene regulation.

History and basic method

The technique was developed in the 1970s, notably by Michael Smith, who used synthetic oligonucleotides to introduce mutations into bacteriophage DNA; Smith shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Kary Mullis for this work and for its connection to the polymerase chain reaction (PCR). In the classic approach, a short synthetic primer containing the desired mutation is annealed to single-stranded template DNA and extended by DNA polymerase to create a mutated copy.

PCR-based methods

Most modern protocols are PCR-based and work on double-stranded plasmid DNA. A pair of complementary primers carrying the mutation is used to amplify the entire plasmid; the parental template — which is methylated because it was grown in bacteria — is then digested with the enzyme DpnI, leaving the newly synthesised mutated plasmid to be transformed into E. coli. Commercial kits (such as QuikChange) made this whole-plasmid method routine. Variations include overlap-extension PCR, which can join fragments and introduce mutations at the junction, and methods for making insertions, deletions, and multiple mutations at once. Saturation mutagenesis extends the idea by randomising one or more codons so that all possible amino-acid substitutions at a site can be tested.

Applications

Site-directed mutagenesis underlies protein engineering: altering active-site residues to test catalytic mechanisms, engineering antibodies and enzymes for industry, creating fluorescent reporter variants, and modelling human disease variants in the laboratory. It complements newer genome-editing tools such as CRISPR–Cas9, which can introduce targeted changes in chromosomal DNA in living cells.
