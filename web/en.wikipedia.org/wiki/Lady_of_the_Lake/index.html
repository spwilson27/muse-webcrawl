Lady of the Lake

The Lady of the Lake (French: Dame du Lac, Breton: Itron al Lenn, Welsh: Arglwyddes y Llyn) is a title used by multiple characters in the Matter of Britain, the body of medieval literature and legend surrounding King Arthur. She is an enchantress who plays a pivotal role in many Arthurian stories, most famously as the figure who gives Arthur his sword Excalibur from her underwater realm and who later receives it back when Arthur is mortally wounded.

Roles and identity

The Lady of the Lake is not a single consistent character across the tradition. In some texts she is a benevolent guardian of Arthur; in others she is the seductress who imprisons the wizard Merlin. Common names associated with the figure include Nimue, Viviane, Elaine, and Niniane. In the French Vulgate Cycle (Lancelot-Grail), she raises the infant Lancelot beneath the surface of her lake after his father's death, earning Lancelot the epithet "du Lac" (of the Lake).

Excalibur

In Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Arthur, after shattering his first sword in the fight with King Pellinore, is led by Merlin to a lake where an arm clothed in white samite rises from the water holding a sword and scabbard. Arthur rows out to take them. Near the end of the romance, the dying Arthur orders Sir Bedivere to cast Excalibur back into the lake; after twice hiding the sword, Bedivere finally throws it, and a hand rises to catch it, brandishes it three times, and vanishes beneath the water.

Merlin's fate

In many versions, the Lady learns magic from Merlin, who falls in love with her. Wishing to be free of his unwanted attentions — or, in some tellings, fearing him — she uses his own spells to seal him in a cave, a hawthorn bush, or a tower of air, from which he cannot escape, though his voice can still be heard. This episode explains Merlin's absence from the later parts of Arthur's reign.

Sources

The figure develops across the thirteenth-century French prose romances, especially the Vulgate Estoire de Merlin and Lancelot propre, and later in the Post-Vulgate Cycle and Malory. Some scholars connect her to Welsh and Celtic water deities and to figures such as the goddess-like enchantresses of earlier Brythonic tradition. Walter Scott's 1810 narrative poem The Lady of the Lake, though set in the Scottish Highlands around Loch Katrine, borrowed the title and popularised the phrase in English.
