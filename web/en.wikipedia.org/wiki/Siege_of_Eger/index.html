The Siege of Eger occurred during the Ottoman wars in Europe in 1552, when Ottoman forces under Kara Ahmed Pasha attempted to capture the castle of Eger in northern Hungary. The small Hungarian garrison under Captain István Dobó — roughly 2,000–2,300 soldiers reinforced by peasants and townspeople, including women — withstood a siege of about five weeks by an Ottoman army numbering in the tens of thousands, forcing the attackers to withdraw before winter. The victory became a celebrated episode of Hungarian national resistance.

Background

After the fall of Buda in 1541, Hungary was divided among the Ottomans, the Habsburgs, and Transylvania. In 1552 Suleiman the Magnificent launched a major campaign that captured Timișoara, Szolnok, and other fortresses; Eger, guarding the road to the mining towns of Upper Hungary, was a key remaining stronghold.

The siege

The Ottoman army bombarded the castle heavily and launched repeated assaults. The defenders repelled the attacks, with women famously pouring hot water, pitch, and oil on the attackers. Despite a huge explosion of a powder store and heavy damage, the garrison held out until the Ottomans, hampered by early cold weather and disease, lifted the siege on 17–18 October 1552.

Legacy

The siege was immortalized in Géza Gárdonyi's novel Eclipse of the Crescent Moon (Egri csillagok, 1899), one of Hungary's most popular historical novels, and in the 1968 film of the same name. Eger fell to the Ottomans only later, in 1596, remaining under Ottoman rule until 1687.
