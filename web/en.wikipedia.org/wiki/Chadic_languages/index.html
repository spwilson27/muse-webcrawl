Chadic languages

Chadic languages are spoken around the Lake Chad basin in Nigeria, Niger, Chad and Cameroon.

The Chadic languages form a branch of the Afroasiatic language family. They are spoken in parts of Nigeria, Niger, Chad, Cameroon and the Central African Republic, in the region around Lake Chad from which the family takes its name. With roughly 150–200 languages, Chadic is the most diverse branch of Afroasiatic by number of languages, though most have small speaker populations.

Classification

Chadic is commonly divided into four branches: West Chadic (including Hausa, Ngas, Bole and Ron languages), Biu–Mandara (Central Chadic), East Chadic, and the isolate-like Masa branch. Joseph Greenberg established Chadic as a coordinate branch of Afroasiatic in the mid-20th century; its internal classification remains an active area of research.

Hausa

By far the largest Chadic language is Hausa, a West Chadic language spoken by tens of millions in northern Nigeria and Niger as a first language and by many more across West Africa as a lingua franca of trade. Hausa is written in both Latin (boko) and Arabic-derived (ajami) scripts and has a substantial literary and media presence, including BBC and Voice of America broadcasts.

Features

Chadic languages are generally tonal, with two or three level tones that distinguish words. Many exhibit complex systems of verbal derivation (pluractional verbs, causatives), gender distinctions in pronouns and nouns, and vowel systems with length contrasts. Most smaller Chadic languages are endangered as speakers shift to Hausa, French or English.
