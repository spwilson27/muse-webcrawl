Spontaneous symmetry breaking

Spontaneous symmetry breaking is a phenomenon in which a system governed by symmetric laws settles into a ground state that is not symmetric. The underlying equations retain the symmetry, but the state of lowest energy does not. It is a key concept in condensed matter physics and the Standard Model of particle physics.

Illustration

A standard picture is the "Mexican hat" potential, with energy of the form

V(φ) = −μ²|φ|² + λ|φ|⁴

The potential is rotationally symmetric, but its minima form a circle of radius |φ| = μ/√(2λ). A system resting at any one minimum has chosen a direction, breaking the symmetry spontaneously. In a ferromagnet below the Curie temperature, the same idea applies: the interactions favour alignment equally in every direction, yet the magnetisation points one way.

Goldstone bosons

Goldstone's theorem states that breaking a continuous global symmetry produces massless scalar particles called Nambu–Goldstone bosons — physically, low-energy excitations such as spin waves in magnets or phonons. When the broken symmetry is a local gauge symmetry, the would-be Goldstone bosons are instead "eaten" by the gauge fields, which acquire mass: this is the Higgs mechanism.

In particle physics

In the electroweak theory, the Higgs field's nonzero vacuum expectation value spontaneously breaks the SU(2) × U(1) symmetry to the electromagnetic U(1), giving mass to the W and Z bosons while leaving the photon massless. The associated particle, the Higgs boson, was discovered at CERN's Large Hadron Collider in 2012. Chiral symmetry breaking in quantum chromodynamics similarly explains the lightness of pions as pseudo-Goldstone bosons.

History

The idea grew out of Werner Heisenberg's ferromagnet models and Lev Landau's theory of phase transitions. Yoichiro Nambu imported the concept into particle physics from superconductivity in 1960, and Jeffrey Goldstone, Abdus Salam, Steven Weinberg, Peter Higgs, François Englert, Robert Brout, and others developed the relativistic and gauge theories in the early 1960s.
