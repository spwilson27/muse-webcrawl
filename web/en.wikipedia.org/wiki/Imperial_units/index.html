The imperial system of units, imperial system or imperial units (also known as British Imperial) is the system of units first defined in the British Weights and Measures Act 1824 and continued to be developed through a series of Weights and Measures Acts and amendments.

The imperial system developed from earlier English units as did the related system of United States customary units. Imperial units were formerly used in most British colonies and are still used for some purposes in the United Kingdom and a few other countries.

Principal units

Length is measured in inches (12 to a foot), feet (3 to a yard), yards (1,760 to a mile), with nautical miles used at sea. Mass is measured in ounces (16 to a pound), pounds (14 to a stone), stones, hundredweights (112 pounds), and tons (2,240 pounds). Volume uses fluid ounces, pints (20 fluid ounces), quarts, and gallons (8 pints).

Metrication

From 1965, the United Kingdom officially adopted the metric system in a gradual process; imperial units remain legal for certain uses, such as miles on road signs, pints for draught beer and milk, and feet and inches in many informal contexts. Most other former British territories have fully metricated, though imperial units persist colloquially in Canada and elsewhere.

Comparison with US customary units

The US customary system derives from pre-1824 English units and differs from imperial in volume measures (the US gallon is smaller) and in the hundredweight (100 lb in the US versus 112 lb imperial) and ton (2,000 lb versus 2,240 lb). In 1959 the English-speaking nations agreed on common definitions for the yard (0.9144 m exactly) and pound (0.45359237 kg exactly).
