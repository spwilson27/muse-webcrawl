National Park Service rustic

National Park Service rustic — sometimes simply called parkitecture — is a style of architecture that developed in the early and middle 20th century in the United States National Park Service (NPS). The style attempted to build structures that blended harmoniously with their natural surroundings, using local materials such as native stone and timber, hand craftsmanship, and forms that echoed pioneer or regional vernacular building traditions.

Development

Early exemplars appeared before the NPS was founded in 1916, notably in the great railroad lodges of Glacier and Yellowstone National Parks. Under landscape architects such as Thomas Chalmers Vint, the Park Service formalized the style in the 1920s and 1930s. Design principles were codified in Albert Good's 1938 publication Park and Recreation Structures. During the Great Depression, labour from the Civilian Conservation Corps built rustic structures in national and state parks across the country.

Characteristics

Rustic buildings feature rough-hewn logs with exaggerated scale, massive stone masonry laid to look natural, battered (sloping) stone foundations, low-pitched roofs with wide eaves, and siting that subordinates the structure to the landscape. Examples include the Old Faithful Inn in Yellowstone, the lodges of the Grand Canyon, and countless trailside museums, ranger stations, and comfort stations.

Influence

The style strongly influenced state park architecture and mountain lodge design throughout the United States, and many NPS rustic structures are listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Interest in "parkitecture" revived late in the 20th century as the buildings themselves became valued historic resources.
