Pietro Alessandro Gaspare Scarlatti (2 May 1660 – 22 October 1725) was an Italian Baroque composer, especially famous for his operas and chamber cantatas. Regarded as the founder of the Neapolitan school of opera, he was the father of two other noted composers, Domenico Scarlatti and Pietro Filippo Scarlatti.

Life

Scarlatti was born in Palermo, then part of the Kingdom of Sicily, and studied in Rome, probably with Giacomo Carissimi. His first opera was produced in Rome in 1679, and he became maestro di cappella to Queen Christina of Sweden. In 1684 he moved to Naples as maestro di cappella to the Spanish viceroy, beginning the long association that made Naples a centre of operatic innovation. After a period in Rome (1703–1708) directing music at Santa Maria Maggiore and for Cardinal Ottoboni, he returned to Naples, remaining there until his death in 1725.

Music

Scarlatti composed more than 100 operas — of which a substantial fraction survive, including La Griselda (1721) — as well as over 600 chamber cantatas, oratorios, masses, motets, serenatas and keyboard toccatas. He standardised the Italian opera overture in three movements (fast–slow–fast), helped establish the da capo aria as the centrepiece of opera seria, and advanced the use of accompanied recitative and the orchestra as an expressive partner to the voice. His operas balance formal elegance with melodic grace, and his counterpoint — visible in his church music — reflects his Roman training.

Legacy

Scarlatti's pupils and imitators carried the Neapolitan style across Europe, shaping the opera of Handel's generation and beyond. Though overshadowed in modern fame by his son Domenico's keyboard sonatas, Alessandro is now recognised as one of the most prolific and influential opera composers of the Baroque era.
