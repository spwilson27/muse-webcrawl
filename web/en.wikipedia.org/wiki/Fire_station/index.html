Fire station

A fire station with its apparatus bays facing the street.

A fire station (also called a fire house, fire hall, firemen's hall, or engine house) is a structure or other area for storing firefighting apparatus such as fire engines and related vehicles, personal protective equipment, fire hoses and other specialized equipment. Fire stations frequently contain working and living space for firefighters and support staff.

In large cities, fire stations may be named for the primary fire companies and apparatus housed there, such as "Ladder 1". The naming of individual stations varies; many are identified by numbers, districts, or the neighbourhoods they serve.

Design and facilities

A fire station will normally have:

  • An apparatus bay, a large garage-like area where vehicles are stored, with large doors opening to the street
  • An office and watch room for the officer on duty and for receiving alarms
  • Living quarters for on-duty crews, including a kitchen, day room, dormitory or individual bedrooms, and bathrooms
  • Areas for decontamination and storage of protective clothing and breathing apparatus
  • Training facilities, sometimes including a drill tower
  • A hose tower or drying room in older stations, used to hang and dry hoses after use

Fire pole

The fire pole (also called a sliding pole or firefighter's pole) allows firefighters to descend quickly from upper-floor living quarters to the apparatus bay. Invented in 1878 by David Kenyon of Chicago's Engine Company 21, it replaced spiral staircases and became an iconic feature of stations, though it has fallen out of favour in new construction because of injury risk and is sometimes replaced by slides or direct ground-floor layouts.

Occupancy

Career fire stations are occupied around the clock by firefighters working in shifts, commonly 24 hours on duty followed by 48 hours off, or variations of that pattern. Volunteer and retained (on-call) stations may be occupied only when crews respond to an alarm, with firefighters summoned from home or work by pager, siren, or radio.

History

Dedicated fire stations emerged in the 19th century as organized municipal fire departments replaced volunteer companies and insurance brigades. Early stations were designed for horse-drawn steam pumpers, with stables at the rear and hay lofts above; the transition to motor apparatus in the early 20th century removed the need for stables but retained the basic layout of bays fronting the street with living quarters above or behind.
