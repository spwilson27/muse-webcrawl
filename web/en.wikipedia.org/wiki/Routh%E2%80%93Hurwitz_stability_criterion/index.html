Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion

In the control system theory, the Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion is a mathematical test that is a necessary and sufficient condition for the stability of a linear time-invariant (LTI) control system. It determines whether all the roots of the characteristic polynomial of a system have negative real parts — without actually solving for the roots.

History

The criterion was proposed independently by Edward John Routh in 1877, who won the Adams Prize for his essay on the stability of motion, and by Adolf Hurwitz in 1895, who derived it in the course of studying the stability of steam-engine governors. It is equivalent to the earlier Routh array procedure still taught in control engineering courses.

Method

Given a characteristic polynomial with real coefficients, the Routh array is a triangular table constructed from alternating even- and odd-indexed coefficients in its first two rows, with later rows computed from the two rows above. The system is stable if and only if every entry in the first column of the array has the same sign (conventionally positive); the number of sign changes in the first column equals the number of roots in the right half of the complex plane. Special cases — a zero in the first column, or an entire row of zeros — are handled by epsilon substitution and by differentiating the auxiliary polynomial from the preceding row, which also reveals roots symmetric about the origin, such as pure imaginary pairs that mark marginal stability.

Applications

Beyond feedback control, the criterion is used in circuit analysis, mechanical vibration and economics wherever the stability of a linear dynamic system reduces to the signs of polynomial roots, and it underlies results on the maximum gain for which a system remains stable.
