Polemon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polemon (Greek: Πολέμων) is the name of several notable figures of the ancient world, including kings of Pontus and Cilicia, the Athenian philosopher Polemon — a pupil of Xenocrates who became head (scholarch) of Plato's Academy around 314/313 BC — and Marcus Antonius Polemo, the 2nd-century AD sophist of Laodicea.

Polemon of Athens

Polemon of Athens (c. 350 – 270/269 BC), son of Philostratus, was a celebrated convert to philosophy. According to the biographical tradition in Diogenes Laërtius, the young Polemon was a notorious drunkard who, bursting into Xenocrates' lecture, was so struck by the discourse on temperance that he reformed and devoted himself to philosophy. He succeeded Xenocrates as scholarch of the Academy, emphasised living according to nature, and wrote on ethics; his pupils included Crates of Athens and Crantor, and through them the young Zeno of Citium, founder of Stoicism, heard his lectures.

Polemon I of Pontus

Polemon I (died 8 BC) was a client king of the Roman Empire in Pontus, Cilicia and the Bosporan Kingdom. The son of the rhetorician Zeno of Laodicea, he was rewarded by Mark Antony and later confirmed by Augustus, receiving Pontus (thereafter Pontus Polemoniacus) and marrying Dynamis, widow of the Bosporan king Asander; later he married Pythodorida of Tralles. He died campaigning against the Aspurgians on the northern Black Sea coast, and his kingdom was divided among his heirs.

Polemo of Laodicea

Marcus Antonius Polemo (c. AD 90 – 144), of Laodicea on the Lycus, was one of the most famous sophists of the Second Sophistic, a favourite of the emperors Trajan and Hadrian and a benefactor of Smyrna, where he taught and was buried. His surviving works include declamations in the voices of the Athenian war-dead fathers Callimachus and Cynaegirus, and a treatise on physiognomy preserved through later adaptations.

Other bearers

  • Polemon II of Pontus (died c. AD 74), last king of Pontus, who ceded his kingdom to Nero.
  • Polemon of Troy (fl. 2nd century BC), a Peripatetic geographer noted for cataloguing votive offerings and inscriptions across Greece.
