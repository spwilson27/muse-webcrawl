Aphorism

Aphorisms are concise statements of principle, often preserved in collections and commonplace books.

An aphorism (from Greek aphorismos, "a marking off", also "rejection, banishment") is a concise, terse, laconic or memorable expression of a general truth or principle. Aphorisms are often handed down by tradition from generation to generation, and are distinguished from other short sayings by their perceived truth and wit.

History

The term was first used in the Aphorisms of Hippocrates, a series of propositions concerning the symptoms and diagnosis of disease and the art of medicine — the first of which begins "Life is short, and art long" (Ars longa, vita brevis). The form was taken up for moral and philosophical reflection: the Maximes of La Rochefoucauld, the Pensées of Pascal, and the aphorisms of Georg Christoph Lichtenberg's waste books are canonical examples. In the 19th and 20th centuries, Friedrich Nietzsche made the aphorism a central philosophical form, notably in Human, All Too Human; other notable aphorists include Oscar Wilde, Karl Kraus, Emil Cioran, Stanisław Jerzy Lec and Nicolás Gómez Dávila.

Related forms

The aphorism overlaps with several neighbouring forms. A maxim is a rule of conduct; a proverb or adage is a traditional saying expressing common experience; an epigram is a witty, often satirical couplet or remark; an apophthegm is a pithy saying attributed to a specific person. A cliché may be regarded as an aphorism that has lost its edge through overuse. In Chinese and other East Asian traditions, the Analects of Confucius and the four-character chengyu idioms perform comparable functions.
