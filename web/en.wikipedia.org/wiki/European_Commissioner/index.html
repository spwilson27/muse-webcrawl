European Commissioner

A European Commissioner is a member of the European Commission, the executive branch of the European Union. Each member state of the EU nominates one commissioner, giving a college of 27 members, including the President of the Commission and the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy, who serves as a Vice-President.

Commissioners do not represent their home countries. Under the treaties, they are required to act independently, in the general interest of the Union, and must neither seek nor take instructions from any government or other body. Each commissioner is assigned a portfolio of policy responsibilities by the President — such as competition, trade, climate action, or the internal market — and is supported by a Directorate-General of the Commission's civil service.

Appointment

Following the European Parliament elections, the European Council proposes a candidate for President of the Commission, whom the European Parliament elects. The President-elect then selects the other commissioners from lists of candidates put forward by the member states. Each nominee appears before the relevant committee of the European Parliament for a confirmation hearing, and the college as a whole must be approved by a vote of the Parliament before taking office for a five-year term.

Role and powers

The Commission holds the near-exclusive right of legislative initiative in the EU: it drafts proposals that are then adopted by the Parliament and the Council. Commissioners are responsible for implementing EU law within their portfolios, for managing the EU budget programmes in their areas, and for enforcing the treaties — including bringing infringement proceedings against member states before the Court of Justice of the European Union. As "guardian of the treaties", the Commission also enforces competition law and can impose large fines on companies.

History

The office descends from the nine-member High Authority of the European Coal and Steel Community, created in 1952 under Jean Monnet. Successive enlargements expanded the college; for a period the larger member states each appointed two commissioners, a practice ended by the Treaty of Nice and the 2004 enlargement, which fixed the principle of one commissioner per member state.
