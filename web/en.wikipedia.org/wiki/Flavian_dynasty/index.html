The Flavian dynasty was a Roman imperial dynasty that ruled the Roman Empire from AD 69 to 96. It comprised three emperors of the gens Flavia — Vespasian and his sons Titus and Domitian — who came to power out of the civil wars of the Year of the Four Emperors (69) and restored stability, rebuilt Rome's finances and monumental centre, and extended Roman rule in Britain and Germany, before Domitian's assassination in 96 ended the line.

Rise

After Nero's suicide in 68, the emperors Galba, Otho and Vitellius fell in rapid succession. Vespasian, a capable general of relatively humble equestrian stock from Reate, had been sent by Nero to suppress the Jewish revolt in Judaea. Proclaimed emperor by the eastern legions on 1 July 69, he defeated Vitellius's forces (his son Titus completing the war in Judaea) and entered Rome in 70. The Senate granted his powers by the lex de imperio Vespasiani.

Vespasian (69–79)

Vespasian restored the treasury — famously taxing public urinals ("pecunia non olet") — purged and restocked the Senate, and launched a major building programme to legitimise the new dynasty, beginning the Flavian Amphitheatre (the Colosseum) on the site of Nero's Golden House lake. In AD 70 Titus captured Jerusalem and destroyed the Second Temple, an event commemorated by the Arch of Titus and by Josephus's history.

Titus (79–81)

Titus, already famous as conqueror of Jerusalem, reigned briefly but won popularity, completing and dedicating the Colosseum (80) and responding energetically to two disasters: the eruption of Vesuvius in 79, which buried Pompeii and Herculaneum, and a fire and plague in Rome in 80. He died of fever in 81.

Domitian (81–96)

The younger son Domitian proved an efficient but autocratic administrator: he strengthened the frontiers (the Chatti campaign, the Limes Germanicus, Agricola's conquests in Britain), fought expensive wars on the Danube against the Dacians, revalued the coinage, and built lavishly, including the great palace on the Palatine. His later reign darkened into treason trials and executions of senators; court conspirators assassinated him on 18 September 96. The Senate damned his memory and elevated the elderly Nerva, beginning the Nerva–Antonine period.

Legacy

The Flavians demonstrated that an emperor could be made outside Rome and from outside the old aristocracy, and set patterns of dynastic propaganda, amphitheatre-building and frontier organisation that later emperors inherited.
