The Radcliffe Camera (colloquially the "Rad Cam") is a landmark building in Oxford, England, designed by James Gibbs in the English Palladian and Baroque styles and built in 1737–1749 to house the Radcliffe Science Library. It stands in Radcliffe Square, in the centre of the city, and now serves as a reading room of the Bodleian Library.

The word camera is Latin for "room" or "chamber". The building is one of the most recognisable symbols of the University of Oxford, and is notable as an early example of a circular library building in England.

History

The building was funded by a bequest from Dr John Radcliffe (1652–1714), physician to William III and Mary II, who left £40,000 for a new library. Francis Smith of Warwick won the initial commission, but after his death the design passed to James Gibbs, whose circular domed design was built between 1737 and 1749. The Camera was originally an independent science library; in 1860 it was taken over by the Bodleian Library, and in 1861 its scientific collections moved to the new University Museum, after which it became the reading room it remains today.

Architecture

The building consists of a rusticated circular drum on a square podium, with three storeys, engaged Corinthian columns and a lead-covered dome. It is constructed in local Headington stone. The interior comprises the Upper and Lower Camera reading rooms, connected underground to the Old Bodleian by the Gladstone Link, opened in 2011.

Use today

The Radcliffe Camera is not open to the general public; access is restricted to Bodleian Library readers, principally Oxford students and researchers. It chiefly holds books in English literature, history and theology kept in high demand by undergraduates.
