Solid-state relay

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A solid-state relay (SSR) is an electronic switching device that switches a load on or off when a small external voltage is applied across its control terminals. Unlike an electromechanical relay, it contains no moving parts: switching is performed by semiconductor devices such as thyristors, triacs, diodes, or transistors. The term is used in analogy to the electromechanical relay, but with "solid state" emphasizing the purely electronic construction.

Operation

In a typical AC solid-state relay, the input signal drives an LED inside an opto-isolator. Light from the LED triggers a phototriac or photovoltaic stack, which in turn gates a power triac or back-to-back thyristors that carry the load current. This provides galvanic isolation of several kilovolts between the control circuit and the load. DC relays instead use MOSFETs or IGBTs as the output element. Many AC SSRs are zero-crossing types, which turn on only when the AC waveform passes near zero volts, reducing electrical noise and inrush stress.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because there are no contacts to wear, bounce, or arc, SSRs offer very long service life, silent operation, fast switching, and resistance to shock and vibration. They are widely used for heater control in industrial ovens, semiconductor equipment, medical devices, and process control where frequent cycling would destroy mechanical relays.

Their disadvantages include a small voltage drop across the output (typically 1–1.6 V for triac types), which dissipates heat and often requires a heatsink at high currents; a small off-state leakage current; higher cost than equivalent electromechanical relays; and susceptibility to damage from voltage transients. SSRs also tend to fail in the closed (shorted) state, an important consideration in safety-critical designs.
