Education in England is overseen by the United Kingdom Government's Department for Education, and is devolved: England's system differs from those of Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. State-funded schooling is provided mainly through local-authority-maintained schools and academies, alongside a significant independent (fee-paying) sector educating roughly 6–7 percent of pupils.

History

Formal education was long dominated by the Church and by grammar schools and the ancient "public schools". The Elementary Education Act 1870 began state provision of primary schooling; education became free in 1891 and compulsory leaving ages rose in steps — 10, then 12 (1899), 14 (1918), 15 (1947) and 16 (1972). The Education Act 1944 ("Butler Act") created free secondary education for all organised as grammar, technical and secondary modern schools under the eleven-plus examination. From the 1960s most areas reorganised into comprehensive schools. The Education Reform Act 1988 introduced the National Curriculum, key stages and national testing; subsequent reforms under all parties created grant-maintained schools, academies and free schools with greater autonomy, inspected by Ofsted (established 1992).

Structure

Children start primary school in the September after their fourth birthday (Reception) and move to secondary school at 11. The National Curriculum is organised into key stages: KS1 (ages 5–7), KS2 (7–11), KS3 (11–14) and KS4 (14–16), ending with GCSE examinations. Pupils must remain in education or training until 18; most take A-levels or vocational qualifications such as T Levels in sixth forms or further education colleges, before higher education at universities, where English students pay regulated tuition fees supported by student loans.

School types

Alongside community schools run by local authorities, the state sector includes academies and free schools run by trusts, foundation and voluntary aided schools (many with a religious character, mostly Church of England or Roman Catholic), a small number of surviving grammar schools selecting by the eleven-plus, and special schools. Independent schools — including famous "public schools" such as Eton and Harrow — set their own curricula but are inspected and mostly enter pupils for the same GCSE and A-level examinations.
