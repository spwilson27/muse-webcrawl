Hydraulic fracturing

Hydraulic fracturing, or fracking, is a well stimulation technique involving the fracturing of formations in bedrock by a pressurised liquid. The process involves the high-pressure injection of "fracking fluid" (primarily water, containing sand or other proppants suspended with thickening agents) into a wellbore to create cracks in deep-rock formations through which natural gas, petroleum, and brine can flow more freely.

Method

Modern shale fracking combines horizontal drilling with multi-stage fracturing: a well is drilled vertically, then curved to run horizontally through a shale formation for one to two miles or more. Water, sand and chemicals are pumped at high pressure in stages along the lateral section, creating a network of small fractures; the sand (proppant) holds the fractures open so oil and gas can flow.

History and impact

Fracturing was first performed experimentally in Kansas in 1947 and commercialised by Halliburton from 1949, initially in vertical wells. The combination with horizontal drilling, pioneered in the Barnett Shale of Texas in the 1990s–2000s (notably by Mitchell Energy), triggered the US "shale revolution", making the United States the world's largest oil and gas producer and greatly expanding natural gas supply.

Controversy

Fracking is controversial because of concerns about groundwater contamination, wastewater disposal (which has been linked to induced earthquakes), methane leakage, air pollution, water use and local impacts. Supporters cite energy security, lower gas prices, and lower CO₂ emissions when gas displaces coal. Several jurisdictions have banned or restricted the practice, including France, and US states such as New York and Vermont.
