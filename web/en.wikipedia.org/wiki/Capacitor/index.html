A capacitor is a device that stores electrical energy by accumulating electric charges on two closely spaced surfaces that are insulated from each other. It is a passive electronic component with two terminals. The ability of a capacitor to store charge is measured by its capacitance, in farads: C = Q/V, the ratio of charge on each conductor to the voltage between them.

History

The first capacitor was the Leyden jar, developed independently by Ewald Georg von Kleist in 1745 and Pieter van Musschenbroek of Leiden in 1746. Daniel Gralath first combined jars in parallel to increase capacity. The term "capacitor" replaced the older "condenser" during the 20th century.

Types and applications

Common types include ceramic, film, electrolytic (aluminium and tantalum), mica, and supercapacitors, which store far larger charges through electrochemical mechanisms. Capacitors are used for energy storage, smoothing power supplies, filtering signals, coupling and decoupling circuits, motor starting, tuning radio receivers, and in memory cells (DRAM).

Behaviour

A capacitor blocks direct current once charged but passes alternating current with a frequency-dependent reactance, XC = 1/(2πfC). In series, capacitances combine reciprocally; in parallel, they add directly.
