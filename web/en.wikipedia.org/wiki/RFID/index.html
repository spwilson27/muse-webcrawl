Radio-frequency identification (RFID) uses electromagnetic fields to automatically identify and track tags attached to objects. An RFID system consists of a reader, which emits radio waves and receives signals back from tags, and the tags themselves, each containing an electronically stored identifier.

How it works

Passive tags have no battery: they harvest energy from the reader's interrogating signal and reply by backscattering a modulated signal. They are cheap and tiny — some are smaller than a grain of rice — but have short ranges. Active tags carry a battery, can broadcast over tens of metres, and may include sensors. Common operating bands are low frequency (around 125 kHz, used for animal identification and access badges), high frequency (13.56 MHz, including NFC and contactless payment cards) and ultra-high frequency (860–960 MHz, used in supply chains, where readers can inventory hundreds of tagged items per second at several metres' distance).

Applications

RFID is used for inventory and supply-chain tracking, electronic toll collection, passports and ID cards, library books, livestock and pet identification, race timing, access control, retail anti-theft, and contactless payment. Its ability to read many items at once without line of sight distinguishes it from barcodes.

History and concerns

The technology descends from Second World War IFF transponders and Léon Theremin's passive listening device "The Thing"; commercial systems developed from the 1970s. Privacy advocates have raised concerns about covert tracking of tagged goods and documents, leading to standards for "kill" commands and shielding.
