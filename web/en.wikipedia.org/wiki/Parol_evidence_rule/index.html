Parol evidence rule

The parol evidence rule is a rule in common law jurisdictions limiting the evidence a party to a written contract may introduce concerning the terms of the agreement. Where the parties have reduced their agreement to a writing intended as the final and complete expression of their bargain (an "integration"), extrinsic evidence — oral statements or prior writings — is generally inadmissible to vary, contradict, or (in the case of a complete integration) supplement the written terms. The word "parol" derives from the Old French for spoken word, though the rule also covers prior written material.

Operation

The threshold question is whether the writing is integrated, and if so, whether it is a partial or complete integration. A merger clause ("this document contains the entire agreement") is strong evidence of complete integration. If the agreement is completely integrated, parol evidence cannot even add consistent terms; if partially integrated, it may add consistent additional terms but not contradict.

Exceptions

Extrinsic evidence remains admissible for many purposes, including:

  • to interpret an ambiguous term;
  • to show fraud, duress, mistake, illegality, or other grounds for invalidating the contract;
  • to prove a condition precedent to the contract taking effect;
  • to establish a subsequent modification or a separate collateral agreement;
  • under trade usage or course of dealing, notably for the sale of goods under the Uniform Commercial Code.

United States

In the United States the rule is reflected in the Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§209–217 and, for goods, UCC §2-202. States differ in how readily they find ambiguity; California's approach, associated with Justice Traynor's opinion in Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. G.W. Thomas Drayage Co. (1968), admits extrinsic evidence more freely to test whether language is reasonably susceptible to a proposed meaning.
