A junior doctor in the United Kingdom is a qualified medical practitioner who is working while in postgraduate clinical training, below the rank of consultant or general practitioner. The term traditionally covered everyone from newly qualified foundation doctors to experienced specialist registrars with a decade or more of postgraduate practice.

Training pathway

After medical school, doctors enter the two-year Foundation Programme (FY1 and FY2). They then apply for specialty training — core training of two to three years followed by higher specialty training — leading after a total of roughly five to ten years to a Certificate of Completion of Training (CCT) and eligibility for consultant or GP posts. General practice training lasts three years after foundation.

Junior doctors are represented professionally by the British Medical Association (BMA) and regulated by the General Medical Council.

Industrial disputes

The junior doctors' contract became a major political issue in England in 2015–2016, when proposed changes by Health Secretary Jeremy Hunt led to the first full walkouts by junior doctors in NHS history. Pay erosion relative to inflation brought further widespread strike action in 2023–2024 during a dispute with the government over "pay restoration".

Terminology change

In 2024, following a referendum of BMA members, the term "junior doctor" was officially replaced with "resident doctor" across the NHS and government, reflecting criticism that "junior" understated their experience and responsibility.

Similar training grades exist in other systems: the nearest equivalents are residents and fellows in the United States, registrars in Australia and South Africa, and house officers in New Zealand.
