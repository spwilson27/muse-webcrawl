Forty acres and a mule

Forty acres and a mule was part of Special Field Orders No. 15, a wartime order proclaimed by Union General William Tecumseh Sherman on January 16, 1865, during the American Civil War, to allot land and surplus Army mules to formerly enslaved people. The orders provided that "each family shall have a plot of not more than forty acres of tillable ground." Sherman's orders specifically allocated "the islands from Charleston, south, the abandoned rice fields along the rivers for thirty miles back from the sea, and the country bordering the St. Johns river, Florida."

Background

As Sherman's army marched through Georgia and the Carolinas, thousands of formerly enslaved people followed in search of freedom and livelihood. On January 12, 1865, Sherman and Secretary of War Edwin Stanton met in Savannah with twenty Black community leaders, who stated that land ownership was essential to their freedom. Four days later Sherman issued the field orders.

Implementation and reversal

Some 40,000 freed people were settled on roughly 400,000 acres of confiscated coastal land in South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida. Later in 1865, however, President Andrew Johnson reversed the policy: the land was returned to its former white owners, and most of the freed families were evicted. The episode became a lasting symbol in African-American memory of a broken promise of economic independence, and the phrase "forty acres and a mule" endures in debates over reparations for slavery.

Legacy

The expectation of land redistribution shaped Reconstruction politics; its failure left most freed people dependent on sharecropping and wage labour on land owned by others. The phrase has been referenced widely in American culture, including in the name of Spike Lee's production company, 40 Acres and a Mule Filmworks.
