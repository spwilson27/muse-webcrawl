The Paris Métro (French: Métro de Paris), short for Chemin de fer métropolitain, is a rapid transit system in the Paris metropolitan area in France. A symbol of the city, it is known for its density within the capital's territorial limits, uniform architecture and unique entrances influenced by Art Nouveau.

The system's first line opened without ceremony on 19 July 1900, in time for the World's Fair (Exposition Universelle). It expanded quickly, and by the 1920s the core network within the city was largely complete. Today it is one of the busiest metro systems in the world, carrying over a billion passengers annually.

History

Paris had plans for an underground railway as early as the 1840s. After decades of dispute between the city and the French state over whether the network should serve local or national interests, construction finally began in 1898 under engineer Fulgence Bienvenüe, known as the "father of the Métro". Line 1, between Porte Maillot and Porte de Vincennes, opened on 19 July 1900.

Extensions into the suburbs followed in the 1930s and after the Second World War. Line 14, opened in 1998, was the first fully automated line of the network. The Grand Paris Express project, under construction since the 2010s, is adding four new automated lines around the capital.

Architecture

The Métro is famous for its Art Nouveau entrances designed by Hector Guimard, of which the simple enamelled signs and the elaborate glass-roofed édicules survive across the city. Stations are typically lined with white bevelled tiles to maximise light, and many feature distinctive themed decoration, such as the Arts et Métiers station clad in copper.

Network

Most lines are underground and relatively short, with closely spaced stations (averaging about 548 metres apart). Several lines (1, 4, 6, 11, 14) use rubber-tyred trains running on concrete tracks, a technology pioneered by the RATP. Lines 1 and 14 operate driverless trains.
