Architecture of Australia

The architecture of Australia spans the building traditions of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, tens of thousands of years old, through the Georgian and Victorian styles imported during British colonisation from 1788, to the distinctively Australian forms — the verandah-wrapped homestead, the Queenslander, the Federation house — and the modern and contemporary architecture of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

Indigenous architecture

Aboriginal groups across the continent built a variety of shelters suited to local climates, from temporary windbreaks to substantial semi-permanent villages. The stone houses of the Gunditjmara people at Lake Condah in Victoria, associated with engineered eel-trap systems, are among the oldest human structures in Australia and form part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing.

Colonial and Victorian eras

The earliest European buildings were utilitarian Georgian structures of convict and military construction, such as the work supervised by Francis Greenway in Sydney. Prosperity from wool and the gold rushes of the 1850s produced grand Victorian public buildings and terraced housing in Melbourne and Sydney, while regional adaptations — wide verandahs, corrugated iron roofs, and lightweight timber construction raised on stumps in Queensland — responded to heat, flood, and termites.

Federation to modernism

Around Federation (1901) an Arts and Crafts-influenced style incorporating Australian motifs flourished. Walter Burley Griffin and Marion Mahony Griffin, who won the competition to design Canberra, introduced Prairie School ideas. After the Second World War a local modernism emerged in the work of Harry Seidler, Robin Boyd, and Roy Grounds, tempered by the climate-responsive "Sydney School".

Contemporary landmarks

The Sydney Opera House (Jørn Utzon and Peter Hall, completed 1973) is Australia's most famous building and a World Heritage site. Later icons include the new Parliament House in Canberra (1988), Melbourne's Federation Square (2002), and the environmentally innovative work of architects such as Glenn Murcutt, winner of the Pritzker Prize in 2002 and noted for lightweight, landscape-responsive houses.
