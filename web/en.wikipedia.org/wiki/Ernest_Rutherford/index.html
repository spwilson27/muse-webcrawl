Ernest Rutherford, 1st Baron Rutherford of Nelson (30 August 1871 – 19 October 1937), was a New Zealand physicist known as the father of nuclear physics. He discovered alpha and beta radiation, formulated the concept of half-life, proposed the nuclear model of the atom, and in 1917 performed the first artificially induced nuclear reaction.

Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (carried out by Geiger and Marsden in his Manchester laboratory) showed that atoms are mostly empty space with mass concentrated in a tiny nucleus. His team later identified the proton. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1908 'for his investigations into the disintegration of the elements' — a chemist's prize that amused the physicist. The element rutherfordium (104) is named for him.

Career

From a farm near Nelson, New Zealand, Rutherford won a scholarship to Cambridge's Cavendish Laboratory, then held chairs at McGill, Manchester and Cambridge. His laboratories trained a generation of Nobel laureates, including Niels Bohr, James Chadwick and John Cockcroft.
