Leo Fender

Leo Fender Born: Clarence Leonidas Fender, August 10, 1909, Anaheim, California, U.S.
Died: March 21, 1991 (aged 81), Fullerton, California, U.S.
Occupation: Inventor, founder of Fender Musical Instruments Corporation
Known for: Telecaster, Stratocaster, Precision Bass

Clarence Leonidas Fender (August 10, 1909 – March 21, 1991) was an American inventor known for founding the Fender Musical Instruments Corporation and designing the company's early instruments, including the Telecaster, Stratocaster, and Precision Bass — instruments that transformed popular music in the twentieth century. Although he could not play the guitar himself, Fender pioneered the mass-produced solid-body electric guitar and electric bass, designing for manufacturability, durability, and ease of repair. After selling Fender to CBS in 1965, he went on to co-found Music Man and later G&L Musical Instruments. He was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1992.

Early life

Fender was born in Anaheim, California, to Clarence Monte Fender and Harriet Elvira Wood, who operated a successful orange grove between Anaheim and Fullerton. As a boy he lost an eye in an accident involving a pair of snips and wore a glass prosthesis for the rest of his life. At age thirteen he became fascinated with electronics, building and repairing radios in a small shop his uncle, who ran an automotive-electric business, helped him set up. After high school he studied accounting at Fullerton Junior College and worked for the California Department of Highways, but his hobby became his profession when the Depression-era economy pushed him toward radio repair full time.

Fender's Radio Service and first instruments

In 1938 Fender opened Fender's Radio Service in Fullerton, selling records and repairing radios, phonographs, and public-address systems. Western swing musicians who rented his PA systems asked him to repair their lap steel guitars and amplifiers, and he began building instruments of his own. With Clayton Orr "Doc" Kauffman, a former Rickenbacker designer, he formed the K&F Manufacturing Corporation in 1945 to make lap steels and amplifiers. Kauffman left in 1946, and Fender renamed the business the Fender Electric Instrument Company.

The Telecaster, Precision Bass, and Stratocaster

Fender's breakthrough was the mass-produced solid-body electric guitar: the single-pickup Esquire (1950), followed by the two-pickup Broadcaster — renamed the Telecaster after a trademark dispute with Gretsch over the name "Broadkaster". Its bolt-on neck, slab body, and modular electronics made it cheap to build and easy to fix — a deliberate contrast to Gibson's crafted archtops. In 1951 he introduced the Precision Bass, the first commercially successful electric bass guitar, whose frets gave bassists "precision" in intonation and whose portability freed them from the upright bass. The Stratocaster followed in 1954, with a contoured body, three pickups, and a synchronized tremolo bridge; it became perhaps the most iconic electric guitar design ever made. Fender also built influential amplifiers, including the Bassman and Twin Reverb.

Sale to CBS and later companies

In poor health and advised by doctors to reduce his workload, Fender sold his companies to CBS in January 1965 for $13 million. He remained as a consultant for a few years, then, after his non-compete obligations lapsed, became president of Music Man in the 1970s, where he helped design the StingRay bass. In 1980 he co-founded G&L Musical Instruments with his old colleague George Fullerton, producing refined versions of his classic designs — the company name stood for "George and Leo" — and he worked in its Fullerton factory until the day before his death.

Death and legacy

Fender, who had long suffered from Parkinson's disease, died on March 21, 1991, in Fullerton. He was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1992, and in 2009 received a Technical Grammy Award for his contributions to recording and music technology. His designs — assembled from bolted-together, interchangeable parts — are still produced in huge numbers, and original Fender instruments of the 1950s and early 1960s are among the most collectible in the world.
