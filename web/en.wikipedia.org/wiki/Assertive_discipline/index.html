Assertive discipline is an approach to classroom management developed by the American educators Lee Canter and Marlene Canter and set out in their 1976 book Assertive Discipline: A Take-Charge Approach for Today's Educator. It became one of the most widely adopted behaviour-management programmes in United States schools in the 1980s and 1990s.

The model

The Canters argued that teachers have the right to teach without disruption and students the right to learn, and that neither non-assertive ("wishy-washy") nor hostile responses to misbehaviour work. An assertive teacher instead: (1) establishes a small number of clear classroom rules; (2) teaches and posts a discipline plan with a hierarchy of consequences — typically a warning, then escalating steps such as time-out, loss of privileges, contact with parents, and referral to the principal; (3) applies the consequences consistently and calmly; and (4) gives generous positive recognition for good behaviour, including praise and communication with parents.

Criticism and development

Critics, including Alfie Kohn, have argued that assertive discipline is behaviourist and compliance-oriented, relying on rewards and punishments rather than intrinsic motivation or student participation in rule-making. Later editions of the Canters' work placed more emphasis on positive reinforcement, relationship-building and teaching responsible behaviour, and the model is now often situated within broader frameworks such as positive behavioural interventions and supports (PBIS).
