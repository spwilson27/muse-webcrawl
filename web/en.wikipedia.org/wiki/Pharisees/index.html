Pharisees

The Pharisees (Hebrew: פְּרוּשִׁים, Pərūšīm, "separated ones") were a Jewish social movement and school of thought in the Levant during the time of Second Temple Judaism. After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, Pharisaic beliefs became the foundational, liturgical, and ritualistic basis for Rabbinic Judaism.

Conflicts between Pharisees and Sadducees took place in the context of much broader and longstanding social and religious conflicts among Jews, made worse by the Roman conquest. Another conflict was cultural, between those who favoured Hellenization (the Sadducees) and those who resisted it (the Pharisees). A specifically religious conflict involved differing interpretations of the Torah and how to apply it to Jewish life, with Sadducees recognising only the Written Torah and rejecting doctrines such as the Oral Torah, the Prophets, the Writings, and the resurrection of the dead.

History

The Pharisees emerged as a distinct group in the Hasmonean period (2nd century BCE), possibly from the Hasideans who opposed the Hellenizing high priesthood. Under Queen Salome Alexandra (76–67 BCE) they gained great influence in the Sanhedrin. Josephus, our principal source, numbers them at about 6,000 in the time of Herod the Great, describing them as the most popular party among the common people and the most precise interpreters of the law.

Beliefs

Unlike the Sadducees, the Pharisees believed in the authority of the Oral Torah—interpretive traditions handed down alongside the written text—as well as in the resurrection of the dead, a world to come, angels and spirits, divine providence combined with human free will, and the coming of the Messiah. Their teachers developed rules of biblical interpretation and applied Torah observance to everyday life outside the Temple, emphasising prayer, study, charity, and ritual purity in the home.

The houses of Hillel and Shammai

Rabbinic literature remembers the late Second Temple Pharisees through the "houses" of two great teachers: the lenient, pragmatic Hillel the Elder and the stricter Shammai. Their disciples' disputes, recorded in the Mishnah, shaped later Jewish law; with rare exceptions, the law follows the House of Hillel.

After the destruction of the Temple

After the Roman destruction of Jerusalem and the Second Temple in 70 CE, the Sadducees and other Temple-based groups disappeared, while the Pharisaic-rabbinic tradition, centred at Yavneh under Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai, reorganised Judaism around synagogue, prayer, and study. The Pharisees' interpretive legacy was codified in the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and Talmud.

In the New Testament

The Gospels frequently depict disputes between Jesus and Pharisees over Sabbath observance, purity, and interpretation of the law, and the word "Pharisee" acquired a pejorative sense of hypocrisy in later Christian usage. Modern scholarship notes that the Gospel portraits are polemical and partial: some Pharisees, such as Gamaliel and Nicodemus, are depicted favourably, and many of Jesus's teachings parallel Pharisaic ones. The apostle Paul identified himself as a Pharisee.
