St. George Island (Alaska)

St. George Island (Aleut: Anax̂) is one of the Pribilof Islands of Alaska in the Bering Sea. It lies about 70 km (43 mi) south of its sister island, St. Paul Island. The island has a land area of about 90 km² (35 sq mi). The only settlement is the city of St. George, home to a predominantly Aleut (Unangan) community. The island's highest point is Ulakaia Hill, about 288 m (945 ft) above sea level.

History

The Pribilof Islands were sighted in 1786 by Russian explorer Gavriil Pribylov, who was searching for the breeding grounds of the northern fur seal. St. George was the first of the group he reached, in June 1786; Pribylov named it for Saint George. The Russians soon established a settlement and brought Aleut people from the Aleutian Islands to hunt fur seals, beginning a long era in which the island's Indigenous residents worked in the commercial seal harvest, first under Russian and, after the Alaska Purchase of 1867, under American administration.

During World War II the island's residents were evacuated to internment camps in southeastern Alaska along with other Aleut communities. The commercial fur seal harvest ended on St. George in the 1980s, though a subsistence harvest continues for the local community.

Geography and wildlife

St. George is of volcanic origin, with a rugged coastline of cliffs and a mostly tundra-covered interior. Its sea cliffs are among the most important seabird breeding colonies in the Northern Hemisphere: an estimated 2.5 million seabirds nest on the island, including huge numbers of red-legged kittiwakes, thick-billed murres, and the world's largest colony of least auklets on the island's lava fields. The surrounding beaches host a large rookery of northern fur seals. The island is part of the Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge.

Community

The city of St. George is a second-class city in the Aleutians West Census Area. Its population is around 70–100 residents, nearly all Alaska Native. The island is accessible by air from St. Paul and Anchorage via a small airport, weather permitting, and by boat. Traditional Unangan culture, Orthodox Christianity, and subsistence hunting and fishing remain central to life on the island.
