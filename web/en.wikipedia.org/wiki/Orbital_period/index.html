Orbital period

The orbital period (also revolution period) is the amount of time a given astronomical object takes to complete one orbit around another object. In astronomy, it usually applies to planets or asteroids orbiting the Sun, moons orbiting planets, exoplanets orbiting other stars, or binary stars. It is usually measured in Earth years for planets and in hours or days for satellites.

Calculation

For a small body orbiting a much larger one in a circular or elliptical orbit, Kepler's third law gives the period:

T = 2π√(a³/μ)

where a is the semi-major axis and μ = GM is the standard gravitational parameter of the central body. For two comparable masses, μ uses the total mass of both bodies. This relation means that period depends only on the size of the orbit and the mass of the system, not on eccentricity.

Types of period

The sidereal period is measured relative to the fixed stars. The synodic period is measured relative to the apparent position of the Sun as seen from another moving body — for example, the interval between successive conjunctions. The anomalistic period runs from one periapsis passage to the next, and differs from the sidereal period when the orbit precesses. For Earth, the tropical year, measured between successive March equinoxes, is about 20 minutes shorter than the sidereal year because of axial precession.
