Scottish Marches

The Scottish Marches were the districts along the Anglo-Scottish border administered from the Middle Ages to the early 17th century by wardens charged with keeping order in a lawless frontier zone. The corresponding English side was the English Marches; together they formed the "Marches" system of border government, divided on each side into East, Middle, and West Marches.

Organisation

Each March was governed by a Warden of the March, usually a great local magnate, assisted by deputies and keepers of castles such as Berwick, Carlisle-facing strongholds on the Scottish side, Edinburgh's oversight notwithstanding. Wardens held courts under the special Border law known as March law (leges marchiarum), administered jointly with their English counterparts at days of truce, where cross-border complaints ("bills") were heard and pledges exchanged. A distinctive institution was the "hot trod" — lawful cross-border pursuit of raiders within six days, announced by horn and hue and cry.

The reiving era

During the Anglo-Scottish wars of the 14th–16th centuries, central control weakened and the Marches became dominated by the Border Reivers — riding families such as the Armstrongs, Elliots, Nixons, and Kerrs — who raided livestock across the line regardless of nationality. The landscape filled with peel towers and bastle houses. The Debatable Land between the Esk and Sark, claimed by neither crown, became a byword for lawlessness. The era produced the Border ballads, many collected by Sir Walter Scott in Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802–03).

End of the Marches

After the Union of the Crowns in 1603, James VI and I renamed the region the "Middle Shires" and suppressed reiving with commissions, executions, and mass transportations (notably of the Grahams to Ireland). The wardenries were abolished, and the border became an internal boundary administered by ordinary sheriffs and justices.
