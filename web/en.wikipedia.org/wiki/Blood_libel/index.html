Blood libel

A medieval depiction associated with a blood libel case (placeholder).

Blood libel (also blood accusation) is an antisemitic canard which falsely accuses Jews of murdering Christian boys in order to use their blood in the performance of religious rituals. These claims are a major theme of the history of antisemitism. The libel typically alleged that Jews required human blood for the baking of matzos at Passover, or for other ritual purposes. Such accusations were entirely false: Jewish law strictly forbids the consumption of blood and any form of human sacrifice.

History

The first recorded blood libel arose in Norwich, England, in 1144, following the death of a young apprentice, William of Norwich. Similar accusations spread across medieval Europe, including at Blois (1171), where the entire Jewish community was burned; Fulda (1235); and Trent (1475), where the case of Simon of Trent became one of the most notorious. Accusations frequently led to massacres, expulsions and the destruction of Jewish communities. Popes including Innocent IV and Gregory X issued bulls condemning the libel, and the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II ordered an investigation that refuted it, yet the accusations persisted for centuries.

Later cases

Blood libels continued into the modern era. The Damascus affair of 1840, in which Jews were accused of murdering a Capuchin friar, provoked international protest. In Austria-Hungary, the Hilsner affair (1899–1900) galvanised debate, with Tomáš Masaryk publicly defending the accused. In Kyiv in 1911–1913, Mendel Beilis was tried for ritual murder and acquitted. As late as 1946, the Kielce pogrom in Poland was partly triggered by a blood libel rumour. Variants of the libel still circulate in some modern propaganda, and historians regard it as one of the most durable and lethal conspiracy theories in European history.
