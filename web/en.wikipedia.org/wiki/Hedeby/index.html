Hedeby

Hedeby (Old Norse: Heiðabýr; German: Haithabu) was an important Danish Viking Age trading settlement near the southern end of the Jutland Peninsula, in present-day Schleswig-Holstein, Germany. Situated at the head of the Schlei inlet on the Baltic Sea, near the shortest overland portage to the River Treene and thus the North Sea, Hedeby flourished from the 8th to the 11th centuries as one of the largest trading centres (emporia) of Northern Europe.

History

Hedeby appears in Frankish annals around 804, when the Danish king Godfred moved merchants there from Reric. Protected by the semicircular rampart that connected with the Danevirke fortifications, the town drew traders from Scandinavia, the Frankish Empire, Frisia, the Slavic lands and as far as Byzantium and the Arab world; Frankish chronicler Rimbert described its multilingual population, and the Arab traveller al-Tartushi visited in the 10th century. Hedeby was a site of early Christian missions by Anskar and had one of Scandinavia's earliest churches and a royal mint.

The town was attacked several times — by Harald Hardrada around 1050 and by West Slavs in 1066 — after which it declined and was abandoned in favour of Schleswig across the Schlei.

Archaeology

The site is one of the most important archaeological locations of the Viking Age. Excavations have uncovered harbour works, workshops, burials, runestones and the wreck of a Viking longship. Together with the Danevirke, Hedeby was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2018 as the "Archaeological Border Complex of Hedeby and the Danevirke". The Viking Museum at Haithabu displays many of the finds.
