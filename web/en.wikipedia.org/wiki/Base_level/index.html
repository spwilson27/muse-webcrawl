Base level


A stream profile approaching base level

In geology and geomorphology, base level is the lower limit for an erosion process. For a river or stream, it is the lowest elevation to which flowing water can cut down its bed: below base level, the stream deposits sediment rather than eroding. The concept was introduced by John Wesley Powell in 1875 and became a foundation of William Morris Davis's cycle of erosion.

Ultimate and local base levels

The ultimate base level is sea level, toward which all streams ultimately drain. Local or temporary base levels occur where a stream meets a resistant rock layer, a lake, or a larger river whose bed it cannot deepen; a lake, for example, acts as base level for streams flowing into it. Knickpoints such as waterfalls and rapids often mark positions where a stream is adjusting to a fall in base level.

Changes in base level

When base level falls — through sea-level drop or tectonic uplift — streams gain erosive energy and incise their valleys, producing terraces and rejuvenated landscapes. When base level rises, as during post-glacial sea-level rise, valleys are drowned (forming rias and estuaries) and streams deposit sediment in their lower reaches. The graded profile of a mature stream is understood as an equilibrium adjusted to its base level.
