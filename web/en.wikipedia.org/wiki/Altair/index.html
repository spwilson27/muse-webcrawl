Altair (Alpha Aquilae, α Aquilae) is the brightest star in the constellation of Aquila, the Eagle, and the twelfth-brightest star in the night sky. It lies about 16.7 light-years from the Sun, making it one of the nearest bright stars. Together with Vega and Deneb it forms the prominent Summer Triangle asterism of northern summer skies. The name derives from the Arabic an-nasr aṭ-ṭāʾir, "the flying eagle."

Properties

Altair is a white main-sequence star of spectral type A7, with about 1.8 times the mass of the Sun and roughly eleven times its luminosity. It is remarkable for its extremely rapid rotation: it spins on its axis in about 9 hours (compared to roughly 25 days for the Sun), reaching equatorial velocities above 280 km/s, a substantial fraction of the speed at which it would fly apart. This rotation bulges the star into an oblate spheroid — interferometric imaging has shown its equatorial diameter to be more than 20 percent greater than its polar diameter — and produces gravity darkening, with the poles hotter and brighter than the equator.

Observation and culture

Altair is flanked by two dimmer stars, Beta and Gamma Aquilae, forming the Eagle's head. In East Asian folklore, Altair represents the cowherd Niulang, separated across the Milky Way from the weaver Zhinü (Vega) and reunited once a year on the seventh day of the seventh month, celebrated in the Qixi and Tanabata festivals.
