In music theory, a perfect fifth is the musical interval corresponding to a pair of pitches with a frequency ratio of 3:2, or very nearly so. In classical music from Western culture, a fifth is the interval from the first to the last of the first five notes in a diatonic scale. The perfect fifth — spanning seven semitones — is one of the most consonant intervals, second only to the octave, and it is fundamental to harmony: it is the interval between the root and the fifth degree of major and minor chords, and strings of most violins, violas and cellos are tuned in perfect fifths.

Tuning

In just intonation the perfect fifth has the exact frequency ratio 3:2. In twelve-tone equal temperament the fifth is slightly narrower, 700 cents versus the just fifth's 701.955 cents — a difference small enough to be acceptable in most contexts. In Pythagorean tuning, built by stacking pure 3:2 fifths, twelve fifths exceed seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma, which historically motivated meantone and well temperaments. Violins, violas and cellos are tuned in pure fifths (C–G–D–A), and much of the cycle of fifths organises Western tonal music theory.

Use in music

The perfect fifth is a building block of the major and minor triads and of "power chords" in rock music, where the root–fifth pair (often with the octave) played with distortion produces a heavy, open sound. Successions of parallel perfect fifths between independent voices were forbidden in strict counterpoint because they hollow out the texture, yet they are characteristic of medieval organum, folk traditions and much rock and metal. The opening of Richard Strauss's Also sprach Zarathustra and the "Twinkle, Twinkle" melody (so–do) offer famous examples.

Acoustics

The consonance of the fifth arises from the near-coincidence of the harmonics of the two tones: the third harmonic of the lower tone coincides with the second harmonic of the upper when the ratio is exactly 3:2. This simple whole-number relationship is a classical example of the link between arithmetic ratios and perceived musical harmony noted since Pythagoras.
