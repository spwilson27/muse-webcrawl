Cohabitation (France)

During cohabitation, the President shares executive power with a hostile Prime Minister.

In French politics, cohabitation is the situation in which the President of the Republic and the Prime Minister belong to opposing political camps, because the majority in the National Assembly differs from the president's party. Under the semi-presidential system of the Fifth Republic, the president appoints the prime minister but must choose someone able to command the Assembly's confidence; when the president's camp loses that majority, the executive becomes a tense two-headed arrangement — a "marriage" between rivals, hence the term.

The three cohabitations

  • 1986–1988: Socialist President François Mitterrand with Gaullist Prime Minister Jacques Chirac, after the right won the 1986 legislative elections.
  • 1993–1995: Mitterrand again, with Prime Minister Édouard Balladur, after the right's landslide of 1993.
  • 1997–2002: President Jacques Chirac with Socialist Prime Minister Lionel Jospin, following Chirac's dissolution of the Assembly and the left's unexpected victory in 1997.

Operation

During cohabitation the prime minister and government direct domestic policy — economics, labour, social affairs — while the president retains influence over foreign affairs and defence, the so-called domaine réservé, though in practice this division is negotiated and contested; both share representation in the European Council. The president can still refuse to sign certain ordinances and decrees, delay legislation by requesting reconsideration, and address the nation, but the day-to-day initiative shifts to Matignon (the prime minister's office).

Constitutional reform

The experience of the third, five-year cohabitation prompted reform: in 2000 the presidential term was shortened from seven years to five (the quinquennat), and in 2001 the electoral calendar was inverted so that legislative elections follow immediately after the presidential election, making cohabitation much less likely. It has not occurred since 2002, though hung parliaments — as after the 2022 and 2024 elections — have revived discussion of how the constitution would accommodate a president facing an Assembly majority of opponents.
