Hammett equation

The Hammett equation in organic chemistry describes a linear free-energy relationship relating reaction rates and equilibrium constants for many reactions involving benzoic acid derivatives with two types of substituent or reaction parameters.

It was developed and published by Louis Plack Hammett in 1937. The basic idea is that for two reactions with two aromatic reactants differing only in one substituent, the change in free energy of activation is proportional to the change in Gibbs free energy.

The equation

The Hammett equation applies the substituent constant σ, derived from the ionization of benzoic acids, and the reaction constant ρ, which depends on the type of reaction but not on the substituent used. Plotting log(k/k0) against σ gives a straight line whose slope is ρ.

Applications

The equation is widely used to predict reaction rates and equilibria, to probe reaction mechanisms, and to quantify electron-donating and electron-withdrawing effects of substituents. Extensions include the Taft equation for aliphatic systems and variants for meta and para substituents.
