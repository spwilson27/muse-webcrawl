Flying shuttle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The flying shuttle was one of the key developments in the industrialisation of textile manufacturing during the early Industrial Revolution. It was patented by John Kay of Bury, Lancashire in 1733. The shuttle — containing a bobbin of weft thread — ran on wheels along a track (the shuttle race) and was knocked rapidly from side to side through the shed of warp threads by a cord mechanism operated by a single weaver pulling a picking stick.

Impact

Previously, weaving wide cloth required two weavers to throw a hand shuttle back and forth; the flying shuttle let one weaver do the work and roughly doubled weaving speed. This created a bottleneck: weavers now consumed yarn faster than spinners could supply it, stimulating the spinning innovations of the following decades — the spinning jenny, water frame and spinning mule. The flying shuttle was gradually adopted in the woollen and cotton trades despite resistance and attacks on Kay's house by workers fearing job losses, and it remained standard on handlooms and early power looms until more rapid shuttle and shuttleless systems appeared.
