Ulysses S. Grant (born Hiram Ulysses Grant, 27 April 1822 – 23 July 1885) was an American military officer and statesman who served as the 18th president of the United States from 1869 to 1877. As Commanding General of the United States Army, he led the Union Army to victory in the American Civil War.

Civil War

A West Point graduate who had left the army in 1854 and struggled in civilian life, Grant re-enlisted in 1861 and rose rapidly in the Western Theater, winning victories at Fort Donelson — where his demand for "unconditional surrender" made his initials famous — Shiloh, and the siege of Vicksburg, which split the Confederacy in 1863. Promoted to lieutenant general and general-in-chief in March 1864, he coordinated all Union armies and, in the Overland Campaign, ground down Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia until Lee surrendered at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865.

Presidency

Elected in 1868 and re-elected in 1872, Grant's administration enforced civil rights for freedpeople during Reconstruction, using federal troops and the Enforcement Acts to suppress the Ku Klux Klan, and supported the Fifteenth Amendment. His presidency also saw the Panic of 1873 and a series of corruption scandals — including the Whiskey Ring and Credit Mobilier affair — involving associates, though Grant himself was regarded as personally honest. He signed the act creating Yellowstone, the world's first national park, in 1872.

Later life

After a celebrated world tour, Grant lost his fortune to a fraudulent investment firm in 1884. Dying of throat cancer, he wrote his Personal Memoirs — published by Mark Twain and regarded as among the finest military memoirs in English — completing them days before his death in July 1885 and securing his family's finances.
