Aaron Copland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Aaron Copland conducting in the 1960s: the composer tall and lean at the podium, baton raised, in rehearsal]

Aaron Copland (14 November 1900 – 2 December 1990) was an American composer, conductor, writer and teacher, the dean of American classical music. In the 1930s–40s he forged the sound the world hears as 'American': open intervals, wide-spaced chords and folk simplicity in the ballets Billy the Kid (1938), Rodeo (1942) and Appalachian Spring (1944, Pulitzer Prize, written for Martha Graham), the wartime Fanfare for the Common Man (1942) and the Lincoln Portrait (1942).

Born in Brooklyn to Lithuanian-Jewish immigrants, Copland studied in Paris with Nadia Boulanger (the first American of her famous studio), absorbed Stravinsky and jazz, and wrote first in a severe modernist idiom before turning, in the Depression, to a music of deliberate accessibility.

The American sound and its costs

Copland's populist turn — folk song in Rodeo (the 'Hoe-Down' dances a genuine cowboy tune), Shaker melody in Appalachian Spring ('Simple Gifts', which he made world-famous) — put concert music at the service of democratic feeling; film scores (Of Mice and Men, The Red Pony, The Heiress, Oscar 1949) carried it to millions. His leftist associations brought a 1953 HUAC subpoena and the cancellation of Lincoln Portrait from Eisenhower's inaugural concert — the era's emblematic cultural witch-hunt.

Teacher and elder

At Tanglewood for decades Copland taught generations (Leonard Bernstein chief among them, their friendship lifelong); his books What to Listen for in Music (1939) taught the public. Later works returned to twelve-tone severity (the Piano Fantasy, 1957; Inscape, 1967). He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and died in 1990, his Americana idiom by then the default sound of American public ceremony.
