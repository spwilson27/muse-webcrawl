Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (ZES) is a disease in which one or more tumours called gastrinomas cause the stomach to secrete excessive amounts of acid, leading to severe, recurrent and often atypically located peptic ulcers. It was described in 1955 by surgeons Robert M. Zollinger and Edwin H. Ellison at Ohio State University.

Cause and presentation

Gastrinomas arise most often in the pancreas and duodenum, usually within the "gastrinoma triangle". The hormone gastrin they secrete drives massive acid production, causing multiple ulcers refractory to standard treatment, gastro-oesophageal reflux, and secretory diarrhoea that can itself be disabling. About a quarter of cases occur as part of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1), an inherited syndrome also involving parathyroid and pituitary tumours; the rest are sporadic, and a majority of sporadic gastrinomas are malignant, though slow-growing.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a markedly elevated fasting serum gastrin together with low gastric pH, confirmed by a secretin stimulation test; imaging and endoscopic ultrasound localise the tumour. High-dose proton pump inhibitors control acid in nearly all patients, transforming a once-fatal disease into a manageable one; surgery may cure localised sporadic cases, while treatment of metastatic disease targets tumour growth and hormone excess.
