History of nursing

The history of nursing traces the development of the care of the sick and injured from ancient religious and charitable practices into the modern regulated profession shaped decisively by Florence Nightingale in the nineteenth century.

Early history

Care of the sick in the ancient world was provided within families and by religious institutions. Early Christian deaconesses visited the sick, and medieval religious orders founded hospitals throughout Europe. In the Islamic world, hospitals (bimaristans) employed attendants of both sexes. After the Reformation, nursing in Protestant countries declined into low-status domestic work, while Catholic orders such as the Daughters of Charity continued organized care.

Nightingale and professionalization

Florence Nightingale's work during the Crimean War (1854–1856), where she and her nurses drastically reduced mortality at the Scutari hospital through sanitation and record-keeping, transformed public perceptions. In 1860 she founded the Nightingale Training School at St Thomas' Hospital in London, establishing nursing as a respectable trained profession for women. Parallel reforms came from figures such as Mary Seacole, Clara Barton (founder of the American Red Cross), and Linda Richards, the first professionally trained American nurse.

Twentieth century and beyond

Nurse registration laws, beginning in the early 1900s, formalized education and licensure. Two world wars expanded nursing roles and numbers; the later twentieth century saw university-based education, advanced practice roles such as nurse practitioners, and a growing male presence in the profession. Today nursing is the largest healthcare profession in many countries, spanning hospital, community, research, and leadership roles.
