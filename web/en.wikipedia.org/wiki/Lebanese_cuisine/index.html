Lebanese cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lebanese cuisine (Arabic: المطبخ اللبناني) is the culinary traditions and practices originating from Lebanon and the wider Levant. It includes an abundance of whole grains, fruits, vegetables, fresh fish and seafood, and uses animal fats sparingly. Poultry is eaten more often than red meat, and when red meat is eaten, it is usually lamb or goat. Dishes use large amounts of garlic, olive oil, herbs, and lemon juice.

Characteristics

Meals are often served as mezze—a selection of small dishes, dips, and salads shared at the table. Well-known dishes include hummus (chickpea and tahini dip), tabbouleh (bulgur, parsley, and tomato salad), baba ghanoush (smoked aubergine dip), fattoush (bread salad with sumac), falafel, shawarma, and kibbeh (bulgur and minced meat), considered the national dish alongside tabbouleh. Pita bread (khubz arabi) accompanies nearly every meal.

Influences and sweets

Lebanese food reflects the many civilizations that ruled the land—Phoenician, Roman, Arab, Ottoman, and French—each layering ingredients and techniques. Sweets include baklava, ma'amoul (filled shortbread), knefeh (cheese pastry soaked in syrup), and muhallabia. Drinks include Lebanese wine from the Beqaa Valley, arak (an aniseed spirit), and strong coffee. Lebanon's cuisine has spread worldwide through its large diaspora and is a core part of Levantine cuisine.
