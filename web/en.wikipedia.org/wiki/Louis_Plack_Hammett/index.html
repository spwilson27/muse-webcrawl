Louis Plack Hammett

Louis Plack Hammett (7 April 1894 – 9 February 1987) was an American physical chemist at Columbia University. He is best known as the founder of the field of physical organic chemistry — a term he coined in his influential 1940 textbook of that name — and for the Hammett equation, a linear free-energy relationship correlating reaction rates and equilibrium constants with substituent effects.

The Hammett equation

log(k/k0) = σρ

Published in 1937, the equation relates the effect of a substituent on a reaction series to two parameters: σ (sigma), measuring the substituent's electron-donating or electron-withdrawing power, derived from the ionisation of benzoic acids, and ρ (rho), measuring the reaction's sensitivity to substituent effects. It was one of the first quantitative structure–reactivity relationships and profoundly influenced mechanistic organic chemistry. Hammett also devised the H0 acidity function for measuring the acidity of superacids, media far more acidic than concentrated sulfuric acid.

Career

Hammett was born in Portland, Maine, studied at Harvard and Columbia, and taught at Columbia from 1920 until 1961. He received numerous honours, including the Priestley Medal in 1961 and the National Medal of Science in 1967. His textbook Physical Organic Chemistry (1940) defined the discipline for a generation.
