180-degree rule

180-degree rule
FieldFilmmaking, cinematography
Also known asAxis of action; crossing the line (when violated)

The 180-degree rule is a basic guideline regarding on-screen spatial relations between two (or more) elements in a film. An imaginary axis, or line, is drawn between the elements — for instance between two characters talking to one another — and the camera should be placed on the same side (within a 180-degree semicircle) of that axis for every shot in the sequence. By keeping the camera on one side of the axis, the relative left-to-right positions of the elements remain consistent from shot to shot, preserving the audience's spatial orientation.

Rationale

If the camera crosses the line between shots, a character who appeared on the left of the frame will suddenly appear on the right, and the direction of their gaze and movement will be reversed. This can confuse or disorient viewers, making it appear that a conversation's participants are looking away from each other or that a moving subject has reversed direction.

Exceptions and related techniques

Filmmakers sometimes deliberately break the rule to convey disorientation, confusion, or a psychological shift. The rule can also be "reset" by moving the camera visibly across the axis within a single continuous shot, or by inserting a neutral shot on the axis itself. Related continuity conventions include the 30-degree rule, eyeline matching, and shot/reverse shot coverage in dialogue scenes.

Examples

Directors known for deliberately violating the axis include Yasujirō Ozu, whose dialogue scenes routinely cross the line, and Stanley Kubrick, who broke the rule in scenes such as the bathroom confrontation in The Shining to unsettling effect.
