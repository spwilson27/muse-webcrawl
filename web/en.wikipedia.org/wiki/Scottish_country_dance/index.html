Scottish country dance (SCD) is a form of social dance involving groups of couples following a choreographed sequence of figures to Scottish folk music. Dancers are arranged in "sets" — most commonly longwise sets of three or four couples — and progress through formations such as reels of three and four, rights and lefts, poussettes, and turns by the hand, so that partners change position through the dance.

Types of dance

Dances are classified by musical tempo and step: quick-time reels and jigs (danced with skip-change and pas-de-basque steps), and the slower, stately strathspey, unique to Scotland, which uses the strathspey travelling step and setting step. Medleys combine strathspey and reel or jig time. Well-known dances include "The Duke of Perth", "Mairi's Wedding" (often a ceilidh favourite), "The Reel of the 51st Division", "The Eightsome Reel" and "The Dashing White Sergeant".

History

Country dancing arrived in Scotland from England and the Continent in the 17th–18th centuries and flourished in assembly rooms and rural halls alike. It declined in the later 19th century as couple dances (waltz, polka) rose, and many dances survived only in fragments. In 1923 Jean Milligan and Ysobel Stewart founded the Scottish Country Dance Society (later the Royal Scottish Country Dance Society, RSCDS) to collect, standardise and teach the dances. The society has published thousands of dances in its books, and new dances continue to be devised worldwide.

Practice

Scottish country dancing is now practised across the world, with RSCDS branches and affiliated groups in North America, Australia, Japan, continental Europe and beyond. Dancers typically wear soft ghillies (dance shoes); men often dance in the kilt at balls. A dance is usually "walked through" and recapitulated with brief calls before being danced, making the form accessible at social events such as ceilidhs, though SCD proper is more formal and technique-oriented than ceilidh dancing.
