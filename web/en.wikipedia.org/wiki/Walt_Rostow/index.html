Walt Rostow

Walt Whitman Rostow (October 7, 1916 – February 13, 2003) was an American economist, professor and political theorist who served as national security advisor to President Lyndon B. Johnson from 1966 to 1969. He was a key architect of United States involvement in the Vietnam War and a prominent advocate of development economics.

The Stages of Economic Growth

Rostow is best known for his 1960 book The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-Communist Manifesto, which proposed that societies pass through five stages: the traditional society; the preconditions for take-off; the take-off; the drive to maturity; and the age of high mass consumption. The model, subtitled as an explicit alternative to Marxist theory, argued that modernisation could be achieved through investment and integration into international markets, and it strongly influenced American foreign aid policy in the developing world.

Government service

Rostow served in the Office of Strategic Services during the Second World War, taught at Oxford, Cambridge, and MIT, and joined the Kennedy administration in 1961 as deputy national security advisor and then chairman of the State Department's Policy Planning Council. As Johnson's national security advisor he was an unyielding advocate of the bombing campaigns against North Vietnam and resisted negotiated de-escalation.

After 1969 he taught at the University of Texas at Austin. His modernization theory has been widely criticised by dependency theorists and others for assuming a single linear path of development modelled on Western history.
