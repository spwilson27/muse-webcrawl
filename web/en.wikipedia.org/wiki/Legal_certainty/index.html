Legal certainty

Legal certainty underpins the rule of law

Legal certainty is a fundamental legal principle according to which the law must be clear, precise, published, and predictable, so that those subject to it can foresee the legal consequences of their actions and organise their affairs accordingly. It is closely associated with the rule of law and is recognised as a general principle of European Union law and of many national legal systems.

Elements

The principle comprises several related requirements. Laws must be accessible — officially published and available to the public — and foreseeable, formulated with sufficient precision that a person can regulate their conduct, if necessary with legal advice. Legal certainty also implies the prohibition of retroactive legislation in most circumstances, the protection of legitimate expectations, the finality of judicial decisions (res judicata), and consistency in the application of rules by courts and administrators.

In European Union law

The Court of Justice of the European Union treats legal certainty as one of the general principles of EU law. It requires that EU rules be clear and predictable for those subject to them, that measures imposing charges not take effect retroactively except in exceptional circumstances, and that limitation periods and deadlines be fixed in advance. In criminal matters the principle overlaps with nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege — no crime or punishment without law — protected by Article 7 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Tension with flexibility

Legal certainty can conflict with the need for law to adapt to changing circumstances and to do justice in individual cases. Legal theorists including Lon Fuller, whose "inner morality of law" demanded clarity and non-retroactivity, and Joseph Raz, who argued that law must be capable of guiding behaviour, have explored this balance. Courts manage the tension through techniques such as prospective overruling, transitional provisions, and the doctrine of legitimate expectation in administrative law.
