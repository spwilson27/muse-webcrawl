ΔT (timekeeping)

In precise timekeeping and astronomy, ΔT is the difference between Terrestrial Time (TT), a uniform time scale defined by atomic clocks and celestial mechanics, and Universal Time (UT1), a time scale determined by the rotation angle of the Earth:

ΔT = TT − UT1

Cause

The Earth's rotation is not constant. Tidal friction from the Moon transfers angular momentum from the Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit, lengthening the day by roughly 2.3 milliseconds per century and making ΔT grow approximately quadratically with time — on average about +31 seconds per century squared. Superimposed on this trend are irregular fluctuations of several milliseconds in the length of day caused by core–mantle coupling, atmospheric and oceanic currents, and large earthquakes, which make ΔT impossible to predict more than a few years ahead.

Before the telescopic era, ΔT is derived from recorded timings of eclipses and lunar occultations. Studies by F. Richard Stephenson and collaborators, using Babylonian, Chinese, Arab, and European records, show ΔT reached roughly 2–3 hours around 500 BC. Extrapolation far into the past places limits on the dates at which total solar eclipses could have been seen from particular sites, a tool used in historical chronology.

Use in eclipse computation

Eclipse prediction software computes the Moon's position in TT; to plot the eclipse path on the rotating Earth, ΔT must be applied. An error of one second in ΔT shifts the predicted path by about 460 metres at the equator.
