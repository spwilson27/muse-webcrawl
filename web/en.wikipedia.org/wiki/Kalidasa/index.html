Kalidasa

Kālidāsa (कालिदास)
Flourished: 4th–5th century CE (probably Gupta era)
Language: Sanskrit
Major works: Abhijñānaśākuntalam (Shakuntala), Meghadūta, Raghuvaṃśa, Kumārasambhava
[Image: Illustration of Shakuntala writing a letter, in the style of Raja Ravi Varma]

Kalidasa (Sanskrit: कालिदास, "servant of Kali") was a classical Sanskrit poet and playwright, widely regarded as the greatest poet of the Sanskrit language and one of the supreme figures of world literature. His life is almost entirely legendary; he is traditionally placed at the court of a king Vikramaditya, most plausibly in the Gupta period of the 4th–5th centuries CE.

Works

Three plays survive: Malavikagnimitram, Vikramorvashiyam, and his masterpiece Abhijnanashakuntalam (The Recognition of Shakuntala), the story of King Dushyanta and Shakuntala, whose lost ring and recovered memory frame a celebrated drama of love and separation. His epic poems include the Raghuvamsha (Dynasty of Raghu) and Kumarasambhava (Birth of Kumara), and his lyric Meghaduta (The Cloud Messenger) — in which an exiled yaksha sends a message to his wife by a rain cloud — is among the most beloved poems in Sanskrit, famous for its descriptions of landscape and longing. The seasonal poem Ritusamhara is also attributed to him.

Reception

Shakuntala was the first Indian literary work widely translated in the West: Sir William Jones's 1789 English version electrified European readers, and Goethe wrote a famous quatrain in its praise. Within India, Kalidasa's command of simile earned him the title Kavikulaguru, "teacher of the clan of poets," and his works remain a touchstone of Sanskrit aesthetics, particularly the theory of rasa (aesthetic flavour).

Legends

Folklore tells that the poet began as a fool who, mocked by a learned princess, prayed to the goddess Kali and was transformed into a genius — tales that say more about his later fame than his life, of which nothing certain is known.
