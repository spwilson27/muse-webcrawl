Cryosurgery

Cryosurgery is the use of extreme cold in surgery to destroy abnormal or diseased tissue; the term comes from the Greek words cryo (κρύο), meaning "icy cold", and surgery (χειρουργία) meaning "hand work". Cryosurgery has been historically used to treat a number of diseases and disorders, especially a variety of benign and malignant skin conditions.

Technique

The most common cryogen is liquid nitrogen (at −196 °C), applied either with a spray gun or a cotton-tipped applicator so that intracellular ice formation ruptures cell membranes and destroys the target tissue. Other agents include carbon dioxide snow and argon gas used through probe-based systems (Joule–Thomson effect). A freeze–thaw cycle is usually repeated to ensure tissue destruction, and the depth of freeze is monitored clinically.

Uses

  • Dermatology — treatment of warts, actinic keratoses, seborrhoeic keratoses, molluscum contagiosum and some small skin cancers.
  • Internal tumors — cryoablation of prostate, liver, kidney and lung tumors using image-guided probes.
  • Gynecology — historically used for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia.
  • Cardiology — cryoablation of abnormal electrical pathways in arrhythmias.

Advantages and risks

Cryosurgery is minimally invasive, can often be performed under local anaesthesia, and generally leaves less scarring than excision. Risks include blistering, pigment changes (particularly hypopigmentation), temporary pain, nerve damage and — if used improperly — insufficient destruction of malignant tissue.
