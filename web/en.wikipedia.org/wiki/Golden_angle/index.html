Golden angle

In geometry, the golden angle is the smaller of the two angles created by dividing the circumference of a circle in the golden ratio; that is, into two arcs such that the ratio of the length of the smaller arc to the length of the larger arc is the same as the ratio of the length of the larger arc to the full circumference of the circle.

Algebraically, if the circumference is divided into arcs of length a (larger) and b (smaller) with (a+b)/a = a/b equal to the golden ratio φ, then the golden angle is the angle subtended by the smaller arc, equal to 2π/φ2 radians, or approximately 137.5077640500378 degrees — often rounded to 137.5°.

Role in phyllotaxis

The golden angle plays a significant role in the theory of phyllotaxis. Successive leaves, florets, or seeds in many plants emerge separated by approximately the golden angle, an arrangement that packs structures efficiently and maximises exposure to light and rain. Because the golden ratio is, in a precise sense, the "most irrational" number, successive elements never line up periodically, producing the spiral patterns visible in sunflower heads and pine cones, whose spiral counts are consecutive Fibonacci numbers.
