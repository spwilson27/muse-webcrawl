Treasure trove

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Treasure trove is an amount of money or coin, gold, silver, plate, or bullion found hidden underground or in places such as cellars or attics, where the treasure seems old enough for it to be presumed that the true owner is dead and the heirs undiscoverable. In English law the term once had a precise common-law meaning: such treasure belonged to the Crown, and concealing it was an offence. The phrase derives from the French trésor trouvé — "found treasure".

England and Wales

Under the common law, treasure trove required that the objects be substantially gold or silver, deliberately hidden with an intention of recovery (animus revertendi), and that the owner be unknown. Casual losses and abandoned items did not qualify, and finds by metal detectorists in the late twentieth century exposed the doctrine's gaps — notably the uncertain treatment of hoards interred with the dead. The Treasure Act 1996 replaced the common law in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland with a broader definition: objects (other than coins) at least 300 years old with a precious-metal content of at least 10 per cent, groups of coins from the same find, and associated objects. Finders must report treasure to the local coroner within 14 days; museums may acquire it after valuation, with a reward normally shared between finder and landowner. The Portable Antiquities Scheme records non-treasure finds voluntarily. Significant finds under the Act include the Staffordshire Hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold (2009) and the Vale of York Hoard. In 2023 the definition was extended to include exceptionally significant objects regardless of material.

Scotland and other jurisdictions

Scots law never restricted the doctrine to precious metal: all ownerless portable antiquities are bona vacantia and belong to the Crown, administered by the King's and Lord Treasurer's Remembrancer, with the Treasure Trove Unit assessing finds and rewards paid at discretion. Many other countries vest archaeological finds in the state wholesale. In the United States there is no general federal treasure-trove law; rights are governed by state property law distinguishing treasure trove from lost, mislaid, and abandoned property, and finds on federal land are regulated by statutes such as the Archaeological Resources Protection Act.

In popular culture

"Treasure trove" has become a general metaphor for any rich, serendipitous cache of valuable things — documents, recordings, data — while buried treasure remains a staple of folklore and fiction from Treasure Island to modern adventure films.
