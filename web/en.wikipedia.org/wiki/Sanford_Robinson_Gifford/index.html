Sanford Robinson Gifford (10 July 1823 – 29 August 1880) was an American landscape painter and one of the leading figures of the second generation of the Hudson River School. He is particularly associated with luminism, the style characterised by calm water, atmospheric haze and glowing, nearly invisible brushwork. Gifford's best-known works depict scenes in the Catskill and Adirondack mountains, New England, and Italy.

Life

Gifford was born in Greenfield, Saratoga County, New York, and grew up in Hudson, New York. He attended Brown University for two years before leaving in 1845 to study art in New York City, drawing from casts at the National Academy of Design and taking instruction in perspective and anatomy. A summer sketching trip to the Catskills and Berkshires in 1846 fixed his vocation as a landscape painter.

From 1855 to 1857 Gifford travelled in Europe, meeting John Ruskin's ideas through the writings then fashionable among landscape painters; in Rome he spent the winter of 1856–57 working alongside Worthington Whittredge and travelled with Albert Bierstadt. During the American Civil War he served in the New York militia, including during the defence of Washington and the period of the New York draft riots. A second European and Near Eastern journey in 1868–69 took him to Italy, Greece, Syria, Egypt and the Holy Land, supplying subjects for the rest of his career.

Work and reputation

Gifford was elected a member of the National Academy of Design in 1854 and exhibited there for more than three decades. Signature paintings include Lake Nemi (1856–57), The Wilderness (1860), A Coming Storm (1863/1880), Kauterskill Clove (1862) and numerous views of Hunter Mountain and the Catskill lakes. His mature style subordinates detail to overall atmospheric effect: distances dissolve in warm haze, water mirrors the sky, and tiny figures give scale to serene expanses.

After his death in 1880, a memorial exhibition at the Metropolitan Museum of Art displayed some 160 of his works, and the sale of his studio collection became a landmark event. Modern scholarship has restored Gifford to a central place among American luminists, with major exhibitions and a catalogue raisonné published in the early 21st century.
