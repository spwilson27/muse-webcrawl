Chaos theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Lorenz attractor, the butterfly-shaped strange attractor of a chaotic system]

Chaos theory is a branch of mathematics focusing on the behaviour of dynamical systems that are highly sensitive to initial conditions — a phenomenon known as the butterfly effect. Small differences in starting conditions yield widely diverging outcomes, making long-term prediction impossible although the systems are deterministic.

Edward Lorenz discovered chaotic behaviour in a simplified weather model in the 1960s, finding that rounding his input data changed the forecast completely; his 1963 paper 'Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow' founded the modern subject.

Key concepts

Chaotic systems exhibit strange attractors — bounded regions of state space with fractal structure — and period-doubling routes to chaos, universal in systems as different as dripping taps and population models. The logistic map shows how simple nonlinear equations generate chaos as a parameter increases; Mitchell Feigenbaum discovered a universal constant (≈ 4.669) governing such transitions.

Chaos appears in weather and climate, fluid turbulence, cardiac rhythms, orbital mechanics, and economics. Chaos theory overlaps with nonlinear dynamics, ergodic theory, and fractal geometry.
