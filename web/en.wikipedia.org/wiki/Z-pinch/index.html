Z-pinch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fusion power research, a z-pinch is a type of plasma confinement system that uses an electrical current in the plasma to generate a magnetic field that compresses it (see pinch). These systems were originally referred to simply as pinch or Bennett pinch, but the introduction of the θ-pinch (theta pinch) led to the need for a clearer, more precise name.

Physics

The name refers to the direction of the current in the device, the z-axis on a normal two-dimensional graph of x and y coordinates. In a z-pinch, a large current runs axially through a cylindrical plasma; the current's own azimuthal magnetic field produces a Lorentz force that squeezes ("pinches") the plasma toward the axis, heating and compressing it. Any setup that produces a pinch effect due to current running in that direction is correctly referred to as a z-pinch system, including early toroidal machines.

Z-pinches suffer from MHD instabilities, notably the sausage (m=0) and kink (m=1) instabilities, which limited early fusion efforts. Modern wire-array z-pinches, driven by machines such as Sandia National Laboratories' Z Pulsed Power Facility, produce enormous X-ray pulses used for inertial confinement fusion experiments and high-energy-density physics; the Z machine has achieved some of the highest temperatures ever produced in a laboratory. Related concepts include the dense plasma focus and the sheared-flow stabilized z-pinch.
