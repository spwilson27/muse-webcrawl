Motu (geography)

A motu (pronounced "moh-too") is a reef islet formed by broken coral and sand, found surrounding an atoll's lagoon. The word is Tahitian and Polynesian in origin and is used throughout the South Pacific, particularly in French Polynesia, to describe the small, low-lying islands that sit on the barrier reef rim of an atoll.

Motu are typically narrow strips of land, rarely more than a few hundred metres wide, covered with coconut palms and other salt-tolerant vegetation. Because they rise only a few metres above sea level, they are vulnerable to storm surges and to sea-level rise associated with climate change.

Formation

Motu form when coral rubble, shells, and sand are deposited by waves and currents on the shallow reef flat of an atoll. Over time, storm events pile material above the high-tide line, and vegetation takes hold, stabilising the deposit. The continual supply of coral debris from the reef allows motu to persist and migrate slowly across the reef flat.

Use and habitation

Many motu are uninhabited, but others support villages, copra plantations, pearl farms, and — in modern times — private resort islands. In the Tuamotu Archipelago and the Society Islands of French Polynesia, the main settlement of an atoll is often located on its largest motu. Fresh water is scarce; inhabitants traditionally rely on rainwater catchment and a thin freshwater lens floating on salt water beneath the island.
