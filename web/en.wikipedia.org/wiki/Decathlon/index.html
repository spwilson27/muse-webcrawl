Decathlon

The decathlon is a combined event in athletics consisting of ten track and field events. The word "decathlon" was formed, in analogy to the word "pentathlon", from the Greek words δέκα (déka, meaning "ten") and ἆθλος (áthlos, or ἆθλον, áthlon, meaning "contest" or "prize"). Events are held over two consecutive days and the winners are determined by the combined performance in all. Performance is judged on a points system in each event, not by the position achieved. The decathlon is contested mainly by male athletes, while female athletes typically compete in the heptathlon.

Traditionally, the title of "World's Greatest Athlete" has been given to the person who wins the decathlon. This began when King Gustaf V of Sweden told Jim Thorpe, the winner at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics, "You, sir, are the greatest athlete in the world." The events are split across two days:

History

The decathlon developed from the ancient pentathlon. Pentathlon competitions were held at the ancient Olympic Games. The modern decathlon was introduced at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics, where Jim Thorpe won by a large margin. The event has been part of the Olympic athletics programme ever since. An official Olympic event for women, the decathlon was historically replaced by the heptathlon (seven events), although a women's decathlon is increasingly contested and governed by World Athletics.

Scoring

The current scoring tables, adopted in 1985, award points for each performance using formulas of the form points = A × (B − P)C for track events and points = A × (P − B)C for field events, where P is the athlete's performance and A, B and C are event-specific constants. The benchmark performances scoring roughly 1,000 points per event were designed to approximate world-class marks.

Records

The men's world record is 9,126 points, set by Kevin Mayer of France in Talence in September 2018. Ashton Eaton of the United States previously held the record with 9,045 points and won Olympic gold in 2012 and 2016. The 9,000-point barrier has also been surpassed by Roman Šebrle of the Czech Republic, the first man to exceed it, in 2001.
