Bioprospecting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

SectorsPharmaceuticals, agriculture, cosmetics, biotechnology
SourcesPlants, microbes, marine organisms, extremophiles
Key frameworksConvention on Biological Diversity (1992); Nagoya Protocol (2010)
Criticism"Biopiracy" — uncompensated use of indigenous knowledge

Bioprospecting (also known as biodiversity prospecting) is the exploration of natural sources — plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms — for commercially valuable genetic and biochemical resources, such as new drugs, enzymes, crop traits, and cosmetic ingredients. Many important medicines derive from natural products, including aspirin (from willow bark compounds), the anticancer drug paclitaxel (from the Pacific yew), and artemisinin (from sweet wormwood, used in traditional Chinese medicine).

Process and examples

Bioprospecting typically involves collecting samples — often guided by ethnobotanical knowledge of traditional uses — then screening extracts for biological activity, isolating active compounds, and developing them commercially. A frequently cited model agreement was the 1991 partnership between Merck & Co. and Costa Rica's INBio institute, under which samples were exchanged for funding, training, and a share of potential royalties, channelling resources to conservation. Marine organisms and extremophiles (microbes living in hot springs, deep-sea vents, and polar regions) are increasingly prospected for novel enzymes stable under industrial conditions.

Ethics and law

The practice has been controversial: critics coin the term biopiracy for cases where companies patent products derived from indigenous knowledge or genetic resources without consent or fair compensation — prominent disputes involved patents on neem, turmeric, and basmati rice. The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) affirmed national sovereignty over genetic resources and the principle of access and benefit-sharing (ABS); its Nagoya Protocol (adopted 2010, in force 2014) established compliance obligations. Debate continues over how to handle digital sequence information derived from genetic resources.
