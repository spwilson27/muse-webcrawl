Wairau Affray

Date17 June 1843
LocationWairau Valley, Marlborough, South Island, New Zealand
CombatantsNew Zealand Company settlers and officials vs. Ngāti Toa chiefs and warriors
Key figuresArthur Wakefield, Henry Thompson; Te Rauparaha, Te Rangihaeata
Casualties22 Europeans killed, 5 wounded; 4 Māori killed, 3 wounded

The Wairau Affray (also known historically as the Wairau Massacre) of 17 June 1843 was the first serious clash of arms between Māori and British settlers in New Zealand after the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840. It took place in the Wairau Valley, near modern Tuamarina in Marlborough, and involved settlers of the New Zealand Company and the Ngāti Toa chiefs Te Rauparaha and Te Rangihaeata.

Background

The New Zealand Company, promoting systematic colonisation, had purchased land around Cook Strait for its Nelson settlement, and claimed — on the basis of a deed of disputed validity — to have bought the Wairau Valley. Ngāti Toa, who had conquered the region in the 1820s and 1830s, denied that the Wairau had been sold. When Company surveyors began work in the valley in early 1843, Ngāti Toa obstructed them non-violently, removing survey equipment and burning a surveyors' hut built of raupō (bulrush).

The clash

On 17 June 1843 a party of about 49 settlers and officials from Nelson, led by Captain Arthur Wakefield and police magistrate Henry Thompson, arrived to arrest Te Rauparaha and Te Rangihaeata for arson. During a tense confrontation, a shot was fired — probably accidentally — killing Te Rangihaeata's wife Te Rongo (also a daughter of Te Rauparaha). In the ensuing fight several settlers were killed; the survivors surrendered, and a number of the prisoners, including Wakefield and Thompson, were then killed, apparently in accordance with utu for the death of Te Rongo. In total 22 Europeans and 4 Māori died.

Aftermath

News of the killings caused panic and demands for military reprisal in the colony. However, Governor Robert FitzRoy investigated and concluded that the settlers had been in the wrong: the land had not been legitimately purchased, and the attempted arrest was unlawful. He declined to take action against Ngāti Toa. The incident exposed the fragility of relations between the Crown, settlers, and Māori and foreshadowed the New Zealand Wars of the following decades. A memorial at Tuamarina cemetery marks the graves of the settlers who died.
