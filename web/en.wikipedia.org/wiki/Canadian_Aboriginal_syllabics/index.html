Canadian Aboriginal syllabics are a family of abugida writing systems used for several Indigenous languages of Canada, including Cree, Ojibwe (Anishinaabemowin), and Inuktitut. The system was devised in the 1840s by James Evans, a Wesleyan missionary working at Norway House in what is now Manitoba, initially for Ojibwe and then adapted for Cree.

Structure

The script is an abugida: each character represents a consonant–vowel syllable. The shape of the character indicates the consonant, while its orientation — rotated through up to four positions — indicates the vowel. Standalone small characters mark final consonants. In Inuktitut, additional dots (long vowel markers) and extra characters accommodate sounds not present in Cree.

History and spread

Evans cast type for his syllabary using improvised materials — legend holds that early type was made from the lead of tea chests and printing done on a homemade press. The system spread rapidly among Cree communities, reportedly achieving high literacy rates, and was adapted by other missionaries and Indigenous communities for languages including Naskapi, Dakota, and Blackfoot (in a modified form). Syllabics remain in everyday use, especially for Inuktitut in Nunavut and Nunavik, where they appear on signage, in government documents and in media.

Unicode

The characters are encoded in the Unicode block "Unified Canadian Aboriginal Syllabics" (U+1400–U+167F) and its extensions.
