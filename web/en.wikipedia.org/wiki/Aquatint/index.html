Aquatint

An aquatint print; the technique produces tonal areas rather than lines.

Aquatint is an intaglio printmaking technique, a variant of etching that produces areas of tone rather than lines. Unlike a line engraving or etching, in which the image is built from lines, the aquatint plate is worked so that large areas hold ink, giving prints a quality reminiscent of watercolour washes and ink drawings. The process was popular in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.

Technique

The artist covers a copper or zinc plate with a porous ground of powdered resin (traditionally rosin), which is heated so the grains adhere. Immersed in acid, the plate is bitten in the tiny channels between the grains, creating a fine network of pits that hold ink and print as tone. Areas intended to stay light are "stopped out" with varnish; progressively longer biting produces progressively darker tones. A plate may be bitten many times to build up a full range of values.

Artists

The technique was refined in the 1760s–70s by Jean-Baptiste Le Prince in France and Paul Sandby in England. Its most famous practitioner is Francisco Goya, whose series Los Caprichos (1799) and The Disasters of War combine aquatint with etching to powerful effect. Aquatint was also widely used for topographical and sporting prints, often printed in colour and finished by hand.
