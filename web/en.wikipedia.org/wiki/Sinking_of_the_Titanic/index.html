Sinking of the Titanic

Date14–15 April 1912
LocationNorth Atlantic Ocean, 370 miles (600 km) southeast of Newfoundland
CauseCollision with an iceberg
Deaths1,490–1,503 (estimates vary)
SurvivorsApproximately 710

RMS Titanic sank in the early morning hours of 15 April 1912 in the North Atlantic Ocean, four days into her maiden voyage from Southampton to New York City. The largest ocean liner in service at the time, Titanic was carrying an estimated 2,224 people when she struck an iceberg at around 23:40 (ship's time) on Sunday, 14 April 1912. Her sinking two hours and forty minutes later, at 02:20 on Monday, 15 April, resulted in the deaths of more than 1,500 people, making it one of the deadliest peacetime maritime disasters in history.

Background

Titanic received several warnings of drifting ice on 14 April but was travelling at near maximum speed — about 22 knots — when lookouts sighted the iceberg. Unable to turn quickly enough, the ship suffered a glancing blow that buckled her starboard hull plates and breached five of her sixteen watertight compartments — one more than she could stay afloat with. The ship's designers, including Thomas Andrews, who was aboard, recognised within an hour that the ship would sink.

Evacuation

The liner carried only enough lifeboats for about half of those aboard, a deficiency that was legal under the regulations of the day. Distress rockets were fired and wireless operators Jack Phillips and Harold Bride sent CQD and SOS calls; the Cunard liner Carpathia, some 58 miles away, raced to the scene. Evacuation was disordered: many boats were launched partially empty, and loading followed a "women and children first" protocol interpreted strictly on the port side by Second Officer Charles Lightoller. Titanic broke in two as she sank, plunging hundreds still aboard into lethally cold water of about −2 °C. Carpathia arrived around 04:00 and rescued roughly 710 survivors.

Aftermath

The disaster provoked worldwide outrage and led to major reforms: the first International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) in 1914, requirements for enough lifeboats for everyone aboard, 24-hour radio watches, and the establishment of the International Ice Patrol. Inquiries in Britain and the United States criticised the ship's speed, the insufficient lifeboats, and the failure of the nearby SS Californian to respond. The wreck was located in 1985 by a team led by Robert Ballard, lying in two pieces at a depth of about 3,800 metres (12,500 ft).
