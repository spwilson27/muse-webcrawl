Depiction of Jesus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The depiction of Jesus in art dates to early Christianity. No physical description of Jesus is given in the New Testament, and the earliest Christian art represented him symbolically — as a fish (ichthys), a lamb, or an anchor — or as the Good Shepherd, a beardless youth. By the 4th century, an increasingly standardised image emerged: a long-haired, bearded man, often robed, which became dominant in Byzantine and Western art for more than a thousand years.

Early and Byzantine images

Frescoes in the Roman catacombs and the Dura-Europos church (3rd century) show Jesus as a young, beardless teacher and miracle-worker. After Christianity became the religion of the Roman Empire, imperial iconography influenced depictions: Christ was shown enthroned in majesty as Christ Pantocrator ("Almighty"), as in the 6th-century encaustic icon at Saint Catherine's Monastery, Sinai, and later the mosaics of Hagia Sophia and Monreale. The Byzantine iconoclasm controversies (8th–9th centuries) centred partly on whether such images were permissible at all.

Medieval and Renaissance development

Western medieval art developed new types — the suffering Christ on the cross (Christus patiens), the Man of Sorrows, the Sacred Heart and the Christ child. Renaissance artists such as Leonardo da Vinci (The Last Supper) and Michelangelo naturalised the figure while retaining the conventional features: shoulder-length brown hair parted in the middle, a beard, and European features. The apocryphal Letter of Lentulus and relic traditions such as the Veil of Veronica and the Shroud of Turin shaped popular expectations of Jesus's appearance.

Race and modern reassessment

Because Jesus was a first-century Galilean Jew, historians and artists have debated the ethnically European features of traditional imagery. Depictions in non-European Christian cultures — Ethiopian, Chinese, Indian, African and Latin American art — have long localised his features. In the modern period, images such as Warner Sallman's Head of Christ (1940) became ubiquitous in American Protestant churches, while forensic and scholarly reconstructions have proposed a shorter, darker-skinned, Middle Eastern appearance for the historical Jesus.
