Lazarus taxon

In paleontology, a Lazarus taxon (plural Lazarus taxa) is a taxon that disappears from the fossil record for one or more periods and later reappears — as if raised from the dead, by analogy with the biblical Lazarus of Bethany. The term was coined by Karl W. Flessa and David Jablonski in the 1980s. The pattern usually reflects gaps in fossilisation or sampling — a taxon surviving in an unrecorded refugium — rather than a genuine extinction and re-evolution, though it can also arise from misidentification of the later specimens.

Explanations

Several mechanisms produce the Lazarus pattern. A lineage may survive at very low abundance or in environments that rarely fossilise (the "refugia" explanation); it may be locally extirpated and later recolonise from elsewhere; or the reappearing fossils may be misidentified convergences — so-called Elvis taxa, which mimic an extinct taxon without being its descendants. Related concepts include the zombie effect, in which fossils are reworked from older rocks into younger layers, and the Signor–Lipps effect, which smears apparent extinctions backwards in time. Lazarus taxa are especially common in the aftermath of mass extinctions such as the end-Permian, when impoverished faunas and poor preservation leave many lineages temporarily invisible.

Living examples ("living fossils")

By extension, the term is applied to living species that were believed extinct before being rediscovered, and to lineages with enormous gaps between their fossil record and the present. Famous cases include:

  • The coelacanth Latimeria, known from fossils ending in the Cretaceous, found alive off South Africa in 1938 (and a second species near Indonesia in the 1990s).
  • The takahe of New Zealand, presumed extinct and rediscovered in 1948.
  • The Laotian rock rat, described in 2005 as a new family but later recognised as a survivor of the Diatomyidae, a rodent family thought extinct for 11 million years.
  • The dawn redwood (Metasequoia), described from fossils in 1941 and found growing in China a few years later.
