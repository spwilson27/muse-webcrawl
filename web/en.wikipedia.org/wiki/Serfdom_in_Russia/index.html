Serfdom in Russia

Russian serfdom
Periodc. 1649 – 1861 (legally binding form)
Abolished3 March 1861 (Emancipation Manifesto)
Peak extentRoughly a third of the total population, and over half of peasants on private estates, in the mid-19th century

Serfdom in Russia was the system of feudal bondage under which the great majority of Russian peasants were legally bound to the land and to their landlords for more than two centuries. It developed gradually from the sixteenth century, was fully codified in the Law Code (Sobornoye Ulozhenie) of 1649, and was abolished by Tsar Alexander II's Emancipation Manifesto of 1861.

Development

From the late 1500s, decrees progressively restricted peasants' traditional right to move between landowners, at first only around St. Yuri's Day and then entirely. The 1649 code removed any time limit on the recovery of runaway serfs, effectively making bondage hereditary. Under Peter the Great and his successors the serfs' status deteriorated further: they could be sold apart from the land, conscripted into the army for 25-year terms, and subjected to corporal punishment by their owners.

Conditions

Serfs owed their lords labour dues (barshchina) or money/goods dues (obrok). Serfdom extended beyond agriculture — household serfs, serf artisans, and even serf theatre troupes and orchestras were maintained on great estates. Resistance ranged from flight to the frontier to large-scale revolts, most famously the Pugachev Rebellion of 1773–75.

Emancipation

Following defeat in the Crimean War, Alexander II concluded that serfdom threatened Russia's military and economic future. The Emancipation Manifesto of 3 March 1861 freed more than 23 million privately held serfs, granting them personal liberty and allotments of land, though redemption payments and communal land tenure limited the reform's benefits for decades.
