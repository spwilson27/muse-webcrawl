Enfilade and defilade

DomainMilitary tactics

Enfilade and defilade are concepts in military tactics. Enfilade fire is gunfire directed along the length of an enemy formation or position, so that a single line of fire passes through the greatest possible depth of the target. Defilade is the protection of a position or vehicle by terrain or fortification that masks it from direct fire and observation — the opposite condition to being enfiladed.

Enfilade

Fire is most effective when it strikes a formation along its longest axis: a line of infantry, a trench, or a column of vehicles presents the maximum target depth, and rounds that miss one element may strike another behind it. Machine guns are therefore commonly sited to deliver flanking, enfilade fire across the front of a defended position, and in fortification design the flanking towers and caponiers of early modern fortresses existed to enfilade the ditch. In naval warfare, the analogous manoeuvre of crossing the T allowed a fleet to enfilade an enemy line, and "raking fire" along a ship's length was especially destructive.

Defilade

A unit in defilade is shielded by a natural or artificial obstacle — a ridge, a building, or a parapet — from the enemy's direct fire and view, while ideally still able to observe or engage. Armoured vehicles use hull-down positions, in which the hull is hidden behind a crest and only the turret is exposed; a fully hidden position is described as turret-down. Artillery and mortars habitually fire from defilade, using indirect fire directed by observers elsewhere.

Historical usage

Both terms come from French: enfiler, "to thread," and défiler, "to file past" or "to take cover." They became standard in English-language military writing during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and remain in routine use in modern doctrine.
