Loading gauge

A loading gauge is a diagram or physical structure defining the maximum height and width dimensions in which railway vehicles may be built or loaded, so that they can pass safely through tunnels, under bridges and past platform edges and trackside structures. The corresponding limit for the infrastructure itself is the structure gauge; the space between the two allows for movement, suspension travel and tolerances.

History

Britain's early railways were built by many independent companies to varying clearances, leaving the British network with one of the most restrictive loading gauges in the world. The standard gauge used on much of the historic network restricts vehicles to roughly 2.8 m in width and under 4 m in height, which limits freight container sizes and double-decker trains. The Berne gauge, agreed internationally in 1912, standardised a more generous profile for much of continental Europe.

Gauges around the world

  • Continental Europe — UIC gauges (GA, GB, GC) permit vehicles up to about 3.15 m wide and over 4 m tall; the GC gauge accommodates double-stack or tall freight traffic on some routes.
  • Great Britain — gauges W6–W12 define progressively larger freight profiles; W10 and W12 allow 9 ft 6 in high-cube containers on standard wagons.
  • North America — AAR "plates" define freight car envelopes; generous clearances permit double-stack container trains and large autorack cars.
  • Japan — narrow-gauge lines have a small loading gauge, while the Shinkansen, built to standard gauge, uses a much larger profile.

Importance

The loading gauge determines the maximum size of rolling stock and freight loads and therefore a railway's capacity and economics. Enlarging gauges on old networks is extremely expensive because bridges and tunnels must be rebuilt, so the historic loading gauge permanently shapes vehicle design — one reason British trains are narrower and, in the case of tilting trains, specially profiled to fit through the infrastructure.
