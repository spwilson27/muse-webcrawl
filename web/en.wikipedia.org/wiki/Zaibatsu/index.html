Zaibatsu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Zaibatsu (財閥, "financial clique" or "wealthy family group") is a Japanese term referring to industrial and financial vertically integrated business conglomerates in the Empire of Japan, whose influence and size allowed control over significant parts of the Japanese economy from the Meiji period to the end of World War II.

History

The great zaibatsu grew out of family-owned merchant and industrial houses that expanded under Meiji industrialization, typically organized around a holding company controlled by a single family, with its own bank financing subsidiaries across mining, shipping, steel, chemicals, and trade. The "Big Four" were Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo, and Yasuda; other major groups included Asano, Furukawa, Nissan, and Nakajima.

The zaibatsu enjoyed close ties to the state and military, and profited from Japan's expansion in the 1930s. After World War II, the Allied occupation under General MacArthur dissolved the zaibatsu holding companies, dispersing family shareholdings, as part of economic democratization. However, many former zaibatsu firms later re-coalesced as keiretsu — looser networks of companies linked by cross-shareholdings and a main bank, exemplified by the modern Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and Sumitomo groups.
