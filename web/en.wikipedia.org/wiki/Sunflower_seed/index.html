Sunflower seed

The sunflower seed is the seed of the sunflower (Helianthus annuus). Strictly it is an achene — a fruit in which the seed is enclosed in a hard husk or hull that does not split open. There are three main types used commercially: linoleic (the most common, for oil and snack use), high oleic, and sunflower oilseed varieties grown for crushing. Whole seeds are eaten as a snack, dehulled kernels are used in baking and confectionery, and the seeds are pressed for sunflower oil, with the remaining meal used as livestock feed.

Nutrition

Sunflower seeds are energy-dense, providing about 584 kilocalories per 100 grams, and are rich in linoleic acid, vitamin E, magnesium, phosphorus, selenium, and B vitamins, as well as phytosterols and phenolic antioxidants. The kernels contain roughly 20% protein and over 50% fat in oilseed types.

Uses

In-shell seeds are roasted and salted and consumed as a snack — a custom especially associated with baseball players and fans in the United States, and widespread in Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union, where semechki are cracked between the teeth. Dehulled seeds appear in breads, granola, trail mixes, and sunflower-seed butter, a peanut-free alternative. Lower-grade seeds are sold as bird feed and pet food. Sunflower oil, especially the high-oleic type, is a major world vegetable oil, and sunflower lecithin is used as an emulsifier.

Production

The sunflower was domesticated in North America and taken to Europe in the 16th century; commercial oil production developed in the Russian Empire in the 19th century. Today Ukraine and Russia are historically the largest producers, followed by Argentina, China, Romania, Turkey, and the United States.
