Armour

Armour (Commonwealth English) or armor (American English) is a covering used to protect an object, individual, or vehicle from physical injury or damage, especially direct contact weapons or projectiles during combat, or from a potentially dangerous environment or activity. Personal armour has been used since antiquity to protect soldiers, and vehicle armour is used on warships, armoured fighting vehicles, and some combat aircraft.

A Gothic plate harness, c. 1480 — the peak of personal armour development before gunpowder weapons dominated.

History

The earliest armour was made of hide, bone, and wood; bronze helmets and cuirasses were worn in the ancient world, and Greek hoplites fought behind bronze-faced shields. Mail (interlinked iron rings) was used by Celts, Romans, and medieval warriors for over a thousand years. From the fourteenth century, articulated plate armour progressively replaced mail in Europe, reaching its fullest development in the Gothic and Maximilian styles of the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. The spread of firearms made thick plate impractical for infantry, though helmets and breastplates persisted, and steel helmets returned in the First World War.

Modern armour

Twentieth-century development returned personal protection through ballistic fabrics: Kevlar and other aramids, ceramic strike plates, and ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene are used in bulletproof vests and helmets. Vehicle armour progressed from riveted and rolled homogeneous steel to composite arrays (such as Chobham armour), explosive reactive armour, slat armour against rocket-propelled grenades, and active protection systems that intercept incoming projectiles.

Animal armour

Many animals have natural armour — shells, scales, osteoderms, and the bony plates of armadillos — which has inspired biomimetic designs in materials engineering.
