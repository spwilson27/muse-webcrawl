Guerrilla warfare

Guerrilla warfare is a form of irregular warfare in which small groups of combatants — often paramilitaries, partisans or insurgents — use hit-and-run tactics, ambushes, sabotage and raids against a larger conventional force. The term comes from Spanish guerrilla ('little war'), from the Peninsular War against Napoleon.

TacticsAmbush, sabotage, raids, mobility
TheoristsMao Zedong, Che Guevara, T. E. Lawrence, Võ Nguyên Giáp
ExamplesPeninsular War, Viet Cong, Afghan mujahideen, Yugoslav Partisans

Strategy

Mao's theory, in On Guerrilla Warfare (1937), describes protracted war in phases from organisation and guerrilla action to mobile and positional warfare, with guerrillas as 'fish' swimming among the 'sea' of the people. Success depends on popular support, terrain, sanctuary and political aims; counterinsurgency seeks to separate insurgents from the population.

History

Examples range from Fabius against Hannibal and Spanish partisans, to Boer commandos, Lawrence of Arabia, WWII resistance movements, the Chinese Civil War, Vietnam and Afghanistan. See Asymmetric warfare and Counterinsurgency.
