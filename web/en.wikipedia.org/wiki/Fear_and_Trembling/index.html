Fear and Trembling

Author: Søren Kierkegaard (as Johannes de silentio)
Original title: Frygt og Bæven
Published: 16 October 1843, Copenhagen
Subject: Faith, ethics, the binding of Isaac

Fear and Trembling (Danish: Frygt og Bæven) is a philosophical work by the Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard, published in 1843 under the pseudonym Johannes de silentio ("John of Silence"). Taking its title from a phrase in the Epistle to the Philippians ("work out your own salvation with fear and trembling"), the book is a meditation on the biblical story of Abraham's willingness to sacrifice his son Isaac, and on the nature of faith.

Content

The work opens with an "attunement" retelling the journey to Mount Moriah four ways, then presents three "problemata". Kierkegaard, through his pseudonym, contrasts the knight of infinite resignation, who renounces what he loves and reconciles himself to the loss, with the knight of faith, who resigns everything yet — "by virtue of the absurd" — believes he will receive it back in this life. Abraham is the supreme knight of faith: commanded by God to kill Isaac, the very child through whom God's promise was to be fulfilled, he suspends the universal ethical prohibition against murder for the sake of his absolute relation to God. This yields the book's most famous doctrine, the teleological suspension of the ethical: for faith, the single individual stands higher than the universal. If Abraham cannot be understood in this way, de silentio argues, then by ethical standards he is simply a murderer.

Reception

Published the same year as Repetition and a few months after Either/Or, Fear and Trembling became one of Kierkegaard's most read and debated works and a founding text for existentialism. Its account of faith as passionate commitment beyond reason influenced theologians such as Karl Barth and Paul Tillich and philosophers including Jacques Derrida, who returned to the Abraham story in The Gift of Death. Critics have questioned whether the "suspension" licenses religious violence, a debate kept alive by the book's refusal to make Abraham ethically intelligible.
