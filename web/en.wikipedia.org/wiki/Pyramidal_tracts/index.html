Pyramidal tracts

The pyramidal tracts include both the corticobulbar tract and the corticospinal tract. These are aggregations of efferent nerve fibers from the upper motor neurons that travel from the cerebral cortex and terminate either in the brainstem (corticobulbar) or spinal cord (corticospinal) and are involved in the control of motor functions of the body.

The corticospinal tract conducts impulses from the brain to the spinal cord. It is made up of the lateral and anterior corticospinal tracts. The decussation (crossing) of the lateral corticospinal tract occurs in the medulla, at the pyramids — hence the name. About 90% of fibers cross at the pyramidal decussation to form the lateral corticospinal tract, while the remainder descend uncrossed in the anterior corticospinal tract.

Clinical significance

Damage to the pyramidal tracts produces the signs of upper motor neuron syndrome, including spasticity, hyperreflexia, and the Babinski sign. Lesions can result from stroke, multiple sclerosis, or spinal cord injury. The corticobulbar tract supplies the motor nuclei of the cranial nerves controlling the face, tongue, and throat.
