Strain gauge

A strain gauge (also sometimes spelled strain gage) is a device used to measure strain on an object. Invented by Edward E. Simmons and Arthur C. Ruge in 1938, the most common type consists of an insulating flexible backing supporting a metallic foil pattern. The gauge is attached to the object by a suitable adhesive; as the object is deformed, the foil is deformed, causing its electrical resistance to change. This resistance change, measured with a Wheatstone bridge, is related to the strain by the gauge factor.

Principle

Under tension, the conductor lengthens and narrows, increasing resistance; under compression the reverse occurs. The gauge factor — the ratio of relative resistance change to strain — is about 2 for metallic foil gauges and much higher, but less linear, for semiconductor gauges. The fine zig-zag pattern lengthens the conductor in a small area and aligns most of it with the measured strain direction, while minimising sensitivity to transverse strain (though not completely).

Applications

Strain gauges are fundamental to experimental stress analysis and to transducers: load cells, torque and pressure sensors, accelerometers, and weighing scales all convert force into strain on a spring element measured by gauges. They are used for structural health monitoring of bridges, aircraft, and machinery, and in geotechnical instruments. Practical measurements must correct for temperature effects and lead-wire resistance, using dummy gauges, bridge completion, and three-wire or four-wire circuits.
