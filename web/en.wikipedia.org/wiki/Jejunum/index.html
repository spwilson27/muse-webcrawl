The jejunum is the middle section of the small intestine in humans and most vertebrates, connecting the duodenum to the ileum. Together with the rest of the small intestine it is the principal site of nutrient absorption; most of the digestion products — amino acids, sugars, fatty acids, vitamins and minerals — are absorbed across its wall into the blood.

Anatomy

The jejunum begins at the suspensory muscle of the duodenum (ligament of Treitz) on the left side of the abdomen. There is no sharp anatomical boundary between the jejunum and the ileum, but the jejunum is conventionally the proximal two-fifths of the small bowel beyond the duodenum. It lies in the central and left abdomen, suspended by the mesentery, through which run the jejunal branches of the superior mesenteric artery and vein.

Structure and function

The inner surface of the jejunum is thrown into tall circular folds (plicae circulares) and covered in finger-like villi and microscopic microvilli, hugely increasing its absorptive area. Compared with the ileum, the jejunum has a thicker wall, a wider lumen, longer vasa recta and fewer Peyer's patches and less mesenteric fat. Its name derives from the Latin jejunus, "fasting" or "empty", because early anatomists found it empty after death.

Clinical significance

The jejunum may be affected by coeliac disease, infections, tumours and volvulus. Because of its central role in absorption, extensive jejunal resection can cause short bowel syndrome. The jejunum is used in reconstructive surgery, for example in Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and in jejunostomy feeding tubes.
