CITES (the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora, also known as the Washington Convention) is a multilateral treaty to protect endangered plants and animals from the threats of international trade. It was drafted at a meeting of the IUCN in 1963, signed in Washington, D.C., on 3 March 1973, and entered into force on 1 July 1975. CITES is one of the largest and oldest conservation agreements in existence; its secretariat, administered by the United Nations Environment Programme, is based in Geneva.

How it works

CITES does not ban trade outright; it subjects international trade in listed species to permits and certificates, issued only when national Scientific and Management Authorities are satisfied that trade will not harm the species' survival. It is legally binding on parties but not self-executing — each party must adopt domestic implementing legislation.

Appendices

  • Appendix I — species threatened with extinction (~1,200 species, including tigers, great apes, and sea turtles). Commercial trade is generally prohibited.
  • Appendix II — species not necessarily now threatened but which may become so if trade is uncontrolled (~39,000 species, including most orchids, cacti, corals, sharks and many timber trees). Trade is regulated by export permit.
  • Appendix III — species protected in at least one member country that has asked CITES for assistance in controlling trade.

Listings are amended at meetings of the Conference of the Parties (CoP), held roughly every three years.

Debates

CITES is best known to the public for the ivory trade: elephants were moved to Appendix I in 1989, and controversial one-off ivory sales from southern African states were later permitted. Continuing controversies include rhino horn, pangolins (moved to Appendix I in 2016), totoaba and vaquita, trophy hunting, and the treaty's effectiveness against illegal wildlife trade, which it addresses in cooperation with bodies such as INTERPOL and the World Customs Organization.
