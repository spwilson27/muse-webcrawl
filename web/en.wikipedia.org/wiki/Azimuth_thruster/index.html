Azimuth thruster

An azimuth thruster is a configuration of marine propellers placed in pods that can be rotated to any horizontal angle (azimuth), making a rudder unnecessary. These give ships and offshore platforms far better manoeuvrability than a fixed propeller and rudder system.

An azimuthing podded propulsion unit

Types

In a mechanically driven unit (such as the Z-drive and L-drive), a diesel engine or electric motor inside the hull drives the propeller through shafts and bevel gears. In an electrically driven podded unit, such as the Azipod developed by ABB and Kværner Masa-Yards, the electric motor is housed inside the pod itself, and the propeller is mounted directly on the motor shaft.

Advantages

  • 360-degree thrust, allowing the vessel to move in any direction and turn in place
  • Excellent low-speed handling, widely used in tugboats, ferries, and dynamic positioning systems
  • No need for a separate rudder or stern thrusters
  • Pods can improve hydrodynamic efficiency and free up internal hull space

History

The modern azimuth thruster was developed in Finland: engineer Josef Becker patented a related Z-drive in Germany in the 1950s, and the Azipod concept was introduced in 1990 through a collaboration between the Finnish Maritime Administration, Kvaerner Masa-Yards, and ABB. Azipods were first installed on the icebreaking tanker Uikku in 1993 and have since become standard on cruise ships and icebreakers, where the pod can also act as an ice-breaking rudder.
