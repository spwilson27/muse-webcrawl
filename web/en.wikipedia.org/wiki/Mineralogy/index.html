Mineralogy is a subject of geology specialising in the scientific study of the chemistry, crystal structure and physical properties of minerals and mineralised artefacts. Specific studies within mineralogy include the processes of mineral origin and formation, classification of minerals, their geographical distribution, and their utilisation.

Mineralogists describe and classify minerals by their chemistry and crystal structure.

History

Early writing on minerals includes Pliny the Elder's Natural History and the Chinese and Islamic traditions of stone lore. The modern science is often dated to Georgius Agricola's De natura fossilium (1546) and De re metallica (1556). The Danish scientist Nicolas Steno observed the constancy of interfacial angles in quartz crystals in 1669, a foundational law of crystallography. In the nineteenth century, chemical analysis and the polarising microscope transformed identification, and James Dwight Dana's System of Mineralogy (1837) established a chemical classification still influential today. X-ray diffraction, pioneered by Max von Laue and the Braggs after 1912, allowed crystal structures to be determined directly.

Physical properties

Minerals are identified by properties including crystal habit and form, hardness (measured on the Mohs scale), lustre, colour and streak, cleavage and fracture, specific gravity, and optical properties under the polarising microscope. Systematic mineralogy organises the roughly 6,000 known mineral species into classes — native elements, sulfides, halides, oxides, carbonates, sulfates, phosphates and silicates — with silicates dominant in the Earth's crust. New species are approved by the Commission on New Minerals, Nomenclature and Classification of the International Mineralogical Association.

Branches

  • Descriptive mineralogy — identification and classification of minerals.
  • Optical mineralogy — study of minerals in thin section under polarised light.
  • Crystallography — the arrangement of atoms in crystalline solids.
  • Economic mineralogy — minerals of commercial importance, ores and gemstones.
  • Environmental and medical mineralogy — mineral–environment and mineral–health interactions, such as asbestos and dust diseases.
  • Biomineralogy — minerals produced by living organisms.
