German East Africa

German East Africa (German: Deutsch-Ostafrika) was a German colony in the African Great Lakes region, which included present-day Burundi, Rwanda, the Tanzania mainland, and a small part of Mozambique. At about 994,000 km² it was Germany's largest colonial possession, nearly three times the size of Germany itself. The colony lasted from 1885 until Germany's defeat in the First World War, after which its territories were divided between Britain, Belgium, and Portugal.

Establishment

The colony originated in treaties signed in 1884 by Carl Peters of the Society for German Colonization with mainland chiefs opposite Zanzibar. An imperial charter in February 1885 placed the acquisitions under German protection, and the German East Africa Company administered the territory until the Reich took over in 1891 following the Abushiri Revolt of 1888–89, a coastal uprising against company rule. The Heligoland–Zanzibar Treaty of 1890 settled boundaries with Britain, which recognized German claims to the mainland while taking Zanzibar as a protectorate.

Administration and economy

German rule, centered on Dar es Salaam, relied on a small European staff, Swahili-speaking administrators (akidas), and the Schutztruppe of African askari soldiers under German officers. The economy was built on plantations of sisal, rubber, cotton, and coffee, supported by forced labour and taxation designed to compel wage work. Two railways were built: the northern Usambara line toward Mount Kilimanjaro and the central line from Dar es Salaam to Kigoma on Lake Tanganyika, completed in 1914.

Resistance: the Maji Maji Rebellion

Forced cotton cultivation provoked the Maji Maji Rebellion (1905–1907), which spread across the south of the colony, uniting many peoples behind the spirit medium Kinjikitile Ngwale's "maji" medicine believed to turn bullets to water. German forces suppressed the rising with scorched-earth tactics; the ensuing famine killed tens or hundreds of thousands of people — estimates of total deaths range from 75,000 to 300,000. The catastrophe led to administrative reforms under Governor Bernhard Dernburg.

First World War and division

In the East African campaign of 1914–1918, the Schutztruppe under Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck, never more than about 15,000 men, mostly askari, waged a guerrilla war against far larger British, Belgian, and Portuguese forces, surrendering only after the armistice in Europe, on 25 November 1918. Under the Treaty of Versailles the colony was partitioned: the bulk became the British mandate of Tanganyika, Rwanda and Burundi went to Belgium as Ruanda-Urundi, and the small Kionga Triangle was ceded to Portuguese Mozambique.
