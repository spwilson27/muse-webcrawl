Congruence (geometry)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geometry, two figures or objects are congruent if they have the same shape and size, or if one has the same shape and size as the mirror image of the other. More formally, two sets of points are called congruent if one can be transformed into the other by an isometry — a combination of translations, rotations, and reflections. Congruent figures can be superimposed exactly on one another.

Congruence of triangles

Two triangles are congruent if their corresponding sides and angles are equal. It is not necessary to check all six measurements: several criteria are sufficient. The standard criteria are SSS (side-side-side), SAS (side-angle-side), ASA (angle-side-angle), AAS (angle-angle-side), and, for right-angled triangles, RHS/HL (right angle–hypotenuse–side). The combination SSA (or ASS) does not in general guarantee congruence — the "ambiguous case" — and AAA determines only similarity, not congruence. In notation, the statement that triangle ABC is congruent to triangle DEF is written ▵ABC ≅ ▵DEF.

Relation to other concepts

Congruence is a stricter relation than similarity, which requires only the same shape, and than equality, which applies to identical objects. In analytic geometry, congruence can be expressed through distance-preserving transformations with orthogonal matrices of determinant ±1. The concept generalises to polygons (congruent when corresponding sides and angles are equal), circles (congruent when radii are equal), and to manifolds in differential geometry. The symbol ≅ was introduced by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in a related form and standardised in the nineteenth century.
