Freehold (law)

Freehold
Legal systemCommon law
Principal typesFee simple, fee tail, life estate
Contrasted withLeasehold (non-freehold) estates

In common law jurisdictions, a freehold is an estate in land of uncertain duration — traditionally, an interest held "freely" by a free tenant — as opposed to a leasehold, which is held for a fixed term. The freehold estates are the fee simple, the fee tail and the life estate. A freehold owner has rights of ownership that, in the case of a fee simple, are effectively permanent and inheritable.

History

The distinction descends from feudal land tenure in medieval England. After the Norman Conquest, all land was held of the Crown, and free tenants held estates that could last indefinitely — for life, or for the tenant and his heirs — whereas unfree (villein) tenure and later leaseholds were for limited durations. The statute Quia Emptores (1290) and the Tenures Abolition Act 1660 simplified the system, and modern reforms (in England, the Law of Property Act 1925) reduced the legal freehold estates to the fee simple absolute in possession and the term of years absolute (leasehold).

Types of freehold estate

  • Fee simple — the largest estate known to the law: absolute ownership for an unlimited time, freely transferable and inheritable. Most owner-occupied homes in England and Wales, the United States and Commonwealth countries are held in fee simple.
  • Fee tail — an estate inheritable only by the direct descendants ("heirs of the body") of the original holder, used historically to keep land in a family; abolished or restricted in most jurisdictions.
  • Life estate — ownership lasting only for the lifetime of a specified person, after which the land passes to a remainderman or reverts.

Freehold and leasehold in practice

In residential property markets, "freehold" houses are typically owned outright including the land, whereas flats are commonly sold leasehold: the buyer owns a long lease (often 99 or 125 years or more) but not the underlying land, and may pay ground rent and service charges to the freeholder. Leasehold structures and their abuses have prompted reform legislation in England and Wales, including rights of enfranchisement allowing leaseholders collectively to buy the freehold.
