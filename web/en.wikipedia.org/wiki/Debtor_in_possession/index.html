A debtor in possession (DIP) is a person or corporation that has filed for bankruptcy protection under Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code but remains in possession of property on which creditors have claims, and continues to operate its business. Under 11 U.S.C. §§ 1107 and 1108, the debtor in possession has most of the rights, powers, and duties of a bankruptcy trustee, subject to supervision by the court and, often, a committee of unsecured creditors.

Rights and duties

The DIP may use, sell, or lease property of the estate in the ordinary course of business, employ professionals, and—uniquely—exercise "avoiding powers" to set aside certain pre-bankruptcy transfers, such as preferences and fraudulent transfers. The DIP must file schedules and reports, pay certain taxes, and act as a fiduciary for creditors. Although the automatic stay protects the DIP from most collection actions, secured creditors may seek relief from the stay.

DIP financing

Because ordinary lenders are reluctant to extend credit to bankrupt companies, the Code authorizes special debtor-in-possession financing under § 364. With court approval, DIP loans can receive administrative priority or even "superpriority" status and liens senior to existing liens ("priming liens"). DIP financing is often supplied by the debtor's existing lenders and can give them considerable influence over the course and outcome of the case.

Displacement of the DIP

On request, the court may appoint a trustee to displace the DIP for cause—including fraud, dishonesty, incompetence, or gross mismanagement—or if appointment is in the interests of creditors (§ 1104). In practice, displacement is uncommon, and the DIP model—leaving existing management in place to reorganize—is a defining feature of American corporate bankruptcy law, contrasted with systems that routinely appoint administrators or receivers.
