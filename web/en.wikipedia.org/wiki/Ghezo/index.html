Ghezo, originally known as Prince Gakpe, was a king of Dahomey (in present-day Benin) who reigned from 1818 to 1858. He seized the throne from his brother Adandozan with the help of the Brazilian slave trader Francisco Félix de Sousa, and his long reign is regarded as a high point of the Dahomean kingdom's power.

Reign

Ghezo reformed the military and administration, expanded the kingdom's territory, and strengthened the famous corps of women soldiers known to Europeans as the Dahomey Amazons. Dahomey's economy depended heavily on the Atlantic slave trade through the port of Ouidah; under British pressure to end the trade, Ghezo gradually encouraged the export of palm oil instead, though slave exports continued for much of his reign. He signed treaties with Britain in the 1850s but resisted fully abolishing the trade, arguing that it was central to his kingdom's customs and revenues.

Death and legacy

Ghezo died in 1858 during a military campaign, and was succeeded by his son Glele. He is remembered in Benin as one of Dahomey's greatest kings, and his reign features in historical traditions and scholarship on pre-colonial West African states.
