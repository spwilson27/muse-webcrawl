Kleptoparasitism

Kleptoparasitism
FieldBehavioural ecology
DefinitionTheft of food or other resources gathered by another animal
ExamplesSkuas and frigatebirds stealing from other seabirds; hyenas taking kills from other predators
Related conceptsParasitism, brood parasitism, cleptobiosis

Kleptoparasitism (from Greek kleptēs, "thief") is a form of feeding in which one animal deliberately takes food, prey or other resources obtained by another animal, including stored food. It is a strategy found across many animal groups — birds, mammals, insects and other invertebrates — and may be intraspecific (between members of the same species) or interspecific.

Forms

Kleptoparasitism may be facultative, used opportunistically when the chance arises, or obligate, where a species obtains much of its food by theft. Classic obligate or near-obligate practitioners include frigatebirds, which harass boobies and other seabirds in flight until they regurgitate their catch, and skuas (jaegers), which chase gulls and terns until they drop or disgorge food. Spotted hyenas and lions steal carcasses from other predators, and many gulls, corvids and raptors steal from conspecifics and other species.

In invertebrates

Among invertebrates, certain spiders (Argyrodes species) live in the webs of larger spiders and steal wrapped prey; "kleptoparasitic" bees (cuckoo bees) appropriate provisions gathered by other bees; and some flies and beetles specialise in stealing food from ants or from predatory insects.

Ecology and evolution

Kleptoparasitism is favoured when hosts handle conspicuous, valuable food items for long periods, when hosts are abundant, and when the thief can assess the profitability of an attack. Producer–scrounger game models are used to study the frequency-dependent balance between producing (finding one's own food) and scrounging. Hosts evolve counter-strategies such as eating quickly, fleeing, mobbing or swallowing food at the capture site.
