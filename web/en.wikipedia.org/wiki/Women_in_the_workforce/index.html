Women in the workforce

Global female labor-force participationRoughly 47–50% (vs. ~80% for men, ILO estimates)
Key issuesGender pay gap, occupational segregation, parental leave, harassment, leadership representation

Women in the workforce describes the participation of women in paid employment. Since the Industrial Revolution, women's share of formal employment has grown substantially, though women remain underrepresented in leadership positions, overrepresented in low-paid and informal work, and typically earn less than men — the phenomenon known as the gender pay gap.

History

Women have always worked, in agriculture, domestic production and family businesses, but industrialization moved paid work into factories and offices. Textile mills in 19th-century Britain and New England employed large numbers of young women. Clerical work feminized in the early 20th century. The two world wars drew women into jobs previously reserved for men — symbolized in the United States by "Rosie the Riveter" — and although many were pushed out after 1945, married women's employment rose steadily through the postwar decades in most Western countries, driven by the growth of service industries, expanding education for women, and feminist movements demanding equal opportunity.

Contemporary situation

Globally, women's labor-force participation is around half that of the working-age population, compared with about 80% for men, with wide regional variation. Women perform a disproportionate share of unpaid care and household work. In developed economies women now form roughly half the workforce and a majority of university graduates, yet hold a minority of senior executive and board positions, and earn on average roughly 80–85 cents per dollar earned by men in many countries, with the gap narrowing slowly.

Legal and policy frameworks

Landmark measures include equal pay legislation (such as the U.S. Equal Pay Act of 1963 and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964), maternity and parental leave policies, anti-harassment laws, board-gender quotas in some European countries, and the International Labour Organization's conventions on equal remuneration and maternity protection.
