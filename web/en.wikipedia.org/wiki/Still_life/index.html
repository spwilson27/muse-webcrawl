Still life

A still life (from the Dutch stilleven) is a work of art depicting mostly inanimate subject matter, typically commonplace objects — food, flowers, plants, rocks, shells, dead animals, and man-made items such as glasses, books, vases and jewellery — arranged by the artist. Still life emerged as an independent genre in Western painting in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.

Origins and development

Still-life elements appear in ancient Egyptian tomb painting, Greek and Roman mosaics (the trompe-l'œil bowls of fruit at Pompeii), and medieval manuscripts. As a distinct genre it flourished first in the Netherlands around 1600, where a wealthy middle class bought pictures of laden tables, flower pieces and game pieces. Flemish and Dutch masters such as Clara Peeters, Pieter Claesz, Willem Heda and Willem Kalf developed highly specialised types: the breakfast piece (ontbijtje), the banquet piece, the flower garland and the vanitas, in which skulls, hourglasses and guttering candles remind viewers of mortality.

Later history

In France, Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin raised humble kitchen subjects to poetic dignity in the eighteenth century. The academic hierarchy of genres ranked still life last, below history painting, yet the genre repeatedly proved a vehicle for experiment: the apples and table-top constructions of Paul Cézanne are foundational to modern art, and Cubists such as Picasso and Braque made the café still life a laboratory for the fragmentation of form. In the twentieth century the genre extended from Morandi's bottles to Pop art's soup cans and, in photography and installation, to ever broader definitions.

Symbolism

Objects in traditional still lifes often carried moral or religious meanings — wilting flowers for transience, butterflies for the soul, imported luxuries for worldly wealth — though historians caution against over-reading every depicted lemon as an allegory.
