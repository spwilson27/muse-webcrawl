Tuskegee syphilis study

The Tuskegee study of untreated syphilis in the Negro male (informally, the Tuskegee syphilis study, the Tuskegee syphilis experiment, or the Tuskegee experiment) was a study conducted between 1932 and 1972 by the United States Public Health Service (PHS) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) on a group of nearly 400 African American men with syphilis in Macon County, Alabama.

The men in the study were told they were receiving free health care from the federal government, and were recruited with promises of free medical care, meals and burial insurance. In fact, the purpose of the study was to observe the effects of the disease when untreated, although by the time it began, it was understood that untreated syphilis had serious health effects. The subjects were not informed of their diagnosis of syphilis, and when penicillin became widely available as an effective treatment in the 1940s, the men were actively prevented from receiving it.

History

The study began in 1932 in collaboration with the Tuskegee Institute (now Tuskegee University) and the Rosenwald Fund. Of the 600 men enrolled, 399 had contracted syphilis before the study began and 201 did not have the disease. The men were mostly poor sharecroppers. Nurse Eunice Rivers, an African American nurse, played a central role in maintaining the men's participation over the decades.

The study was meant to last six months but continued for 40 years. It was only ended in 1972 after the whistleblower Peter Buxtun, a PHS venereal disease investigator, took his concerns to the press; the Associated Press published the story in July 1972, causing public outrage.

Consequences and legacy

By the end of the study, 28 men had died directly of syphilis, 100 died of related complications, at least 40 wives had been infected, and 19 children were born with congenital syphilis. In 1973, a class-action lawsuit led to a $10 million settlement for the surviving participants and their families, along with lifetime medical benefits. The scandal led to major changes in US research regulation: the National Research Act of 1974 created the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects, whose Belmont Report established the principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice that govern modern institutional review boards and informed consent.

In 1997, President Bill Clinton formally apologised on behalf of the federal government to the study's surviving participants. The Tuskegee study remains one of the most notorious examples of unethical human experimentation and a lasting factor in mistrust of medical institutions among African Americans.
