Strangler fig

Strangler fig is the common name for a number of tropical and subtropical plant species in the genus Ficus, including Ficus aurea, Ficus benghalensis and Ficus citrifolia, that share a distinctive "strangling" growth habit. Their seeds germinate high in the canopy of a host tree, and the young fig grows as an epiphyte, sending aerial roots down to the ground. Once rooted in the soil the fig grows rapidly, enveloping and eventually killing the host, and can stand as an independent tree on a hollow lattice trunk.

Life cycle

Strangler fig seeds are dispersed by fruit-eating birds and mammals, and lodge in moist crevices in a host's bark, where they germinate. The seedling lives as an epiphyte, drawing water from rain and nutrients from litter, while thin roots descend the host's trunk. On reaching the ground these roots thicken, fuse into a sleeve around the host, and begin to compete for light, water and nutrients. The host, shaded by the fig's expanding crown and girdled by its roots, commonly dies and rots away, leaving the fig as a self-supporting tree with a hollow core.

Ecology

Because they fruit year-round in many forests, strangler figs are keystone resources for frugivores — birds, bats and primates — during seasons when other fruit is scarce. Like all figs, each species depends on a specific pollinating fig wasp. The hollow trunks and root cavities of mature stranglers shelter bats, birds and invertebrates.

Cultural significance

Strangler figs growing over the stonework of abandoned temples are a celebrated sight at Angkor in Cambodia, where Ficus and silk-cotton roots embrace ruins such as Ta Prohm. In software engineering, the "strangler fig pattern" borrows the name for the incremental replacement of a legacy system.
