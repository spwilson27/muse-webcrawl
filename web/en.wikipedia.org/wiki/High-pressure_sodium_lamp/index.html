High-pressure sodium lamp

A high-pressure sodium lamp is a type of electric gas-discharge lamp that uses sodium vapour in an excited state at high pressure to produce light. Developed commercially in the 1960s, these lamps became one of the most common sources for street lighting, industrial lighting, and plant growth lighting because of their high luminous efficacy.

Construction

Unlike low-pressure sodium lamps, which use a glass discharge tube, the high-pressure sodium lamp requires an arc tube made of translucent alumina ceramic, which can withstand the corrosive sodium vapour and the high operating temperatures. The arc tube contains sodium, mercury, and a starting gas, typically xenon. The lamp requires a ballast and, in most designs, an ignitor to strike the arc.

Light characteristics

High-pressure sodium lamps emit a golden-white to orange light with a correlated colour temperature around 2,000–2,200 K. Pressure broadening of the sodium emission lines produces a broader spectrum than the near-monochromatic output of low-pressure sodium lamps, giving a modest colour rendering index of roughly 20–25. Efficacies commonly reach 100–150 lumens per watt.

Applications and decline

The lamps were widely used for roadway and area lighting from the 1970s onward. Since the 2010s they have been progressively replaced by LED luminaires, which offer better colour rendering, instant restrike, and comparable or better efficiency, although high-pressure sodium lamps remain common in horticulture because of their red-rich spectrum.
