East of Eden is a 1955 American drama film directed by Elia Kazan and written by Paul Osborn, loosely based on the fourth and final part of John Steinbeck's 1952 novel of the same name. It stars James Dean — in the first of his three major film roles — as Cal Trask, a restless young man in California's Salinas Valley on the eve of the First World War, alongside Julie Harris, Raymond Massey, Burl Ives, Richard Davalos and Jo Van Fleet.

Plot

In 1917, in the Salinas Valley, lettuce farmer Adam Trask raises his twin sons, Cal and Aron, alone, having told them their mother died when they were infants. Aron, dutiful and pious, resembles his father; Cal is troubled and brooding, and believes himself innately bad. Cal discovers that his mother, Kate, is alive and running a brothel in nearby Monterey. When Adam loses his fortune in a failed scheme to ship lettuce east in refrigerated railcars, Cal resolves to win his father's love by earning the money back speculating in bean futures as war approaches. He borrows the stake from Kate. The beans succeed and Cal offers the profit to Adam at a birthday celebration — but Adam rejects the gift, valuing Aron's announcement of his engagement to Abra more. Devastated, Cal reveals the truth about Kate to Aron, who enlists in a drunken despair and ships out to the war; Adam suffers a stroke on hearing the news. At the dying Adam's bedside, Abra persuades Cal to stay, and father and son are reconciled as Adam asks Cal to dismiss the nurse — his first request granted, implying forgiveness.

Production

Kazan shot the film in CinemaScope, largely on location in Monterey and the Salinas Valley and at the Warner Bros. studios in Burbank. Steinbeck approved of Dean's casting after meeting him. The off-screen tension between Dean and Massey — encouraged by Kazan to deepen their characters' estrangement — became part of the film's legend. East of Eden was the only one of Dean's three starring films released during his lifetime; he was killed in a car crash in September 1955.

Reception and legacy

The film competed for the Palme d'Or at the 1955 Cannes Film Festival, where Kazan won the award for Best Dramatic Film. Jo Van Fleet won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress, and the film received further Oscar nominations for Dean (Best Actor, posthumous), Kazan (Best Director) and Osborn (Best Screenplay). It is now regarded as a classic of 1950s American cinema and the performance that established Dean as a cultural icon of youthful alienation.
