Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever

Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is a viral disease caused by the Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever virus, a nairovirus of the family Bunyaviridae. It is transmitted mainly by Hyalomma ticks and by contact with the blood or tissues of infected livestock or patients.

Signs and symptoms

Illness typically begins suddenly with fever, muscle aches, dizziness, neck and back pain, headache, sore eyes, and sensitivity to light. As it progresses, severe bruising, nosebleeds, uncontrolled bleeding and other hemorrhagic signs may appear. Case fatality rates in outbreaks are commonly 10–40%.

Distribution and history

The disease was first described in Crimea in 1944 and later shown to be identical to an illness identified in the Congo in 1956, giving the virus its combined name. It is endemic across parts of Africa, the Balkans, the Middle East, and Asia.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment is mainly supportive; the antiviral ribavirin has been used with uncertain benefit. Prevention relies on tick avoidance, protective clothing, safe handling of livestock, and strict infection control in healthcare settings. The WHO lists CCHF as a priority disease for research and development.
