Jean Piaget

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean Piaget observing a child at play with toys in his Geneva study centre
Jean Piaget observing a child at play with toys in his Geneva study centre

Jean Piaget (9 August 1896 – 16 September 1980) was a Swiss psychologist known for his work on child development. His theory of cognitive development — children pass through four stages (sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational), actively constructing knowledge through assimilation and accommodation — dominated developmental psychology and education for much of the twentieth century. He founded the field he called genetic epistemology.

Life and method

A prodigy naturalist (publishing on molluscs at 11), Piaget turned to psychology in Paris, standardising reasoning tests, and became fascinated not by children's scores but by their wrong answers — whose systematic logic his clinical interviews (later with his own three children) revealed. At Geneva's Rousseau Institute and the International Center for Genetic Epistemology he spent six decades mapping how concepts — object permanence, conservation, number, morality — are built.

The stage theory and its critics

Piaget's stages are now read as approximations: infant research (Baillargeon) finds competencies far earlier than he allowed; Vygotsky's social account and cross-cultural studies qualify the universality and timing; schooling shifts performance. Yet his constructivism — learners as active builders — remains the default grammar of developmental science and much classroom practice (discovery learning, readiness debates).
