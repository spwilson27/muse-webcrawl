Free Exercise Clause

The Free Exercise Clause is part of the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, providing that "Congress shall make no law … prohibiting the free exercise" of religion. Together with the Establishment Clause, it forms the religion clauses of the First Amendment. The Supreme Court has applied it against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment since Cantwell v. Connecticut (1940).

Doctrine

The Court has long distinguished between religious belief, which is absolutely protected, and religiously motivated conduct, which may be regulated. In Sherbert v. Verner (1963) and Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972), the Court required a compelling state interest to justify laws substantially burdening religious practice.

Employment Division v. Smith and RFRA

In Employment Division v. Smith (1990), the Court held that neutral, generally applicable laws do not violate the clause even if they incidentally burden religion. Congress responded with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA), restoring the compelling-interest test for federal law; the Court struck down RFRA's application to the states in City of Boerne v. Flores (1997), prompting many states to enact their own versions.

Recent cases

Later decisions have expanded free-exercise protections in the funding context — including Trinity Lutheran Church v. Comer (2017), Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue (2020) and Carson v. Makin (2022) — holding that states may not exclude religious institutions from generally available public benefits solely because of their religious character or use.
