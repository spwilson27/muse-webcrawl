Macaque

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Japanese macaque (snow monkey) soaking in a hot spring, snow on its head]
A Japanese macaque (snow monkey) soaking in a hot spring, snow on its head

The macaques (genus Macaca) are a genus of Old World monkeys comprising some 23 species, distributed more widely than any other primate genus except humans — from North Africa and Gibraltar through South and Southeast Asia to Japan. They include the Rhesus macaque, foundational to medical research (the Rh blood factor is named for it), the Japanese macaque famous for hot-spring bathing, and the Barbary macaque, Europe's only wild primate.

Biology and society

Macaques are medium-sized, ground-and-tree monkeys with cheek pouches, omnivorous diets and complex matrilineal societies governed by dominance hierarchies softened by grooming alliances. Several species are highly adaptable commensals of humans — temple monkeys, crop raiders, urban troops — which brings them into both veneration (as Hanuman's kin in India) and conflict.

Research and culture

Rhesus and long-tailed (crab-eating) macaques are the most used non-human primates in biomedical research — polio vaccines, neuroscience and the first cloned primates (Zhong Zhong and Hua Hua, 2018) — making their welfare and sourcing persistent ethical issues. Japanese macaques' potato-washing, documented from 1953, became the classic (if partly mythologised, via the 'hundredth monkey' story) case of animal culture.

Conservation

A quarter of macaque species are threatened, notably the lion-tailed macaque of the Western Ghats and the Tonkean and crested macaques of Sulawesi. The long-tailed macaque was uplisted to Endangered in 2022, citing trade for research and consumption — a listing contested by laboratory-animal industries.
