Muscle spindle

Muscle spindles are stretch receptors within the body of a skeletal muscle that primarily detect changes in the length of the muscle. They convey length information to the central nervous system via afferent nerve fibers. This information can be processed by the brain as proprioception. The responses of muscle spindles to changes in length also play an important role in regulating the contraction of muscles, for example, by activating motor neurons via the stretch reflex to resist muscle stretch.

Structure

Muscle spindles are found within the belly of muscles, between extrafusal muscle fibers. They are fusiform (spindle-shaped), and the specialized fibers that make up the muscle spindle are called intrafusal muscle fibers. Intrafusal fibers lie parallel to the extrafusal fibers and are innervated by both sensory and motor neurons. The two types of intrafusal fibers are nuclear bag fibers and nuclear chain fibers; a typical spindle contains several of each. The sensory endings of the primary (type Ia) and secondary (type II) afferent fibers wrap around the central, non-contractile region of the intrafusal fibers.

Function

When a muscle is stretched, the intrafusal fibers are stretched as well, deforming the sensory endings and increasing firing in the afferent fibers. The type Ia afferents are especially sensitive to the rate of change of muscle length, while type II afferents signal static length. This afferent signal is transmitted to the spinal cord, where it monosynaptically excites alpha motor neurons supplying the same muscle, producing the stretch reflex. Gamma motor neurons innervate the contractile ends of the intrafusal fibers, adjusting the sensitivity of the spindle so that it remains responsive across a range of muscle lengths — a process known as fusimotor drive.

Clinical significance

Abnormalities of muscle spindle function contribute to disorders of muscle tone, such as the increased stretch reflexes and spasticity seen after upper motor neuron lesions. Muscle spindles are also central to clinical tests of deep tendon reflexes, such as the knee-jerk reflex.
