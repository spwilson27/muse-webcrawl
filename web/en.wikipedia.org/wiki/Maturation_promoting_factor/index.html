Maturation promoting factor

Maturation promoting factor (MPF), also called mitosis-promoting factor or M-phase-promoting factor, is a protein complex that drives cells into mitosis and meiosis. It consists of the enzyme cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1, historically called cdc2) bound to its regulatory partner cyclin B. The discovery of MPF was a landmark in cell-cycle research.

Discovery

MPF was first identified in the early 1970s by Yoshio Masui and Clement Markert, who found that cytoplasm taken from maturing frog oocytes could induce immature oocytes to enter meiosis. The factor was purified in the late 1980s and shown to be identical to the mitotic kinase studied in yeast and sea urchin embryos, revealing a universal mechanism of cell-cycle control.

Mechanism

As cyclin B accumulates through interphase, it binds CDK1, but the complex is kept inactive by inhibitory phosphorylation. At the onset of mitosis, the phosphatase Cdc25 removes these phosphates in a positive-feedback switch, activating MPF. Active MPF phosphorylates hundreds of substrates, causing chromosome condensation, nuclear-envelope breakdown and spindle assembly. MPF is then switched off by the anaphase-promoting complex, which targets cyclin B for destruction, allowing the cell to exit mitosis.

Significance

The work on MPF and cyclins earned Leland Hartwell, Tim Hunt and Paul Nurse the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their discoveries of key regulators of the cell cycle.
