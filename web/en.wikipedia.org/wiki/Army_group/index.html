Army group

An army group is a military organisation consisting of several field armies, which is self-sufficient for indefinite periods. It is usually responsible for a particular geographic area. An army group is the largest field formation in many armed forces, including the United States Army and the German Wehrmacht of the Second World War. Army groups may be multi-national formations. For example, during the Second World War, the Southern Group of Armies (also known as the US 6th Army Group) comprised the French First Army and the US Seventh Army.

Organisation

An army group typically comprises between 400,000 and 1,000,000 soldiers, plus support personnel and staff. In NATO terminology, an army group is often designated by a number (for example, the 21st Army Group) or a letter (for example, German Army Groups A, B and Centre). The commander is usually a full general or field marshal. In the Soviet Red Army, the comparable formation was called a front.

History

First World War

Army groups were formed by several armies during the First World War as the scale of operations outgrew single army headquarters. The German Army formed Heeresgruppen from 1915 onwards, each coordinating several armies along a sector of the front.

Second World War

On the Eastern Front, German army groups such as Army Group North, Army Group Centre and Army Group South each controlled hundreds of thousands of men across vast fronts. The Western Allies fielded formations including the British-led 21st Army Group under Bernard Montgomery and the US 12th Army Group under Omar Bradley, the largest American formation of the war. The Soviet Union's offensives were conducted by fronts such as the 1st Ukrainian Front and the 1st Belorussian Front, coordinated by the Stavka.

Cold War and after

NATO planning retained the army group concept: the Northern Army Group (NORTHAG) and Central Army Group (CENTAG) were to defend West Germany during the Cold War. Most modern armies rarely deploy formations of this size, though the term persists in doctrine.
