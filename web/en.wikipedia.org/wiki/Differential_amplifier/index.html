Differential amplifier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A differential amplifier is a type of electronic amplifier that amplifies the difference between two input voltages but suppresses any voltage common to the two inputs. It is the fundamental building block of the operational amplifier and of most modern analogue integrated circuits. Its ability to reject common-mode signals — such as noise picked up equally by both leads of a sensor cable — makes it indispensable in instrumentation, audio, and communications equipment.

Operation

In an ideal differential amplifier the output voltage is proportional only to the differential input:

Vout = Ad (V+ − V−)

where Ad is the differential gain. A real amplifier also responds slightly to the average of the two inputs, the common-mode signal; the ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain is the common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR), usually expressed in decibels. High CMRR values are achieved by close matching of the input devices and by using a constant current source in the shared tail connection.

The long-tailed pair

The classic implementation, the long-tailed pair (or differential pair), consists of two transistors — originally vacuum tubes, now bipolar transistors or MOSFETs — whose emitters (or sources) share a common current source. An input signal applied to one base increases that transistor's collector current and decreases the other's; the output is taken between the two collectors for a fully differential signal, or from one collector for a single-ended output. The topology was developed in the 1930s and used extensively in early analogue computers and in the input stages of operational amplifiers such as the µA741.

Applications

  • Input stage of operational amplifiers and comparators
  • Instrumentation amplifiers for strain gauges, thermocouples, and bridge sensors
  • Balanced audio lines and microphone preamplifiers
  • Emitter-coupled logic (ECL) digital circuits
  • Low-voltage differential signalling (LVDS) receivers in high-speed data links
