An oligosynthetic language (from Greek oligos, "few") is a type of language — in practice hypothetical or constructed — in which all words are formed by compounding a very small inventory of morphemes, perhaps only a few hundred. The term was coined by Benjamin Lee Whorf, who suggested that Classical Nahuatl might be such a language.

Characteristics

In a strictly oligosynthetic language, words would be built up in long, precisely analysable chains: a small set of semantic primitives combined according to fixed rules would generate the entire vocabulary, so that any word could be decomposed into its elementary meaning-bearing units. This contrasts with polysynthetic languages, which have large morpheme inventories, and with natural languages generally, in which many words are arbitrary and unanalysable.

Assessment

Linguists generally regard Whorf's claim about Nahuatl as unfounded — Nahuatl is polysynthetic with a large morpheme stock — and no natural language is accepted as truly oligosynthetic. The idea survives mainly in constructed-language design, where aUI and similar philosophical languages attempt vocabularies built from a few dozen primitives, and in discussions of semantic primes in linguistics.
