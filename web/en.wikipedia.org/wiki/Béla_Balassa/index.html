Béla Alexander Balassa (April 6, 1928 – May 10, 1991) was a Hungarian-American economist who served as a professor at Johns Hopkins University and was a consultant to the World Bank. He is best known for the Balassa–Samuelson effect and for his taxonomy of stages of economic integration.

Béla Balassa, specialist in international trade and development.

Life and work

Born in Budapest, Balassa obtained a law degree at the University of Budapest and left Hungary after the 1956 uprising. He earned a PhD in economics at Yale (1959), taught at Yale and Columbia, and moved to Johns Hopkins in 1966. From 1966 until his death he was also a consultant to the World Bank, advising governments in dozens of developing countries.

In The Theory of Economic Integration (1961) he proposed the now-standard stages of integration: free trade area, customs union, common market, economic union, and total economic integration. His 1964 work on purchasing power parity doctrine showed that productivity differences between traded and non-traded goods explain systematic differences in price levels between rich and poor countries — the Balassa–Samuelson effect (independently described by Paul Samuelson). Balassa was a prominent advocate of export-oriented development and a critic of import-substitution industrialisation.
