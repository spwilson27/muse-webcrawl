The Cheyenne (Cheyenne: Tsêhéstáno) are an Indigenous people of the Great Plains. Their ethnic identity merges two Algonquian-speaking tribes, the Tsétsêhéstâhese (the Cheyenne proper) and the Só'taeo'o (Suhtai). The Cheyenne Nation is currently divided into two federally recognized tribes: the Southern Cheyenne, who are enrolled in the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes of Oklahoma, and the Northern Cheyenne, enrolled in the Northern Cheyenne Tribe of the Northern Cheyenne Indian Reservation in Montana.

History

Originally a settled, earth-lodge farming people in the region of present-day Minnesota, the Cheyenne migrated westward during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, adopting a fully nomadic horse culture centred on bison hunting on the High Plains. They became famed as warriors and traders and formed a governing system of forty-four peace chiefs and warrior societies, including the Dog Soldiers. The nineteenth century brought escalating conflict with the United States, including the Sand Creek massacre of 1864, the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876 (fought alongside the Lakota and Arapaho), and the Northern Cheyenne Exodus of 1878–79, when Dull Knife and Little Wolf led their people north from Indian Territory toward their homeland.

Culture

Central institutions of traditional Cheyenne life included the Keeper of the Sacred Arrows, the Sun Dance ceremony, and the Council of Forty-four. The Cheyenne language, a tonal Algonquian language, is now endangered, with revitalisation efforts under way in both Montana and Oklahoma.

Population

At the time of their first contact with Europeans, the Cheyenne were living in what is now Minnesota. Today enrolled citizens number in the thousands in each of the two tribes, with the Northern Cheyenne Reservation centred at Lame Deer, Montana, and Southern Cheyenne communities in western Oklahoma.
