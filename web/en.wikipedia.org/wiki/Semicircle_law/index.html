Wigner semicircle distribution

The Wigner semicircle distribution, named after the physicist Eugene Wigner, is a probability distribution supported on a finite interval [−R, R] whose density function is a scaled semicircle. It is famous as the limiting distribution of the eigenvalues of many random symmetric matrices: Wigner's semicircle law states that the empirical distribution of eigenvalues of an n × n Hermitian random matrix with independent entries of mean 0 and variance σ² converges, as n → ∞, to the semicircle distribution with radius R = 2σ√n (suitably normalized).

Eigenvalues of a large random matrix accumulate in a semicircular profile.

Density

For radius R, the density is

f(x) = (2 / πR²) √(R² − x²) for −R ≤ x ≤ R, and 0 otherwise.

The distribution has mean 0 and variance R²/4. Its moments vanish in odd order; the even moments are given by the Catalan numbers, a fact that connects the semicircle law to free probability theory, where it plays the role of the central limit distribution — the "free Gaussian."

Context

Wigner introduced the law in the 1950s in his statistical theory of nuclear energy levels, modeling the Hamiltonian of a heavy nucleus as a random matrix. The result underlies random matrix theory's applications in nuclear physics, quantum chaos, number theory, wireless communications, and statistics. Robust versions hold for broad classes of entry distributions, a phenomenon known as universality; local eigenvalue statistics on microscopic scales are described by related results such as the Tracy–Widom distribution for the largest eigenvalue.
