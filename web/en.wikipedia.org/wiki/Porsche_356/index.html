Porsche 356

The Porsche 356 is a sports car that was produced by the German company Porsche from 1948 to 1965. It was the company's first production automobile: a lightweight, rear-engined, rear-wheel-drive two-door car available in coupé and convertible forms. Around 76,000 were built in total.

History

The first 356, designed by Ferry Porsche and Erwin Komenda, was built in Gmünd, Austria, using many components from the Volkswagen Beetle, including its air-cooled flat-four engine, transmission and suspension. The first cars had aluminium bodies; after production moved to Stuttgart in 1950, bodies were made of steel. The design evolved through the pre-A, 356A, 356B and 356C series, with steadily more powerful engines culminating in the high-performance Carrera models using the four-cam Fuhrmann engine.

Models

Body styles included the Coupé, Cabriolet, Speedster (a stripped-down, low-priced open model aimed at the American market), Convertible D and Roadster. The final 356C, introduced in 1963, featured disc brakes all round. Production ended in 1965, by which time the 356 had been joined — and then replaced — by the Porsche 911.

Motorsport

The 356 was highly successful in racing, winning its class at Le Mans and scoring victories in events such as the Mille Miglia, Targa Florio and Carrera Panamericana, from which the "Carrera" name was taken.

Legacy

Today the 356 is a sought-after collector's car, with early "pre-A" and Speedster models commanding especially high prices, and it established the engineering layout and design language that defined Porsche for decades.
