Population decline

Population decline, also known as depopulation, is a reduction in a region's human population over time, arising when deaths and emigration exceed births and immigration. Once rare at national scale, sustained decline has become common in countries where fertility has fallen well below the replacement level of about 2.1 children per woman.

Causes

The principal cause is the demographic transition: as societies urbanise and develop, fertility falls, and once the large cohorts of the high-growth era age and die, deaths outnumber births. Emigration — notably from parts of Eastern Europe after EU enlargement — can intensify natural decrease. Historically, epidemics, famine and war caused abrupt depopulation, as with the Black Death.

Examples

Japan's population peaked in 2008 and has fallen every year since. Russia, Ukraine, Bulgaria, the Baltic states and several Balkan countries have lost substantial population shares since 1990. China's population began declining in 2022, and United Nations projections anticipate decline across most of Europe and East Asia during the twenty-first century, with the world population projected to peak in the 2080s.

Consequences and responses

Decline is associated with population ageing, shrinking workforces, strained pension systems, abandoned housing and rural service closures, but also with reduced environmental pressure. Governments have responded with pro-natalist incentives, family benefits and immigration policies, though such measures have generally slowed rather than reversed decline.
