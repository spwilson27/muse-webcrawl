Ptolemy IV Philopator

Reign: 221–204 BC
Predecessor: Ptolemy III Euergetes
Successor: Ptolemy V Epiphanes
Consort: Arsinoe III
Dynasty: Ptolemaic

Ptolemy IV Philopator (Greek: Πτολεμαῖος ὁ Φιλοπάτωρ, "Ptolemy, beloved of his father"; c. 244 – July/August 204 BC) was the fourth pharaoh of Ptolemaic Egypt, reigning from 221 to 204 BC. He succeeded his father Ptolemy III Euergetes and is often regarded as the pharaoh under whom the long decline of Ptolemaic power began, although he won a notable victory at the Battle of Raphia.

Reign

On his accession Ptolemy IV fell under the influence of the courtier Sosibius, who purged several members of the royal family, including the king's uncle Lysimachus, his brother Magas, and his mother Berenice II. In 219 BC the Seleucid king Antiochus III invaded Coele-Syria, beginning the Fourth Syrian War. Ptolemy's ministers used the delay to raise a large army, including for the first time a phalanx of native Egyptians. At the Battle of Raphia in 217 BC, Ptolemy's forces decisively defeated Antiochus, one of the largest battles of the Hellenistic age. The arming of native Egyptians, however, encouraged revolts in Upper Egypt, where the pharaohs Hugronaphor and Ankhwennefer later established a breakaway kingdom.

Ptolemy married his sister Arsinoe III, who bore him a son, the future Ptolemy V Epiphanes. Later sources portray Ptolemy IV as devoted to luxury and religious cult — particularly that of Dionysus — and more interested in literature and pageantry than government. He is credited with building the enormous ship tessarakonteres. He died in 204 BC in obscure circumstances, and his death was concealed for a time by his courtiers Agathocles and Sosibius.

Legacy

The traditional judgement of Ptolemy IV as a weak and dissolute ruler derives largely from Polybius and later historians; modern scholarship has taken a more nuanced view, noting the administrative continuity of his reign and the scale of the Raphia achievement.
