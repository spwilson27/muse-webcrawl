Lope Félix de Vega Carpio (25 November 1562 – 27 August 1635) was a Spanish playwright, poet and novelist of the Baroque era, one of the key figures of the Spanish Golden Age of literature. His reputation in the canon of Spanish literature is second only to Miguel de Cervantes, who dubbed him "the Phoenix of Wits" (el Fénix de los ingenios) and the "Monster of Nature" for his extraordinary productivity.

Life

Born in Madrid to a family of modest means from Cantabria, Lope showed precocious talent, studying with the Jesuits and (briefly) at Alcalá. His turbulent life included naval service with the Spanish Armada (he sailed in 1588), exile from Madrid for libellous verses about an actress who had spurned him, secretarial posts to nobles, two marriages, numerous love affairs, and late ordination as a priest in 1614, none of which slowed his writing. His death in 1635 was marked by public mourning in Madrid.

Works

Lope claimed to have written some 1,800 full-length plays (comedias); about 450 survive or are attributed to him with confidence. In his treatise Arte nuevo de hacer comedias (1609) he codified the comedia nueva: mixing tragedy and comedy, breaking the classical unities, and writing in varied verse metres for a paying public. Famous plays include Fuenteovejuna (about a village that collectively takes responsibility for killing its tyrannical lord), El perro del hortelano (The Dog in the Manger), La dama boba (The Lady Nit-Wit) and El caballero de Olmedo. He also wrote epic and lyric poetry, the prose pastoral romance La Arcadia, and the dialogued novel La Dorotea (1632), considered his prose masterpiece.

Legacy

Lope effectively created the Spanish national theatre and dominated its stages for half a century. His works remain staples of Spanish-language theatre, and his Madrid house is preserved as the Lope de Vega House Museum.
