Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God

"Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" is a sermon written and preached by the American theologian and philosopher Jonathan Edwards. Edwards preached it on July 8, 1741, in Enfield, Connecticut (then part of the Massachusetts Bay Colony), during the First Great Awakening, a wave of religious revival sweeping through New England. The sermon, based on the text of Deuteronomy 32:35 ("Their foot shall slide in due time"), combines vivid imagery of hell and divine wrath with an exposition of the precarious position of the unconverted sinner, and is regarded as a classic of American literature and a landmark of revivalist preaching.

Summary

Edwards argues that sinners are always exposed to sudden and unexpected destruction, like a person sliding on slippery ground or walking across a rotten bridge over a fiery pit. In one of its most famous images, God holds the sinner "over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider, or some loathsome insect over the fire". The sermon develops ten considerations showing that it is only God's arbitrary will and forbearance that keeps the wicked from falling into hell at any moment, before an application pressing the hearers — especially those who considered themselves moral or secure — to flee from the wrath to come. Nothing the unconverted person does, Edwards insists, gives them any security for a single moment.

Delivery and effect

Edwards had preached the sermon earlier at his own church in Northampton with little recorded effect. At Enfield, however — reputedly a congregation noted for its complacency — his delivery, calm and level in tone rather than theatrical, reportedly produced an extraordinary response: listeners moaned, cried out and clung to the pillars of the meeting-house, and Edwards was interrupted by the congregation's outcries before he could finish. Accounts describe him asking for quiet so that he could conclude. The sermon was published in Boston in 1741 and went through numerous editions.

Legacy

The sermon is Edwards's most famous work and one of the most frequently anthologised texts in American literary history. It is widely taught in American schools as an example of Puritan theology, Great Awakening rhetoric and figurative language. Modern readers debate its significance: for some it epitomises the terrifying "fire-and-brimstone" character of early American religion, while scholars note that it represents only part of Edwards's thought, which also emphasised divine beauty and love.
