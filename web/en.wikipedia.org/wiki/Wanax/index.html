Wanax

Wanax (Greek: ϝάναξ, later ἄναξ, anax) is an ancient Greek title meaning "king", "lord" or "master". In Mycenaean Greek, written in Linear B as wa-na-ka, it designated the supreme ruler of a Bronze Age palace state.

Mycenaean usage

Linear B tablets from Pylos, Knossos and other palaces show the wanax at the apex of Mycenaean society, controlling land, workshops and religious offerings, and distinct from local officials such as the qa-si-re-u (basileus). The wanax held an estate (temenos) and appointed officials including the ra-wa-ke-ta (lawagetas), the second figure of the kingdom. When the palaces fell around 1200 BCE, the institution disappeared with them.

Later usage

In Homer, anax survives as an elevated, somewhat archaic term: Agamemnon is styled "lord of men" (anax andrōn), and the word is applied especially to gods — Zeus and Apollo are frequently "anax". The ordinary Greek word for king in the classical period became basileus, while anax retained poetic and religious colouring. The feminine form anassa ("queen", "lady") is likewise used of goddesses and royal women.
