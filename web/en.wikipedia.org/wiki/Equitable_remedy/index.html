Equitable remedies are judicial remedies developed by the English Courts of Chancery, as distinct from the legal remedies available in the common law courts. Because equity developed to correct the rigidity of the common law, equitable relief is discretionary rather than available as of right, and is generally granted only where damages would be an inadequate remedy.

Principal remedies

The main equitable remedies include the injunction (an order to do or refrain from doing something), specific performance (an order to perform a contract, common for land sales where each parcel is unique), rescission (setting aside a contract), rectification (correcting a document that fails to record the parties' agreement), an account of profits, and equitable compensation. Interim forms, such as the freezing (Mareva) injunction and the search (Anton Piller) order, developed in the twentieth century.

Principles

Equitable relief is governed by the maxims of equity: "he who seeks equity must do equity"; "he who comes to equity must come with clean hands"; equity will not assist a volunteer; and delay defeats equity (laches). In the United States, the merger of law and equity in federal procedure (1938) preserved the substantive distinction, and the Seventh Amendment jury right still turns on whether a claim is legal or equitable in nature. The Supreme Court's decision in eBay Inc. v. MercExchange (2006) restated the four-factor test for permanent injunctions.
