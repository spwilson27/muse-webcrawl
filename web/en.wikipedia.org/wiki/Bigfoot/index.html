Bigfoot, also commonly called Sasquatch, is a large, hairy, ape-like creature said to inhabit the forests of North America, particularly the Pacific Northwest. It is a prominent subject of folklore and cryptozoology. Scientists generally dismiss Bigfoot as a combination of folklore, misidentification, and hoaxes, noting the lack of physical evidence such as a body or bones.

[Image: Frame 352 of the 1967 Patterson–Gimlin film, showing the alleged Bigfoot]

Description and reports

Witnesses describe a bipedal creature 1.8–2.7 metres (6–9 ft) tall, covered in dark hair, with a pronounced brow ridge and a strong, unpleasant odour. Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest have long told of wild men of the woods; the name "Sasquatch" derives from the Halkomelem word sásq'ets. The name "Bigfoot" was coined in 1958 after large footprints were found at a construction site near Bluff Creek, California.

Evidence and analysis

Proposed evidence includes footprints and casts, hair samples, and film—most famously the 1967 Patterson–Gimlin film from Bluff Creek, which proponents regard as genuine and critics as a hoax. DNA analyses of purported samples have consistently identified known animals such as bears. Anthropologist Grover Krantz was a rare academic defender; primatologist Jane Goodall expressed openness to the possibility.

Cultural impact

Bigfoot is a staple of North American popular culture, appearing in films, television (such as Finding Bigfoot), advertising, and tourism, with festivals and museums in places like Willow Creek, California, and Harrison Hot Springs, British Columbia.
