The pitch drop experiment is a long-term experiment at the University of Queensland in Brisbane, Australia, that measures the flow of a piece of pitch over many years. It demonstrates that pitch, which appears solid and can be shattered with a hammer, is in fact a liquid of extremely high viscosity. The experiment is listed by Guinness World Records as the world's longest-running laboratory experiment.

History

The experiment was set up in 1927 by Professor Thomas Parnell, the university's first professor of physics. He poured heated pitch into a sealed funnel and allowed it to settle for three years; in 1930 the stem of the funnel was cut, allowing the pitch to begin flowing. A drop forms and falls roughly once every decade, though the rate varies with ambient temperature — it increased after air conditioning was installed in the building.

After Parnell's death, custodian John Mainstone oversaw the experiment for 52 years. Famously, for most of its history no one ever saw a drop fall: drops in 1988 and 2000 fell while Mainstone was away, and a webcam failure in 2000 meant the eighth drop was not captured. Mainstone and Parnell were awarded the satirical Ig Nobel Prize in Physics in 2005. Mainstone died in 2013, months before the ninth drop fell in April 2014 — that drop collided with earlier fallen pitch in the beaker and did not fully detach, and the beaker's contents had to be handled so the experiment could continue. Custodianship passed to Andrew White.

From the drop intervals, the viscosity of the pitch has been calculated to be about 2.3 × 108 poise — roughly 230 billion times the viscosity of water. A parallel experiment at Trinity College Dublin, begun in 1944, captured a drop on camera in 2013.
