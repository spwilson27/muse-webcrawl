Calcineurin

Calcineurin is a calcium- and calmodulin-dependent serine/threonine protein phosphatase, also known as protein phosphatase 2B. It is the only phosphatase directly activated by calcium signalling and plays a central role in coupling rises in intracellular Ca²⁺ to changes in gene expression and cell function.

Structure

Calcineurin is a heterodimer: a catalytic A subunit (about 60 kDa) containing binding sites for calmodulin and for the regulatory B subunit, and a calcium-binding B subunit resembling calmodulin. Its activity is controlled by an autoinhibitory domain that is displaced when Ca²⁺-bound calmodulin binds.

Role in immunity

In T cells, antigen-receptor stimulation raises intracellular calcium, activating calcineurin. Calcineurin then dephosphorylates the transcription factor NFAT (nuclear factor of activated T cells), which moves into the nucleus and switches on genes including interleukin-2, driving T-cell activation and proliferation.

Clinical significance

This pathway is the target of the immunosuppressant drugs ciclosporin (cyclosporine) and tacrolimus (FK506). Each drug first binds an intracellular immunophilin (cyclophilin or FKBP respectively); the drug–protein complex then blocks calcineurin, preventing NFAT activation and suppressing the immune response. These calcineurin inhibitors revolutionised organ transplantation from the 1980s onward, though they carry significant side effects, including kidney toxicity. Calcineurin also functions in the heart (pathological hypertrophy), skeletal muscle, and the brain, where it is abundant and involved in synaptic plasticity and memory; its regulation by the protein RCAN1 (DSCR1), encoded in the Down syndrome critical region, is of research interest.
