Tetrachromacy

Tetrachromacy
DefinitionColour vision based on four independent channels of spectral information
Common inMany fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and insects
Human caseRare; some women carry a fourth cone pigment gene — a few demonstrated functionally
ContrastTrichromacy (most mammals, incl. humans), dichromacy

Tetrachromacy is the condition of possessing four independent channels for conveying colour information, derived from four types of cone cells in the eye, each sensitive to a different region of the spectrum. Most humans are trichromats, with three cone types; tetrachromats can in principle discriminate vastly more colours — up to an estimated 100 million, against about one million for trichromats.

In animals

Tetrachromacy is widespread and probably ancestral among vertebrates: many fish (including goldfish and zebrafish), amphibians, reptiles and birds possess a fourth cone class sensitive to ultraviolet or violet light. Birds use it in foraging and mate choice — some flower and plumage patterns visible only in UV — while certain mantis shrimp go far beyond, with twelve or more spectral channels. Most placental mammals lost two of the four ancestral cone classes, becoming dichromats; Old World primates later re-evolved trichromacy through duplication of an opsin gene.

In humans

Because the red and green opsin genes lie on the X chromosome, women who carry variant pigment genes on each copy can express four cone types. Studies estimate that a substantial minority of women are anatomical tetrachromats, but rigorous testing (notably by Gabriele Jordan and colleagues) has confirmed only a small number of functional tetrachromats who genuinely perceive extra colour dimensions. Whether the brain of a typical four-cone observer exploits the extra channel remains an open research question.
