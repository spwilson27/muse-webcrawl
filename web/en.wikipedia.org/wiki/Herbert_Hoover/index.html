Herbert Hoover

Herbert Clark Hoover (August 10, 1874 – October 20, 1964) was an American politician and engineer who served as the 31st president of the United States from 1929 to 1933. A member of the Republican Party, he held office during the onset of the Great Depression. Before his presidency, Hoover was a successful mining engineer and directed humanitarian relief in war-torn Europe.

Early life and engineering career

Hoover was born in West Branch, Iowa, to a Quaker family and was orphaned by the age of nine. He was raised by relatives in Oregon and was a member of the first graduating class of Stanford University, earning a degree in geology in 1895. He became a highly successful mining engineer, working in Australia and China — where he and his wife, Lou Henry Hoover, were present during the Boxer Rebellion — and building a worldwide consulting practice that made him wealthy.

Humanitarian work

When the First World War broke out in 1914, Hoover organized the return of stranded Americans from Europe and then founded the Commission for Relief in Belgium, which fed millions of people in German-occupied Belgium and northern France. After the United States entered the war, President Woodrow Wilson appointed him head of the U.S. Food Administration, and after the armistice he directed the American Relief Administration, delivering food to famine-stricken Central and Eastern Europe and Soviet Russia.

Secretary of Commerce and election of 1928

Hoover served as Secretary of Commerce under Presidents Warren G. Harding and Calvin Coolidge from 1921 to 1928, promoting government efficiency, standardization, aviation, radio regulation, and his philosophy of voluntary cooperation between government and business. His direction of relief after the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 made him a national hero. He won the Republican nomination in 1928 and defeated Democrat Al Smith in a landslide.

Presidency 1929–1933

Hoover took office amid prosperity, but the stock market crash of October 1929 plunged the country into the Great Depression. He responded with public works, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, and appeals for voluntary action, but he resisted direct federal relief and large-scale intervention, and he signed the Smoot–Hawley Tariff in 1930, which raised duties and worsened the collapse of world trade. The eviction of the Bonus Army marchers from Washington in 1932 further damaged his reputation. Shantytowns of the homeless became known as "Hoovervilles". He was defeated by Franklin D. Roosevelt in a landslide in the 1932 election.

Later life and legacy

Hoover lived for more than three decades after leaving office, writing his memoirs and leading two commissions on the reorganization of the executive branch. He advised later presidents and regained some public respect for relief work after the Second World War. He died in New York City in 1964, aged 90, and is buried beside his wife in West Branch, Iowa. Historians generally rank his presidency poorly because of the Depression, while crediting his humanitarian record as among the most distinguished of any president before or after office.
