Confederados

The Confederados are an ethnic and cultural sub-group in Brazil, descendants of Confederate Americans who emigrated from the United States after the American Civil War (1861–1865). Between roughly 1865 and the following decades, thousands of Southerners — estimates range from several thousand to 20,000 — settled in Brazil, where slavery remained legal until 1888 and land was cheap, encouraged by Emperor Dom Pedro II, who sought experienced cotton farmers and offered subsidised passage and land.

The Campo cemetery and chapel near Santa Bárbara d'Oeste, a centre of Confederado settlement.

Settlement

Settlements were founded in several regions, most durably at Santa Bárbara d'Oeste and Americana in São Paulo state, whose name itself derives from the American colony. Other colonies, such as those in Pará and in Espírito Santo, largely failed or dispersed. Many emigrants eventually returned to the United States, but a permanent community remained and assimilated into Brazilian society while retaining distinctive traditions.

Culture today

Modern Confederados are overwhelmingly Portuguese-speaking and integrated into Brazilian life, but descendants maintain an association that holds an annual festival, the Festa Confederada, featuring period dress, Southern American food and music. The display of Confederate symbols at such events has attracted controversy, particularly as debate over Confederate monuments has intensified in the United States, and the community's history is intertwined with the legacies of slavery in both countries.
