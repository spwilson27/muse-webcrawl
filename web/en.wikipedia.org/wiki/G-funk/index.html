G-funk

G-funk (short for gangsta funk) is a subgenre of hip hop music that emerged in the early 1990s on the West Coast of the United States, principally in Los Angeles. Developed above all by producer Dr. Dre, G-funk layered melodic synthesizer lines, deep bass, and samples drawn from 1970s funk — especially Parliament-Funkadelic — over slow, heavy beats, creating the dominant sound of commercial rap in the mid-1990s.

Characteristics

G-funk tracks characteristically feature high-pitched portamento synthesizer leads (the "whiny" lead adapted from P-funk), deep and slow basslines, samples or replays of funk grooves, female backing vocals, and laid-back delivery over tempos around 90–100 beats per minute. Its subject matter grew out of gangsta rap: street life in Los Angeles, cars and lowrider culture, partying, and marijuana.

History

Prototypes appeared on Above the Law's Livin' Like Hustlers (1990), produced with input from Cold 187um, but the genre crystallised with Dr. Dre's solo debut The Chronic (1992), whose singles "Nuthin' but a 'G' Thang" and "Let Me Ride" brought the sound to a mass audience. Snoop Dogg's Doggystyle (1993) debuted at number one in the US, and Warren G's "Regulate" (1994, with Nate Dogg) and Tupac Shakur's All Eyez on Me (1996) extended G-funk's chart dominance. The deaths of Tupac Shakur (1996) and the collapse of Death Row Records ended its commercial peak, though its synthesiser melodies and funk basslines have been revived by later artists such as Kendrick Lamar on To Pimp a Butterfly (2015).
