Steamboat

A steamboat is a boat that is propelled primarily by steam power, typically driving paddle wheels or propellers. Though the term usually refers to inland and river vessels — above all the shallow-draught paddle steamers of American rivers — steam propulsion in various forms powered most of the world's shipping from the mid-nineteenth century until the rise of diesel engines.

History

Early experiments included John Fitch's steamboat on the Delaware River in 1787 and William Symington's Charlotte Dundas (1803), which towed barges on the Forth and Clyde Canal. Commercial success came with Robert Fulton's North River Steamboat (the "Clermont") in 1807, which ran between New York City and Albany on the Hudson River. In 1811 the New Orleans, built at Pittsburgh, became the first steamboat on the Mississippi system, inaugurating an era of river commerce.

The riverboat era

Western-river steamboats were wooden-hulled, shallow-draught stern- or side-wheelers with high-pressure engines, carrying passengers and freight between cities such as St. Louis, Cincinnati, Louisville, Memphis, and New Orleans. They were famously dangerous — boiler explosions, fires, and snags sank hundreds — and famously celebrated in American culture, notably in Mark Twain's Life on the Mississippi (1883), drawn from his years as a river pilot. Steamboats were also central to the California Gold Rush (via Panama and around Cape Horn) and to the colonization of inland Australia on the Murray–Darling rivers.

Decline and legacy

Railroads displaced river steamboats from the later nineteenth century, and diesel towboats replaced steam on most working rivers by the mid-twentieth century. A small number of historic paddle steamers survive as museum ships and excursion vessels, including the Belle of Louisville (1914) and the preserved Waverley in Britain, the world's last seagoing paddle steamer. Steam at sea persisted longer in the form of steamships and steam turbines, which still propel some naval vessels and LNG carriers.
