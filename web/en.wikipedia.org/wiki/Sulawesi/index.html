Sulawesi, formerly known in English as Celebes, is one of the four Greater Sunda Islands of Indonesia. It lies between Borneo to the west and the Maluku Islands to the east, and is the world's eleventh-largest island. Sulawesi is instantly recognizable for its distinctive shape: four long peninsulas radiating from a central core, separated by three deep gulfs — Tomini, Tolo, and Boni. The island is divided into six provinces, with Makassar in the south the largest city.

Geography and geology

Sulawesi sits at the junction of several tectonic plates and is geologically complex, having been assembled from colliding island arcs and fragments of continental crust. The island is mostly mountainous, with the highest peak, Mount Latimojong, rising about 3,478 metres. Earthquakes and volcanic activity are common. Wallace's Line, the famous biogeographical boundary proposed by Alfred Russel Wallace, passes through the Makassar Strait west of the island.

Biodiversity

Because of its long isolation and complex origins, Sulawesi has an exceptionally high level of endemism. Characteristic native mammals include the babirusa ("pig-deer"), the anoa (dwarf buffalo), spectral tarsiers, crested black macaques, and a radiation of endemic squirrels and rats; the island also hosts the maleo, a mound-building bird that incubates its eggs in geothermally heated sand. Sulawesi's ancient lakes, such as Lake Poso and the Malili lake system, contain unique radiations of freshwater fishes, snails, and shrimps. Offshore, the coral reefs of Bunaken and the Wakatobi islands are among the richest in the Coral Triangle.

History and people

Sulawesi contains some of the world's oldest known cave art: hand stencils and animal paintings in the Maros–Pangkep karst of South Sulawesi have been dated to at least 40,000–45,000 years ago. The island's major ethnic groups include the Makassarese and Bugis of the south — famed as seafarers whose pinisi schooners traded across the archipelago and to northern Australia — the Minahasan people of the north, and the Toraja of the highlands, renowned for elaborate funeral rituals and tongkonan houses. The kingdoms of Gowa and Bone contested the south in the 17th century before Dutch colonial rule was consolidated. Sulawesi was also the scene of the controversial Indonesian National Museum–era revolts of the 1950s, the Permesta rebellion, and religious conflict in Poso around 1998–2001.

Economy

Agriculture (rice, cocoa, coffee, cloves, and coconut), fishing, nickel mining and smelting (notably around Morowali and Sorowako), and forestry anchor the economy. The Tana Toraja highlands and the diving resorts of the north are major tourist draws.
