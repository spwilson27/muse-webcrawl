Direct current

Direct current (red) flows at a constant value, unlike alternating current (green), which oscillates.

Direct current (DC) is the one-directional flow of electric charge. Unlike alternating current (AC), in which the current periodically reverses direction, DC maintains a constant polarity. Sources of direct current include batteries, solar cells, fuel cells and dynamo-type generators fitted with commutators; electronic power supplies also convert AC to DC for most consumer electronics.

History

Direct current was the basis of the first commercial electric power distribution systems, developed by Thomas Edison in the 1880s. In the subsequent "war of the currents", AC — championed by George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla — prevailed for general distribution because transformers allow AC voltages to be raised for efficient long-distance transmission and lowered for safe use. Edison's DC networks were eventually converted or retired, the last in New York City in 2007.

Applications

Nearly all electronic devices run internally on DC: computers, phones, radios and LED lighting. DC is essential for electroplating and electrolysis, battery charging, and the traction systems of many railways and electric vehicles. Long-distance high-voltage direct current (HVDC) transmission has returned to favour for submarine cables and linking asynchronous grids, since it avoids some losses and stability problems of very long AC lines.
