Freight car

A freight car (known in British usage as a goods wagon or freight wagon) is a railway vehicle designed for the carriage of cargo rather than passengers. Freight cars are a type of rolling stock hauled by locomotives in a freight train, and they are built in many specialized forms to suit particular commodities.

Types

Common types include the boxcar, fully enclosed for general merchandise; the hopper car, open or covered, which discharges bulk loads such as coal or grain through bottom doors; the gondola, an open-topped car for dense bulk materials; the flatcar, for machinery, containers, and other loads that need no protection from the weather; the tank car, for liquids and gases; and the refrigerator car, insulated or mechanically cooled for perishable goods. Intermodal traffic uses well cars and flatcars carrying standardized shipping containers and truck trailers.

Construction and operation

Modern freight cars typically run on two four-wheel bogies (trucks in North American usage), are joined by automatic couplers, and are braked by a continuous air-brake system. In North America cars are identified by reporting marks and classified under the AAR (Association of American Railroads) reporting mark system; loads are limited by axle loading, commonly up to 36 tonnes per axle for heavy-haul service.

History

Freight cars developed from the horse-drawn chaldrons of early colliery railways into the four-wheel wagons of the early steam era, and in North America grew into large bogie vehicles of 40, 50, and eventually 60 feet and more. Unit trains — entire trains of a single commodity in identical cars — became a hallmark of bulk railroading in the later twentieth century.
