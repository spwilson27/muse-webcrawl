Dublin Lock-out

The Dublin Lock-out was a major industrial dispute between approximately twenty thousand workers and three hundred employers in Dublin, Ireland, lasting from 26 August 1913 to 18 January 1914. The dispute, centred on the right of workers to unionise, is often seen as the most severe and important industrial struggle in Irish history.

Background

Dublin in 1913 had some of the worst slum conditions in the United Kingdom, with many workers living in single-room tenements and employed casually on low wages. James Larkin's Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU) had rapidly organised unskilled workers, winning gains through sympathetic strike action. William Martin Murphy, Dublin's leading Catholic businessman and owner of the Dublin United Tramway Company, led employers determined to break the union and required his employees to sign a pledge renouncing ITGWU membership.

The dispute

The lock-out began during Horse Show week in August 1913, when tram workers abandoned their vehicles in support of dismissed colleagues. Murphy and allied employers locked out workers who refused to sign the pledge. On 31 August 1913 — "Bloody Sunday" — police baton-charged a banned rally on Sackville Street (now O'Connell Street), injuring hundreds; two workers, James Nolan and John Byrne, died of their injuries. During the dispute, the Irish Citizen Army was founded to protect strikers, and food ships sent by British trade unions, including the Narcissus, sustained the locked-out families. A scheme to send strikers' children to sympathetic homes in Britain collapsed amid religious controversy.

Outcome and legacy

By January 1914, with funds exhausted, workers drifted back to work, many forced to sign the pledge. Although the lock-out ended in a tactical defeat, the ITGWU survived, and the dispute became a foundational episode in Irish labour history. The poet W. B. Yeats addressed the city's atmosphere in "September 1913", and the events were commemorated extensively at their centenary in 2013.
