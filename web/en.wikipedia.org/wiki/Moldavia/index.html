Moldavia

Moldavia (Romanian: Moldova) is a historical region and former principality in Eastern Europe, corresponding roughly to the lands between the Eastern Carpathians and the Dniester River. Once a medieval state founded in the fourteenth century, historical Moldavia was divided by the Treaty of Bucharest (1812), which ceded its eastern half, Bessarabia, to the Russian Empire. Today the western part belongs to Romania (the region of Moldavia within Romania), while the east forms the independent Republic of Moldova, with smaller portions in Ukraine.

History

The Principality of Moldavia emerged in the mid-1300s under Dragoș and Bogdan I, freed from Hungarian overlordship. Its greatest ruler, Stephen the Great (1457–1504), defended it against the Ottoman Empire, Hungary and Poland, and founded dozens of churches, several of which — the painted monasteries of southern Bukovina — are now UNESCO World Heritage sites. From the sixteenth century Moldavia was an Ottoman vassal, though never a directly ruled province. In 1859 Moldavia united with Wallachia under Alexandru Ioan Cuza, forming the basis of modern Romania.

Geography and identity

Historical Moldavia spanned the Prut and Dniester basins, including Bessarabia, Bukovina, and at times the Budjak steppe on the Black Sea. The name derives from the Moldova River. The population speaks Romanian (historically called Moldovan), and the relationship between Moldavian regional identity and Romanian national identity — and between Romania and the Republic of Moldova — remains politically sensitive.
