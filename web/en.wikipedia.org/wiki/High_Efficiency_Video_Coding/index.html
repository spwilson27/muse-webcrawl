High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC), also known as H.265 and MPEG-H Part 2, is a video compression standard designed to substantially improve coding efficiency compared with its predecessor, H.264/MPEG-4 AVC. HEVC offers roughly double the data compression ratio at the same level of video quality, or substantially improved quality at the same bit rate. It supports resolutions up to 8192×4320 (8K UHD).

The standard was developed by the Joint Collaborative Team on Video Coding (JCT-VC), a collaboration between the ITU-T Video Coding Experts Group and the ISO/IEC Moving Picture Experts Group. The first version was ratified in 2013.

Technical features

Where H.264 divides frames into macroblocks of up to 16×16 pixels, HEVC uses coding tree units (CTUs) of up to 64×64 pixels, subdivided recursively in a quadtree structure. This allows much more flexible partitioning: large smooth areas can be coded cheaply while detailed regions get smaller blocks. Other improvements include 35 directional modes for intra prediction (versus 9 in H.264), improved motion vector prediction, sample adaptive offset filtering, and tile and wavefront parallel processing for multicore encoding and decoding.

These gains come at the cost of considerably higher computational complexity — encoding HEVC can be many times slower than H.264, though hardware encoders and decoders have become widespread in phones, televisions, and computers.

Profiles and tiers

HEVC defines profiles that set permitted tools: Main (8-bit, 4:2:0), Main 10 (10-bit), and Main Still Picture, with later extensions for higher bit depths, 4:2:2 and 4:4:4 chroma, screen content, and multiview/3D. Two tiers (Main and High) and a set of levels constrain bit rate and resolution.

Licensing and adoption

HEVC's patent licensing — split among several pools (MPEG LA, HEVC Advance/Access Advance, Velos Media) plus unaffiliated holders — was more complex and costly than H.264's, which slowed adoption on the open web and motivated the royalty-free AV1 format. Nevertheless HEVC became standard for 4K television broadcasting, UHD Blu-ray, Apple devices (including HEIF photos and FaceTime), and much streaming at high resolutions.
