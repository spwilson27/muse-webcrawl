Glycosphingolipid


A glycosphingolipid: a ceramide lipid with a carbohydrate head group.

Glycosphingolipids are a subtype of glycolipids containing the amino alcohol sphingosine. They are composed of a hydrophobic ceramide backbone — sphingosine linked to a fatty acid — bearing a hydrophilic carbohydrate head group, and are found in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane of animal cells, where their sugar residues face the extracellular environment.

Classification and structure

The simplest glycosphingolipids are the cerebrosides, with a single glucose (glucocerebroside) or galactose (galactocerebroside, abundant in myelin) attached to ceramide. Globosides carry additional neutral sugars. Gangliosides, concentrated in nervous tissue, are distinguished by one or more sialic acid (N-acetylneuraminic acid) residues; they are named in the Svennerholm system (GM, GD, GT and so on, denoting the number of sialic acids). Sulfatides carry a sulfate group on their sugar. Together with cholesterol and sphingomyelin, glycosphingolipids cluster into membrane microdomains often called lipid rafts.

Function and clinical significance

Cell-surface glycosphingolipids act as receptors, antigens and mediators of cell recognition: the ABO blood-group antigens include glycosphingolipid species, and gangliosides serve as binding sites for bacterial toxins such as cholera toxin (which binds GM1) and tetanus toxin. Their stepwise degradation in lysosomes requires specific enzymes and activator proteins; failure of this catabolism causes the sphingolipidoses, a group of lysosomal storage disorders including Gaucher disease (glucocerebrosidase deficiency), Tay–Sachs and Sandhoff diseases (hexosaminidase deficiencies), Fabry disease and Niemann–Pick disease. Altered glycosphingolipid profiles are also studied as tumour markers and as targets in immunotherapy.
