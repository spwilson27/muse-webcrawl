Hangman's fracture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hangman's fracture is the name given to a fracture of the pars interarticularis of the axis (the second cervical vertebra, C2), occurring on both sides. Medically it is a form of traumatic spondylolisthesis of the axis. The name refers to the injury pattern produced by judicial hanging, though in modern practice the fracture is most often caused by road traffic accidents and falls.

Mechanism

The fracture results from hyperextension and axial loading of the neck, often combined with flexion or distraction. In judicial hanging with a submental knot, extreme hyperextension and distraction disrupt the C2–C3 junction and injure the spinal cord, causing death. In accidental cases, such as a chin striking a dashboard in a car crash, the mechanism is typically hyperextension with axial loading, and the spinal canal often widens at the fracture site, so neurological injury is less frequent than might be expected.

Classification and treatment

The Levine and Edwards classification, a modification of the Effendi system, grades the fracture by displacement, angulation and instability. Stable injuries are usually treated with immobilisation in a cervical collar or halo vest. Unstable fractures may require surgical stabilisation, by anterior fusion of C2–C3 or by direct screw fixation of the pars (the Judet procedure).

History

The injury pattern in executed prisoners was described in the early twentieth century, and Schneider and colleagues coined the term "hangman's fracture" in 1965 for similar fractures seen in accident victims. Wood-Jones had earlier analysed the skeletal remains of executed Nubian prisoners, noting the characteristic C2 injury.
