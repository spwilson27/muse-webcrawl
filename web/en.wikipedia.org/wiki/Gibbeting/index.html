Gibbeting

Gibbeting is the use of a gallows-type structure — typically a tall post fitted with a cage or chains of iron — from which the dead or dying bodies of criminals were hung as a public display. In England the practice, sometimes called "hanging in chains", was imposed after execution as an additional punishment and deterrent, the body being exhibited at a prominent place such as a crossroads, a harbour entrance, or near the scene of the crime, sometimes for years until only bones remained.

History in Britain

Executed pirates were gibbeted along the Thames and at ports throughout the 17th and early 18th centuries; the pirate Captain Kidd's body was displayed at Tilbury Point after his execution in 1701. The practice was formalised by the Murder Act 1751, which provided that the bodies of executed murderers were never to be buried directly but instead handed to surgeons for dissection or hung in chains. Gibbet cages were shaped loosely to the body and manufactured by blacksmiths at the expense of the county.

Abolition

Growing public distaste, the unpleasantness of displays sited near expanding towns, and the spectacle of crowds gathering to watch bodies decay led to the practice's decline. The last gibbetings in England took place in 1832 — the collier William Jobling at Jarrow and the murderer James Cook at Leicester, whose gibbet was removed after a few days following complaints — and hanging in chains was formally abolished by statute in 1834. A handful of original gibbet posts and cages survive in British museums and in situ, notably at Caxton Gibbet in Cambridgeshire and Winter's Gibbet in Northumberland.

Elsewhere

Analogous displays of executed offenders on gallows, wheels, and cages were common across early modern Europe and were exported to colonial settings, where the bodies of executed rebels, pirates, and escaped slaves were gibbeted at harbour mouths and road junctions as warnings.
