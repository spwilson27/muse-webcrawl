Quintus Aurelius Symmachus

Born: c. 345, Rome
Died: c. 402
Occupation: Statesman, orator, letter-writer
Known for: Defence of Roman paganism; prefect of Rome (384); consul (391)
Children: Quintus Fabius Memmius Symmachus

Quintus Aurelius Symmachus (c. 345 – c. 402) was a Roman statesman, orator, and man of letters, the leading figure of the senatorial aristocracy of late fourth-century Rome and the most famous defender of Rome's traditional religion against the rising Christian establishment of the empire.

Life and career

Born into one of Rome's wealthiest senatorial families, Symmachus rose through the cursus honorum: he was proconsul of Africa in 373, and in 384 became praefectus urbi (prefect of the city of Rome). He was consul in 391 alongside the usurper Eugenius's supporter Flavius Eutolmius Tatianus. After the defeat of Eugenius at the Battle of the Frigidus in 394, Symmachus reconciled with the emperor Theodosius I and remained influential until his death around 402. His son married into the family of the Anicii, and the famous Symmachus–Nicomachus ivory diptychs commemorate the family alliance.

The Altar of Victory controversy

Symmachus is best remembered for his third Relatio (384), an official dispatch to the emperor Valentinian II pleading for the restoration of the Altar of Victory in the Senate house and of state subsidies for the Vestal Virgins, which the emperor Gratian had removed in 382. His plea—"we contemplate the same stars; the same sky is common to us all; the same universe encompasses us. What does it matter what practical system we adopt in our search for the truth? Not by one avenue only can we arrive at so tremendous a secret"—was answered point by point by Ambrose, Bishop of Milan, and the appeal failed. The exchange is a locus classicus for the religious conflicts of late antiquity.

Writings

Nine books of Symmachus's letters (some 900 items) survive, modelled on Pliny the Younger's published correspondence and prized as a source for late Roman aristocratic society. Forty-nine of his official dispatches (Relationes) as prefect also survive, together with fragments of his speeches. His contemporaries, including the Christian poet Prudentius (who wrote a poem against him), treated him as the greatest Latin orator of his age.
