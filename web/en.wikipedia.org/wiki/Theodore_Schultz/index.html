Theodore William Schultz (30 April 1902 – 26 February 1998) was an American agricultural economist who won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1979, shared with Sir Arthur Lewis, for pioneering research into economic development, especially the economics of agriculture and of poor countries. He is also known as a founder of human capital theory.

Career

Raised on a South Dakota farm, Schultz studied at South Dakota State College and earned his PhD at the University of Wisconsin–Madison (1930). He taught at Iowa State College, where his defence of a controversial pamphlet on wartime margarine restrictions led to his resignation in protest in 1943; he then joined the University of Chicago, chairing its economics department (1946–1961). In Transforming Traditional Agriculture (1964) he argued that farmers in poor countries are rational, efficient allocators of the resources they have — "poor but efficient" — and that development requires investment in new inputs, research, and, above all, education. His 1960 presidential address to the American Economic Association, "Investment in Human Capital," helped launch human capital as a central concept in economics.
