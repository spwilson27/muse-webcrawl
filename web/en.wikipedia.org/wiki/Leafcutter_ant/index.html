Leafcutter ant

Leafcutter ants, also called leaf-cutting ants, are fungus-growing ants of the genera Atta and Acromyrmex, comprising roughly four dozen species native to the tropical forests and grasslands of Central and South America and parts of the southern United States. They are named for their habit of cutting fragments of fresh vegetation — leaves, flowers, and grasses — and carrying them back to their nests in long, conspicuous processions.

Fungus agriculture

The ants do not eat the leaves directly. Instead, the cut vegetation is pulped and used as substrate for gardens of a fungus (family Lepiotaceae) cultivated in underground chambers. The fungus digests the plant material — including compounds toxic to most herbivores — and produces nutrient-rich structures called gongylidia, on which the ants feed. This mutualism, which originated tens of millions of years ago, is one of the most sophisticated examples of agriculture outside human society. The ants in turn protect their gardens from pests and pathogens, including with antibiotic-producing bacteria carried on their bodies.

Castes and colonies

Colonies of Atta can number several million workers organised into size-based castes: tiny minims tend the fungus gardens and ride on leaf fragments as guards, mediae forage and cut vegetation, majors defend the colony with powerful jaws, and a single long-lived queen — which may live for well over a decade — lays all the eggs. Mature nests are enormous underground complexes with hundreds of chambers that can move many tonnes of soil and rival small towns in their effect on the surrounding forest.

Ecological role

Leafcutter ants are dominant herbivores of the Neotropics, harvesting an estimated 12–17% of leaf production in some forests and thereby shaping plant communities, nutrient cycling, and soil turnover. Their defoliation also makes them serious pests of crops and forestry plantations across Latin America.
