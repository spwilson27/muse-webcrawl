Random graph

In mathematics, random graph is the general term for probability distributions over graphs. Random graphs may be described simply by a probability distribution, or by a random process which generates them. The theory of random graphs lies at the intersection between graph theory and probability theory, and is a key topic in discrete mathematics, network science and statistical physics.

An Erdős–Rényi random graph

Erdős–Rényi models

The classical models were introduced by Edgar Gilbert and by Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi around 1959. In the G(n, p) model, a graph is constructed on n vertices by including each possible edge independently with probability p. In the G(n, M) model, a graph is chosen uniformly at random from all graphs with n vertices and exactly M edges. The two models are closely related and share most asymptotic properties.

Phase transitions

Erdős and Rényi discovered striking threshold phenomena: as the average degree passes 1 (that is, p ≈ 1/n), a "giant component" containing a positive fraction of all vertices suddenly emerges, while all other components remain of logarithmic size. Other properties — connectivity, Hamiltonicity, containing a fixed subgraph — also appear suddenly at sharp threshold probabilities. A random graph with p somewhat above (ln n)/n is connected with high probability.

Other models

Many further models capture real-world networks better than the classical model: the Watts–Strogatz model (small-world networks with high clustering and short paths), the Barabási–Albert model (preferential attachment producing scale-free degree distributions), random geometric graphs, exponential random graph models, and the configuration model with a prescribed degree sequence. Random graphs also underpin the probabilistic method in combinatorics, pioneered by Erdős, which proves the existence of graphs with given properties by showing a random graph has them with positive probability.
