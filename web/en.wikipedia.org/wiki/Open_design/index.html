Open design

Open design: design information is shared so anyone can make and modify the product.

Open design is the development of physical products, machines and systems through the use of publicly shared design information, applying open-source principles — free access to the design files, permission to modify and redistribute them, and collaborative improvement — to tangible objects rather than only to software. The movement is closely linked to open-source hardware, the maker movement, fab labs and digital fabrication tools such as 3D printers and CNC machines.

Principles and licensing

Open design requires that design documentation — CAD files, bills of materials, assembly instructions — be published in editable formats under licences that permit study, modification, manufacture and sale. Licences adapted to hardware include the CERN Open Hardware Licence and the TAPR Open Hardware License, alongside Creative Commons licences. A widely cited definition was developed by the Open Design Working Group of the Open Knowledge Foundation.

Examples

  • RepRap — the self-replicating 3D printer project, foundational to desktop 3D printing
  • Open Source Ecology — the Global Village Construction Set of open industrial machines
  • Arduino and related open electronics platforms
  • WikiHouse — open design system for digitally fabricated housing
  • Precious Plastic — open machinery for small-scale plastic recycling

Challenges

Unlike software, physical products incur material costs, tooling requirements and safety/liability issues, and patent law rather than copyright governs many designs. Documentation quality and the "editable source" problem — sharing native CAD rather than derived meshes — remain practical obstacles to genuinely open physical design.
