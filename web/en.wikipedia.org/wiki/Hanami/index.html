Hanami

Hanami (花見, "flower viewing") is the Japanese traditional custom of enjoying the transient beauty of flowers, almost always referring to cherry blossoms (sakura), and less frequently plum blossoms (ume). From the end of March to early May, cherry trees bloom all over Japan, and around the second week of January on the island of Okinawa. The blossom forecast (sakura-zensen) is followed closely in the media, and people hold outdoor parties under the trees day and night.

History

Flower viewing began in the Nara period (710–794) with plum blossoms imported from Tang China, and shifted to cherry blossoms during the Heian period (794–1185), when the imperial court held viewing parties recorded in The Tale of Genji. The samurai adopted the cherry blossom as a symbol of the brevity of life; under the Tokugawa shogunate, commoners in Edo developed their own mass hanami at places such as Ueno and Asukayama. The philosopher Motoori Norinaga's famous poem made the cherry blossom an emblem of Japanese spirit (yamato-gokoro).

Modern practice

Today, people gather in parks, spread blue tarpaulins, and share food and drink under the blossoms; evening viewing is called yozakura ("night sakura"). Famous sites include Himeji Castle, Mount Yoshino, and Tokyo's Ueno Park and Shinjuku Gyoen. The custom has spread abroad with Japan's gift plantings, notably the 1912 gift of cherry trees to Washington, D.C., around the Tidal Basin.

Symbolism

Because the blossoms last only a week or two, hanami is closely linked to mono no aware, a gentle sadness at the transience of things, a core aesthetic concept in Japanese culture.
