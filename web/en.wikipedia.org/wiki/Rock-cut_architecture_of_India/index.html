Rock-cut architecture of India

The Kailasa temple, Ellora — the largest monolithic rock excavation

Indian rock-cut architecture is religious architecture created by carving solid natural rock. The surviving examples are mostly in India, where more than 1,500 rock-cut structures are known. Many contain works of art of global importance, and most are elaborately decorated. The earliest caves were natural formations used by people in the Mesolithic and Neolithic periods, but sculpted rock-cut temples and monasteries began around the 2nd century BCE.

Early Buddhist and Jain caves

The earliest surviving rock-cut chambers are the Barabar Caves of Bihar, donated by the Mauryan emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE to the Ajivika sect, with their famous polished interiors. Buddhist cave monasteries followed, especially in the Western Ghats of Maharashtra: sites such as Bhaja, Karla, Kanheri and Ajanta preserve chaitya prayer halls and vihara monasteries cut between the 2nd century BCE and the 7th century CE. Ajanta's caves are celebrated for their murals and sculpture.

Hindu and later monuments

From the Gupta period onwards, Hindu rock-cut architecture flourished. The Pallava workshops at Mahabalipuram created monolithic ratha temples and carved reliefs in the 7th century. The Rashtrakuta-period Kailasa temple at Ellora (Cave 16), excavated in the 8th century, is the largest monolithic rock-cut temple in the world, carved downward from a single basalt cliff. Ellora also preserves Buddhist and Jain caves, making it a monument of three religions. Other major sites include Elephanta, Badami, and the Jain caves at Udayagiri and Khandagiri in Odisha.

Technique

Rock-cut buildings were produced by excavation rather than construction: workers cut into the living rock, often working from the top down for monolithic temples, removing material to reveal the structure. The architecture imitates earlier wooden forms — barrel-vaulted chaitya roofs reproduce timber and thatch construction in stone.
