Legal transplant

A legal transplant is the moving of a rule or a system of law from one country to another, or to a people who previously had no such rule. The term was coined by the Scottish legal scholar Alan Watson in his 1974 book Legal Transplants: An Approach to Comparative Law, and the concept is a central topic of comparative law.

History

Transplants are arguably the most common source of legal change. The reception of Roman law across medieval and early modern Europe spread the ius commune; Napoleon's civil code was carried across Europe and Latin America; European colonial powers imposed their laws on Africa and Asia; and Meiji Japan deliberately adopted French and German codes. In the 19th century Turkey imported the Swiss civil code almost wholesale.

Debate

Watson argued that transplants are "socially easy" and that legal rules often develop in isolation from the society they govern. Critics such as Pierre Legrand reply that a rule's meaning is inseparable from its culture, so that what crosses borders is only "a meaningless form of words"; others, such as Otto Kahn-Freund, placed transplants on a spectrum of transferability. The related phenomenon of legal pluralism arises where transplanted law coexists with indigenous custom.
