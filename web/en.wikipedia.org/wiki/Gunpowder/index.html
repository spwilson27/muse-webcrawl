Gunpowder

Gunpowder, also called black powder, is the earliest known chemical explosive, consisting of a mixture of sulphur, charcoal (carbon) and potassium nitrate (saltpetre) — typically about 75% saltpetre, 15% charcoal and 10% sulphur. Invented in China by the 9th century, it revolutionised warfare worldwide.

CompositionPotassium nitrate, charcoal, sulphur
InventedChina, by 9th century AD; formula recorded 1044 (Wujing Zongyao)
UsesFireworks, firearms, cannon, mining, blasting
Superseded bySmokeless powder (1880s) for firearms

History

Chinese alchemists seeking elixirs discovered the mixture; by the Song dynasty it powered fire arrows, bombs and fire lances. Knowledge spread via the Mongol world to the Islamic lands and Europe by the 13th century — Roger Bacon and 'Berthold the Black' are associated with early European recipes. Cannon and handguns followed, reshaping fortification, navies and empires, and gunpowder underlay the 1605 Gunpowder Plot in England.

Properties

Gunpowder is a low explosive that deflagrates rather than detonates, producing hot gas and dense white smoke. Corned (grained) powder burns more consistently. It remained the sole practical explosive and propellant until dynamite and smokeless powder in the 19th century; today it is used mainly in fireworks, fuses, blanks and historic firearms.
