Mission control center

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mission control center
FunctionGround facility directing spaceflight operations
Famous centresHouston (Johnson Space Center), Korolyov (TsUP), Beijing Aerospace Flight Control Center
Houston, Apollo era.

A mission control center is a facility that manages spaceflight, from launch to landing, by monitoring telemetry and commanding the vehicle through a team of flight controllers seated at consoles — each responsible for one system (flight dynamics, guidance, electrical, life support, propulsion, and the flight surgeon for crew health) under a flight director who holds final authority. The model was created at NASA by Christopher C. Kraft Jr. and copied worldwide.

The room's architecture is a theatre of expertise: the flight director's back room of specialists ('the trench' of trajectory officers at Houston), voice loops linking consoles and the spacecraft's CapCom — traditionally an astronaut — and the big front screen plotting the ground track. When Houston lost contact with a vehicle, or Korolyov with a station, the room's discipline was the mission's lifeline.

Other centres

Russia's TsUP in Korolyov runs Soyuz and its station segment; ESA's ESOC in Darmstadt controls European missions; China's Beijing centre runs its crewed programme with notable public spectacle. Commercial operators — SpaceX's Hawthorne 'mission control' with its glass gallery — adapted the form for the camera age, but the console disciplines descend directly from Kraft's 1960s template.
