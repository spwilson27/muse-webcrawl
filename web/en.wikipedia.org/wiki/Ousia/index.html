Ousia (Ancient Greek: οὐσία) is an important philosophical and theological term, originally from Greek philosophy, usually translated as "substance" or "essence". It is the abstract noun formed from the feminine present participle of the verb "to be" (εἶναι), and thus signifies the being or essential nature of a thing. Through its Latin renderings substantia and essentia, the term underlies much of Western metaphysics and Christian doctrine.

In Greek philosophy

Plato uses ousia for the true reality of things — above all the Forms — as against the world of becoming. Aristotle made the term the centre of his Categories and Metaphysics. In the Categories he distinguishes primary ousia — the individual substance, such as this man or this horse, which is neither said of nor present in a subject — from secondary ousia, the species and genera to which individuals belong. In the Metaphysics the inquiry into being is largely an inquiry into ousia, analysed through form and matter and through actuality and potentiality. The question of what counts as primary substance — individual, form, or the composite — has been debated ever since.

In Christian theology

Imported into theology, ousia became the pivotal term of the Trinitarian controversies of the fourth century. The Nicene Creed (325, revised 381) confesses the Son to be homoousios — "of the same substance" — with the Father, against the Arian view that he was of a merely similar (homoiousios) or different substance. In the settlement associated with the Cappadocian Fathers, the Godhead is described as one ousia in three hypostaseis (persons). Conversely, in Christology the Council of Chalcedon (451) defined Christ as one person in two natures (physeis). Latin writers such as Tertullian had rendered the scheme as una substantia, tres personae, though the mismatch between Greek hypostasis and Latin substantia — both literally "that which stands under" — caused long misunderstanding between East and West.

Later usage

The term persists in philosophy in discussions of substance, and modern Greek uses ουσία for both "essence" and a chemical substance. Martin Heidegger treated ousia — understood as constant presence — as the fateful Greek determination of being that his own work sought to question.
