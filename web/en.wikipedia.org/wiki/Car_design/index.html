Car design

Designers sculpting a full-scale clay model of a new car. (Image placeholder)

Car design, or automotive design, is the process of developing the appearance — and to some extent the ergonomics — of motor vehicles, including automobiles, motorcycles and trucks. It is practised by design teams within car manufacturers, usually led by a chief designer, together with engineering departments that determine packaging, safety and manufacturability. The field combines industrial design, graphic design and an understanding of aerodynamics, materials and brand identity.

The design process

A new car's design typically begins with market research and a design brief, followed by hundreds of concept sketches exploring styling themes. Selected directions are developed as digital models using computer-aided design and virtual reality, and as physical clay models — often full-scale — which designers sculpt and refine under varied lighting. Interior design proceeds in parallel, covering the dashboard, seats, controls and infotainment.

Once a theme is chosen, the design is "frozen" and handed to engineers, who must reconcile the styling with crash safety, pedestrian protection, aerodynamics, visibility and production costs. Concept cars shown at motor shows test public reaction to new styling ideas and brand directions.

Elements of design

Key visual elements include the car's proportions and stance, its silhouette, the "greenhouse" (glazed area), wheel size and placement, the face formed by headlights and grille, and surface detailing — the creases and curves of the body panels. Designers speak of a brand's "design language": recurring cues, such as BMW's kidney grille or Audi's single-frame grille, that make vehicles recognisable across a range.

History

Early automobiles were styled by coachbuilders in the tradition of horse-drawn carriages. Harley Earl at General Motors in the 1920s and 1930s established the modern manufacturer design studio, introducing annual model changes, concept cars and features such as tailfins. European houses such as Pininfarina, Bertone and Italdesign (Giorgetto Giugiaro) shaped post-war sports-car and hatchback design, while figures such as Virgil Exner, Raymond Loewy and, later, Chris Bangle and J Mays influenced mass-market styling. In the 21st century, electric vehicles have brought new design freedoms — flat floors, short bonnets and full-width light bars — along with aerodynamic optimisation for range.
