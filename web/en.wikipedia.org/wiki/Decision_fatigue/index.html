Decision fatigue

In decision making and psychology, decision fatigue refers to the deteriorating quality of decisions made by an individual after a long session of decision making. It is now understood as one of the causes of irrational trade-offs in decision making. For instance, judges in court have been shown to make poorer quality decisions later in the day than they do earlier.

The concept is closely related to ego depletion, a hypothesis proposed by social psychologist Roy Baumeister and colleagues, which held that self-control and decision making draw on a limited pool of mental resources that can be exhausted. Subsequent research and replication attempts have produced mixed support for the strong version of the ego depletion model, and the underlying mechanisms remain debated.

Effects

Decision fatigue has been invoked to explain a range of phenomena: consumers becoming more likely to accept defaults or make impulsive purchases after making many choices; clinicians ordering more unnecessary tests or antibiotics later in their shifts; and professionals avoiding decisions altogether or deferring them when fatigued.

A widely discussed 2011 study by Danziger, Levav and Avnaim-Pesso examined parole decisions by Israeli judges and reported that favourable rulings dropped dramatically within each decision session and returned to a higher level after a food break. Later analyses questioned how much of the pattern reflected decision fatigue as opposed to scheduling artefacts, such as the ordering of cases, but the study remains a prominent illustration of the concept.

Criticism

Large-scale registered replication reports have found little or no evidence for the classic ego-depletion effect, leading some researchers to argue that decision fatigue effects are smaller, more conditional, or differently caused than originally proposed. Others maintain that real-world studies of judges, physicians and consumers show robust patterns consistent with fatigue, whatever the underlying laboratory mechanism.

Mitigation

Strategies commonly suggested for reducing the impact of decision fatigue include automating or routinising trivial choices, making important decisions earlier in the day, using checklists and defaults, and structuring decision sessions with breaks.
