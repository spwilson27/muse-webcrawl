Nicolaus August Otto (10 June 1832 – 26 January 1891) was a German engineer who developed the compressed-charge, four-stroke internal combustion engine — the Otto cycle engine — which provided the practical foundation for the modern petrol engine and the automobile age.

Early engines

After a career as a travelling salesman, Otto became interested in the gas engines of Étienne Lenoir. In 1864 he founded with Eugen Langen the company N. A. Otto & Cie in Cologne — the world's first engine factory and the ancestor of today's Deutz AG. Their atmospheric free-piston gas engine of 1867 won a gold medal at the Paris Exhibition for its fuel economy, and thousands were sold.

The four-stroke engine

In 1876 Otto built a four-stroke engine operating on the cycle of intake, compression, power and exhaust. Crucially, compressing the fuel–air mixture before ignition made the engine far more efficient, quieter and more powerful than its predecessors. His 1877 German patent became the subject of famous litigation: it was invalidated in 1886 when the court recognised the prior description of the four-stroke principle by the French engineer Alphonse Beau de Rochas (1862). The judgment opened the German market to competitors at exactly the moment Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach — formerly Otto's own employees — and Karl Benz were creating the first automobiles.

Legacy

More than a century later, nearly all spark-ignition piston engines still operate on the Otto cycle, and in German the petrol engine is commonly called the Ottomotor.
