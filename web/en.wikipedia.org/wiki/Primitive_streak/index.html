Primitive streak

The primitive streak establishes bilateral symmetry in the early embryo.

The primitive streak is a structure that forms in the early embryo during gastrulation, in birds, reptiles, and mammals (including humans). It appears as a thickened band on the surface of the epiblast at the posterior (caudal) end of the embryonic disc, and through it cells ingress to form the mesoderm and endoderm. Its appearance marks the establishment of bilateral symmetry and the head-to-tail axis of the body.

SystemEmbryogenesis
StageGastrulation (human: about day 14–15)
Gives rise toMesoderm and definitive endoderm (via ingression)
Key structuresPrimitive node (Hensen's node), primitive groove, primitive pit

Formation

The streak forms from a local thickening of epiblast cells that proliferate and converge toward the midline. Along its centre runs the primitive groove; at its anterior end lies the primitive node, whose pit is the site through which cells pass to form the notochordal process. Signalling molecules including Nodal, Wnt, and FGF control its induction and patterning; the node acts as an "organiser" analogous to the Spemann organiser in amphibians.

Fate

Cells ingressing through the streak displace the hypoblast to form the definitive endoderm, and spread between epiblast and endoderm as mesoderm. By the end of gastrulation the streak regresses toward the tail; remnants in the sacrococcygeal region normally disappear, but persistence is associated with sacrococcygeal teratomas.

Ethical and legal significance

The appearance of the primitive streak — after which twinning can no longer occur — is used in many jurisdictions as a boundary in embryo research regulation; the Warnock Report (1984) recommended, and UK law adopted, a 14-day limit on keeping human embryos alive in vitro.
