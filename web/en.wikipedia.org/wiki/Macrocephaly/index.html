Macrocephaly is a condition in which the head circumference is larger than average for the age and sex of the subject, conventionally defined as a measurement above the 97th percentile, or more than two standard deviations above the mean. It may be a normal, inherited (familial) trait requiring no treatment, or it may signal an enlarged brain (megalencephaly) or other underlying pathology such as hydrocephalus, subdural fluid collections, or a genetic syndrome.

Causes

Causes are commonly divided into megalencephaly (genuinely enlarged brain tissue) and other causes of a large head. Familial macrocephaly, an autosomal-dominant trait in which a parent also has a large head, is the most common benign cause. Pathological causes include hydrocephalus; enlarged extra-axial spaces in infancy (benign enlargement of the subarachnoid spaces); metabolic disorders; skeletal dysplasias such as achondroplasia; neurofibromatosis type 1; and overgrowth syndromes including Sotos syndrome, Weaver syndrome, and PTEN hamartoma tumour syndromes (e.g. Cowden syndrome and Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome). Mutations in PTEN are also associated with macrocephaly in some individuals with autism spectrum disorder — macrocephaly occurs in a substantial minority of autistic children. Rachitic and storage diseases, and chronic subdural haematoma or effusion, can also enlarge the head.

Evaluation and management

Assessment includes plotting serial head circumference measurements on growth charts, measuring the parents' head circumferences, developmental assessment, and neurological examination. Red flags — head circumference crossing percentiles upward, developmental delay or regression, or signs of raised intracranial pressure — prompt neuroimaging, usually cranial ultrasound in young infants or MRI. Benign familial macrocephaly needs no treatment beyond monitoring; treatment of pathological causes is directed at the underlying condition, such as shunt placement for hydrocephalus.
