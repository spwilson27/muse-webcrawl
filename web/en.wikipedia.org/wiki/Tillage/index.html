Tillage

Labelled placeholder: mouldboard ploughing, the archetypal primary tillage.

Tillage is the agricultural preparation of soil by mechanical agitation of various types, such as digging, stirring, and overturning. Examples of human-powered tilling methods using hand tools include shovelling, picking, mattock work, hoeing, and raking. In contrast, mechanised agriculture uses ploughs, harrows, cultivators and rotavators drawn by tractors or animals.

Purposes

Tillage is performed to prepare a seedbed, incorporate crop residues, fertiliser and amendments, control weeds, level the surface, and manage soil moisture. Primary tillage (such as mouldboard ploughing) loosens and turns the soil deeply, while secondary tillage (harrowing, cultivating) produces a finer tilth and kills young weeds.

Types

  • Conventional tillage: full-width inversion, leaving little residue on the surface.
  • Conservation tillage: reduced or strip tillage retaining at least 30% residue cover.
  • No-till farming: seeds drilled directly into undisturbed soil, relying on herbicides or cover crops for weed control.

Environmental effects

Intensive tillage accelerates erosion by wind and water, oxidises soil organic matter, and can destroy soil structure and biology. These concerns motivated conservation tillage and no-till systems, which trade some weed-control and residue-management challenges for improved soil carbon, moisture retention and reduced erosion.
