Estate in land

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An estate in land is an interest in real property measured by duration — the “time in the land” that a holder enjoys. The doctrine, inherited from feudal England, underlies the common law of land: since all land is held (in theory) of the Crown, what subjects own is not the land itself but estates in it. The principal estates are the fee simple (potentially perpetual, the closest to absolute ownership), the life estate (lasting for a life), and the leasehold or term of years; future interests (reversions and remainders) complete the system.

The Law of Property Act 1925 reduced the estates capable of existing at law in England and Wales to two — the fee simple absolute in possession and the term of years absolute — with all other interests taking effect in equity behind a trust. Fee simple can be qualified (determinable fees, fees on condition subsequent) and, in the United States, is the standard form of ownership, subject to taxes, zoning and eminent domain. The estate doctrine explains conveyancing language (“to A and his heirs”) and the arithmetic of future interests that still occupies property lawyers.
