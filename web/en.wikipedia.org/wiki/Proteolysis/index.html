Proteolysis is the breakdown of proteins into smaller polypeptides or individual amino acids. It is catalysed by enzymes called proteases (also termed peptidases or proteinases), which hydrolyse the peptide bonds linking amino acids, generally with the aid of a water molecule; uncatalysed hydrolysis of a peptide bond is extraordinarily slow, with a half-life of hundreds of years. Proteolysis is indispensable to living cells: it digests dietary protein, recycles damaged or short-lived cellular proteins, activates precursor proteins and hormones, controls the cell cycle and signalling pathways, and executes programmed cell death. Because cleavage is essentially irreversible, proteolytic systems are among the most tightly regulated in the cell.

Mechanisms

Proteases are classified by the residue or cofactor at their active site into families including serine proteases (such as trypsin, chymotrypsin and the caspases, which are cysteine proteases), aspartic proteases (such as pepsin, renin and HIV protease), metalloproteases (dependent on a metal ion, usually zinc), threonine proteases (the proteasome) and glutamic proteases. They are further divided into endopeptidases, which cut internal bonds of a chain, and exopeptidases, which trim amino acids from the N-terminus (aminopeptidases) or C-terminus (carboxypeptidases). Many proteases are synthesised as inactive precursors called zymogens—for example trypsinogen and proinsulin's precursor processing—so that their activity can be switched on only at the right time and place, by proteolytic removal of an inhibitory segment, in some cases by another protease in a cascade.

Biological roles

Digestion

In animals, dietary protein is hydrolysed in the stomach by pepsin and in the small intestine by pancreatic trypsin, chymotrypsin and carboxypeptidases, with brush-border peptidases completing the reduction to absorbable amino acids and small peptides. Similar digestive proteolysis occurs in carnivorous plants and in the lysosome-like vacuoles of microorganisms.

Protein turnover and quality control

Cells continuously destroy their own proteins. In eukaryotes the principal route for selective destruction is the ubiquitin–proteasome system: proteins marked with chains of the small protein ubiquitin are unfolded and fed into the barrel-shaped proteasome, whose threonine protease sites shred them into short peptides. This pathway removes misfolded proteins, enforces the timing of the cell cycle by destroying cyclins, and generates the peptides displayed by MHC class I molecules in antigen presentation. Non-selective bulk degradation, including of whole organelles, occurs by autophagy in lysosomes, whose acid proteases are called cathepsins.

Activation and signalling

Limited proteolysis switches many proteins on. Examples include the maturation of insulin from proinsulin; the digestive zymogen cascades; the blood-clotting cascade, in which a sequence of serine proteases culminates in thrombin converting fibrinogen to fibrin; the complement system of innate immunity; and the caspase cascade of apoptosis, in which initiator caspases activate executioner caspases that dismantle the cell. Proteases also shed cell-surface receptors, process peptide hormones and neuropeptides, and remodel the extracellular matrix during development, wound healing and, pathologically, cancer metastasis.

Regulation

Cells and blood are rich in protease inhibitors—serpins, cystatins, α2-macroglobulin and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteases (TIMPs)—that restrain proteases once their work is done. Compartmentalisation (sequestering proteases in lysosomes or secretory granules), zymogen activation, local pH and targeted ubiquitination provide further layers of control. Failure of control is destructive: unregulated proteolysis contributes to emphysema (neutrophil elastase destroying lung tissue when its inhibitor α1-antitrypsin is deficient), arthritis, pancreatitis and neurodegeneration.

Medical and biotechnological importance

Protease inhibitors are major drugs: HIV protease inhibitors transformed AIDS therapy; ACE inhibitors, though directed at a protease (angiotensin-converting enzyme), are mainstays of hypertension treatment; and proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib are used against multiple myeloma. In biotechnology, proteases are produced industrially for detergents, leather processing, cheese-making (chymosin) and molecular biology (proteinase K, TEV protease, restriction-like site-specific cleavage). In the laboratory, controlled proteolysis with trypsin is the standard first step in mass-spectrometry-based proteomics.
