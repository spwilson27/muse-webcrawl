Union of Utrecht

The Union of Utrecht (Dutch: Unie van Utrecht) was a treaty signed on 23 January 1579 in the Dutch city of Utrecht, uniting the northern provinces of the Netherlands, until then under the control of Habsburg Spain, into a confederation in rebellion during the Eighty Years' War. It is generally regarded as the foundation document of the Dutch Republic (the Republic of the Seven United Netherlands).

Background and terms

The Union was a response to the Union of Arras (January 6, 1579), by which the southern, Catholic provinces reconciled with Spain. The northern provinces—including Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, and Groningen, soon joined by cities in Flanders and Brabant—pledged to act "as if they were only one province" in war and diplomacy, while retaining their own laws, privileges, and (nominally) religious arrangements; in practice the Union guaranteed freedom of conscience to individuals, with the provinces free to regulate public worship. John of Nassau, stadtholder of Gelderland, was a principal driving force.

Legacy

Two years later the signatory provinces issued the Act of Abjuration (1581), formally deposing Philip II. The Union functioned as the Republic's de facto constitution until 1795, shaping a confederal state in which sovereignty rested with the provincial States and their assembly, the States General.
