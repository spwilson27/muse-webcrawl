The Moscow Trials were a series of three show trials held in the Soviet Union between August 1936 and March 1938, at the height of Joseph Stalin's Great Purge. Senior Communist Party leaders and other prominent figures were accused of espionage, sabotage, and conspiring with Leon Trotsky and foreign powers to assassinate Stalin and other Soviet leaders, restore capitalism, and dismember the USSR. The trials were widely publicised spectacles in which the defendants publicly confessed to fantastic crimes; most were sentenced to death and executed. Historians now regard the charges as fabricated and the confessions as extracted by threats, torture, and promises of clemency.

The three trials

The Trial of the Sixteen (August 1936) prosecuted the alleged "Trotskyite-Zinovievite Terrorist Centre," including Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev, Lenin's former comrades; all sixteen were shot. The Trial of the Seventeen (January 1937), against the "Parallel Anti-Soviet Trotskyist Centre" of Karl Radek and Yuri Piatakov, produced thirteen death sentences and prison terms for the rest. The Trial of the Twenty-One (March 1938), against the "Bloc of Rightists and Trotskyites," targeted Nikolai Bukharin and Alexei Rykov, former premier Genrikh Yagoda, and others; eighteen were executed. A separate secret military trial in June 1937 destroyed the Red Army high command, including Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky.

Conduct and evidence

State prosecutor Andrey Vyshinsky demanded the defendants be "shot like dirty dogs." There was no material evidence; confessions were the prosecution's foundation, consistent with Vyshinsky's doctrine that confession is the "queen of evidence." Some confessions contained demonstrable impossibilities — one defendant described a 1932 meeting at Copenhagen's Hotel Bristol, which had been demolished in 1917. Many foreign observers at the time nevertheless accepted the trials' authenticity; the American ambassador Joseph E. Davies reported them genuine. The Dewey Commission of 1937, chaired by John Dewey, investigated and declared the charges baseless.

Significance

The trials eliminated the Old Bolshevik leadership and legitimised the wider terror of 1936–38, in which hundreds of thousands were executed. In his 1956 "Secret Speech," Nikita Khrushchev denounced the trials as frame-ups based on falsified confessions. Bukharin, Radek's co-defendants, and most other victims were formally rehabilitated in 1988 during glasnost.
