An origin of replication is a particular sequence in a genome at which replication is initiated. Propagation of the genetic material between generations requires timely and accurate duplication of DNA by semiconservative replication prior to cell division to ensure each daughter cell receives the full complement of chromosomes. This can either involve the replication of DNA in living organisms such as prokaryotes and eukaryotes, or that of DNA or RNA in viruses, such as double-stranded RNA viruses.

Synthesis of daughter strands starts at discrete sites, termed replication origins, and proceeds in a bidirectional manner until all genomic DNA is replicated. Despite the fundamental nature of these events, organisms have evolved surprisingly divergent strategies that control replication onset. Although the specific replication origin organization structure varies from species to species, some common characteristics are shared.

In bacteria

Most bacteria have a single circular chromosome and, typically, a single origin of replication. The best-characterized bacterial origin is that of Escherichia coli, called oriC. It spans about 245 base pairs and contains several binding sites for the initiator protein DnaA, including a set of 9-bp repeats known as DnaA boxes, together with an AT-rich region (the DNA unwinding element) where the two strands are first separated. Binding of DnaA, assisted by architectural proteins such as IHF and HU, melts the AT-rich region and recruits the replicative helicase DnaB, which in turn loads primase and DNA polymerase III holoenzyme to form two replication forks that proceed in opposite directions around the chromosome.

Regulation

Because fast-growing bacteria can initiate new rounds of replication before earlier rounds have finished (producing cells with multiple partially replicated chromosomes), initiation at oriC is tightly regulated. Mechanisms include sequestration of newly replicated, hemimethylated oriC by the SeqA protein, titration of DnaA by additional chromosomal binding sites such as datA, and regulatory inactivation of DnaA through ATP hydrolysis stimulated by the Hda protein and the sliding clamp.

In archaea

Archaeal chromosomes are circular like those of bacteria, but their replication machinery resembles the eukaryotic system. Depending on the species, archaeal genomes may contain one or several origins. In Sulfolobus species, three origins fire on a single chromosome. The initiator protein Orc1/Cdc6, homologous to eukaryotic Orc and Cdc6, recognizes origin recognition boxes (ORBs) in the origin DNA.

In eukaryotes

Eukaryotic chromosomes are linear and much larger than those of prokaryotes, so replication begins at many origins — tens of thousands in a human cell — distributed along each chromosome. Origins are licensed during the G1 phase of the cell cycle when the origin recognition complex (ORC), Cdc6 and Cdt1 load inactive MCM2-7 helicase complexes onto DNA. At the onset of S phase, kinases including CDK and DDK activate a subset of licensed origins, recruiting Cdc45 and GINS to form the active CMG helicase. More origins are licensed than are normally used; dormant origins can fire under replication stress to help complete synthesis.

Yeast origins

In the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, origins are defined by short, specific sequences called autonomously replicating sequences (ARS), which contain an essential 11-bp ARS consensus sequence (ACS) bound by ORC. In fission yeast and metazoans, origins are not defined by a strict consensus sequence; instead they are influenced by chromatin structure, transcription, and epigenetic features, and initiation can occur within broad zones.

In viruses

Many viral genomes possess their own origins of replication recognized by virus-encoded initiator proteins. The origin of the tumour virus SV40, for example, is bound by the viral large T antigen, which also acts as the replicative helicase. Adenoviruses and bacteriophages such as lambda use distinct strategies, including protein priming and, in the case of lambda, an origin (oriλ) within a coding region.
