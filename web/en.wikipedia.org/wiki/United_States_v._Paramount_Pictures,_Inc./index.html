United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc., 334 U.S. 131 (1948) (also known as the Paramount Decree), was a landmark United States Supreme Court antitrust case that decided the fate of movie studios owning their own theatre chains and holding exclusivity rights on which theatres would show their movies. It changed the way Hollywood films were produced, distributed, and exhibited, and is widely regarded as having ended the old Hollywood studio system.

Background

The U.S. Department of Justice had sued the eight major studios — the "Big Five" (Paramount, Loew's/MGM, Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox, RKO) and the "Little Three" (Universal, Columbia, United Artists) — in 1938 over practices including block booking (forcing theatres to buy films in bundles), blind bidding, price fixing, and clearance arrangements that disadvantaged independent exhibitors. After a 1940 consent decree failed to resolve the issues, litigation resumed and a district court ruled against the studios in 1946.

Decision

Writing for the Court, Justice William O. Douglas held that the studios' practices violated the Sherman Antitrust Act, condemned block booking as an unlawful extension of copyright monopoly, and remanded the question of theatre divestiture. The ensuing Paramount Decrees required the Big Five to divest their theatre chains (spinning them off as independent companies such as United Paramount Theatres and Loew's Theatres) and barred the challenged distribution practices.

Effects

Divestiture broke the vertical integration at the heart of the studio system, accelerated the rise of independent production and talent agencies, and — together with television — contributed to the decline of classical Hollywood cinema. The decrees remained in force for over 70 years until the Department of Justice moved to terminate them; a court formally ended them in 2022 after a two-year sunset period.
