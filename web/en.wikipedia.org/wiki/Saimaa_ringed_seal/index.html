Saimaa ringed seal

The Saimaa ringed seal (Pusa saimensis, formerly Pusa hispida saimensis) is a subspecies (or, in some classifications, a species) of the ringed seal found only in Lake Saimaa, Finland. It is one of the few exclusively freshwater seals in the world and one of the most endangered pinnipeds, though decades of conservation have slowly increased its numbers.

Evolution and description

Saimaa ringed seals were isolated from the Baltic Sea when the land rose after the last Ice Age, becoming landlocked in the lake roughly 8,000–9,500 years ago. Adults are 130–145 cm long and weigh 40–60 kg, with dark grey fur patterned with distinctive light rings; each seal's pattern is unique, allowing researchers to identify individuals photographically. Pups are born in February–March in lairs dug into snowdrifts on the lake ice, where the snow provides insulation and concealment.

Conservation

Hunting and bounties reduced the population to perhaps a hundred animals by the mid-twentieth century. The seal was protected in 1955, and conservation measures — including restrictions on gillnet fishing during the pupping season, protected areas, and artificial snowdrifts piled up in mild winters — have raised numbers to roughly 400–480. Remaining threats include drowning in fishing nets, disturbance of lairs, shoreline development and winters with too little snow because of climate change.

In Finnish culture

The Saimaa ringed seal is a beloved national symbol of Finnish nature conservation. It featured on the Finnish five-penni coin and serves as the emblem of the Finnish Association for Nature Conservation.
