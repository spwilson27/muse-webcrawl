Oslofjord

Location: Southeastern Norway

Length: ~100 km (to Oslo)

Connects: Skagerrak to Oslo

Counties: Oslo, Akershus, Østfold, Vestfold, Buskerud

The Oslofjord (Norwegian: Oslofjorden) is an inlet of the Skagerrak in southeastern Norway, extending about 100 kilometres northward from an imaginary line between the Færder and Torbjørnskjær lighthouses to the capital, Oslo. Despite its name it is geologically not a true fjord but a rift valley — part of the Permian Oslo Graben — flooded by the sea.

Geography

The fjord narrows dramatically at the Drøbak Sound, where the Oscarsborg Fortress guards the passage to Oslo; beyond it the inner fjord splits into the Vestfjorden and Bunnefjorden around the Nesodden peninsula. Towns on its shores include Moss, Drøbak, Horten, Tønsberg and Fredrikstad. Numerous islands, such as Hovedøya and Gressholmen near Oslo, are popular recreation areas.

History

The fjord has been a strategic waterway since the Viking Age. On 9 April 1940, during the German invasion of Norway, coastal guns and torpedoes at Oscarsborg sank the heavy cruiser Blücher in the Drøbak narrows, delaying the occupation of Oslo and allowing the king, government and gold reserves to escape.

Environment and recreation

About 40% of Norway's population lives within an hour's drive of the Oslofjord. The inner fjord long suffered pollution from sewage and industry, though cleanup has restored bathing water quality; the outer fjord supports cod, mackerel and herring fisheries. Ferries, including the Oslo–Copenhagen and Oslo–Kiel routes, and thousands of leisure boats make it one of Scandinavia's busiest recreational waters.
