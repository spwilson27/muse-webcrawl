Timotheus
Active4th century BC (fl. c. 380–350 BC)
Diedc. 340s BC (according to tradition, before finishing the Mausoleum)

Timotheus (Greek: Τιμόθεος) was a Greek sculptor of the 4th century BC, one of the four celebrated masters — with Scopas, Bryaxis and Leochares — who carved the sculpture of the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus. He is among the earliest sculptors of the late Classical generation whose work softens the severity of the 5th century into the grace characteristic of the age of Praxiteles.

Epidaurus

Before the Mausoleum, Timotheus worked at the sanctuary of Asclepius at Epidaurus, where building inscriptions (c. 380 BC) record payments to him for models (paradeigmata) and for the acroteria — the crowning ornaments — of the Temple of Asclepius. Fragments of those acroteria, including a wind-blown Nike and an Amazon, survive in Athens and show his mastery of drapery in motion. The inscriptions make him one of the few Greek sculptors whose employment terms are epigraphically documented.

Mausoleum at Halicarnassus

For the tomb of Mausolus at Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum), designed by Satyros and Pythius and completed by Artemisia II, Pliny assigns Timotheus the south side of the monument. Tradition held that he died before the building was finished, and that his fellow sculptors completed his section without pay, "considering that it would be a memorial of their own glory and art" — a famous story illustrating the artists' pride in the Wonder.

Leda and other attributions

The composition of Leda and the Swan — Leda protecting the swan (Zeus in disguise) from an eagle — is widely attributed to Timotheus on the basis of the numerous Roman copies and its balanced, S-curved figure style. Pliny also mentions a Diana by Timotheus that stood in the Temple of Apollo on the Palatine in Rome, and a statue of Ares attributed to him (sometimes reassigned to Scopas) was set up at Halicarnassus.
