Calafia

OriginLas Sergas de Esplandián (c. 1510)
AuthorGarci Rodríguez de Montalvo
RoleQueen of the island of California

Calafia is a fictional queen in the 16th-century Spanish romance Las Sergas de Esplandián by Garci Rodríguez de Montalvo. She ruled the island of California, a mythical land of Black Amazons rich in gold and pearls, located 'on the right hand of the Indies'.

When Spanish explorers applied the name California to the peninsula (and later the wider region) of western North America, they carried Calafia's island into real geography; she is often cited as the namesake origin of California.

In the romance

Calafia leads her warrior women, armed with gold weapons and riding griffins, to aid the Muslim defence of Constantinople against a Christian crusade, where she confronts the knight Esplandián. She is portrayed as magnificent, courageous, and eventually converted and married within the romance's plot.

Legacy

Calafia has been reclaimed in modern art and literature as a founding figure of Californian identity, depicted in murals such as the 'Calafia' works in San Francisco and celebrated in Chicano and feminist retellings. A statue and mural traditions keep the character prominent in state lore.
