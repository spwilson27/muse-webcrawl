Max Newman

Maxwell Herman Alexander Newman FRS (7 February 1897 – 22 February 1984), known as Max Newman, was a British mathematician and codebreaker. His work in topology, and his lectures on mathematical logic, influenced Alan Turing; during the Second World War he led the "Newmanry" section at Bletchley Park that mechanised the breaking of the German Lorenz cipher, leading to the Colossus computer; and after the war he founded the computing machine laboratory at the University of Manchester.

Early career

Born in Chelsea, London, to a German immigrant father (born Neumann), Newman won a scholarship to St John's College, Cambridge. His studies were interrupted by the First World War. He became a fellow of St John's in 1923 and wrote an influential textbook, Elements of the Topology of Plane Sets of Points (1939). His 1935 lectures on the foundations of mathematics, attended by Turing, introduced the Entscheidungsproblem and prompted Turing's work on computable numbers.

Bletchley Park

Newman joined the Government Code and Cypher School in 1942 and proposed mechanising the attack on the Lorenz (Tunny) cipher used by the German High Command. His section, the Newmanry, developed the Heath Robinson machine and then Tommy Flowers's Colossus — the world's first programmable electronic digital computer — which began operating in early 1944 and greatly accelerated Tunny decryption.

Manchester

In 1945 Newman took the chair of mathematics at Manchester and established its Royal Society Computing Machine Laboratory. With Turing and others he planned an ambitious computing programme, obtained funding, and recruited engineers including Freddie Williams and Tom Kilburn, whose "Baby" machine ran the first stored program in June 1948. Newman also pursued work in combinatorial topology and gave his name to Newman's lemma in rewriting theory.
