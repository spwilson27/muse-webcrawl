The Song of Hiawatha

The Song of Hiawatha is an 1855 epic poem in trochaic tetrameter by the American poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow which features Native American characters. The epic relates the fictional adventures of an Ojibwe warrior named Hiawatha and the tragedy of his love for Minnehaha, a Dakota woman. Events in the story are set in the Pictured Rocks area of Michigan on the south shore of Lake Superior. Longfellow's poem, though a work of imaginative literature, is based on oral traditions and earlier writings of Native peoples, in particular the ethnographic work of Henry Rowe Schoolcraft.

Summary

The poem recounts Hiawatha's birth—his mother Wenonah dies, and he is raised by his grandmother Nokomis—his childhood learning from the animals and birds, his fasting and vision quest, his invention of picture-writing, his building of a birch canoe, his courtship and marriage to Minnehaha ("Laughing Water"), and his feats, including the battles with his father Mudjekeewis and the magician Pearl-Feather, and the slaying of the sturgeon Mishe-Nahma. After Minnehaha dies in a famine winter, and as European missionaries arrive, Hiawatha counsels his people to receive them and departs alone in his canoe toward the sunset, into the "Land of the Hereafter".

Sources and reception

Longfellow drew the name Hiawatha from the historical Iroquois leader and co-founder of the Iroquois Confederacy, but transferred him to Ojibwe legend, chiefly as collected in Schoolcraft's writings; the poem's metre was adapted from the Finnish national epic, the Kalevala. Published in 1855, it was an immediate and enormous popular success—one of the best-selling poems of the 19th century—but was also widely parodied, including by Lewis Carroll, and has since been criticized for romanticizing and misrepresenting Native American cultures, even as it remains one of the best-known works of American literature.
