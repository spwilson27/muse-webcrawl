Anu (Sumerian: An, "sky, heaven"; Akkadian: Anu) is the supreme sky god of the Mesopotamian pantheon, the divine personification of the sky itself and the ultimate source of authority among the gods. Although rarely the focus of personal devotion and only occasionally active in myth, Anu stood at the head of the pantheon for over two thousand years: his name is written with the sign for "god" (dingir), his number in the scholarly god-lists is 60, the base of the Mesopotamian sexagesimal system, and kingship itself was said to have been "lowered from heaven" by his decree.

Role and character

Anu dwells in the highest of the three stacked heavens, enthroned on a dais of lapis lazuli, attended by his vizier Ilabrat. From there he decrees the destinies of gods and men, presides over the divine assembly, and allocates domains: in the division of the cosmos Anu takes the sky, Enlil the earth and its winds, and Enki the sweet waters of the Abzu. His remoteness is proverbial in the myths — he is often an arbiter rather than an actor — but he can intervene decisively: in the Epic of Gilgamesh it is Anu who, at his daughter Ishtar's complaint, creates the Bull of Heaven to punish Gilgamesh; in the myth of Adapa he summons the first sage to heaven and, through a misunderstanding engineered by Enki, sees him refuse the food of immortality. In Enuma Elish Anu is the grandson of Apsu and Tiamat and the father of Enki (Ea), and he yields the leading role in creation to the younger generation.

Cult

Anu's great cult centre was Uruk, where the Eanna ("House of Heaven") precinct was sacred to him and later to Inanna/Ishtar; in the Seleucid period (third–second centuries BC) Uruk experienced a remarkable theological revival in which Anu and his consort Antu were elaborated into near-monotheistic supreme deities, and the ziggurat and temples of the city were rebuilt for his worship. He was also venerated at Der, Assur and Babylon, and appears at the head of god-lists and royal inscriptions throughout Mesopotamian history — for example as the god who grants kingship to Hammurabi in the prologue of his law code.

Family and equivalents

In the earliest Sumerian tradition An's consort is the earth goddess Ki, and heaven and earth (an-ki) together form the cosmos; later his wife is Antu. God-lists make Enlil his son (elsewhere Enlil's parentage differs), and Adad, the storm god, and Inanna both belong to his line. With the decline of Mesopotamian religion under Persian, Greek and Parthian rule, Anu's cult at Uruk faded in the early centuries AD, closing a career that began in the earliest written records of the fourth millennium BC.
