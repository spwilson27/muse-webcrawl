Damascus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Umayyad Mosque in Damascus at dusk, its courtyard and minarets]

Damascus is the capital and largest city of Syria, and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, settled by the 3rd millennium BC in the fertile Ghouta oasis watered by the Barada River. Capital of the Aramaean kingdom of Aram-Damascus in the Iron Age, successively Persian, Greek, Roman, Byzantine and Arab, it became in 661 the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate — the first great imperial capital of Islam — and remained a major centre of trade, scholarship and craft (damask fabric, Damascus steel) under Ayyubids, Mamluks and Ottomans. Its Old City, with the Umayyad Mosque built over Roman and Byzantine temples, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Ancient and classical city

Damascus appears in Egyptian records of the 15th century BC and in the Bible as the capital of Aram. Conquered by Alexander, ruled by Seleucids and (from 64 BC) Rome, it was the city near which, by Christian tradition, Paul the Apostle converted on the road from Jerusalem. Roman Damascus received a grid plan, temples (the great temenos of Jupiter became the cathedral, then the mosque) and hippodrome; it prospered on the incense and silk routes between Mesopotamia, Arabia and the Mediterranean.

Umayyad capital and after

Mu'awiya made Damascus his capital in 661; under Abd al-Malik and al-Walid I the Umayyad Mosque (705–715) rose on the ancient sacred site, one of Islam's masterpieces. After the Abbasids moved the capital to Baghdad (750), Damascus became a provincial city — though a rich one — fought over by Fatimids, Seljuks, Crusader-era powers, Nur al-Din and Saladin (both buried there), sacked by the Mongols (1260) and Timur (1401), and governed by Ottoman pashas for four centuries until 1918.
