K-means clustering

K-means clustering is a method of vector quantization, originally from signal processing, that is popular for cluster analysis in data mining and machine learning. It aims to partition n observations into k clusters, in which each observation belongs to the cluster with the nearest mean (cluster centre, or centroid), which serves as a prototype of the cluster.

Problem definition

Given a set of observations (x1, x2, …, xn) in d-dimensional space, k-means clustering seeks a partition into k sets S1, …, Sk minimising the within-cluster sum of squares — the sum, over all clusters, of the squared Euclidean distances of points to their cluster's centroid. This objective makes k-means equivalent to partitioning the data into Voronoi cells around the centroids. Finding the exact optimum is NP-hard in general, so heuristic iterative algorithms are used in practice.

Lloyd's algorithm

The standard algorithm, commonly called k-means and often known as Lloyd's algorithm (proposed by Stuart Lloyd in 1957 and published in 1982), alternates two steps:

  • Assignment step: assign each observation to the cluster whose centroid is nearest.
  • Update step: recompute each centroid as the mean of the observations assigned to its cluster.

The steps repeat until assignments no longer change. The algorithm always converges (the objective decreases at every step), but possibly to a local optimum, so multiple restarts with different seeds are common. The choice of initial centroids matters greatly; the k-means++ seeding method (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007) spreads initial centres apart and gives an expected O(log k) approximation guarantee.

Choosing k and limitations

The number of clusters k must be specified in advance. Heuristics for choosing it include the elbow method, silhouette scores, and information criteria. K-means assumes clusters are roughly spherical and comparably sized; it is sensitive to outliers and to the scaling of the features, and it imposes hard assignments of points to single clusters — soft alternatives are provided by Gaussian mixture models fitted with expectation–maximisation.

Applications

Applications include customer segmentation in marketing, image compression and colour quantization, document clustering, vector quantization in speech coding, and cell segmentation in biology and medical imaging.
