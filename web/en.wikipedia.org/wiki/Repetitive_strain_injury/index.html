Repetitive strain injury

A repetitive strain injury (RSI) is an injury to part of the musculoskeletal or nervous system caused by repetitive use, vibrations, compression or sustained awkward positions. RSIs are also known as cumulative trauma disorders, repetitive stress injuries and occupational overuse syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically develop gradually and include aching or throbbing pain, stiffness, weakness, numbness or tingling in the affected limb, most commonly the hands, wrists, forearms, elbows, shoulders and neck. Specific conditions grouped under the RSI umbrella include carpal tunnel syndrome, tendinitis, tenosynovitis, bursitis, trigger finger and De Quervain syndrome.

Causes

RSIs arise from microtrauma to muscles, tendons and nerves from tasks repeated at high frequency or force without adequate recovery. Contributing factors include poor ergonomics, static posture, cold environments, vibration from tools and psychosocial stress. Occupations at elevated risk include computer users, assembly-line workers, musicians, meat packers and construction workers.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention emphasises ergonomic workstation design, task rotation, scheduled rest breaks and exercises. Treatment ranges from rest and activity modification, ice, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physiotherapy and splinting, to corticosteroid injections or surgery in persistent cases. Prognosis is generally good with early intervention; chronic cases can become disabling.

History

Bernardino Ramazzini described overuse injuries in workers in the early 1700s. The term rose to prominence in the 1980s amid controversy in Australia, where an "epidemic" of arm pain among keyboard workers prompted debate over the role of organic injury versus psychosocial factors.
