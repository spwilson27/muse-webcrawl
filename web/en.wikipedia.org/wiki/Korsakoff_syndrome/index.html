Korsakoff syndrome

Korsakoff syndrome (also called Korsakoff psychosis, amnesic-confabulatory syndrome, or historically alcoholic Korsakoff syndrome) is a chronic memory disorder caused by a deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1), most often resulting from alcohol use disorder, though it can also arise from malnutrition, eating disorders, hyperemesis, dialysis, or other causes. It is characterised by severe anterograde and retrograde amnesia together with confabulation — the unintentional filling of memory gaps with fabricated accounts, which the patient believes to be true.

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically cannot form new memories and may lose memories reaching back years before onset, while procedural memory and intelligence are relatively spared. Confabulation ranges from brief plausible fabrications to elaborate invented autobiographies. Apathy and lack of insight are common. The syndrome is frequently the chronic stage following an episode of Wernicke encephalopathy; together the two are termed Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.

Cause and pathophysiology

Thiamine deficiency damages diencephalic structures — particularly the mammillary bodies, the dorsomedial nucleus of the thalamus, and periaqueductal regions — that are critical for memory consolidation. The syndrome was described in detail by the Russian neuropsychiatrist Sergei Korsakoff in a series of papers in 1887–1891.

Treatment and prognosis

Urgent treatment with high-dose parenteral thiamine is essential and may partly reverse symptoms if given early; established Korsakoff syndrome is often permanent, though a minority of patients recover substantially with abstinence and nutritional support. Management includes rehabilitation with memory aids and highly structured environments. Prevention centres on thiamine supplementation in at-risk patients and, in some countries, fortification of foods.
