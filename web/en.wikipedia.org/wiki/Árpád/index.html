Árpád (Hungarian pronunciation: [ˈaːrpaːd]; c. 845 – c. 907) was the head of the confederation of the Magyar tribes at the end of the 9th and beginning of the 10th centuries. According to Hungarian chronicles he was elected leader after the death of his father Álmos and led the Hungarians into the Carpathian Basin in the Hungarian conquest (Honfoglalás) of around 895. He is regarded as the founder of the Hungarian nation, and the ruling house descended from him — the Árpád dynasty — governed the Kingdom of Hungary until 1301.

SOURCES and life

The main sources for Árpád's life are the Byzantine emperor Constantine VII's De Administrando Imperio and later Hungarian chronicles such as the Gesta Hungarorum of Anonymus and the Chronicon Pictum, which mix fact with legend. Constantine records that the Hungarians (or "Turks") elected Árpád as their prince "according to the custom of the Khazars" after his father Álmos was killed or displaced in Transylvania.

The conquest

Driven westward by Pecheneg attacks and allied at times with Byzantium and Moravia, the seven Magyar tribes crossed the Verecke Pass into the Carpathian Basin about 895. Under Árpád's leadership they defeated the Bulgarians, Moravians, and local Slavic rulers, and consolidated control of the basin by about 900. Tradition credits Árpád with founding a settlement at Óbuda and with campaigns as far as Italy.

Death and legacy

Árpád died around 907, the year of the Hungarian victory over a Bavarian army at the Battle of Pressburg (Pozsony), which secured the conquest. He was succeeded by his son Zoltán. Árpád remains a central figure of Hungarian national identity: his statue stands at Heroes' Square in Budapest, and the colossal Feszty Panorama depicts his arrival in the basin.
