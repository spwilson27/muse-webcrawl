Cognitive dissonance

In the field of psychology, cognitive dissonance is the mental discomfort experienced by a person who holds two or more contradictory beliefs, ideas, or values at the same time, or who performs an action contradictory to their beliefs. The discomfort motivates the person to change a belief or behaviour to reduce the inconsistency.

Theory

Leon Festinger proposed the theory in 1957, drawing on his study of a cult whose prophecy of the end of the world failed — reported in When Prophecy Fails (1956) — where members responded to disconfirmation by increasing their proselytising rather than abandoning their beliefs.

Classic paradigms

In the induced-compliance paradigm, Festinger and Carlsmith (1959) paid participants either $1 or $20 to lie about a boring task; those paid $1 later rated the task more enjoyable, having insufficient external justification for lying. In the free-choice paradigm, people upgrade the option they chose and downgrade the one they rejected after deciding ("spreading of alternatives"). Effort justification describes the increased valuation of goals achieved through severe initiation.

Reduction strategies

Dissonance can be reduced by changing a belief, changing behaviour, acquiring new information that outweighs the dissonant belief, or trivialising the importance of the inconsistency.
