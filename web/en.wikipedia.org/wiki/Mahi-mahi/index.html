Mahi-mahi

Mahi-mahi
Scientific nameCoryphaena hippurus
FamilyCoryphaenidae
Other namesCommon dolphinfish, dorado
HabitatTropical and subtropical oceans worldwide

The mahi-mahi (Coryphaena hippurus), also known as the common dolphinfish or dorado, is a surface-dwelling ray-finned fish found in off-shore temperate, tropical and subtropical waters worldwide. It is one of only two members of the family Coryphaenidae, the other being the pompano dolphinfish. "Mahi-mahi" means "very strong" in Hawaiian. Despite the name dolphinfish, it is unrelated to dolphins.

Description

Mahi-mahi have compressed bodies and a blunt, steep forehead; mature males develop a prominent bony crest. Their colouration is brilliant — golden on the sides, with bright blues and greens on the back — and fades rapidly after death. They are fast-growing, short-lived fish, rarely exceeding four to five years, and typically weigh 7–13 kg, though specimens over 18 kg occur. They feed on flying fish, crabs, squid and other small fish, and are themselves preyed on by tuna, marlin and sharks.

Fisheries and cuisine

A popular game fish and food fish, mahi-mahi is caught commercially and recreationally throughout its range, notably off Hawaii, the Caribbean, and the Pacific coast of Central America. Its firm, mild flesh is served grilled, blackened or in tacos. Because of its fast growth and high reproduction rate, it is considered a relatively sustainable seafood choice, although consumers are advised about moderate mercury content.

Aquaculture

The species' extremely rapid growth has made it a candidate for offshore aquaculture, with research and pilot farming operations in several countries.
