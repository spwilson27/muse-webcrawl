LocationItria Valley, Apulia, Italy

A trullo (plural trulli) is a traditional Apulian stone hut with a conical roof, found principally in the Itria Valley of Apulia (Puglia) in southern Italy. Trulli are built by the dry-stone method — without mortar — from roughly worked limestone slabs gathered from the surrounding fields, with thick walls and a roof of thin limestone tiles (chiancarelle) arranged in a self-supporting cone, often topped by a decorative pinnacle.

History

Dry-stone conical buildings have prehistoric precedents around the Mediterranean, but the trulli of Apulia are generally dated from the medieval period onwards, with the greatest concentration built between the sixteenth and early twentieth centuries as dwellings and farm buildings. The town of Alberobello contains over 1,500 trulli and is the best-known example.

A popular (though disputed) account holds that trulli were built without mortar so they could be quickly dismantled — by pulling out a keystone so the cone collapsed — when royal inspectors came to assess taxes on new settlements, the area having been settled under a pragmatica of the Kingdom of Naples that taxed permanent dwellings. Whatever its truth, the dry-stone technique suited a region of abundant fieldstone and scarce timber.

Features

Trullo walls are very thick, giving interiors that stay cool in summer and warm in winter, though they make the internal space small. Many trulli bear whitewashed symbols on their cones — religious, zodiacal, or magical signs. Multi-cone complexes join several trulli together, the largest being the two-storey Trullo Sovrano in Alberobello. Since 1996 the trulli of Alberobello have been designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and many are now used as homes, shops, restaurants and holiday accommodation.
