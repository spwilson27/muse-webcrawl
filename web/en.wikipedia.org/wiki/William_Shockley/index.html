William Shockley

William Bradford Shockley Jr. (February 13, 1910 – August 12, 1989) was an American physicist who shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics for his research on semiconductors and the discovery of the transistor effect.

William Bradford Shockley Jr. (February 13, 1910 – August 12, 1989) was an American inventor, physicist, and eugenicist. He was the manager of a research group at Bell Labs that included John Bardeen and Walter Brattain. The three scientists were jointly awarded the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics for "their researches on semiconductors and their discovery of the transistor effect".

Early life and Bell Labs

Shockley was born in London to American parents and raised in California. He earned his B.S. from Caltech in 1932 and his PhD in physics from MIT in 1936, then joined Bell Telephone Laboratories. During the Second World War he worked on radar and anti-submarine operations research.

The transistor

Returning to Bell Labs after the war, Shockley led the solid-state physics group seeking a semiconductor amplifier. In December 1947 Bardeen and Brattain demonstrated the point-contact transistor; Shockley, feeling slighted at being excluded from the patent, soon developed the junction transistor (conceived 1948, described in his 1950 book Electrons and Holes in Semiconductors), which became the dominant commercial design.

Silicon Valley and controversy

In 1955 Shockley founded Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory in Mountain View, California, attracting brilliant young scientists — but his abrasive management drove away the "traitorous eight" (1957), who founded Fairchild Semiconductor, seeding Silicon Valley. Shockley Transistor Corporation never prospered. He later became a professor at Stanford, where his public advocacy of eugenics and claims about race and intelligence made him deeply controversial and damaged his reputation. He died in Stanford, California, in 1989.
