Wahdat al-wujud

Waḥdat al-wujūd (Arabic: وحدة الوجود, "unity of being" or "oneness of existence") is a philosophy in Sufi Islam associated principally with the Andalusian mystic and philosopher Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240). Although Ibn Arabi himself did not use the term, it became the standard label for his metaphysical doctrine, developed by his followers and expounded in works such as the Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam (The Bezels of Wisdom) and al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya (The Meccan Revelations).

Doctrine

The doctrine holds that wujūd — being or existence — is one and that this single existence is God's. All created things exist only in and through the one existence; they have no existence in themselves. The world is thus a theatre of divine self-disclosure (tajallī), in which the divine names and attributes are manifested in created forms. Ibn Arabi expressed this with the formula "He/not He" (huwa lā huwa): every thing is God in respect of its existence, yet not God in respect of its particular essence.

Closely connected is the concept of the al-insān al-kāmil, the Perfect Human, who realizes in himself the fullness of the divine names, and of the fixed entities (al-aʿyān al-thābita), the eternally known prototypes of things in God's knowledge.

Reception and controversy

Waḥdat al-wujūd became the dominant metaphysical framework of much later Sufism, influencing orders and thinkers from Anatolia and Persia to India and Southeast Asia, and poets such as Rumi's commentators, Jami, and Yunus Emre. It was, however, sharply criticized by some jurists — most famously Ibn Taymiyya — who charged that it effaced the distinction between Creator and creation and amounted to pantheism or monism. In Mughal India, the Naqshbandi shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi countered it with his doctrine of waḥdat al-shuhūd ("unity of witnessing"), arguing that the unity experienced by the mystic is a unity of perception, not of being itself.

Modern scholarship debates how far Ibn Arabi's own teaching should be equated with the later systematized doctrine, pointing out that the phrase was coined by his interpreters (and critics) rather than by the master himself.
