Kashubian language

Kashubian
Native to: Poland
Region: Pomerania
Native speakers: about 87,000 (2011 census)
Language family: Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > West Slavic > Lechitic > Pomeranian
Official status: recognised regional language in Poland

Kashubian (Kashubian: kaszëbsczi jãzëk; Polish: język kaszubski) is a West Slavic language belonging to the Lechitic subgroup, spoken by the Kashubians in the historical region of Pomerania in north-central Poland. It is the only surviving descendant of the Pomeranian languages and is closely related to Polish, with which it shares much vocabulary and grammar.

History

Kashubian evolved from the Pomeranian dialects spoken by Slavic tribes along the southern Baltic coast. Written Kashubian appeared sporadically from the 15th century, mostly in religious texts. In the 19th century, Florian Ceynowa became the first activist and writer to develop a Kashubian literary standard. After the Second World War the language declined under pressure from standard Polish, but it has experienced a revival since 1989.

Status

Since 2005 Kashubian has been recognised in Poland as a regional language — the only one with that status in the country. It is taught in some schools in the Pomeranian Voivodeship, used in local media, and appears on bilingual place-name signs in a number of communes. The 2011 census recorded about 108,000 people declaring Kashubian identity, with around 87,000 reporting use of the language at home.

Phonology and orthography

Kashubian is noted among Slavic languages for features such as the vowel ë (schwa), the development of soft labials, and a distinctive system of mobile stress in northern dialects versus fixed initial stress in the south. Its alphabet is based on the Latin script with additional letters such as ã, é, ë, ò, ô and ù.
