The studio system is a method of filmmaking wherein the production and distribution of films is dominated by a small number of "major" film studios. The term is most often used in reference to the system of Hollywood during the Golden Age from the 1920s to the early 1960s, in which eight major studios controlled virtually every aspect of the industry through vertical integration.

Structure

The "Big Five" majors — Paramount, Loew's (MGM), Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox, and RKO — owned production lots, global distribution networks, and chains of first-run theatres in major cities. The "Little Three" — Universal, Columbia, and United Artists — distributed but owned no theatres. Studios employed actors, directors, writers, and technicians under long-term exclusive contracts, assigned projects factory-style, and sold films to exhibitors through block booking and blind bidding.

The star system and the Code

Studios manufactured star personas, controlled publicity, and enforced morality clauses. From 1934 the industry self-censored under the Motion Picture Production Code, administered by Joseph Breen, shaping the content of classical Hollywood cinema. Independent producers such as Samuel Goldwyn, David O. Selznick, and Walt Disney released through the majors' distribution arms.

Decline

The Supreme Court's 1948 decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc. forced theatre divestiture and outlawed block booking. Stars such as James Stewart won profit-participation deals through powerful agencies like MCA; television shrank audiences; and by the 1960s the contract system had collapsed, replaced by the package-unit system in which each film is assembled independently.
