Rhynchocephalia

Rhynchocephalia is an order of lizard-like reptiles whose only living representative is the tuatara of New Zealand. Although superficially resembling lizards, tuatara belong to a distinct lineage that diverged from the ancestors of lizards and snakes more than 240 million years ago.

Class Reptilia
Living genus Sphenodon
Closest living relatives Squamata (lizards and snakes)
Former range Worldwide during the Mesozoic

Evolutionary history

Rhynchocephalians originated in the Triassic and diversified into many forms. Fossils include terrestrial insect-eaters, specialized herbivores and the marine pleurosaurs with elongated bodies adapted for swimming. The group declined as squamates diversified, though the reasons were probably complex rather than simple competitive replacement.

Tuatara anatomy

Tuatara possess a distinctive skull and dentition: teeth are fused to the jawbone rather than set in sockets, and a single row of lower teeth fits between two upper rows. They retain a prominent parietal or “third” eye beneath the skin of the top of the head, especially visible in juveniles. Their metabolism and activity are adapted to cooler temperatures than those preferred by most reptiles.

Conservation

Tuatara once lived on New Zealand’s main islands but were eliminated there largely by introduced predators. They survive naturally on offshore islands and have been reintroduced to protected sanctuaries. Their slow growth, late maturity and temperature-dependent sex determination make them vulnerable to predators and climate warming.

Taxonomy

Rhynchocephalia and Squamata together form Lepidosauria. Some classifications use Sphenodontia for a subgroup within Rhynchocephalia, and usage varies. The living species has also been debated: populations were long divided into Sphenodon punctatus and Sphenodon guntheri, though many modern treatments recognize one species with geographically distinct populations.
