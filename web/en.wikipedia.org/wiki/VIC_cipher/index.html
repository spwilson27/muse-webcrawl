VIC cipher

The VIC cipher was a complex pencil-and-paper cipher used by Soviet intelligence officers during the early Cold War. It is named after the Soviet spy Reino Häyhänen, codenamed "Victor" (VIC), who defected to the United States in 1957 and explained the cipher to the FBI. The cipher had been used to encipher a message concealed in a hollow nickel — the famous "Hollow Nickel Case" — recovered in Brooklyn in 1953, which resisted cryptanalysis for four years until Häyhänen revealed the method.

Structure

The VIC cipher is remarkable for its sophistication as a hand cipher. It combines several stages:

  • A straddling checkerboard that converts plaintext letters into digits, giving frequent letters single digits and others two digits.
  • A lagged Fibonacci generator seeded from memorised material (including a date, a phrase, and a personal number) that produces long sequences of pseudo-random digits used as keys.
  • Two transpositions, one of them disrupted (with irregular column lengths), whose keys are derived from the generated digit sequences rather than from keywords.

Because the transposition keys are pseudo-random and single-use, and the checkerboard output is further scrambled, the cipher offered security far beyond most hand systems, and its full procedure demonstrates how much of modern block cipher design — diffusion through transposition and confusion through substitution — can be implemented with pencil and paper.

History

The cipher was used by Häyhänen's network, which included the spy Rudolf Abel. The hollow nickel containing a microfilm message in VIC cipher was accidentally spent in 1953 and passed to a newspaper boy, who noticed its odd weight; the FBI could not read the message until Häyhänen defected in Paris in 1957 and reconstructed the system, leading to Abel's arrest and conviction.
