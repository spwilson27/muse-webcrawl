Croissant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Golden croissants on a bakery tray, one cut to show the layers]

A croissant is a French pastry made of a leavened, laminated dough — dough layered with butter, rolled and folded repeatedly (lamination) to produce a flaky, crescent-shaped, golden bread. It is a staple of the French Breakfast and of cafés worldwide, eaten plain or filled with chocolate (pain au chocolat), almond cream, ham and cheese.

The croissant descends from the Austrian kipferl, a crescent roll documented for centuries; the laminated version was developed in Paris in the mid-19th century after August Zang opened a Viennese bakery there in 1838–39. The romantic story that it commemorates the 1683 siege of Vienna — or Marie Antoinette — is legend rather than documented history.

Contents
1. Production

Production

A yeasted dough (détrempe) is wrapped around a butter block (beurre de tourage), rolled and folded in "turns" to create dozens of layers — classically 27 — then shaped, proofed and baked to a crisp shell and honeycomb interior. Industrial versions are produced frozen worldwide.
