The common sunflower (Helianthus annuus) is a species of large annual forb in the daisy family Asteraceae, native to North America. It is widely cultivated worldwide as a crop for its edible oily seeds, for sunflower oil, as livestock forage, and as an ornamental plant. Wild plants are much-branched with many flower heads; cultivated forms usually bear a single very large head on a stout stem that can reach 3 metres or more in height.

Description

What appears to be a single flower is actually a capitulum: a broad central disc of many small disc florets surrounded by showy yellow ray florets. The seeds in the disc are arranged in striking interlocking spirals related to the Fibonacci sequence and the golden angle. Young flower heads exhibit heliotropism, tracking the sun across the sky, a behaviour that increases photosynthesis and seed yield; mature heads generally face east.

History and cultivation

The sunflower was domesticated by Indigenous peoples of North America several thousand years ago, and was taken to Europe by Spanish explorers in the sixteenth century. It became a major oilseed crop especially in Russia and later Ukraine, which with Russia have been among the world's largest producers. In the United States, the sunflower is the state flower of Kansas. Sunflowers have also been used for phytoremediation, absorbing heavy metals and radioactive contaminants from soil, including after the Chernobyl and Fukushima disasters.

Uses

Sunflower seeds are eaten as a snack, ground into sunflower butter, and pressed for a light vegetable oil rich in linoleic or oleic acid depending on variety. The seed cake remaining after oil extraction is used as animal feed, and the plant features prominently in art — most famously the Sunflowers series by Vincent van Gogh — and as a symbol of Ukraine.
