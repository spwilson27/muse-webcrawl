John Bull

First appearance: 1712, in John Arbuthnot's satires

National personification of: England / Great Britain / the United Kingdom

John Bull is a national personification of England, and sometimes of the whole United Kingdom, especially in political cartoons and similar graphic works. He is usually depicted as a portly, prosperous middle-aged man in a tailcoat, breeches and a top hat, often wearing a waistcoat patterned with the Union Flag and accompanied by a bulldog.

Origins

The character was created by the Scottish satirist John Arbuthnot in his 1712 pamphlet series The History of John Bull, part of the satire later published as part of Law Is a Bottomless Pit, which mocked Britain's involvement in the War of the Spanish Succession. Arbuthnot's John Bull was an honest, plain-spoken cloth merchant, quick-tempered and fond of litigation.

Development

During the Napoleonic Wars and the nineteenth century, cartoonists developed John Bull into the familiar figure of the sturdy English everyman. He was used extensively in British recruiting and propaganda during both World Wars, and remains a recognisable symbol, comparable to America's Uncle Sam or France's Marianne, though his use declined in the later twentieth century.
