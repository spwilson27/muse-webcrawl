Roman numeral analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In music theory, Roman numeral analysis is the use of Roman numerals to represent chords within the context of a musical key. The Roman numeral denotes the scale degree of the chord's root, while case, symbols, and added figures indicate the chord's quality and any extensions or inversions. The system allows musicians to describe harmonic progressions abstractly, independent of the particular key in which they occur.

Notation

In a major key, the diatonic triads are labelled I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, and vii°, where uppercase numerals indicate major chords, lowercase numerals indicate minor chords, and the degree symbol (°) indicates a diminished chord. In minor keys the tonic chord is i, and chords borrowed from the parallel major are commonly shown through accidentals, such as ♭VI or the raised leading-tone V.

Additional symbols describe chord structure:

  • Figured bass figures (e.g. 6, 4/3, 2) indicate inversions, following figures used in figured bass.
  • Seventh chords add a superscript 7; half-diminished chords use ø7.
  • Secondary functions are written as fractions, such as V/V ("five of five"), indicating a chord tonicizing another scale degree.
  • Neapolitan and augmented sixth chords have conventional labels (N, It+6, Fr+6, Ger+6).

Function

Roman numeral analysis groups chords into the harmonic functions of tonic, subdominant (or predominant), and dominant. Analysts use it to compare progressions across works, describe cadences (such as the perfect authentic cadence V–I), and teach common-practice harmony. A famous progression, the descending-fifths sequence, can be written I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V.

History

The practice developed during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries from thoroughbass traditions and the theories of Jean-Philippe Rameau, who identified chord roots and fundamental bass lines. Hugo Riemann's function theory in Germany offered a related system using T, S, and D symbols, and Roman numeral analysis became standard in Anglophone harmony pedagogy during the twentieth century.
