The Ottoman Turkish alphabet (Ottoman Turkish: الفبا, elifbâ) was the version of the Perso-Arabic script used to write Ottoman Turkish from the early centuries of the Ottoman Empire until 1928. Written from right to left, it comprised some 35 letters for the literary language, heavily supplemented by Arabic and Persian vocabulary and conventions.

Characteristics

The script was an imperfect match for Turkish. Arabic's writing system distinguishes three short vowels only by optional diacritics and represents a set of consonants shaped to Semitic phonology; Ottoman Turkish, by contrast, has eight vowels and vowel harmony. Several Arabic letters therefore represented the same Turkish sound — for example, multiple letters for /s/, /z/, /t/, and /h/ were used according to the Arabic spelling of loanwords — while single letters had to do duty for several Turkish vowels (و for o, ö, u, ü, and v) and for consonant pairs such as /k/, /g/, /ğ/, and /n/ represented by ك. The letter ڭ (kef-i nûnî) could denote the velar nasal /ŋ/ of older Turkish. Additional Persian letters (پ pe, چ çe, ژ je, گ gef) served sounds absent from Arabic.

Because of these mismatches, readers relied heavily on context, and the script was widely blamed in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries for Turkey's low literacy. Reformers debated amendments, including proposals to write vowels fully, to print letters unjoined (Enver Pasha's wartime "separated script" for military correspondence), and, finally, wholesale Latinisation on the model of Azerbaijan.

Abolition

On 1 November 1928, the Grand National Assembly of Turkey adopted Law No. 1353 introducing a new Latin-based Turkish alphabet designed by a language commission under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk. From 1 January 1929 the Arabic-based alphabet was banned from official and public use, and the Nation's Schools (Millet Mektepleri) campaigned to teach the new script to adults. The old alphabet survives in scholarly editions, in Ottoman archival studies, and in religious and calligraphic contexts.
