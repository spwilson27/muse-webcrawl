The mole, symbol mol, is the SI unit for amount of substance. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076×10²³ elementary entities — the Avogadro constant — whether atoms, molecules, ions or electrons. This definition, fixed exactly, has applied since the 2019 revision of the SI.

The mole lets chemists count particles by weighing: one mole of a substance has a mass in grams numerically equal to its relative formula mass (its molar mass). A mole of carbon-12 atoms weighs 12 g and a mole of water about 18 g; a mole of any ideal gas occupies about 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure.

History

The concept grew from Avogadro's hypothesis (1811) that equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules. The constant is named for Avogadro, though he never measured it; Jean Perrin's experiments on Brownian motion (c. 1908) gave good values and confirmed the reality of atoms.

Calculations

Amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol). Moles link laboratory measurements to balanced equations, and stoichiometry is built on mole ratios.
