Cathars

The Cathars (from Greek καθαροί katharoi, "the pure ones") were adherents of a Christian dualist or Gnostic movement that flourished in parts of Europe between the 12th and 14th centuries, particularly in the Languedoc region of southern France and in northern Italy. Calling themselves "Good Christians", they were condemned as heretics by the Roman Catholic Church and were destroyed in the Albigensian Crusade and the medieval Inquisition.

Beliefs and organisation

Cathar theology was dualist: the material world was regarded as the creation of an evil power — identified with Satan or the God of the Old Testament — while the good God of the New Testament was purely spiritual. They rejected the Catholic sacraments, the Incarnation in a material body, and the veneration of the cross. Their central rite was the consolamentum, a spiritual baptism by laying on of hands that made a believer into one of the perfecti ("Perfects"), an ascetic elite who abstained from meat, sex, and property and prayed the Lord's Prayer. Ordinary adherents, the credentes ("believers"), supported the Perfects and often received the consolamentum only on their deathbeds.

Persecution and destruction

After appeals and missions (including those of St Dominic) failed to uproot the movement, Pope Innocent III proclaimed the Albigensian Crusade in 1209. It opened with the massacre of Béziers, whose population was slaughtered, and degenerated into a war of conquest that brought Languedoc under the French crown by the Treaty of Paris (1229). The Dominican-led Inquisition then hunted the remaining Cathars; the last great refuge, Montségur, fell in 1244 and over 200 Perfects were burned. Underground remnants survived into the early 14th century — the revival led by Pierre Authié was betrayed by the informer Arnaud Sicre, and the last known Occitan Perfect, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321.

Legacy

Catharism remains a potent theme in the regional identity of the modern French south, promoted in tourism around the "Cathar castles", and is a staple subject of popular history and fiction, though historians debate how coherent and organised the "Cathar Church" described by its inquisitors really was.
