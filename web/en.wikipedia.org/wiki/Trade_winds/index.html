Trade winds

The trade winds are the steady prevailing winds that blow toward the equator from the northeast in the Northern Hemisphere and from the southeast in the Southern Hemisphere. They form the surface branch of the tropical Hadley cell circulation: air rises near the equator in the Intertropical Convergence Zone (the doldrums), flows poleward aloft, descends in the subtropical highs around 30° latitude, and returns equatorward at the surface, where the Coriolis effect deflects it westward. The trades are among the most reliable winds on Earth and blow over roughly half the planet's surface, mainly over the oceans.

History

Portuguese mariners recognised the pattern of the Atlantic trades in the 15th century during their voyages down the African coast. The winds were vital to sailing routes in the Age of Sail: Europeans crossed the Atlantic to the Americas riding the northeast trades and returned in the mid-latitude westerlies, and clipper routes and the Manila galleons depended on them. The term derives from the early modern English sense of "trade" as a regular track or course.

Effects

The trade winds drive westward ocean currents and tropical weather. Where they converge near the equator, air rises to form towering cumulus and heavy rain; where they blow over land they can produce deserts on western continental margins. The trades steer tropical cyclones westward, and their strength in the Pacific is linked to the El Niño–Southern Oscillation: weakening trades precede El Niño events, while strengthened trades characterise La Niña.
