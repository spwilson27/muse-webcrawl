Physical fitness is a state of health and well-being and, more specifically, the ability to perform aspects of sports, occupations, and daily activities. It is generally achieved through proper nutrition, moderate-to-vigorous physical exercise, and sufficient rest, and it is regarded as a measure of the body's ability to function efficiently and effectively without undue fatigue.

Components

Health-related components are cardiorespiratory endurance (the efficiency of heart, lungs, and blood vessels, often indexed by VO2 max), muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, and body composition. Skill-related components — agility, balance, coordination, power, reaction time, and speed — matter more for athletic performance.

Effects of exercise

Regular aerobic and resistance training lowers the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, several cancers, depression, and all-cause mortality; strengthens bone and muscle; and improves sleep, cognition, and immune function. Public-health authorities such as the World Health Organization recommend that adults accumulate 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity (or 75–150 minutes of vigorous) aerobic activity per week, plus muscle-strengthening exercise on two or more days. Large cohort studies find benefit even below these thresholds, with the steepest gains in moving from inactivity to modest activity.

History

Concern with fitness is ancient — from Greek gymnastics to 19th-century physical culture movements — but modern fitness science grew from military needs in the world wars and from post-war alarm at declining youth fitness, leading to national fitness tests, the aerobics movement of the 1970s, and today's quantified, wearable-tracked exercise culture.
