Sous-vide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sous-vide (French for "under vacuum"; pronounced soo-VEED), also known as low-temperature, long-time (LTLT) cooking, is a method of cooking in which food is sealed in an airtight container — usually a vacuum-sealed plastic pouch — and placed in a water bath held at a precisely regulated temperature, typically lower than that used for conventional cooking, for an extended period.

Method

The water bath is maintained by an immersion circulator or similar device to within a fraction of a degree. Because the food cooks at exactly the target serving temperature, it cannot overcook beyond that temperature, and the entire piece reaches uniform doneness from edge to centre. Long cooking times tenderise tough cuts by converting collagen to gelatin while retaining moisture. Meats are often seared briefly before or after the bath to develop a browned crust, which the water bath itself cannot produce.

History

The technique was described by Sir Benjamin Thompson in 1799, but was developed in its modern form in the 1970s by French chef Georges Pralus at the restaurant Troisgros and by food scientist Bruno Goussault, who studied its effects on different foods. It became a signature method of avant-garde restaurants such as elBulli and The French Laundry, and spread to home kitchens in the 2010s with affordable immersion circulators and popular books such as Modernist Cuisine.

Safety

Precise temperature control is also a food-safety matter: cooking at low temperatures for long times can allow anaerobic pathogens such as Clostridium botulinum to grow if food is held too long in the "danger zone", so established time–temperature tables for pasteurisation should be followed, and vacuum-packed cooked foods should be chilled and stored properly.
