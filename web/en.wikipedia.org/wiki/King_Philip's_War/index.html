King Philip's War

King Philip's War (sometimes called the First Indian War, Metacom's War, Metacomet's War, Pometacomet's Rebellion, or Metacom's Rebellion) was an armed conflict in 1675–1678 between a group of indigenous peoples of the Northeastern Woodlands against the United Colonies (the English colonies in New England) and their indigenous allies. The war is named for Metacom (or Metacomet), the Wampanoag chief who adopted the name Philip because of the friendly relations between his father Massasoit and the Mayflower Pilgrims. The war continued in the most northern reaches of New England until the Treaty of Casco Bay was signed in April 1678.

Historical background

For more than half a century, the Wampanoag had maintained an uneasy peace with the colonists of Plymouth Colony through Massasoit's alliance. By the 1670s, however, relentless English expansion into native lands, the imposition of colonial law on tribal matters, and pressure to convert to Christianity had eroded the relationship. The immediate spark came in June 1675, when Plymouth Colony executed three Wampanoag men for the murder of John Sassamon, a Christian Native American who had informed the colony of Metacom's preparations for war.

Course of the war

Metacom's warriors struck first in late June 1675, raiding the border town of Swansea. The conflict rapidly expanded across Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, and Maine, drawing in the Narragansett, Nipmuck, and other peoples. In September 1675 colonial forces suffered a series of defeats, including the Battle of Bloody Brook. In December 1675 the United Colonies attacked the Narragansett's fortified village in the Great Swamp Fight, killing hundreds and driving the Narragansett fully into the war. Through the winter and spring of 1676, Native forces raided towns within 20 miles of Boston, but dwindling food and ammunition weakened them. Metacom was killed on August 12, 1676, in the Miery Swamp near Mount Hope, shot by John Alderman, a Native American soldier in Benjamin Church's company. His body was drawn and quartered, and his head was displayed at Plymouth for decades.

Aftermath and significance

The war was one of the bloodiest relative to population in American history: a significant percentage of the English colonial population was killed, and the Native population of southern New England was devastated, with thousands killed and many more sold into slavery in the West Indies. Twelve of the region's ninety towns were destroyed. The war shattered Native power in southern New England and opened vast areas to English settlement, while the colonists' victory came at enormous economic and human cost, paid off in debt over the following decades.
