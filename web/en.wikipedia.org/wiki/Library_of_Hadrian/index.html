The Library of Hadrian was a large library and cultural complex in ancient Athens, Greece, created by the Roman emperor Hadrian in about AD 132. Its ruins lie on the north side of the Acropolis, near the Roman Agora and the modern Monastiraki Square.

Despite its conventional name, the building was more than a library: following the model of the fora of Rome, it comprised a large rectangular courtyard surrounded by colonnades, with gardens and pools, lecture halls, reading rooms, and rooms for the storage of books. The traveler Pausanias described it as one of Hadrian's most splendid gifts to Athens, mentioning its hundred columns of Phrygian marble, gilded ceilings, and alabaster decoration.

History

The complex measured roughly 122 by 82 metres, enclosed by a wall of limestone with a propylon entrance on its west side in the Corinthian order. The library proper stood on the eastern side, where niches in the walls held wooden cupboards (armaria) for scrolls; the collection is thought to have held around 20,000 scrolls.

The building was badly damaged during the Herulian invasion of AD 267 and was repaired in the early 5th century by the prefect Herculinus. In the Byzantine period three churches were built within its ruins, the remains of which survive. During the Ottoman period the courtyard served as the seat of the Voivode of Athens and later as barracks and a marketplace area.

Excavations

Systematic excavations began in the 19th century and have continued under the Greek Archaeological Service. The most visible remains today are the west facade with its row of Corinthian columns and part of the north wall. The site is open to visitors and stands beside the Monastiraki metro station in central Athens.
