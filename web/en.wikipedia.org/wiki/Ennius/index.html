Ennius

Quintus Ennius (239 – c. 169 BC) was a writer and poet who lived during the Roman Republic. He is often considered the father of Roman poetry. He was born in the Messapian city of Rudiae, in what is now southern Italy, spoke Greek, Oscan and Latin — boasting that he had "three hearts" — and was brought to Rome by Cato the Elder.

Life

Ennius served as a soldier in Sardinia, taught Greek and Latin in Rome, and accompanied Marcus Fulvius Nobilior on campaign in Aetolia, gaining Roman citizenship in 184 BC. He lived frugally on the Aventine Hill and was honoured after death with a statue on the tomb of the Scipios, tradition held.

Works

His masterpiece was the Annales, an epic poem in 18 books covering Roman history from Aeneas to his own day — the first great Latin epic and the first to use Greek dactylic hexameter, which became the standard metre of Latin epic for Virgil and others. He also wrote tragedies adapting Euripides, comedies, satires (the genre he effectively founded in Latin, the Saturae), and the philosophical poem Epicharmus. Only fragments, around 600 lines of the Annales, survive, quoted by later authors.

Influence

Ennius shaped Latin poetic diction profoundly. Lucretius and Virgil drew on him extensively — the Aeneid echoes Ennian lines — even as Cicero preserved many fragments by quotation. His famous line "Moribus antiquis res stat Romana virisque" ("On ancient customs and men the Roman state stands") became proverbial.
