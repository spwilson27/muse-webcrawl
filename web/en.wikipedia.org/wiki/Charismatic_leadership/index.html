Charismatic authority

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Charismatic authority is a concept developed by the sociologist Max Weber in Economy and Society (1922). It is one of Weber's three forms of authority or legitimate domination, alongside traditional authority (resting on custom) and rational-legal authority (resting on rules and offices). Charismatic authority rests on devotion to the exceptional character of an individual leader — their heroism, sanctity or exemplary qualities — as perceived by their followers.

Charisma, for Weber, exists only in the recognition of followers and must be continually proved by success; it is inherently unstable and hostile to routine administration and economic regularity. After the leader's death or failure it typically undergoes “routinisation” — transformation into traditional or bureaucratic forms, through designation, heredity or the charisma of office. The concept has been applied to religious founders, revolutionary leaders and modern politicians, and borrowed (often loosely, to mean personal charm or media appeal) by management literature as “charismatic leadership”.

Analysis

Weber treated charisma as the great revolutionary force in traditional societies, breaking the hold of custom. Political scientists caution that attributions of charisma are retrospective and relational — a product of crisis, followers' needs and media framing as much as of the leader's gifts — and distinguish personal charisma from the institutional charisma of offices such as the papacy or presidency.
