Crossbreed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A crossbreed is an organism with purebred parents of two different breeds, varieties, or populations. Crossbreeding, sometimes called "designer crossbreeding", is the process of breeding such an organism, often with the intention to create offspring that share the traits of both parents, or producing an organism with hybrid vigor.

In animal breeding, crossbreds are crosses between two purebred individuals of different breeds, usually registered with a breed association but sometimes with a foundation registry. In some cases, crossbreeding is used deliberately in plant breeding to produce new varieties combining desirable characteristics from both parent lines.

Designer crosses

A designer crossbreed or designer breed is a crossbred animal that has purebred parents, usually registered with a breed registry, bred in order to combine positive characteristics of the two parents. The practice is most common in dogs; well-known examples include the Labradoodle (Labrador Retriever × Poodle), the Cockapoo (Cocker Spaniel × Poodle), and the Goldendoodle. Such crosses are sometimes given portmanteau names.

Advantages

Crossbreeding is often practiced to introduce genetic diversity, reduce the incidence of inherited disorders common in closed purebred populations, and exploit heterosis (hybrid vigor), in which the offspring outperform either parent in growth, fertility, or hardiness. In livestock production, systematic crossing schemes are widely used in cattle, pigs, sheep, and poultry.

Criticism

Some registries and breeders object to crossbreeding because offspring traits are less predictable than those of purebreds, and because unplanned crosses can produce animals that are difficult to place. Deliberate hybridisation in plants and animals must also be distinguished from hybrids between different species.
