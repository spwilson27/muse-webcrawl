Siege of Vicksburg

The siege of Vicksburg (May 18 – July 4, 1863) was the final major military action of the Vicksburg campaign of the American Civil War. Union Major General Ulysses S. Grant's Army of the Tennessee drove Confederate Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton's army into the fortified city of Vicksburg, Mississippi, on the Mississippi River, besieged it for forty-seven days, and compelled its surrender on July 4, 1863. Together with the fall of Port Hudson days later, the victory gave the Union control of the Mississippi River and split the Confederacy in two; it is widely regarded, with Gettysburg — fought the same week — as a turning point of the war.

Background

Vicksburg, perched on high bluffs above a hairpin bend of the Mississippi, was the key to the river — "the Gibraltar of the Confederacy." Grant's brilliant spring 1863 campaign crossed the river below the city, won battles at Port Gibson, Raymond, Jackson, Champion Hill, and the Big Black River, and trapped Pemberton inside the Vicksburg lines by mid-May.

The siege

Direct assaults on May 19 and May 22 were repulsed with heavy loss, after which Grant settled into a formal siege: thirteen approach trenches crept toward the Confederate works, supported by constant artillery and naval bombardment from Admiral Porter's gunboats. Miners detonated huge charges under the 3rd Louisiana Redan on June 25 and July 1, though the breaches gained little. Inside the city, soldiers and civilians endured shrinking rations — mule meat and pea bread — disease, and life in caves dug into the loess hillsides to escape the shelling. Outside, General Joseph E. Johnston gathered a relief force at Jackson but never attacked Grant's covering army under Sherman.

Surrender and consequences

With his men starving and no relief coming, Pemberton surrendered on July 4, 1863; nearly 29,500 Confederate soldiers laid down their arms and were paroled. Union casualties in the siege were about 4,800 against some 3,200 Confederate battle casualties, plus the army lost as prisoners. President Lincoln wrote that "the Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea." The twin July victories at Gettysburg and Vicksburg marked the strategic turning point of the war, and Grant's success propelled him to command of all Union armies in 1864. Bitterness over the surrender date meant Vicksburg famously declined to celebrate the Fourth of July for generations.
