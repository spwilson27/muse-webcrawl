In Japanese history, the Sesshō (摂政) was a regent who ruled on behalf of a child, female, or otherwise incapacitated emperor, while the Kampaku (関白) was the chief advisor and de facto regent for an adult emperor. Together the two titles — collectively Sekkan (摂関) — represented the apex of court power in the Heian period and beyond.

Fujiwara dominance

From the ninth to the eleventh century the northern house of the Fujiwara clan monopolized the regency through the system known as Sekkan seiji ("regency government"). Fujiwara regents married their daughters to emperors and ruled as maternal grandfathers or fathers-in-law of the sovereign. Fujiwara no Yoshifusa became the first non-imperial sesshō in 858, and Fujiwara no Michinaga (966–1027) brought the system to its height.

Later history

The rise of rule by retired emperors (insei) weakened the regents in the late eleventh century, but the titles survived. In later centuries the kampaku was drawn from the five regent houses (go-sekke). Toyotomi Hideyoshi, a commoner by birth, famously obtained the title of kampaku in 1585 to legitimize his rule. The offices were abolished in the Meiji Restoration of 1868, though a form of the sesshō was revived in the Imperial House Law for regencies during a sovereign's minority — most recently exercised by Crown Prince Hirohito for the Taishō Emperor from 1921 to 1926.
