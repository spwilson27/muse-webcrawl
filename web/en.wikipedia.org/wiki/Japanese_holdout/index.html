Japanese holdout

Japanese holdouts (Japanese: 残留日本兵, zanryū nipponhei, "remaining Japanese soldiers") were soldiers of the Imperial Japanese Army and Imperial Japanese Navy during the Pacific Theatre of World War II who continued fighting after the war's end in August–September 1945. Some did not believe the war was over, some had never received word of the surrender, and others hid from Allied forces out of duty or fear. Holdouts were discovered across the Pacific islands and Southeast Asia for decades; the last widely publicised cases emerged in 1974.

Notable holdouts

  • Hiroo Onoda — an intelligence officer on Lubang Island in the Philippines who had orders never to surrender. He was found in 1974, when his former commanding officer was flown in to relieve him of duty formally. His companions had been killed in skirmishes over the years; his memoir is titled No Surrender: My Thirty-Year War.
  • Shoichi Yokoi — a sergeant discovered by hunters on Guam in January 1972, where he had lived in a dugout for 27 years, mostly alone after his last companions died. On his return to Japan he famously said, "It is with much embarrassment that I return."
  • Teruo Nakamura — a Taiwan-born volunteer found on Morotai Island, Indonesia, in December 1974, generally regarded as the last holdout to be repatriated.
  • Yūichi Akatsu — a lance corporal who hid on Lubang from 1944 until surrendering in 1950.
  • Group holdouts — parties of soldiers were found on islands such as Anatahan (1951) and Mindoro in the early post-war years; some incidents involved holdouts attacking local inhabitants.

Reasons and circumstances

Imperial Japanese military indoctrination treated surrender as dishonour worse than death, and orders were given never to surrender or commit suicide. Wartime leaflets announcing Japan's surrender were often dismissed by holdouts as enemy propaganda, and isolated garrisons on remote islands had no reliable communication. Some later reports — including claims of pairs of aged soldiers in the Philippines in 2005 — were investigated but never verified.

In popular culture

The holdouts became enduring symbols of duty and the costs of war. Onoda's story inspired books, films and documentaries, and echoes appear in episodes of television series such as Gilligan's Island and The Six Million Dollar Man, as well as in Werner Herzog's 2021 novel The Twilight World.
