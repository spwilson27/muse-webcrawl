The Okefenokee National Wildlife Refuge is a 402,000-acre (1,627 km²) National Wildlife Refuge located in Charlton, Ware, and Clinch Counties in Georgia, and Baker County in Florida, United States. The refuge is administered from offices in Folkston, Georgia. It protects the greater part of the Okefenokee Swamp, one of the largest intact freshwater wetlands in the United States, and was established in 1937 by President Franklin D. Roosevelt to preserve the swamp's wildlife and habitat.

Ecology

The refuge encompasses cypress forests, pine islands, wet prairies and shrub swamps fed almost entirely by rainfall. It is home to an estimated 12,000 American alligators, Florida black bears, bobcats, river otters, white-tailed deer, and more than 200 species of birds, including the endangered red-cockaded woodpecker in its longleaf pine uplands. The refuge's dark, tannin-stained "blackwater" channels drain into the Suwannee and St. Marys rivers.

Protection and threats

Much of the refuge is designated wilderness. Major fires, notably the Honey Prairie fire of 2011 and the West Mims fire of 2017 — which began on refuge land and burned some 152,000 acres — are a natural and recurring part of the ecosystem. Conservation groups have contested proposals to mine titanium-bearing minerals at Trail Ridge, just east of the swamp, arguing that mining could lower the water table that keeps the swamp wet. The refuge, together with the swamp, has also been discussed as a potential UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Visitor use

Roughly 400,000 people a year enter through the main eastern entrance near Folkston (with the Suwannee Canal Recreation Area), the northern Stephen C. Foster State Park entrance, and the western Okefenokee Swamp Park entrance. Activities centre on boating and paddling along marked canoe trails, boardwalks, wildlife observation and fishing.
