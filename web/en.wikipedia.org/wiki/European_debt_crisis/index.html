PeriodLate 2009 – mid-2010s

The European debt crisis, often called the eurozone crisis, was a crisis of sovereign debt and banking that struck several member states of the euro area from late 2009. Countries that had borrowed cheaply after adopting the euro — above all Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Cyprus — found themselves unable to refinance their debts once the global financial crisis exposed weak public finances and banking systems. Without national currencies to devalue or central banks able to act as lenders of last resort, governments faced soaring bond yields and turned to rescue loans from their eurozone partners, the European Central Bank and the International Monetary Fund (the "Troika").

Course of the crisis

The crisis began in Greece, where a newly elected government revealed in October 2009 that the budget deficit was far larger than previously reported — eventually revised to 15.4 percent of GDP. Credit rating downgrades pushed Greek borrowing costs beyond reach, and in May 2010 the eurozone and IMF agreed a €110 billion rescue alongside the creation of the temporary European Financial Stability Facility (EFSF). Ireland followed in November 2010 after guaranteeing its collapsing banks, and Portugal in May 2011. In 2012 Greece underwent the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history (the "private sector involvement" haircut), Spain received a bank recapitalisation programme of up to €100 billion, and Cyprus in 2013 became the first case in which uninsured depositors were bailed in. A permanent rescue fund, the European Stability Mechanism, began operating in October 2012.

Resolution and legacy

The decisive moment came on 26 July 2012, when ECB President Mario Draghi pledged to do "whatever it takes to preserve the euro", followed by the Outright Monetary Transactions programme, which calmed bond markets without ever being used. Bailout programmes were accompanied by austerity policies — tax rises, spending cuts and structural reforms — that deepened recessions in the affected countries; Greek unemployment exceeded 27 percent and youth unemployment rose far higher, fuelling political upheaval. Greece required a third bailout in August 2015 after a referendum and a near-exit from the euro ("Grexit"). The crisis also drove institutional reform: the European banking union, with ECB supervision of major banks from 2014, and tighter fiscal rules under the Fiscal Compact. Although acute market stress subsided by the mid-2010s, high public debt, especially in Greece and Italy, remained a lasting legacy.
