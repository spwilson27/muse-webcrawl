The Guinness family is an extensive Anglo-Irish family noted for its achievements in brewing, banking, politics and philanthropy, and for its creation of the Guinness stout empire. Founded by Arthur Guinness, who in 1759 took a 9,000-year lease on the St James's Gate brewery in Dublin, the family rose from the Irish Protestant professional class to become one of the wealthiest and most prominent dynasties in Ireland and Britain.

Branches

The family is conventionally divided into branches. The brewing line descends through Arthur's son Arthur II and includes Benjamin Lee Guinness, the first Guinness to sit as Lord Mayor of Dublin and restorer of St Patrick's Cathedral at his own expense; his son Edward Guinness, created Earl of Iveagh, became one of the richest men in the British Isles and a great philanthropist, funding the Iveagh Trust housing and (with his brother Arthur) the Guinness Trust in London. A banking line descends from Samuel Guinness, a Dublin goldsmith, and produced the Barons Moyne, including the politician Walter Guinness, 1st Baron Moyne, assassinated in Cairo in 1944, and the writer and socialite Bryan Guinness, whose wife Diana Mitford later married Oswald Mosley. A third group, descended from Arthur's brother Samuel, is nicknamed the "Guinness clergy" for its many Anglican clergymen.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Under the family the brewery became the largest in the world, was floated on the London Stock Exchange in 1886, and the family dominated its board for generations; Rupert Guinness, 2nd Earl of Iveagh, chaired the company for over three decades. Family members entered both Houses of Parliament, served in government, and intermarried with the British and Irish aristocracy; later generations included the socialites and "Bright Young Things" of the 1920s–30s, the racing driver and writer Loel Guinness, and the heiress-turned-activist figures of the modern era. The brewing interest merged into Grand Metropolitan and then Diageo in 1997, ending direct family control, though descendants remain prominent in Irish public life, agriculture and conservation.
