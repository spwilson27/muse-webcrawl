Figured bass is musical notation in which numerals and symbols (often accidentals) are added beneath a bass line to indicate the intervals, chords, and non-chord tones that a keyboard or other chord-playing musician should improvise above it. It was the standard shorthand of the basso continuo in Baroque music (roughly 1600–1750) and remained in use into the Classical era.

How it works

The figures give intervals above the bass note. A bare note implies a root-position triad (5/3, usually unwritten); "6" indicates a first-inversion triad (6/3); "6/4" a second-inversion triad; "7" a seventh chord; and combinations such as "6/5", "4/3", and "4/2" specify inversions of seventh chords. Sharps, flats, and naturals in the figures alter the note a third above the bass (or the interval they precede), and a slash through a figure raises that note a semitone. A "tasto solo" marking cancels the chords altogether. The figures say nothing about octave, spacing, doubling, or rhythm: realising them stylishly — choosing voicings, adding ornaments and melodic filler — was an improvisatory art expected of every continuo player.

Practice and decline

The system arose around 1600 with the new monodic style and composers such as Giulio Caccini and Lodovico Viadana, whose Cento concerti ecclesiastici (1602) provided an early published organ continuo part. Treatises by Agazzari, Praetorius, and later C. P. E. Bach codified its practice. As composers of the later 18th century wrote out parts in full, figured bass faded, surviving longest in church music, opera recitative, and pedagogy. Its harmonic logic underlies the Roman-numeral analysis and chord-symbol shorthand (such as jazz lead-sheet symbols) used today, and historically informed performers still realise continuo parts in Baroque performance.
