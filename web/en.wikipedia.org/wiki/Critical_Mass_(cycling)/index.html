Critical Mass (cycling)

Critical Mass is a form of direct action in which people meet at a set location and time and travel as a group through their neighbourhoods on bicycles. The idea is for people to group together to make it safe for each other to ride bicycles through their streets, based on the old adage: there's safety in numbers.

History

Critical Mass rides began in San Francisco in 1992, initially called "Commute Clot", and spread to hundreds of cities worldwide. The name comes from Ted White's 1992 documentary Return of the Scorcher, which described how cyclists in China negotiate unsignalled intersections by waiting until enough riders accumulate to push through as a group — a critical mass. Rides typically take place on the last Friday of each month, are decentralised and leaderless, and are often described by participants not as protests but as "coincidence" or celebrations of cycling.

Criticism and conflicts

Critical Mass rides have been controversial because a large group of cyclists can block traffic, and some rides involve deliberately obstructing streets. Disputes with motorists and police have led to arrests and clashes in several cities, including a widely publicised confrontation in New York City during the 2004 Republican National Convention period. Supporters argue the rides are no more disruptive than car traffic and highlight the vulnerability of cyclists and the need for better cycling infrastructure.
