Video editing is the post-production process of selecting, arranging, and modifying video shots to create a finished work — a film, television programme, advertisement, or online video. An editor works with raw footage to choose the best takes, establish pace and structure, and combine picture with dialogue, music, sound effects, titles, and visual effects.

History

Early editing was physical: film editors cut and spliced strips of film. For videotape, linear editing copied shots in sequence from one tape to another using edit controllers — a slow process in which changing an early shot meant redoing everything after it. The advent of computer-based non-linear editing (NLE) systems, pioneered by systems such as the CMX 600 in the 1970s and made practical with Avid Media Composer (1989), Adobe Premiere (1991), and Final Cut Pro (1999), allowed random access to footage and instant rearrangement without degrading the source. Today, editing is done almost entirely in software, increasingly on consumer devices and in the cloud.

Process

  • Logging and assembly — reviewing footage, selecting takes, and building a rough assembly in story order.
  • Rough cut — establishing structure and pacing without fine detail.
  • Fine cut / picture lock — frame-accurate trimming of edits, transitions (cuts, dissolves, wipes), and pacing.
  • Sound editing and mixing — dialogue cleanup, music, effects, and levels.
  • Colour correction and grading — balancing shots and establishing a look.
  • Titles, graphics, and mastering — adding credits and exporting delivery formats.

Techniques and concepts

Editors use techniques such as cutting on action, the J and L cuts (where audio leads or trails the picture), match cuts, montage, and cross-cutting to shape continuity and meaning. The Kuleshov effect — the way context changes the perceived meaning of a shot — is a foundational principle. Editing craft is recognized professionally, including the Academy Award for Best Film Editing.
