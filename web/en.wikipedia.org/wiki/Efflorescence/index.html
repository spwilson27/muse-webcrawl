Efflorescence

Efflorescence is the migration of a salt to the surface of a porous material, where it forms a coating. The essential process involves a salt solution being wicked into the material, transported toward the surface, and deposited as water evaporates, leaving a whitish, powdery or crystalline residue.

On buildings

On brick, concrete and stone, efflorescence is usually cosmetic, appearing as white deposits of salts such as calcium carbonate or sodium sulfate drawn from within the material or the soil. Persistent efflorescence can indicate excess moisture, and heavier salt crystallisation beneath the surface (subflorescence) can cause spalling and damage.

In chemistry

In chemistry, the term also describes the loss of water of crystallisation from a hydrated salt when exposed to air, as with washing soda (sodium carbonate decahydrate) becoming powdery on standing.

Prevention and removal

Prevention focuses on reducing water ingress through good detailing, damp-proofing and low-salt materials; deposits can be removed by dry brushing and washing, sometimes with dilute acid cleaners.
