Simla Convention

Signed3 July 1914
LocationSimla, British India
PartiesGreat Britain, Tibet; China initialled but did not ratify
SubjectStatus of Tibet; boundaries

The Simla Convention, also known as the Simla Accord, was a treaty concerning the status of Tibet negotiated at Simla (now Shimla) in 1913–1914 by representatives of Great Britain, Tibet and the Republic of China. The convention was signed by Britain and Tibet on 3 July 1914; the Chinese plenipotentiary initialled the draft but his government repudiated the initialling and refused to sign the final agreement.

Terms

The convention divided Tibet into "Outer Tibet" and "Inner Tibet" on the model of the earlier settlement in Mongolia. China was to recognise Outer Tibet's autonomy under Chinese suzerainty, to abstain from interfering in its administration, and not to convert Tibet into a Chinese province. Attached maps delimited the boundary between Tibet and China and, in a separate bilateral exchange between Britain and Tibet, the boundary between Tibet and British India along the Himalayan crest — the McMahon Line, named after Britain's foreign secretary and plenipotentiary Sir Henry McMahon.

Aftermath and legacy

Because China never ratified the convention, its legal status has remained contested. China regards the convention and the McMahon Line as invalid, products of an era when Tibet, in Beijing's view, had no authority to conclude treaties. India, by contrast, treats the McMahon Line as the lawful boundary in the eastern Himalayas. The disagreement over the line's eastern sector, where China claims the territory it calls South Tibet (administered by India as Arunachal Pradesh), was a principal cause of the Sino-Indian War of 1962 and remains unresolved in the ongoing boundary dispute between the two countries.
