Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) is a legally non-binding resolution passed by the United Nations General Assembly on 13 September 2007. It sets out the individual and collective rights of Indigenous peoples, including their rights to culture, identity, language, employment, health, education, and self-determination, and establishes a universal framework of minimum standards for their survival, dignity, and well-being.

History

The declaration was the product of more than two decades of negotiation. Work began in 1982 in the UN Working Group on Indigenous Populations, producing a draft by 1993, and negotiations between Indigenous representatives and states continued at the Human Rights Council, which adopted the text in 2006. When the General Assembly finally voted in 2007, 143 states voted in favour, 11 abstained, and four — Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the United States — voted against, though all four later reversed their positions and endorsed the declaration.

Content

The declaration contains 46 articles. Among its key provisions:

  • Article 3 affirms Indigenous peoples' right to self-determination.
  • Articles 11 and 28 address redress for lands, territories, and cultural property taken without consent.
  • Article 19 requires states to consult and cooperate in good faith to obtain free, prior and informed consent before adopting measures that may affect Indigenous peoples; similar consent provisions appear in Articles 29 and 32 regarding hazardous materials and development projects.
  • Article 26 affirms rights to the lands, territories, and resources traditionally owned or occupied.
  • Article 46 safeguards the territorial integrity and political unity of existing states, providing that nothing in the declaration authorizes action dismembering sovereign states.

Status and influence

As a General Assembly declaration, UNDRIP is not legally binding in itself, though some of its provisions are argued to reflect customary international law. It has nevertheless influenced national legislation and policy, including Canada's federal UNDRIP Act of 2021, jurisprudence of regional human rights bodies, and the interpretation of treaties such as ILO Convention No. 169. Debates continue over the meaning of free, prior and informed consent and over implementation.
