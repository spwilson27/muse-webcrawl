Diphtheria

SpecialtyInfectious disease
SymptomsSore throat, fever, swollen neck glands, pseudomembrane in throat
CauseCorynebacterium diphtheriae (toxin-producing strains)
PreventionDiphtheria toxoid vaccine (DPT/DTaP/Tdap)
TreatmentAntitoxin, antibiotics (erythromycin, penicillin)

Diphtheria is an infection caused by the bacterium Corynebacterium diphtheriae. Most infections are asymptomatic or mild, but toxigenic strains produce a powerful exotoxin that can cause a thick grey pseudomembrane in the nose, throat and airways — potentially obstructing breathing — and systemic complications including myocarditis, nerve damage and kidney failure. Historically one of the leading causes of death in children, diphtheria has become rare where vaccination coverage is high; before vaccines it killed tens of thousands annually in industrialised countries alone, and was known as the "strangling angel of children".

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms begin two to five days after exposure: sore throat, low-grade fever, weakness and swollen neck glands ("bull neck"). The toxin kills local tissue, forming the adherent pseudomembrane whose removal causes bleeding. Cutaneous diphtheria causes chronic skin ulcers, mainly in tropical regions and among marginalised populations. Even with treatment, the case fatality rate is 5–10%, highest in the very young and old.

History and vaccination

The disease was named by Pierre Bretonneau in 1826 from the Greek diphthera (leather), for the membrane. The bacterium was identified by Klebs and Loeffler in 1883–84; Roux and Yersin demonstrated the toxin in 1888; and Emil von Behring's antitoxin (1890) won the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Gaston Ramon's toxoid (1923) allowed mass immunisation, and the diphtheria–tetanus–pertussis (DPT) vaccine from the 1940s nearly eliminated the disease in wealthy countries. A major epidemic in the former Soviet Union in the 1990s (over 150,000 cases) followed the collapse of vaccination programmes, and outbreaks have since occurred amid war and displacement in Yemen, Venezuela, Bangladesh and elsewhere.
