Csound

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Csound programs consist of an orchestra file defining instruments and a score file scheduling notes.

Csound is a domain-specific computer programming language and software environment for audio synthesis and music composition. It is one of the oldest and most powerful sound synthesis systems still in use, descending directly from the MUSIC-N family of programs pioneered by Max Mathews at Bell Labs. The name reflects its implementation in the C programming language.

History

Csound was developed by Barry Vercoe at the MIT Media Lab in 1985–86, based on his earlier Music 11 system. It was written in C to make the MUSIC-N paradigm portable across the computers of the day. Over subsequent decades an international community of developers — including John ffitch, who maintained the canonical version for many years — extended it to more than a thousand unit generators ("opcodes") for synthesis, sampling, physical modelling, granular processing, spectral processing, spatialization, and algorithmic composition. Csound 6, released in 2013, modernized the parser and added features such as arrays and on-the-fly compilation; development continues as free and open-source software under the LGPL.

Language structure

A traditional Csound performance combines two texts:

  • An orchestra (.orc), which defines instruments as networks of opcodes — oscillators, filters, envelopes, and effects — connected through variables operating at audio rate, control rate, or initialization time.
  • A score (.sco), which schedules note events for those instruments over time, with parameters such as start time, duration, pitch, and amplitude.

Modern Csound files typically merge both into a single unified .csd file with XML-like tags. Csound can run in real time, responding to MIDI and Open Sound Control input, or render offline at arbitrarily high quality. Front ends such as CsoundQt (now Cabbage-adjacent environments) and the live-coding tooling around it support interactive use, and the engine can be embedded in applications, mobile apps, and web browsers via WebAssembly.

Use

Csound is used in academia for teaching computer music, by composers for electroacoustic works, and as an embedded sound engine in software and games. The Csound Book (2000), edited by Richard Boulanger, is its best-known reference. Although text-based programming has a steep learning curve compared with graphical systems such as Max or Pure Data, Csound's precision, portability, and enormous opcode library have kept it in continuous use for nearly four decades.
