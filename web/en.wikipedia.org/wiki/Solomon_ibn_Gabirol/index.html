Solomon ibn Gabirol

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Solomon ibn Gabirol or Solomon ben Judah (c. 1021 – c. 1058), known in Latin as Avicebron, was an 11th-century Andalusian Jewish philosopher and poet. One of the leading figures of the golden age of Jewish culture in Spain, he wrote influential works in both Hebrew and Arabic, and for centuries his philosophical masterpiece was read by Christian scholastics who did not know its author was Jewish.

Life

Ibn Gabirol was born in Málaga and educated in Zaragoza, where his precocious talent attracted the patronage of the courtier Yekutiel ben Isaac. After Yekutiel's execution in 1039, the young poet, orphaned and chronically ill, wandered between patrons and cities, including Granada, where he quarrelled with Samuel ibn Naghrillah. His short life was marked by poverty and ill health, and later legend embellished his death with tales of a rival's jealousy.

Works

As a poet, ibn Gabirol composed hundreds of Hebrew liturgical and secular poems, many still used in synagogue services. His long philosophical poem Keter Malkhut ("The Royal Crown") is a masterpiece of medieval Hebrew literature, blending cosmology with devotion.

His chief philosophical work, written in Arabic and preserved in Latin translation as Fons Vitae ("The Fountain of Life"), argues that everything except God is composed of universal matter and form — a doctrine of universal hylomorphism that influenced Franciscan thinkers such as Duns Scotus. The Latin world attributed the work to "Avicebron", long assumed to be a Muslim or Christian author; only in the 1840s did the scholar Solomon Munk identify Avicebron with ibn Gabirol. His ethical treatise Improvement of the Moral Qualities and his anthology The Choice of Pearls (of debated attribution) were also widely read.
