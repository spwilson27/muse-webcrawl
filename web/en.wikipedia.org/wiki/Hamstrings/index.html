Hamstrings

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In human anatomy, a hamstring is any one of the three posterior thigh muscles located between the hip and the knee: the semitendinosus, the semimembranosus and the biceps femoris. Collectively, they are known as the hamstrings and form the bulk of the back of the thigh.

Structure

The three hamstring muscles arise from the ischial tuberosity of the pelvis (the long head of biceps femoris being an exception in part) and cross both the hip and knee joints. The semitendinosus and semimembranosus insert on the medial side of the upper tibia, while the biceps femoris inserts on the head of the fibula laterally. The muscles are supplied principally by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve; the short head of biceps femoris is supplied by the common fibular division.

Strict anatomical definitions vary: some authors include only muscles that meet criteria of common origin, insertion and nerve supply, which can exclude the hamstring part of adductor magnus.

Function

The hamstrings act across two joints. They flex the knee and extend the hip, and play an important role in walking, running, jumping and controlling trunk flexion. They also help to stabilise the knee joint, working with the anterior cruciate ligament to prevent the tibia sliding forwards.

Clinical significance

Hamstring strains are among the most common injuries in sports involving sprinting and sudden changes of direction, such as football and athletics. A "pulled hamstring" typically involves tearing of muscle fibres, most often in the biceps femoris, causing pain in the posterior thigh and a period of rehabilitation lasting weeks. Strength and flexibility training, including eccentric exercises such as Nordic hamstring curls, are used to reduce injury risk.
