Anger

EmotionBasic emotion
Facial cuesLowered brows, staring, pressed lips, flared nostrils
PhysiologyIncreased heart rate, blood pressure, adrenaline and noradrenaline

Anger, also known as wrath or rage, is an intense emotional state involving a strong, uncomfortable and non-cooperative response to a perceived provocation, hurt, or threat. A person experiencing anger will often experience physical effects, such as increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, and increased levels of adrenaline and noradrenaline.

Psychology

Psychologists distinguish between anger as a normal, healthy emotion and anger that becomes chronic or uncontrollable, as in intermittent explosive disorder. Anger can be an active or a passive emotion: actively, the angry person responds with aggression toward the perceived source of provocation; passively, with silence, sulking or tension. According to appraisal theories, anger arises when a person judges that a goal has been blocked by another's blameworthy action.

Anger management strategies include cognitive restructuring, relaxation techniques and learning communication skills. Psychologist Charles Spielberger distinguished state anger, trait anger and the modes of anger expression (anger-in, anger-out and anger control).

Philosophical and religious views

Seneca's De Ira treated anger as a kind of temporary madness to be avoided entirely, while Aristotle held that anger at the right things, in the right degree and at the right time could be virtuous. In Catholic tradition, wrath is one of the seven deadly sins. Buddhist texts likewise treat anger (dosa) as one of the three poisons, while also recognising righteous indignation in some traditions.
