Providence is the capital and most populous city of the U.S. state of Rhode Island. Located at the head of Narragansett Bay on the Providence River, it had a population of 190,934 at the 2020 census, making it the third most populous city in New England after Boston and Worcester, and the centre of a metropolitan area of about 1.6 million that covers all of Rhode Island and part of Massachusetts. Founded in 1636 by the religious exile Roger Williams, Providence is one of the oldest cities in the United States and was one of the first industrialised cities in the country, famed in the 19th century for textiles, machinery, silverware and jewellery manufacturing. Today its economy is anchored by education and health care: the city is home to Brown University of the Ivy League and to the Rhode Island School of Design (RISD), and it styles itself the "Creative Capital".

History

Roger Williams, banished from the Massachusetts Bay Colony for his views on religious freedom and Native land rights, was sheltered through the winter of 1635–36 by the Narragansett people and in the spring of 1636 obtained land from the sachems Canonicus and Miantonomi at the head of Narragansett Bay. In gratitude for "God's merciful providence" he named the settlement Providence. The town became a refuge for dissenters—"shelter for persons distressed of conscience"—and under its 1663 royal charter Rhode Island practised a degree of religious liberty unusual in the colonial world; the Touro Synagogue (1763) in nearby Newport and Providence's First Baptist Church in America (founded 1638, present building 1775) witness to that heritage.

Providence grew slowly as a farming and trading port, overshadowed by Newport until the American Revolution: British occupation wrecked Newport's trade while Providence prospered on privateering and on the China trade after the war (the merchant John Brown, whose family endowed Brown University, sent the first Rhode Island ship to Canton). Providence vessels also participated extensively in the Atlantic slave trade, a history the city has publicly reckoned with in recent decades. In the 19th century the city industrialised rapidly along the Blackstone and Woonasquatucket rivers: textile mills, the Gorham Manufacturing Company's silverware, Brown & Sharpe's precision tools and machine tools, and a jewellery industry that once produced a quarter of the nation's jewellery made "the Beehive of Industry" one of America's wealthiest small cities, swollen by Irish, Italian, Portuguese, French-Canadian and other immigration.

The 20th century brought decline as textiles moved south and then overseas; the 1938 New England hurricane flooded downtown catastrophically, and mid-century highway building and urban renewal demolished whole neighbourhoods. From the 1970s, under mayors including Vincent "Buddy" Cianci Jr., the city pursued a celebrated turnaround: rivers that had been paved over were uncovered and lined with parks in the 1990s, the riverfront WaterFire art installation (first lit in 1994) drew crowds downtown, the Providence Place mall and a convention centre opened, and the Capital Center district rose around the relocated Amtrak and commuter-rail station. Cianci's resignation in 2002 following a federal corruption conviction (Operation Plunder Dome) shadowed the renaissance he had promoted, but the universities, hospitals and a growing food and arts scene continued to anchor recovery.

Geography and climate

Providence covers about 20.6 square miles (53 km²), of which roughly 2 square miles is water, at the confluence of the Woonasquatucket and Moshassuck rivers forming the Providence River, a tidal arm of Narragansett Bay. The city is built on seven hills, as its founder's contemporaries noted—Constitution Hill, College Hill, Federal Hill, Smith Hill, Tockwotten, Weybosset and Christian Hill among them. College Hill, on the East Side, holds the largest intact collection of colonial-era houses in America along Benefit Street's "Mile of History". The climate is humid continental bordering on humid subtropical, with warm, humid summers, cold winters and nor'easters; the city is vulnerable to hurricane storm surge, against which the Fox Point Hurricane Barrier was built after 1938 and Hurricane Carol (1954).

Demographics

The 2020 census recorded 190,934 residents. Providence is one of the most diverse small cities in New England: Hispanic or Latino residents form the largest group (about 44 percent, predominantly Dominican, Puerto Rican and Guatemalan), alongside non-Hispanic white (about 33 percent), Black (about 13 percent) and Asian (about 6 percent) populations, with significant Portuguese, Italian, Irish, Cape Verdean, Liberian and Southeast Asian communities. The city is younger than the state as a whole, reflecting its large student population.

Economy and education

The largest employers are hospitals (Rhode Island Hospital and the Lifespan system) and universities. Brown University (founded 1764, in Providence since 1770) and RISD (1877) share College Hill; Johnson & Wales University, Providence College, Rhode Island College and the University of Rhode Island's Providence campus add thousands more students. The city is the seat of state government, a regional banking and insurance centre, and home to the corporate headquarters of Textron and (historically) Citizens Financial Group. The "Knowledge District" redevelopment of former Interstate 195 land has sought to grow medical and technology employment around Brown's Warren Alpert Medical School.

Culture

Providence's cultural institutions include the RISD Museum, the Tony Award-winning Trinity Repertory Company, the Providence Performing Arts Center, the Veterans Memorial Auditorium ("The Vets"), and AS220, an influential unjuried arts collective. WaterFire—more than eighty braziers burning on the downtown rivers, accompanied by music—remains its signature public event, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors in a season. Federal Hill is the traditional Italian-American quarter, famous for restaurants; the city is also known for Rhode Island specialities such as coffee milk, "New York System" hot wieners, johnnycakes, and Del's frozen lemonade. The writer H. P. Lovecraft was born and spent most of his life in Providence, and the city hosts the NecronomiCon convention in his honour.

Sports

The Providence Bruins of the American Hockey League play at the Amica Mutual Pavilion (formerly the Dunkin' Donuts Center), which also hosts Providence College Friars basketball. The city formerly hosted the NFL's Providence Steam Roller (1925–1931, champions in 1928) and is home to Brown Bears and other collegiate teams. The minor-league baseball Pawtucket Red Sox, long based in neighbouring Pawtucket, left Rhode Island for Worcester in 2021.
