Outcome bias

The same decision is judged differently depending on how it turns out

Outcome bias is an error made in evaluating the quality of a decision when the outcome of that decision is already known. In particular, people tend to rate a decision as better when it produces a favourable outcome than when the same decision, made with the same information, produces an unfavourable one. An influential early demonstration was made by Baron and Hershhey (1988), who showed that subjects rated decisions (such as a surgeon's decision to operate, or a gamble) more positively when told the outcome was good.

Outcome bias is related to, but distinguishable from, hindsight bias: hindsight bias is the tendency to remember one's past predictions as more accurate than they were, whereas outcome bias affects the evaluation of the decision itself. Both are examples of the broader tendency to judge by results — colloquially, "resulting".

Consequences

The bias matters wherever decision quality is assessed: in medicine, where identical clinical decisions may be judged harshly only when a patient is harmed; in business, where lucky managers are rewarded as if skilful; in law, where negligence judgments can be swayed by the severity of the harm; and in accident investigations, where knowledge of the outcome can make the causal path seem more foreseeable than it was. Decoupling decision review from outcome knowledge — for example, blinding reviewers to outcomes — is one proposed remedy.
