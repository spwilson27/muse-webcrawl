York is a cathedral city in North Yorkshire, England, with Roman origins, sited at the confluence of the rivers Ouse and Foss. It is the traditional county town of Yorkshire and one of the best-preserved historic cities in England, famous for its medieval city walls, its vast Gothic cathedral, York Minster, and its remarkably intact old centre, including the Shambles, a street of overhanging timber-framed buildings.

History

The city was founded by the Romans in 71 AD as Eboracum, a fortress of the Ninth Legion on the River Ouse. It became one of the principal towns of Roman Britain; the emperor Septimius Severus died there in 211 AD, and Constantine the Great was proclaimed emperor in the city in 306 AD. After the Roman withdrawal, York was taken by the Angles and became the capital of the kingdom of Northumbria, a centre of learning under Alcuin of York.

In 866 the city was captured by the Great Heathen Army of the Vikings and renamed Jórvík, becoming a major trading port of the Danelaw. After the Norman Conquest, William the Conqueror devastated the region in the Harrying of the North, but the city recovered to become a wealthy centre of the wool trade and the seat of the Archbishop of York, the second-highest office in the Church of England. York Minster, the largest Gothic cathedral in Northern Europe, was built over roughly 250 years, between about 1220 and 1472.

The city suffered during the English Civil War, when it was besieged by Parliamentarian forces in 1644. In the Victorian era York became an important railway hub and a centre of chocolate manufacture, home to Rowntree's and Terry's. The railway legacy is celebrated by the National Railway Museum, one of the most visited museums in Britain outside London.

Geography and governance

York stands on flat ground in the Vale of York, about 22 miles north-east of Leeds and 55 miles east of the Pennines. It is a unitary authority area, administered by the City of York Council, and lies within the ceremonial county of North Yorkshire. The historic centre is enclosed by medieval walls, among the most complete in England, punctuated by gatehouses known locally as "bars".

Economy and tourism

Tourism is central to the modern economy; attractions include York Minster, the Jorvik Viking Centre, the York Castle Museum, Clifford's Tower, and the preserved medieval streets. The University of York, founded in 1963, and York St John University are major employers, and the city has grown sectors in science, technology, and insurance.

Transport

York railway station is a key junction on the East Coast Main Line, with fast services to London King's Cross (about two hours), Edinburgh, and Manchester. The city is served by the A64 and lies near the A1(M) motorway.
