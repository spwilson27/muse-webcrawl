Volcanic gas

Volcanic gases are gases given off by active (or, at times, by dormant) volcanoes. These include gases trapped in cavities (vesicles) in volcanic rocks, dissolved or dissociated gases in magma and lava, or gases emanating from lava or volcanic vents and fumaroles. The collective term for all volcanic gases is volcanic exhalations.

Composition

The principal components of volcanic gases are water vapour (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), sulfur — either as sulfur dioxide (SO2, at high temperatures) or hydrogen sulfide (H2S, at low temperatures) — nitrogen, argon, helium, neon, methane, carbon monoxide and hydrogen. Water vapour is typically the most abundant, often making up more than 60% of total emissions, with carbon dioxide typically accounting for 10 to 40%.

Magmatic gases

Magma contains dissolved volatile components. As magma rises towards the surface, pressure decreases and volatiles exsolve, forming bubbles. This degassing drives explosive eruptions: the expansion of gas fragments the magma into ash and pumice. The amount and composition of gas released is a key tool for forecasting eruptions.

Hazards

Volcanic gases can be dangerous. Carbon dioxide, being denser than air, can pool in low-lying areas and asphyxiate people and animals — as happened catastrophically at Lake Nyos in Cameroon in 1986, releasing a CO2 cloud from a volcanic lake. Sulfur dioxide causes acid rain and respiratory problems; hydrogen fluoride can contaminate water and grazing land. Eruptions also inject sulfur gases into the stratosphere, forming aerosols that cool the global climate.

Measurement

Volcanologists measure gas composition and emission rates using remote sensing (such as correlation spectrometry for SO2 flux), direct sampling of fumaroles, and satellite instruments. Changes in gas ratios often precede eruptions.
