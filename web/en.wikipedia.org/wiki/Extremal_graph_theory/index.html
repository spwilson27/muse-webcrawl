Extremal graph theory

Extremal graph theory is a branch of combinatorics and graph theory that studies how the maximum or minimum possible values of graph parameters (such as the number of edges) relate to other parameters, and what the extremal graphs that achieve those bounds look like. It is closely related to fields such as extremal combinatorics and Ramsey theory.

Turán's theorem

The founding result of the field is Turán's theorem (1941), which determines the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex graph that contains no complete subgraph Kr+1. The unique extremal graph is the Turán graph, a complete balanced r-partite graph. For triangle-free graphs (r = 2), Mantel's theorem (1907) gives the bound ⌊n²/4⌋.

Other problems

The Turán-type problem asks for the extremal number ex(n, H), the maximum edges in an n-vertex graph avoiding a forbidden subgraph H. Famous results include the Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem for bipartite forbidden graphs and the Erdős–Stone theorem. The field also studies saturation, extremal problems for hypergraphs, and the stability of near-extremal structures. The Erdős–Sós conjecture and work of Simonovits on stability have been central themes.
