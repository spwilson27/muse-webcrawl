The caplock mechanism, or percussion lock, is a system for igniting the propellant in a firearm using a percussion cap — a small copper or brass cup containing a shock-sensitive explosive compound, typically mercury fulminate. It superseded the flintlock in the early 19th century and was the dominant ignition system for military and sporting arms until the spread of self-contained metallic cartridges.

Operation

The cap is placed on a hollow metal cone, the nipple, which communicates with the barrel's breech. When the trigger is pulled, the hammer (or cock) falls and strikes the cap, crushing the explosive compound against the nipple. The resulting flash passes through the nipple and ignites the main powder charge. Compared with a flintlock, the caplock is far more reliable in wet weather, has a shorter lock time, and does not require the careful knapping and priming of a flint.

History

The Scottish clergyman Alexander John Forsyth patented the first percussion ignition system in 1807, using loose fulminate powder in a "scent-bottle" lock. The self-contained cap, developed in the following two decades by several inventors including Joshua Shaw and François Prélat, made the system practical. Most European and American armies converted their flintlock muskets or adopted new percussion arms during the 1830s–1850s; percussion rifles and revolvers saw extensive service in the American Civil War.

Percussion ignition survives today in the muzzleloading sport, in specialist forms including the in-line ignition systems of modern hunting muzzleloaders.
