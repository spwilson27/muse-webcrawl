Inclusion–exclusion principle

In combinatorics, a combinatorial principle, the inclusion–exclusion principle is a counting technique which generalizes the familiar method of obtaining the number of elements in the union of two finite sets. It is conceptually similar to the principle of counting shown in Venn diagrams.

Statement

Symbolically, for two sets A and B: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. For three sets, |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A∩B| − |A∩C| − |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C|. In general, for n sets, the size of the union equals the sum over all non-empty subsets S of the index set of (−1)|S|+1 times the size of the intersection of the sets in S.

Applications

The principle is used to count derangements, numbers divisible by a given set of primes, and in probability to compute the probability that at least one of several events occurs. It also underlies the sieve methods of number theory, including the Legendre sieve and Brun's sieve, and the principle of Möbius inversion on partially ordered sets, of which inclusion–exclusion is a special case.

History

While the principle was known in some form earlier, it is often attributed to Abraham de Moivre (1718), though it also appears in work by Daniel da Silva (1854) and J. J. Sylvester (1883).
