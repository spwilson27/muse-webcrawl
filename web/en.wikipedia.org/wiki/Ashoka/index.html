Ashoka

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: An Ashokan pillar with its polished shaft and inscribed edicts]

Ashoka (c. 304 – 232 BCE) was the third ruler of the Maurya Empire, and the most celebrated king of ancient India. He reigned from about 268 to 232 BCE over an empire stretching from Afghanistan to Bengal. After witnessing the carnage of his conquest of Kalinga, Ashoka embraced Buddhism and devoted the rest of his reign to spreading dhamma — a code of piety, tolerance and non-violence — through edicts inscribed in local scripts and languages on rocks and pillars from Kandahar to Karnataka, the earliest royal inscriptions of India that can still be read today.

From conquest to dhamma

Little reliable is known of Ashoka's early life; Buddhist legends credit him with a ruthless rise. In about 261 BCE he conquered the coastal kingdom of Kalinga at a cost his own edicts put at 100,000 killed and 150,000 deported — figures he records with remorse. Thereafter he renounced aggressive war, banned royal hunts, restricted slaughter, planted trees and medicinal herbs, dug wells, and appointed officers of dhamma to promote welfare and tolerance among all sects.

Legacy

Ashoka sent emissaries across Asia — tradition says his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta carried Buddhism to Sri Lanka — and his patronage helped transform Buddhism into a world religion. Forgotten in India for two millennia, he was recovered in the 19th century when his inscriptions were deciphered (James Prinsep, 1837). Independent India adopted his Sarnath lion capital as its state emblem and the 24-spoked Ashoka Chakra on its flag.
