Regular moon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The Galilean moons of Jupiter are regular satellites

In astronomy, a regular moon or regular satellite is a natural satellite following a relatively close, stable and circular orbit around its parent planet, generally in the same direction as the planet's rotation (a prograde orbit) and near the plane of the planet's equator. Regular moons are thought to have formed in situ, from the same circumplanetary disc of gas and dust that surrounded the growing planet, in contrast to irregular moons, which are captured objects on distant, eccentric and often retrograde orbits.

Characteristics

Regular moons typically orbit within a few dozen planetary radii, have low orbital eccentricity and inclination, and are often locked in orbital resonances and synchronous rotation. Because they formed from a disc, their systems often resemble miniature solar systems, with composition gradients — rockier bodies closer in, icier bodies farther out — reflecting the temperature structure of the circumplanetary disc.

Examples

The classic examples are the four Galilean moons of Jupiter (Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto) and the large moons of Saturn (including Titan, Rhea and Iapetus), Uranus (Miranda through Oberon) and Neptune's large moon Triton, which is an exception: Triton's retrograde orbit shows it is a captured Kuiper belt object despite its size and circular orbit. Earth's Moon is also exceptional — it formed from a giant impact rather than a circumplanetary disc, though its near-circular, low-inclination orbit is "regular" in geometry. Most of the known moons of the giant planets are irregular satellites, small captured asteroids and comet nuclei discovered in surveys since 2000.
