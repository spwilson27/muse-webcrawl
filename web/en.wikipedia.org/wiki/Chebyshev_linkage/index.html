Chebyshev linkage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Animation of the Chebyshev linkage tracing its characteristic flat-bottomed curve]

The Chebyshev linkage is a mechanical linkage invented by the Russian mathematician Pafnuty Chebyshev in the nineteenth century that converts rotational motion into approximate straight-line motion. Chebyshev devised it in connection with his work on the "plantigrade machine", a walking mechanism he exhibited at the Exposition Universelle in Paris in 1878.

Design

The linkage is a four-bar mechanism with particular proportions. With the frame (fixed link) of length 4 units, the crank is 2 units, the connecting rod (coupler) is 5 units in total, and the rocker is 2.5 units; the tracing point is at the midpoint of the coupler. As the crank rotates, the tracing point follows a curve with a long, nearly straight segment at the bottom and a rounded return at the top — a shape well suited to the gait of a walking machine, in which the foot must move flat along the ground and then lift and return.

Context

Chebyshev's linkage emerged from his broader programme of applying mathematics to mechanism design; his work on approximation theory — including the Chebyshev polynomials — grew out of the problem of making linkages trace straight lines with minimal deviation. Unlike the exact straight-line mechanisms of Peaucellier and Lipkin, the Chebyshev linkage gives an approximation, but with a much simpler construction.
