The Spaghetti Western is a broad subgenre of Western films produced by Italian studios from the mid-1960s to the late 1970s. The term, coined by critics outside Italy, reflects the genre's Italian origin; Italians call them western all'italiana. Roughly 600 "Eurowesterns" were made, most of them Italian or Italian-Spanish co-productions shot in the deserts of Almería in southern Spain, whose arid landscapes stood in for the American Southwest and Mexico.

Sergio Leone and the genre's defining style

The genre ignited internationally with Sergio Leone's A Fistful of Dollars (1964) — an unauthorized remake of Kurosawa's Yojimbo — starring a then little-known television actor, Clint Eastwood, as the laconic Man with No Name. Leone's "Dollars Trilogy," completed by For a Few Dollars More (1965) and The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (1966), and his Once Upon a Time in the West (1968) defined the style: extreme close-ups and vast landscapes, operatic ritualized gunfights, amoral bounty hunters instead of heroic lawmen, cynical violence, and Ennio Morricone's revolutionary scores mixing electric guitar, whistling, whip-cracks, and choir.

Other directors and the genre cycle

Sergio Corbucci's Django (1966), with Franco Nero dragging a coffin through the mud, was even more brutal and spawned dozens of unofficial sequels; his The Great Silence (1968) is often ranked beside Leone's best. Sergio Sollima's The Big Gundown (1966) and Damiano Damiani's A Bullet for the General (1966) added explicit politics — the "Zapata westerns" set amid the Mexican Revolution, read as allegories of Third World liberation. Hundreds of cheaper imitations followed, sliding by the 1970s into self-parody and comedy, notably the Terence Hill–Bud Spencer Trinity films.

Characteristics and legacy

Spaghetti Westerns were typically shot silent or post-synchronized with multilingual casts dubbing dialogue in post-production, giving the films their slightly detached, stylized quality. Morally their worlds are greed-driven frontiers where the classical Western's pieties are stripped away — a darkness that suited the Vietnam-era mood and fed back into Hollywood through Sam Peckinpah and the revisionist Western. Quentin Tarantino, who calls The Good, the Bad and the Ugly the greatest film ever made, revived the genre openly in Django Unchained (2012). The genre's imagery — the poncho, the cigarillo, the desert standoff — remains the popular shorthand for the Western itself.
