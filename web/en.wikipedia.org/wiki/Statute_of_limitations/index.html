Statute of limitations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A statute of limitations, also known as a period of prescription in civil law systems, is a law passed by a legislative body to set the maximum time after an event within which legal proceedings may be initiated. When the period expires, claims are time-barred (subject to rules on accrual — when the clock starts — and tolling, which pauses it for minority, concealment or absence). Limitation periods vary by claim: personal-injury and contract claims often run 2–6 years; property and some statutory claims longer; fraud and latent-damage regimes add discovery rules.

Rationale and exceptions

Justifications include evidentiary reliability (memories fade, documents vanish), diligence by claimants, and defendants' repose. Criminal law applies limitations to most offences but commonly none to murder, and — by treaty (the 1968 UN Convention) and custom — none to war crimes and crimes against humanity. Politically charged extensions recur for child sexual abuse (revival/"lookback" windows in several US states and elsewhere) and for financial and corruption offences. In private international law, limitation is variously treated as substantive or procedural, affecting cross-border claims.
