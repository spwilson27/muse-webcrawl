The Holy See (Latin: Sancta Sedes) is the supreme governing body of the Catholic Church, centred on the Pope as Bishop of Rome, and a sovereign entity in international law in its own right. It is distinct from Vatican City State, the small territorial state created by the Lateran Treaty of 1929 to guarantee the Holy See's independence.

St Peter's Basilica in Vatican City, the territorial seat of the Holy See since 1929.

Terminology and status

"See" derives from the Latin sedes (seat), referring to the episcopal throne of the Bishop of Rome. The Holy See comprises the Pope and the Roman Curia — the Secretariat of State, dicasteries (formerly congregations), tribunals and other institutions through which the Pope governs the universal Church of some 1.3 billion Catholics. Under canon law it is the juridical person of the Church's central government.

History

The popes ruled the Papal States in central Italy for a millennium until Italy's unification completed with the capture of Rome in 1870. Refusing to recognise the loss, successive popes remained within the Vatican as the "prisoner in the Vatican" question dragged on until the Lateran Treaty of 1929 between Pius XI and Mussolini's Italy created Vatican City and settled the Roman Question. The Holy See's diplomatic activity is much older than the modern state: papal legates and nuncios have represented it at courts for centuries.

International role

The Holy See maintains diplomatic relations with around 180 states and is a permanent observer at the United Nations (since 1964) and a member or observer of many international organisations. Its diplomacy is conducted by the Secretariat of State and a worldwide network of apostolic nunciatures. It concludes concordats regulating the Church's position in particular countries, and it acts internationally on issues from disarmament and development to religious freedom.

Distinction from Vatican City

Vatican City State is the territory — 44 hectares enclosed within Rome — over which the Pope is absolute monarch, created to ensure the Holy See's visible independence. Ambassadors are accredited to the Holy See, not to Vatican City, and it is the Holy See that belongs to international bodies and operates the Church's central administration, which largely works from within Vatican territory.
