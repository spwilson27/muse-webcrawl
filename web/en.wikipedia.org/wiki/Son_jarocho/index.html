Son jarocho

Son jarocho ("Veracruz sound") is a regional folk musical style from the Mexican state of Veracruz, on the Gulf of Mexico. It is a fusion of Spanish (especially Andalusian), Indigenous, and West African musical elements formed during the colonial era, traditionally performed at social gatherings called fandangos. Its most famous song worldwide is "La Bamba", brought to international attention by Ritchie Valens in 1958 and by Los Lobos in 1987.

A son jarocho ensemble: arpa jarocha, requinto jarocho, and jarana jarocha.

Instruments and performance

The core ensemble consists of:

  • Jarana jarocha — a small five-string (in five courses... in modern form, five strings) rhythm guitar strummed in syncopated patterns that carry the harmony.
  • Requinto jarocho — a small four-string guitar played with a long pick, carrying the melody and improvisations.
  • Arpa jarocha — a large wooden folk harp providing melody and bass lines.
  • Leona — a large plucked bass guitar, and sometimes pandero (tambourine), quijada (donkey jawbone), and cajón — added in modern ensembles.

Singers exchange improvised and traditional verses (coplas and décimas) in call-and-response over a repeating harmonic cycle. At a fandango, dancers perform zapateado footwork on a wooden platform, the tarima, which acts as a percussion instrument around which the musicians gather.

Repertoire and revival

Standard sones include "La Bamba", "El Colás", "El Siquisirí", "La Bruja", "El Pájaro Cú", and "La Iguana". The style was popularised nationally in the 1940s–50s by groups such as Andrés Huesca y sus Costeños and Conjunto Medellín, then declined as tastes changed. From the 1970s, bands led by Mono Blanco (founded 1977) sparked a movimiento jaranero revival centred on community fandangos in Los Tuxtlas and southern Veracruz, a movement that spread to Mexican-American communities in the United States — notably through Los Angeles band Los Cambalache and through Los Lobos, whose repertoire draws on the tradition. The fandango-based community music-making of son jarocho has become a model for participatory folk revivals elsewhere.
