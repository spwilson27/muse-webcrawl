A lunar month is a unit of time defined by the orbital period of the Moon. Because the Moon can be measured against different reference points, astronomy recognises several distinct lunar months. The one used in most lunar calendars is the synodic month — the interval between successive new moons — averaging 29.53 days.

Types of lunar month

The sidereal month (27.32 days) is the time the Moon takes to return to the same position against the fixed stars. The synodic month is longer (29.53 days) because the Earth–Moon system advances around the Sun meanwhile, so the Moon must travel further to regain the same phase. The anomalistic month (27.55 days) runs from perigee to perigee as the lunar orbit's elliptical axis rotates, while the draconic month (27.21 days) runs between passages through the same orbital node and determines the rhythm of eclipses.

Calendars

Lunar calendars, such as the Islamic calendar, consist of 12 synodic months totalling about 354 days, roughly 11 days short of the solar year, so their months migrate through the seasons. Lunisolar calendars, such as the Hebrew and Chinese calendars, intercalate an extra month periodically to keep lunar months aligned with the solar year.
