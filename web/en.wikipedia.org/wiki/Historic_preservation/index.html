Historic preservation

Historic preservation (in British usage, part of conservation) is the practice of protecting and maintaining buildings, structures, sites, districts and landscapes of historical, architectural or cultural significance. It encompasses documentation, restoration, rehabilitation and adaptive reuse, and is carried out by government agencies, non-profit organisations and private owners.

History

Organised preservation emerged in the 19th century: figures such as John Ruskin and William Morris (who founded the Society for the Protection of Ancient Buildings in 1877) argued against destructive "restoration." In the United States, early efforts saved George Washington's Mount Vernon, and preservation law developed through the Antiquities Act (1906), the creation of the National Park Service (1916), and the National Historic Preservation Act of 1966, which created the National Register of Historic Places and the system of State Historic Preservation Offices. The demolition of New York's Pennsylvania Station in 1963 galvanised modern landmarks legislation.

Practice and debates

Preservation standards, such as the U.S. Secretary of the Interior's Standards, distinguish preservation, rehabilitation, restoration and reconstruction. Internationally, the Venice Charter (1964) and UNESCO's World Heritage Convention (1972) guide conservation practice. The field continues to debate the treatment of recent and modernist heritage, the preservation of intangible and vernacular culture, and tensions between preservation, development and housing supply.
