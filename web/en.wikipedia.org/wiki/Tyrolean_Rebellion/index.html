Tyrolean Rebellion

The Tyrolean Rebellion of 1809 (German: Tiroler Volksaufstand) was a peasant uprising in the County of Tyrol, then under Bavarian and French control, against the occupation forces of Napoleon during the War of the Fifth Coalition. The rebellion was led by Andreas Hofer, an innkeeper from the Passeier Valley, and won a series of remarkable early victories before being crushed in the autumn of 1809.

Background

By the Treaty of Pressburg in 1805, Austria ceded Tyrol to Bavaria, an ally of Napoleonic France. Bavarian reforms — heavy taxation, conscription, and interference with the Catholic Church and Tyrol's traditional privileges — provoked widespread resentment among the deeply conservative, devout Tyrolean population. When Austria went to war with France again in 1809, Tyroleans rose in support.

The uprising

In April 1809 the rebels captured Innsbruck and defeated Bavarian and French forces. Hofer's irregulars won repeated victories at the Battles of Bergisel near Innsbruck, most famously in May and August 1809, driving the occupiers from the city. However, after Austria's defeat at Wagram and the armistice of Znaim, the Tyroleans were left to fight alone.

Defeat and aftermath

Reinforced Franco-Bavarian forces reoccupied Innsbruck in November 1809 after the fourth Battle of Bergisel. Hofer was betrayed, captured in January 1810, and executed by firing squad at Mantua on 20 February 1810. He became a folk hero and a central figure of Tyrolean and Austrian national identity.
