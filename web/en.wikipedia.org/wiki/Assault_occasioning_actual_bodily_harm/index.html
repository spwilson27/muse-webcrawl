Assault occasioning actual bodily harm

Assault occasioning actual bodily harm (often abbreviated to assault ABH) is a criminal offence in England and Wales, created by section 47 of the Offences against the Person Act 1861. It is triable either way (in the magistrates' court or the Crown Court) and carries a maximum sentence of five years' imprisonment.

Elements of the offence

The offence requires proof of an assault or battery that "occasions" actual bodily harm. "Actual bodily harm" was interpreted in case law (notably R v Miller [1954] and R v Chan-Fook [1994]) as harm that is more than transient or trifling, and it can include recognisable psychiatric injury, though not mere emotions such as fear or panic. The mens rea is simply that of the underlying assault or battery: the prosecution does not need to prove that the defendant intended or foresaw the bodily harm.

Relationship to other offences

Section 47 sits between common assault (section 39 of the Criminal Justice Act 1988) and the more serious offences of wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm under section 20, and causing grievous bodily harm with intent under section 18, of the 1861 Act. Since amendments introduced by the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022, assaults on emergency workers may attract an increased maximum sentence under related legislation.

Sentencing

Sentencing is governed by guidelines of the Sentencing Council, which categorise offences by culpability and harm. Depending on the category, sentences range from a fine or community order up to custody approaching the five-year maximum, particularly where the offence is racially or religiously aggravated or committed against a vulnerable victim.
