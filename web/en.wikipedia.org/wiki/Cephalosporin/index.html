Cephalosporin

The cephalosporins are a class of β-lactam antibiotics originally derived from the fungus Acremonium (formerly called Cephalosporium). Together with the related cephamycins they form the cephem group. Like penicillins, they kill bacteria by interfering with the synthesis of the peptidoglycan layer of bacterial cell walls, binding to penicillin-binding proteins and preventing cross-linking.

History

Cephalosporin compounds were first isolated in 1945 by the Italian pharmacologist Giuseppe Brotzu from cultures taken near a sewage outfall off Sardinia. The active nucleus, 7-aminocephalosporanic acid (7-ACA), was isolated at Oxford by Guy Newton and Edward Abraham, enabling the production of semi-synthetic derivatives; the first commercial cephalosporin, cephalothin, was launched in 1964.

Generations

Cephalosporins are conventionally grouped into generations by their spectrum of activity. First-generation agents (such as cephalexin and cefazolin) are most active against Gram-positive cocci. Second-generation drugs (such as cefuroxime) add Gram-negative coverage. Third-generation agents (such as ceftriaxone and cefotaxime) penetrate the central nervous system and are strongly active against Gram-negative bacteria. Fourth-generation cefepime combines broad Gram-positive and Gram-negative activity, while fifth-generation agents such as ceftaroline are notable for activity against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).

Resistance and adverse effects

Bacteria resist cephalosporins mainly through β-lactamase enzymes — including extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) — altered penicillin-binding proteins, and reduced membrane permeability. Common adverse effects include gastrointestinal upset and hypersensitivity reactions; cross-reactivity with penicillin allergy is now considered much lower than once believed, on the order of 1–2%.
