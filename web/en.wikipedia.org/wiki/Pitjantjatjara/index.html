Pitjantjatjara

The Pitjantjatjara (pronounced roughly "pigeon-jarra") are an Aboriginal Australian people of the Central Australian desert, closely related to the Yankunytjatjara and Ngaanyatjarra, with whom they form the Anangu cultural bloc. Pitjantjatjara is also the name of their language, a dialect of the Western Desert language group and one of the most widely spoken Aboriginal languages in Australia.

Country and history

Pitjantjatjara country covers the far northwest of South Australia and adjoining parts of the Northern Territory and Western Australia, including the region of Uluru. European contact came late, in the early 20th century, and many Pitjantjatjara moved to missions such as Ernabella (Pukatja), founded in 1937 and notable for encouraging language and craft. In the 1950s–60s, British nuclear tests at Maralinga and the Woomera rocket range displaced some communities.

Land rights

The Pitjantjatjara Land Rights Act 1981 (South Australia) granted inalienable freehold title to about 103,000 km² — the Aṉangu Pitjantjatjara Yankunytjatjara (APY) Lands — making it one of Australia's landmark land-rights achievements. In 1985, title to Uluru-Kata Tjuta National Park was handed back to its traditional owners and leased back to the national parks agency.

Language and culture

The Pitjantjatjara language has a few thousand speakers and is taught in APY schools; a complete Bible translation was published in 2019 after decades of work. Cultural life is governed by Tjukurpa (the Dreaming law). Ernabella Arts, founded 1948, is Australia's oldest continuously running Indigenous art centre, famous for batik and later painting and ceramics.
