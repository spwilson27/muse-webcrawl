Chief of police

A chief of police is the title typically given to the top official in the chain of command of a police department, particularly in North America. The chief is responsible for the administration, operations and discipline of the department and is usually appointed by, and accountable to, the local government executive or a police board or commission.

United States

American police chiefs head municipal police departments ranging from a handful of officers to the largest, the New York City Police Department (whose head holds the title of police commissioner). Chiefs set policy, manage budgets, hire and discipline officers, and serve as the department's public spokesperson. They are usually appointed officials rather than elected — a contrast with the county sheriff, who in most states is elected. Chiefs typically serve at the pleasure of the appointing authority and turnover is frequent; the average tenure in large cities is only a few years.

Canada and elsewhere

In Canada, chiefs of municipal and regional police services are appointed by local police services boards. The title or its equivalents are used in many other jurisdictions worldwide, though duties and appointment methods vary. In the United Kingdom the equivalent head of a territorial force is a chief constable, and in many countries policing is centralised under national commanders.
