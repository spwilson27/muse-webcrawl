Truro Cathedral, formally the Cathedral Church of the Blessed Virgin Mary, is an Anglican cathedral in Truro, Cornwall, England. It was the first cathedral built on a new site in England since the Reformation, following the creation of the Diocese of Truro in 1876 under Bishop Edward White Benson, later Archbishop of Canterbury. The cathedral is one of only three in the United Kingdom with three spires, the others being Lichfield and St Mary's in Edinburgh.

History

The foundation stone was laid in 1880; the architect was John Loughborough Pearson, a leading figure of the Gothic Revival. Part of the 16th-century parish church of St Mary was incorporated into the building as its south aisle. The first section was consecrated in 1887, and the cathedral was completed in 1910 when the western towers were opened. The central tower rises to about 76 metres (250 ft), and the western spires to about 61 metres (200 ft).

Features

The cathedral is noted for its fine Gothic Revival architecture, its baptistery designed by Pearson's son Frank, and a notable collection of stained glass by the firm Clayton and Bell. Benson originated the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols at Truro in 1880, a service later made famous by King's College, Cambridge. The cathedral holds the world's oldest complete set of Cornish-language inscriptions in its memorials, and it maintains a strong choral tradition.
