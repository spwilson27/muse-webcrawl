Tangentopoli

Tangentopoli
Also known as: Bribesville
Country: Italy
Period: 1992–1994
Investigation: Mani pulite

Tangentopoli (Italian pronunciation: [tandʒenˈtɔːpoli]; from tangente, meaning "kickback", and -poli, meaning "city" in Greek) is the name given to the system of widespread political corruption in Italy that was uncovered by the Mani pulite ("Clean Hands") judicial investigations in the early 1990s. The scandal led to the collapse of the First Italian Republic and the disappearance of many long-dominant political parties.

Background

For decades, Italian public contracts had routinely carried an illicit surcharge — the tangente — distributed among the governing parties and their intermediaries. The funds financed party operations, patronage networks and personal enrichment, and the practice was widely understood to be an unofficial cost of doing business with the state.

The investigation

The scandal broke on 17 February 1992, when Milanese prosecutors arrested Mario Chiesa, a member of the Italian Socialist Party and director of a municipal retirement home, after he was caught accepting a bribe. Chiesa's subsequent cooperation with magistrates — notably Antonio Di Pietro — opened investigations that spread from Milan across the country, implicating thousands of politicians, civil servants and businesspeople.

Consequences

The investigations destroyed the credibility of the parties that had governed Italy since the Second World War. The Christian Democracy and the Socialist Party dissolved, and the 1994 general election — won by Silvio Berlusconi's newly formed Forza Italia — is conventionally taken to mark the beginning of the Second Republic. Socialist leader Bettino Craxi, twice prime minister, fled to Tunisia in 1994 and died in exile in 2000.

Legacy

Tangentopoli remains a byword in Italian public life for systemic corruption, and the term is still invoked whenever new kickback scandals emerge.
