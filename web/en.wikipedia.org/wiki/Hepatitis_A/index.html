Hepatitis A

Hepatitis A is an infectious disease of the liver caused by the hepatitis A virus (HAV), a picornavirus spread mainly through the faecal–oral route — typically via contaminated food or water, or close contact with an infected person. Unlike hepatitis B and C, it does not cause chronic liver disease.

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of about two to six weeks, symptoms may include fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, loss of appetite, fever, and jaundice. Many infections, especially in young children, are asymptomatic. Symptoms usually last less than two months; the disease only rarely causes acute liver failure, a risk that rises with age and pre-existing liver disease.

Prevention and treatment

An effective inactivated vaccine has been available since the 1990s and is recommended for travellers to endemic regions and at-risk groups; good hygiene and safe water also prevent spread. There is no specific antiviral treatment — care is supportive, and recovery confers lifelong immunity.

Epidemiology

Tens of millions of infections occur worldwide each year, historically concentrated in regions with poor sanitation; incidence has fallen sharply where childhood vaccination is routine.
