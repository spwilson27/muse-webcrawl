Amplifier

An amplifier, electronic amplifier or (informally) amp is an electronic device that can increase the magnitude of a signal (a time-varying voltage or current). It is a two-port electronic circuit that uses electric power from a power supply to increase the amplitude (magnitude of the voltage or current) of a signal applied to its input terminals, producing a proportionally greater amplitude signal at its output.

The amount of amplification provided by an amplifier is measured by its gain: the ratio of output voltage, current, or power to input. An amplifier is defined as a circuit that has a power gain greater than one.

History

The first practical electronic amplifier was the Audion triode vacuum tube, invented by Lee de Forest in 1906, which made radio broadcasting, long-distance telephony and sound film practical. Vacuum-tube amplifiers dominated electronics until the transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, enabled smaller, cooler and more efficient solid-state designs. Harold Black's negative feedback amplifier (1927) greatly improved linearity, and the operational amplifier became a fundamental building block of analogue electronics.

Types and classification

Amplifiers are described by their input and output properties and by the portion of the input cycle during which the active device conducts: class A devices conduct for the whole cycle, class B for half, class AB in between, and class C for less than half, trading efficiency against distortion. Switching (class D) amplifiers use pulse-width modulation and achieve very high efficiency, and are common in modern audio equipment. Amplifiers are also categorised by function, as in audio power amplifiers, radio-frequency amplifiers, instrumentation amplifiers and distributed amplifiers used in communications.

Properties

Key specifications include gain, bandwidth, efficiency, linearity, noise figure, output dynamic range and slew rate. Ideally an amplifier increases signal amplitude without adding noise or distortion; practical designs balance these properties against power consumption, cost and stability, often using negative feedback to reduce distortion and set a precise gain.
