Breakfast cereal

Breakfast cereal is a prepared food made from processed cereal grains — principally maize (corn), wheat, oats, and rice — typically eaten at breakfast, most often in a bowl with milk. Ready-to-eat cereals include flakes, puffs, shreds, and granola-style mixtures, many of them sweetened and marketed especially to children; hot cereals include porridge and oatmeal, which require cooking. The modern packaged cereal industry arose in the United States in the late 19th century and is dominated by companies such as Kellogg's (now WK Kellogg Co and Kellanova), General Mills, and Post.

History

Hot grain dishes are ancient — porridge has been eaten since the Neolithic — but packaged cold cereal began with health reformers. In 1863 James Caleb Jackson, a New York sanatorium operator, produced "granula", hard bran nuggets that required soaking. At the Battle Creek Sanitarium in Michigan, John Harvey Kellogg developed granola-like products and, with his brother W. K. Kellogg, flaked cereals in the 1890s; W. K. Kellogg founded the company that launched Corn Flakes in 1906. Former patient C. W. Post built a rival empire with Grape-Nuts (1897) and Post Toasties. Puffed grains (Quaker's Puffed Wheat and Rice), sugar-coated cereals of the 1950s, cartoon mascots, and toy premiums in boxes made cereal a fixture of childhood, while television advertising spread the products worldwide.

Production

Cereals are manufactured by cooking grains with flavourings and sweeteners, then shaping by flaking, shredding, puffing (gun-puffing or extrusion), or baking, and finally toasting, drying, and often coating with sugar or frosting and fortifying with vitamins and minerals. Hot cereals are less processed, consisting of rolled, steel-cut, or milled grains.

Nutrition and criticism

Cereals range widely in nutritional quality. Plain oat and shredded-wheat products are high in fibre and low in sugar, and fortification has contributed meaningfully to intakes of iron, folate, and B vitamins. However, many children's cereals are high in added sugar — some exceeding 40% sugar by weight — and have been criticised by health campaigners and regulators, leading to voluntary and mandatory reformulation and restrictions on advertising to children in several countries. Consumer groups regularly rate brands, and sugar content has declined in many markets since the 2010s.
