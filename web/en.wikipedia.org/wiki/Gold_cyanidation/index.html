Gold cyanidation

Gold cyanidation (also known as the cyanide process or the MacArthur–Forrest process) is a hydrometallurgical technique for extracting gold from low-grade ore through the process of converting the gold into a water-soluble coordination complex. It is the most commonly used leaching process for gold extraction. Cyanidation is also widely used in silver extraction, usually after froth flotation.

History

The process was patented by John Stewart MacArthur, working with brothers Robert and William Forrest, in 1887. The first successful installation was at the Crown Mine in New Zealand in 1889. By 1891 the process was in use on the Witwatersrand in South Africa, and it played a major role in opening up gold fields that could not be worked by gravity methods alone.

Chemistry

Finely ground ore is mixed with a dilute solution of sodium cyanide. Gold dissolves in the presence of oxygen according to Elsner's equation:

4 Au + 8 NaCN + O2 + 2 H2O → 4 Na[Au(CN)2] + 4 NaOH

The dissolved gold is then recovered from solution, commonly by adsorption onto activated carbon (the carbon-in-pulp and carbon-in-leach processes) or by zinc precipitation in the Merrill–Crowe process.

Environmental concerns

Because cyanide is highly toxic, cyanidation is controversial. Tailings containing cyanide can contaminate water supplies; the 2000 Baia Mare cyanide spill in Romania released about 100,000 cubic metres of cyanide-contaminated water into the Danube river system. Many operations detoxify spent solutions, for example with the INCO SO2/air process, before discharge. Some jurisdictions have banned cyanide mining outright.
