Saber-toothed cat

A saber-toothed cat is any member of various extinct groups of predatory mammals characterised by long, curved, saber-shaped canine teeth that protruded from the mouth even when it was closed. The best-known example is Smilodon of the Americas, but saber teeth evolved independently in several lineages, including the machairodont felids, the nimravids, the barbourofelids, and even the marsupial-related thylacosmilids of South America — a striking case of convergent evolution.

Evolution and distribution

Saber-toothed predators appeared repeatedly over the last 40 million years. True saber-toothed cats belong to the subfamily Machairodontinae within Felidae, ranging across Africa, Eurasia and the Americas from the Miocene until the end of the Pleistocene, roughly 10,000 years ago. Genera include Machairodus, Homotherium (the scimitar-toothed cat, with shorter, serrated canines) and Smilodon, whose upper canines could reach 28 cm in Smilodon populator.

Biology and hunting

Despite their fearsome teeth, saber canines were relatively fragile and could break if they struck bone. Studies of skull and neck anatomy suggest these cats used powerful forelimbs to grapple and immobilise large prey, then delivered a precise bite to the throat with the mouth opened at a very wide gape — up to 120 degrees in Smilodon, compared with about 65 degrees in a modern lion. Their bite force at the canines was comparatively weak, compensated by strong neck muscles driving the head downward.

Extinction

The last saber-toothed cats vanished during the Quaternary extinction event at the end of the last ice age, alongside much of the megafauna on which they specialised. Climate change and human activity are both implicated. Thousands of Smilodon fatalis specimens have been recovered from the La Brea Tar Pits in Los Angeles.
