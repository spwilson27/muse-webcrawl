The Lacrimosa (Latin for "weeping" or "tearful") is the final stanza of the Dies Irae sequence in the Roman Catholic Requiem Mass. Its text, describing the tearful day of judgment and pleading for mercy, has been set to music by countless composers of Requiems, and the word has become a byword for mournful, lachrymose music.

Lacrimosa dies illa,
Qua resurget ex favilla
Judicandus homo reus.
Huic ergo parce, Deus...

"Tearful will be that day, on which from the ashes shall rise the guilty man to be judged; spare him therefore, O God."

In Mozart's Requiem

The Lacrimosa of Mozart's Requiem in D minor, K. 626, is among the most famous passages in Western music. Mozart died on 5 December 1791 having composed only the first eight bars of the Lacrimosa's vocal parts and supporting material; according to tradition he broke down weeping while working on it. The movement was completed by his pupil Franz Xaver Süssmayr for the version of the Requiem delivered to the anonymous commissioner, Count Franz von Walsegg, and its swelling, sighing figures have made it the emotional climax of the work.

Other settings

Notable Lacrimosas appear in the Requiems of Berlioz, Verdi (whose dramatic setting forms part of his Messa da Requiem), Fauré, Dvořák, Britten (interwoven with Wilfred Owen's poetry in the War Requiem), and Zbigniew Preisner, whose Lacrimosa from his Requiem for My Friend achieved wide popular fame.
