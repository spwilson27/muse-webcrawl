Melarsoprol

Melarsoprol is an arsenical drug used in the treatment of late-stage African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness), particularly the form caused by Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense that has invaded the central nervous system. It is one of the oldest and most dangerous drugs still in front-line use, and it is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Type: trypanocide (arsenical)
Use: second-stage sleeping sickness
Route: intravenous
Serious risk: reactive encephalopathy

History

Melarsoprol was developed in 1949 by the Swiss chemist Ernst Friedheim, who combined the arsenical melarsen oxide with the chelating agent dimercaprol (BAL) to reduce toxicity. For decades it was the only drug that could cross the blood–brain barrier and cure second-stage disease caused by T. b. rhodesiense, and it remained essential long after safer drugs appeared for the gambiense form.

Mechanism

The drug's trivalent arsenic binds the parasite's trypanothione metabolism: melarsoprol reacts with trypanothione to form an adduct (Mel T) that inhibits trypanothione reductase, crippling the trypanosome's defence against oxidative damage.

Toxicity

Melarsoprol is notorious for its side effects. The most feared is reactive encephalopathy, an immune-mediated brain inflammation that occurs in roughly 3–10% of patients and is fatal in about half of cases. Overall, the drug itself kills an estimated 3–5% of patients treated. Its solvent, propylene glycol, is also painful on injection. Newer, safer treatments such as fexinidazole and the combination therapy NECT have reduced reliance on melarsoprol for gambiense disease, but it remains a mainstay for rhodesiense infections.
