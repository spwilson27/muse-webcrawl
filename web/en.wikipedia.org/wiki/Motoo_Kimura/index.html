Motoo Kimura (木村 資生, November 13, 1924 – November 13, 1994) was a Japanese theoretical biologist and population geneticist. He is best known for the neutral theory of molecular evolution, proposed in 1968, which holds that most evolutionary changes at the molecular level are caused by genetic drift of mutant alleles that are selectively neutral, rather than by natural selection.

Career

Kimura studied botany at Kyoto University before turning to genetics under the influence of Hitoshi Kihara. He spent time in the United States working with Sewall Wright and others, and returned to Japan's National Institute of Genetics in Mishima, where he spent most of his career developing mathematical models of allele frequency change.

The neutral theory

Observing that the rate of amino-acid substitution in proteins was roughly constant and too high to be explained by selection alone, Kimura argued that the majority of mutations are neutral. The theory transformed evolutionary biology by providing a "null hypothesis" for molecular evolution and underpinning the molecular clock concept.

Honours

Kimura was elected a foreign member of the Royal Society and the U.S. National Academy of Sciences, and received the Darwin Medal in 1992.
