Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid

Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is an aminopolycarboxylic acid with the formula [CH2N(CH2CO2H)2]2. This white, water-soluble solid is widely used to dissolve limescale and, above all, as a chelating agent: its molecule can bind metal ions through six donor atoms (two nitrogen and four oxygen), wrapping around a single ion in an octahedral complex and thereby sequestering it.

Synthesis and history

EDTA was first prepared in 1935 by Ferdinand Münz at IG Farben, who sought a substitute for citric acid in the German textile industry. Commercial production grew rapidly after the Second World War, and EDTA became the archetypal chelating agent. Annual production amounts to tens of thousands of tonnes.

Applications

EDTA binds Fe3+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Pb2+ and many other metal ions with high affinity. In industry it is used to soften water, prevent metal-catalysed oxidation, and stabilise products ranging from detergents and cosmetics to processed foods, where it preserves colour and flavour by removing trace metals. In the laboratory it is the titrant of choice in complexometric titrations for measuring water hardness.

In medicine, the calcium-disodium salt (calcium disodium EDTA) is used to treat lead poisoning, carrying the metal out in the urine; disodium EDTA is also an anticoagulant for blood samples, since it chelates the calcium needed for clotting. Chelation therapy for cardiovascular disease, by contrast, is not supported by mainstream evidence. EDTA is poorly biodegradable and persists in the environment, where it can remobilise heavy metals — a concern that has driven interest in biodegradable alternatives such as MGDA and GLDA.
