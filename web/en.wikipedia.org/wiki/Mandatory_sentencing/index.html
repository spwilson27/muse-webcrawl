Mandatory sentencing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mandatory sentencing requires that offenders serve a predefined term of imprisonment for specified crimes, removing or restricting judicial discretion in sentencing. Instruments include mandatory minima (floors below which judges may not go), three-strikes laws (California's of 1994, moderated by referendum in 2012), truth-in-sentencing rules, and the mandatory death or life sentences some systems impose for murder (whose mandatory death penalty the Privy Council and several constitutional courts have struck down as inhuman in Caribbean and African cases).

Mandatory penalties expand in waves of penal populism (the US federal anti-drug minima of 1986–88, with their 100-to-1 crack/powder disparity, reduced to 18-to-1 in 2010) and contract as their costs accumulate: discretion shifts from judges to prosecutors (who choose the charge), racial and geographic disparities widen, prisons fill, and marginal deterrence is doubted by the research consensus (see Deterrence (penology)). Reform — safety valves, judicial departure power, retroactive relief (the US First Step Act, 2018) — has been a rare bipartisan cause in the United States, while many systems (including England and Wales's mandatory life sentence for murder, with judicial tariff-setting) retain mandatory frames softened by judicial calibration.
