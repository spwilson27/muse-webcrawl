Theoretical physics

Theoretical physics is a branch of physics that employs mathematical models and abstractions of physical objects and systems to rationalise, explain and predict natural phenomena. It is contrasted with experimental physics, which uses experimental tools to probe those phenomena. Theoretical physicists develop mathematical frameworks — such as classical mechanics, electromagnetism, quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics and general relativity — from which testable predictions can be derived. Progress in science often depends on the interplay of the two: a theory may predict phenomena decades before instruments can detect them, as when general relativity predicted gravitational waves (1915) a century before their direct detection (2015).

Overview

Theoretical physics begins with a set of assumptions or postulates and works out their logical consequences mathematically. Unlike a purely mathematical theory, a physical theory must connect to measurable quantities; its worth is judged by the accuracy, range and economy of its predictions. In practice theorists use a mixture of analytic work, order-of-magnitude estimates, and large-scale computation. Some theoretical work is closely tied to current experiments ("phenomenology"), while other work explores mathematical structures — string theory, for example — whose experimental signatures, if any, are remote.

History

Mathematical description of nature began in earnest with astronomy and with Newton's Principia (1687), which unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries produced analytical mechanics (Lagrange, Hamilton), the field theory of electromagnetism (Maxwell, 1865) and thermodynamics and statistical mechanics (Clausius, Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs). The twentieth century brought two revolutions: relativity (Einstein, 1905 and 1915) and quantum mechanics (Planck, Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac and others, 1900–1930). Their combination in quantum field theory underlies the Standard Model of particle physics, completed in the 1970s. Theoretical physics became a distinct professional specialisation in the early twentieth century, institutionally centred in places such as Göttingen, Copenhagen, Cambridge and Princeton.

Mainstream and frontier theories

Well-established frameworks include general relativity (gravity and cosmology), quantum mechanics and quantum field theory, the Standard Model, and the ΛCDM model of cosmology. Open problems driving current research include the reconciliation of gravity with quantum mechanics (quantum gravity), the nature of dark matter and dark energy, the origin of neutrino masses, the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe, and the foundations of quantum mechanics. Proposed frameworks such as string theory, loop quantum gravity and various grand unified theories aim to extend or unify the established picture.
