Shamela

An Apology for the Life of Mrs. Shamela Andrews, usually known simply as Shamela, is a satirical novella published anonymously in April 1741. It is a parody of Samuel Richardson's hugely successful epistolary novel Pamela; or, Virtue Rewarded (1740), and it is generally attributed to Henry Fielding, who had already burlesqued Pamela in verse ("Pamela Censured" debates and the pamphlet wars of 1741) and would attack Richardson again in Joseph Andrews (1742), which began as another Pamela parody.

Content

Where Richardson's Pamela Andrews is a fifteen-year-old servant girl whose chastity, besieged by her master Mr. B, is rewarded with marriage, Fielding's Shamela is a knowing, manipulative "sham" — the title punning on sham and Pamela. In her letters Shamela reveals that her supposed virtue is a calculated performance designed to trap her master (here Mr. Booby) into marriage; that her "Vartue" is a commodity she prices and schedules with her mother and adviser; and that she maintains a lover, Parson Williams, on the side — in Fielding's version she is no innocent but the mother of Williams's child. The clergymen who champion Pamela in Richardson's orbit appear as venal hypocrites, and the book revels in exposing the sexual and mercenary machinery Richardson's moral fable pretended not to have.

Significance

Shamela is a landmark of literary parody and of the anti-Pamela controversy that reshaped the early English novel. Critics prize it as arguably the wittiest of the Pamela satires — Fielding's compressed letters skewer Richardson's style, sentimentality, and bourgeois sexual economics. The work helped establish Fielding's comic-epistolary technique, and its heroine's name became shorthand for sham virtue. Modern editions typically print Shamela together with Joseph Andrews.
