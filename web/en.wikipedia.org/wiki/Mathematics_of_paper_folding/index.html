Mathematics of paper folding

The mathematics of paper folding explores the geometric and algebraic structures that arise from folding a flat sheet of paper, a subject closely connected with the art of origami. Although paper folding had been practised for centuries as a craft, it was only in the late twentieth century that it received rigorous mathematical treatment, transforming it into a field with applications in engineering, robotics, and computational geometry.

History

Early mathematical interest in paper folding appeared in works such as T. Sundara Row's Geometric Exercises in Paper Folding (1893), which used folding to make classical constructions accessible. In the 1980s and 1990s, researchers including Humiaki Huzita, Jacques Justin, and Koshiro Hatori formalised the basic operations of origami into a small set of axioms. Robert J. Lang and others subsequently developed computational methods for designing complex origami figures from crease patterns.

The Huzita–Hatori axioms

The Huzita–Hatori axioms describe the seven distinct ways a single crease can be formed by aligning points and lines on a sheet of paper. The first six were enumerated by Huzita in 1989; the seventh was discovered independently by Hatori and others. These axioms are more powerful than the classical compass-and-straightedge constructions: they allow the trisection of angles and the doubling of the cube, because folding can solve general cubic equations, whereas Euclidean tools solve only quadratics.

Flat folding

A central question is when a crease pattern can be folded flat. Maekawa's theorem states that at any interior vertex of a flat-foldable pattern, the numbers of valley and mountain folds differ by exactly two. Kawasaki's theorem states that a crease pattern folds flat at a vertex if and only if the alternating sum of its sector angles equals 180 degrees. Deciding flat foldability of a whole pattern is NP-hard, a result associated with Marshall Bern and Erik Demaine.

Rigid origami and applications

Rigid origami treats the faces between creases as rigid plates joined by hinges. Patterns such as the Miura fold, originally developed for deployable solar arrays, are now used in satellite design, medical stents, packaging, and metamaterials. Folding algorithms by Demaine, Lang, and others allow a computer to compute crease patterns for arbitrary three-dimensional forms.
