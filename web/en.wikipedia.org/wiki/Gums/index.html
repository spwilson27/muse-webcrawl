Gums

The gums, or gingiva (singular gingiva), are the soft mucosal tissues that surround the teeth and cover the alveolar processes of the jaws. Together with the periodontal ligament, cementum, and alveolar bone, the gums form the periodontium, the supporting structure of the teeth.

Anatomy

The gingiva is divided into three types: the marginal (free) gingiva, which forms a collar around each tooth; the attached gingiva, firmly bound to the underlying bone and stippled in texture; and the interdental gingiva (papilla), which fills the spaces between adjacent teeth. Healthy gums are typically coral pink in colour — though pigmentation varies with melanin — firm in consistency, and knife-edged at the margin where they meet the tooth. The shallow groove between the tooth and the free gingiva is called the gingival sulcus.

Function

The gums seal and protect the deeper periodontal tissues from mechanical injury and bacterial invasion, providing a barrier against the oral environment while anchoring the teeth in their sockets.

Diseases

Inflammation of the gums, gingivitis, is caused by bacterial plaque accumulation and is reversible with good oral hygiene. Untreated, it may progress to periodontitis, in which the attachment and bone supporting the teeth are destroyed. Gums may also recede (gingival recession), exposing tooth roots, and can be affected by systemic conditions such as scurvy, a vitamin C deficiency that historically caused swollen, bleeding gums in sailors.
