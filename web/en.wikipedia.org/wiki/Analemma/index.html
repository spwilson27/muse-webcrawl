In astronomy, an analemma is a diagram showing the position of the Sun in the sky as seen from a fixed location on Earth at the same mean solar time over the course of a year. The resulting curve resembles a long, slender figure eight, with one lobe larger than the other. The analemma can be captured photographically by exposing images at the same time of day at intervals of several days over a year on a single frame.

Causes

The shape results from two effects. First, the tilt of Earth's axis (about 23.4°) relative to its orbital plane makes the Sun's declination vary between +23.4° and −23.4° over the year, producing the north–south extent of the curve. Second, the eccentricity of Earth's orbit makes the apparent solar time run ahead of or behind mean clock time by up to about 16 minutes — the equation of time — displacing the Sun east or west of its mean position and giving the curve its east–west width. Because Earth's perihelion (early January) falls near the December solstice, the two effects combine to make the southern (winter, in the northern hemisphere) lobe of the figure larger than the northern lobe.

On globes and sundials

Analemmas are often printed on terrestrial globes, typically in the Pacific Ocean, where there is empty space, usually accompanied by the equation of time for correcting sundial readings. Sundial makers may engrave an analemma-shaped correction scale so that a gnomon's shadow can be read as mean time rather than apparent solar time.

Other planets

Every planet has an analemma, but its shape depends on the planet's axial tilt, orbital eccentricity, and the timing of perihelion relative to the solstices. Mars has a teardrop-shaped analemma; Jupiter's is a simple ellipse; Saturn's combines a small loop with a large ellipse, resembling a teardrop.
