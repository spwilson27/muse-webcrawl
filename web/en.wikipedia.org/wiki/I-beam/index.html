I-beam

I-beam
Also called: H-beam, universal beam, rolled steel joist
Components: Two flanges joined by a web
Materials: Structural steel, aluminum, timber (engineered joists)
Uses: Building frames, bridges, machinery

An I-beam, also known as an H-beam, universal beam (UB), or rolled steel joist (RSJ), is a beam with an I- or H-shaped cross-section. The horizontal elements of the section are called flanges, and the vertical element connecting them is the web. I-beams are usually made of structural steel and are used in construction and civil engineering as primary load-bearing members.

Design

The I-section is efficient because it places most material in the flanges, far from the neutral axis, where bending stresses are greatest, giving a high second moment of area and bending stiffness for its weight. The flanges resist the bending moment, while the thinner web primarily resists shear forces and spaces the flanges apart. The same geometry makes wide-flange sections weaker in torsion and lateral buckling than closed sections, so long beams may need lateral bracing.

Types and standards

Standard shapes include American wide-flange (W) shapes, standard (S) beams with sloped inner flange faces, and European IPE and HE (HEA/HEB) profiles. Sizes are designated by nominal depth and weight per unit length. Engineered wood I-joists use laminated veneer lumber flanges with an oriented strand board web, widely used for floors.

History

The method of producing an I-beam from a single piece of rolled steel was patented by Alphonse Halbou of the firm Forges de la Providence in 1849. Rolled wrought-iron and then steel beams enabled the skeleton-frame construction that made skyscrapers possible in the late 19th century.
