Impulse control

Impulse control is the ability to resist temptations, urges, or impulses that might otherwise lead to behaviour that is harmful, premature, or socially inappropriate. It is one of the central components of self-control and of the executive functions carried out largely by the prefrontal cortex of the brain.

Psychology

In psychology, impulse control is studied as part of personality, development, and psychopathology. Children gradually acquire impulse control as the prefrontal cortex matures, a process that continues into early adulthood. Poor impulse control is associated with conditions such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), substance use disorders, and the impulse control disorders recognised in psychiatric classification, which include intermittent explosive disorder, kleptomania, and pyromania.

Neuroscience

Neuroimaging studies link impulse control to activity in the ventromedial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and to signalling by the neurotransmitters dopamine and serotonin. Delay of gratification experiments, such as the Stanford marshmallow experiment, have been used to measure individual differences in the capacity to inhibit impulses in favour of larger later rewards.

Disorders and treatment

When impulse control fails persistently and causes distress or impairment, clinicians may diagnose an impulse control disorder. Treatments include cognitive behavioural therapy, which teaches patients to identify triggers and rehearse alternative responses, and medication in some cases. Impulse control problems can also arise as a side effect of dopaminergic drugs used to treat Parkinson's disease.
