Heliades

In Greek mythology, the Heliades (Ancient Greek: Ἡλιάδες, "children of the sun") were the daughters of the sun god Helios and the Oceanid Clymene, and sisters of Phaethon. Their names, as variously given by Hyginus and other sources, include Merope, Helia, Aegle, Lampetia, Phoebe, Aetheria and Dioxippe.

Myth

When their brother Phaethon, attempting to drive the chariot of the sun, lost control and was struck down by Zeus to save the earth from burning, the Heliades gathered at the river Eridanus, into which his body had fallen, and mourned him without ceasing. In their grief the gods transformed them into poplar (or alder) trees, and their tears into amber—an aetiological myth explaining the amber found along the Eridanus, identified with the Po or the Rhône. The story is told most famously in Book 2 of Ovid's Metamorphoses; it also appears in Apollonius Rhodius' Argonautica, where the Argonauts hear the sisters' lament, and is reflected in Euripides' lost play Phaethon. A related male figure, the Heliadae, were the seven sons of Helios and Rhodos in Rhodian tradition, credited with the invention of astronomy and navigation.
