Mountain pass theorem

The mountain pass theorem is an existence theorem from the calculus of variations, proved by Antonio Ambrosetti and Paul Rabinowitz in 1973. It guarantees the existence of a saddle point — a critical point that is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum — for a sufficiently well-behaved functional defined on a Banach space. The theorem is a fundamental tool in the study of nonlinear partial differential equations, where it is used to prove the existence of nontrivial solutions.

Intuition

The name comes from a geographical picture. Imagine a traveller who wants to walk from a point in one valley to a point in another valley, separated by a ring of high mountains. Whatever path the traveller chooses, it must cross the mountain ring at some highest point. Among all possible paths, consider the one whose highest point is as low as possible; that optimal crossing point is a mountain pass. The theorem asserts that, under suitable conditions, such a minimax point must be a critical point of the elevation function.

Statement

Let I be a C1 functional on a Banach space satisfying the Palais–Smale compactness condition. Suppose I(0) = 0, and suppose there are constants ρ, α > 0 such that I ≥ α on the sphere of radius ρ about 0, while there exists a point e outside that sphere with I(e) ≤ 0. Then the minimax value

c = inf over paths γ from 0 to e of maxt∈[0,1] I(γ(t))

satisfies c ≥ α and is a critical value of I; that is, there exists a point u with I(u) = c and I′(u) = 0.

Applications

The mountain pass theorem is widely used to obtain solutions of semilinear elliptic equations, Hamiltonian systems and other nonlinear problems where variational methods apply. Variants without the Palais–Smale condition, and refinements locating multiple critical points, have been developed by many authors.
