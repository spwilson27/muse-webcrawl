Guilt (emotion)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Guilt is a moral emotion that occurs when an individual believes or realises — accurately or not — that they have compromised their own standards of conduct or have violated universal moral standards, and bear significant responsibility for that violation. It is closely related to the concept of remorse, as well as shame, in that it is characterised by a painful appraisal of a specific behaviour and motivates reparative action.

Psychology

Psychologists distinguish guilt, focused on a behaviour ("I did a bad thing"), from shame, focused on the self ("I am bad"). Because it attaches to actions rather than identity, guilt is generally considered more adaptive: experimental research shows guilt-prone people are more empathic, take others' perspectives more readily, and are less prone to anger and aggression. Psychoanalytic theory, beginning with Freud, linked guilt to the superego and the resolution of the Oedipus complex.

Excessive and irrational guilt

Disproportionate or misplaced guilt is a feature of several disorders: it appears in depression, obsessive–compulsive disorder (including scrupulosity), post-traumatic stress disorder and the "survivor guilt" felt by those who live through disasters that killed others. Therapies typically work by testing the person's actual responsibility and encouraging apology, restitution or self-forgiveness.

Cultural and religious dimensions

Anthropologists have contrasted "guilt cultures", in which behaviour is regulated by internal conscience, with "shame cultures" regulated by public honour — a distinction associated with Ruth Benedict and now considered an oversimplification. Major religions institutionalise guilt through concepts of sin, confession, penance and atonement.
