Hydrogel

A hydrogel is a network of cross-linked hydrophilic polymer chains that can absorb and retain large amounts of water — sometimes more than 99% water by weight — while maintaining its structure. The term was introduced in the literature in the 1960s, following the pioneering work of Otto Wichterle and Drahoslav Lím, who developed poly(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) hydrogels in 1960, leading to soft contact lenses.

Properties

Hydrogels may be formed by chemical (covalent) or physical cross-linking. Natural hydrogels include agar, gelatin, alginate, collagen, and hyaluronic acid; synthetic examples include polyacrylamide, polyvinyl alcohol, and polyethylene glycol gels. "Smart" hydrogels swell or shrink in response to stimuli such as temperature, pH, ionic strength, or light. Superabsorbent polymers, used in disposable diapers, are highly cross-linked polyelectrolyte hydrogels.

Applications

Applications include contact lenses and intraocular lenses, wound dressings, drug-delivery systems, tissue-engineering scaffolds, absorbent hygiene products, water-retaining granules in agriculture, electrophoresis gels, sensors, and soft robotics. Hydrogels that combine high toughness with stretchability ("double-network" gels) are an active research area.
