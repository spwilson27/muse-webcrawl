Brickwork
MaterialBricks and mortar
CraftBricklaying, masonry
Common bondsStretcher bond, Flemish bond, English bond, header bond
UsesWalls, paving, chimneys, arches, tunnels

Brickwork is masonry produced by a bricklayer using bricks and mortar. Typically, rows of bricks called courses are laid on top of one another to build up structures such as walls. Brick is a versatile and durable material with a history stretching back thousands of years to the mud-brick and fired-brick architecture of the ancient Near East; brickwork remains one of the most common construction methods worldwide.

Terminology

A brick laid with its long face exposed is a stretcher; with its short end exposed, a header. The horizontal mortar joint is the bed, and vertical joints are perpends. Cut pieces used to close bonds include queen closers and bats. A wall one brick thick is described by its width in brick lengths or by nominal dimensions, and wythe (in North American usage) denotes a vertical section one unit thick.

Bonds

The pattern in which bricks overlap — the bond — distributes loads and prevents continuous vertical joints. In stretcher bond, all bricks are stretchers, typical of modern cavity walls. English bond alternates courses of headers and stretchers and is exceptionally strong; Flemish bond alternates header and stretcher within each course, giving a chequered face; other patterns include English cross bond, Dutch bond, monk bond, and decorative herringbone for paving. Brickwork is finished with struck, weathered, flush or recessed pointing, which strongly affects the wall's appearance and weather resistance.

Structural behaviour and modern practice

Brick masonry is strong in compression but weak in tension, so traditional brickwork relies on mass and geometry — thick walls, piers, buttresses and arches — while modern practice adds reinforcement, ties between the leaves of cavity walls, and movement joints to accommodate thermal and moisture expansion. Fired clay brickwork is fire-resistant and long-lived; notable historic brick architecture ranges from Mesopotamian ziggurats and Roman brick-faced concrete to the Brick Gothic of northern Europe and the great railway viaducts of the industrial age.
