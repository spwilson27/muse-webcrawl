Cognac

Cognac (French pronunciation: [kɔɲak]) is a variety of brandy named after the commune of Cognac, France. It is produced in the surrounding wine-growing region in the departments of Charente and Charente-Maritime. Under French appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC) rules, cognac must be made from specified grape varieties, distilled twice in copper pot stills, and aged at least two years in French oak barrels from Limousin or Tronçais.

Production

The base wine, made chiefly from Ugni blanc grapes, is thin and acidic. It is distilled in copper alembics in two stages: the first produces the cloudy brouillis (about 28–32% alcohol), and the second distillation, the bonne chauffe, isolates the heart of the spirit, the eau-de-vie, at up to 72% ABV. The eau-de-vie is aged in oak casks, where a portion — the part des anges, "angels' share" — evaporates each year. The master blender (maître de chai) marries eaux-de-vie of different ages and crus to produce a consistent house style.

Grades and crus

Age designations are governed by the youngest eau-de-vie in the blend: VS (Very Special, at least two years), VSOP (Very Superior Old Pale, at least four years), and XO (Extra Old, at least ten years since 2018). The delimited region is divided into six crus, with Grande Champagne and Petite Champagne considered the finest; a blend of the two with at least half Grande Champagne may be labelled Fine Champagne.

History and houses

Dutch merchants began distilling the local wine in the seventeenth century so it would survive sea transport; the double-distilled product proved better than the original wine. The great houses — Hennessy (founded 1765 by the Irish officer Richard Hennessy), Martell (1715), Rémy Martin (1724), and Courvoisier — dominate an export trade in which the overwhelming majority of production is sold abroad, notably to the United States and China.
