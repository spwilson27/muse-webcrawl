Dumping (pricing policy)

In international trade, dumping is a pricing policy in which a company exports a product at a price lower than the price it normally charges in its own home market, or below its cost of production. Under the rules of the World Trade Organization, dumping itself is not illegal, but importing countries may impose anti-dumping duties when dumping causes or threatens material injury to a domestic industry.

Definition and measurement

Dumping is measured by comparing the export price with the "normal value" of the product — usually the price in the exporter's home market, or a constructed value based on production costs plus a reasonable profit margin. The difference is the dumping margin. Investigations are typically initiated on complaint by a domestic industry and conducted by national trade authorities, such as the U.S. Department of Commerce and International Trade Commission, or the European Commission.

Types and motives

Economists distinguish persistent dumping, based on price discrimination between markets with different demand elasticities; sporadic dumping, the disposal of surplus stock; and predatory dumping, in which a firm prices below cost to drive out competitors and later raise prices. Critics argue that anti-dumping measures are frequently used as a form of protectionism, while supporters regard them as a necessary remedy against unfair trade practices, particularly where exporters benefit from state subsidies or non-market-economy cost structures.
