Surreal number

The surreal numbers grow in a binary-tree-like construction

The surreal numbers are a class of numbers that includes the real numbers, the ordinal numbers, and many infinite and infinitesimal quantities. They were invented by John Horton Conway around 1970 while analysing the endgames of Go, and popularised in Donald Knuth's 1974 novella Surreal Numbers. Conway named them "surreal" in homage to Salvador Dalí's surrealism.

Construction

A surreal number is written {L | R}, where L and R are sets of previously created surreal numbers, with the condition that no member of R is less than or equal to any member of L. Numbers are created in "days": on day 0, the number 0 = { | } is born. On day 1 come 1 = {0 | } and −1 = { | 0}. Later days generate dyadic fractions such as 1/2 = {0 | 1}, integers of ever greater size, and eventually the infinite ordinal ω = {0, 1, 2, 3, ... | } and the infinitesimal ε = {0 | 1, 1/2, 1/4, ...}.

Properties

The surreals form an ordered field — in fact a proper class rather than a set — containing the reals and the ordinals as substructures. Every real number is a surreal, but the surreals also include infinitely large numbers such as ω and infinitely small ones such as 1/ω. Conway showed that the rules of combinatorial game theory are embedded in the construction: each surreal is a game position, and games such as Hackenbush correspond to numbers. The class of all surreals is sometimes denoted No.

Applications

Beyond game theory, surreal numbers have been explored in nonstandard analysis as an alternative to hyperreal numbers, and Conway's construction inspired the theory of "sign sequences" and the Gonshor extension of surreal exponentiation.
