Gabriel's horn, formed by rotating y = 1/x about the x-axis

Gabriel's horn (also called Torricelli's trumpet) is a geometric figure formed by rotating the graph of y = 1/x, for x ≥ 1, about the x-axis. Discovered by Evangelista Torricelli in 1643, it has the striking property of enclosing a finite volume while having an infinite surface area.

The paradox

Using calculus, the volume can be computed as π cubic units — exactly the volume of a cylinder of radius 1 and height 1 — while the surface area integral diverges, giving an infinite area. This leads to the popular "painter's paradox": the horn could be filled with a finite amount of paint, yet painting its inner surface would require infinitely much. The paradox dissolves on noting that a mathematical surface has zero thickness, whereas real paint coats a layer of finite thickness, so a "filled" horn is automatically coated.

Name

The name refers to the Archangel Gabriel, who in tradition blows a horn to announce Judgement Day, associating the infinitely long horn with the divine. Torricelli's result, obtained before calculus was formalised, startled contemporaries including Hobbes and helped motivate the development of integration theory.
