Queen Anne style architecture

Queen Anne style architecture was one of a number of popular Victorian architectural styles that emerged in the United States and other Anglophone countries from roughly 1880 to 1910, and in Britain from the 1870s to the 1900s. Despite the name, it has little to do with the reign of Queen Anne (1702–1714); the British version drew on a free, picturesque interpretation of earlier English architecture, while the American version developed into one of the most elaborate and asymmetric domestic styles ever built.

British Queen Anne

The British style was launched by architects such as Richard Norman Shaw and W. E. Nesfield, who combined Dutch and Flemish gables, red brick, sash windows and oriel windows in a "free" Renaissance manner — seen in Shaw's Bedford Park, London's first garden suburb.

American Queen Anne

The American style, spread by pattern books and the first generation of professional architects, is defined by:

  • Steeply pitched, irregular roofs with cross-gables, dormers and towers or turrets;
  • Wrap-around porches with turned spindles and brackets;
  • Varied wall textures — patterned shingles, half-timbering, brick and stone on the same facade;
  • Asymmetrical facades, stained-glass windows and prominent chimneys.

Elaborate spindlework gave rise to the "Eastlake" variant. The style fell from favour around 1910 with the rise of the Colonial Revival and bungalow, but survives as the archetypal American Victorian house — the San Francisco "Painted Ladies" are a famous row.
