Terrace (agriculture)

Terrace (agriculture)
PurposeEnables farming on slopes; controls erosion and water runoff
Famous examplesBanaue rice terraces (Philippines), Ifugao; Andean terraces; Yuanyang terraces (China)
TypesBench terraces, contour terraces, paddy terraces, dry stone terraces

In agriculture, a terrace is a levelled section of a hilly cultivated area, designed to slow surface runoff, conserve soil and make farming possible on steep slopes. Terracing is one of humanity's oldest land-management techniques, practised independently in Asia, the Mediterranean, Africa and the pre-Columbian Americas.

Forms and construction

Bench terraces are formed by cutting into a slope to create flat platforms retained by earthen banks or stone walls. In wet-rice regions such as the Philippines, southern China, Vietnam, Bali and Japan, flooded paddy terraces are engineered with bunds and shared irrigation channels that move water step by step down the hillside. In the Andes, Inca and pre-Inca peoples built stone-faced terraces (andenes) that also created frost-buffering microclimates for experimental crops, as at Moray. Mediterranean dry terraces support olives, vines and citrus.

Benefits and decline

Terraces reduce erosion, increase water infiltration and create arable land in mountains, but they demand heavy maintenance labour. Rural depopulation has led to abandonment and collapse of terraces in many regions, worsening erosion and landslide risk; conversely, several terrace landscapes are now protected as cultural heritage, including the Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras and the Honghe Hani Terraces, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Modern use

Soil-conservation programmes promote contour terracing on mechanised farmland in the Americas and China, and terracing remains central to mountain agriculture worldwide.
