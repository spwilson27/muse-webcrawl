Consumer choice

The theory of consumer choice is the branch of microeconomics that relates preferences to consumption expenditures and consumer demand curves. It models how consumers, subject to a budget constraint, allocate income among goods and services to maximise utility.

Basic model

Consumers choose a bundle of goods given prices and income. Preferences are represented by indifference curves and, if well-behaved, by a utility function. The optimum occurs where the budget line is tangent to the highest attainable indifference curve — where the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio.

Effects of changes

  • Income effect and substitution effect decompose price changes (Slutsky and Hicks decompositions).
  • Normal vs inferior goods; Giffen goods as a theoretical exception to the law of demand.
  • Consumer surplus measures welfare gain.

Extensions and criticism

Revealed preference theory (Paul Samuelson) derives preferences from observed choices without assuming utility functions. Behavioural economics challenges the model with bounded rationality, framing effects, choice overload, and biases such as confirmation bias. Intertemporal choice, uncertainty (expected utility), and household models extend the framework.
