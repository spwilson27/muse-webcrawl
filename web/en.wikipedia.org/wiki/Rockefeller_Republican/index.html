Rockefeller Republican

IdeologyModerate-to-liberal Republicanism; fiscal moderation; internationalism

The term Rockefeller Republican refers to a member of the Republican Party (United States) holding moderate-to-liberal views on domestic issues, similar to those of Nelson Rockefeller, governor of New York from 1959 to 1973 and vice president under Gerald Ford. Rockefeller Republicans were typically fiscally moderate, supportive of organized labour and civil rights, internationalist in foreign policy, and concentrated in the Northeastern United States.

Beliefs

Rockefeller Republicans accepted much of the New Deal regulatory and welfare framework, supported infrastructure spending and public universities, backed the Civil Rights Movement earlier and more consistently than Southern Democrats, and favoured an active, anti-communist but internationalist foreign policy. Figures associated with the faction include Thomas Dewey, Dwight D. Eisenhower (in part), Henry Cabot Lodge Jr., Jacob Javits, Clifford Case, Edward Brooke, and William Scranton.

Rise and decline

The faction dominated Northeastern Republican politics and contested control of the national party with its conservative wing led by Robert A. Taft and, later, Barry Goldwater. Goldwater's capture of the 1964 nomination — at whose convention Rockefeller was famously booed — marked the conservatives' ascendancy; Rockefeller himself served as Gerald Ford's vice president (1974–1977) but was dropped from the 1976 ticket under conservative pressure. The election of Ronald Reagan in 1980 consolidated conservative dominance, and as the parties sorted ideologically the moderate Republican faction shrank dramatically; by the 21st century the term survived mainly as a historical label or a pejorative (comparable to "RINO") applied to remaining moderates such as those in New England.

Legacy

Historians see the Rockefeller Republicans as evidence that the modern alignment of "liberal Democrat / conservative Republican" is a recent development. Surviving analogues are sometimes called "Main Street Republicans," distinct from the older "Wall Street" moderate establishment the term originally described.
