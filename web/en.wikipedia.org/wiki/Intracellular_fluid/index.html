Intracellular fluid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diagram of intracellular and extracellular fluid compartments

The intracellular fluid (ICF) is all fluid contained within the cells of the body. It is the largest of the body's fluid compartments: in a typical adult human, total body water is about 60% of body mass, of which roughly two-thirds (about 40% of body weight, or some 28 litres) is intracellular, the remainder forming the extracellular fluid.

Composition

Intracellular fluid is rich in potassium (K+), magnesium and phosphate ions, and contains a high concentration of proteins, whereas the extracellular fluid is rich in sodium and chloride. This ionic asymmetry is maintained by membrane transport processes, above all the sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase), and is essential for cell volume regulation, electrical excitability and many metabolic processes.

Function

The cytosol—the fluid matrix of the cell in which organelles are suspended—is the site of most metabolic activity, including glycolysis, protein synthesis and many signalling pathways. Movement of water between the intracellular and extracellular compartments is governed by osmotic gradients across the cell membrane.
