Elsa Triolet (born Elsa Yuryevna Kagan, Russian: Эльза Юрьевна Каган; 24 September 1896 – 16 June 1970) was a Russian-French novelist and translator, and the first woman to win the Prix Goncourt. She was the younger sister of Lilya Brik and the wife and muse of the French poet and novelist Louis Aragon.

Life

Born in Moscow into a Jewish family of Latvian origin, Elsa Kagan studied architecture and moved in the circles of the Russian avant-garde; the Futurist poet Vladimir Mayakovsky was in love with her sister Lilya and Elsa herself translated his work. Her first marriage, to the French cavalry officer André Triolet, took her to Tahiti and then Paris; her first books, written in Russian (In Tahiti, 1925; Strawberries and Other Tales), grew out of that period.

In Paris in November 1928 she met Louis Aragon at the café La Coupole; they married in 1939 and remained together for the rest of their lives. Aragon's celebrated poetry cycle Les Yeux d'Elsa ("Elsa's Eyes", 1942) took its name from her. During the German occupation, Triolet was active in the French Resistance and in clandestine publishing circles, and her short-story collection Le premier accroc coûte deux cents francs ("The First Tear Costs Two Hundred Francs", the coded phrase announcing the Normandy landings) won the Prix Goncourt for 1944, awarded in 1945 — making her the prize's first female laureate.

Works

Writing increasingly in French, Triolet produced a substantial body of fiction, including Bonsoir Thérèse (1938), Le Cheval blanc (1943), Le Rendez-vous des étrangers (1956), Le Grand Jamais (1965), and Écoutez-voir (1968). She also wrote a biography of Chekhov, worked as a journalist, translated Russian literature into French (including Chekhov and Mayakovsky), and championed Franco-Soviet cultural exchange. Together with Aragon she was a prominent figure of the French Communist cultural world, though she later expressed disillusionment with Stalinism, notably over the treatment of her sister and brother-in-law Osip Brik's circle.
