Carburizing

[Image placeholder: Glowing steel gear being case-hardened in a carburizing furnace]

Carburizing, or carburising, is a heat treatment process in which iron or steel absorbs carbon while the metal is heated in the presence of a carbon-bearing material, such as charcoal or carbon monoxide. The intent is to make the metal harder by producing a case — a hard, wear-resistant outer surface layer — over a tougher, more ductile core. The process is one of the oldest forms of case hardening.

Process

In modern practice, parts are heated to around 900 °C (1,650 °F) in a carbon-rich atmosphere. Carbon diffuses into the surface, with case depth (typically 0.1 to 1.5 mm) depending on time and temperature, following an approximately square-root relationship with time. After carburizing, parts are quenched and tempered to develop the final hard microstructure of the case.

Methods

  • Pack carburizing — parts are packed in a carbonaceous powder such as charcoal in a sealed box.
  • Gas carburizing — parts are heated in a furnace atmosphere of carbon monoxide and hydrocarbon gases.
  • Liquid carburizing — parts are immersed in a molten cyanide or carbonate salt bath.
  • Vacuum (low-pressure) carburizing — parts are carburized at low pressure with hydrocarbon gas such as acetylene, allowing precise control and reduced oxidation.

Applications

Carburizing is widely used for gears, bearings, camshafts, and other components requiring a hard wear-resistant surface combined with a core able to withstand shock loads.
