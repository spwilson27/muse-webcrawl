Harry Cohn

Harry Cohn (July 23, 1891 – February 27, 1958) was an American film producer and studio executive who co-founded Columbia Pictures and served as its president from 1932 until his death. One of the most feared of the classic Hollywood studio bosses, he built Columbia from a minor "Poverty Row" outfit into a major studio.

Career

Born in New York City, Cohn worked his way up from song plugger and trolley conductor to secretary to Carl Laemmle at Universal. In 1919 he co-founded CBC Film Sales Corporation — nicknamed "Corned Beef and Cabbage" for its low-budget output — with his brother Jack Cohn and Joe Brandt. The company was renamed Columbia Pictures in 1924, and Harry moved west to run production in Hollywood.

Columbia under Cohn

Cohn signed director Frank Capra, whose It Happened One Night (1934) swept the Academy Awards and gave the studio prestige. Under Cohn, Columbia produced such films as Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, Gilda, All the King's Men, From Here to Eternity, On the Waterfront, and The Bridge on the River Kwai, and built stars including Rita Hayworth, Kim Novak, William Holden, and Glenn Ford.

Known for his abrasive, autocratic style, Cohn famously kept a portrait of Mussolini in his office and was the archetype of the vulgar mogul, yet he remained personally involved in casting, scripts, and editing. He died of a heart attack in Phoenix, Arizona, in 1958.
