Our Mutual Friend

Our Mutual Friend
Author: Charles Dickens
Published: 1864–65 (serial); 1865 (book)
Publisher: Chapman & Hall
Preceded by: Great Expectations (1861)
Genre: Novel

Our Mutual Friend, written in 1864–65, is the last novel completed by Charles Dickens and is one of his most sophisticated works, combining savage satire with a richly plotted mystery. It was published in monthly parts, as was Dickens's usual practice, and illustrated by Marcus Stone. The novel centres on John Harmon, heir to a fortune made from London's dust heaps, who is believed drowned after returning to England to claim his inheritance — a condition of which is that he marry Bella Wilfer, a young woman he has never met.

Plot summary

The novel opens on the Thames, where Gaffer Hexam and his daughter Lizzie fish corpses from the river. A body identified as John Harmon's is recovered — but Harmon is in fact alive, living under the names John Rokesmith and Julius Handford, and acting as secretary to the Boffins, the genial dust-contractor's servants who inherit the fortune in his place. As "our mutual friend" of the title, he observes Bella — who, under the Boffins' roof, is tested and ultimately transformed from a self-confessed mercenary into a woman who marries Rokesmith for love.

Around this central plot Dickens builds a sweeping panorama of Victorian London society: the Veneerings and their parvenu dinner parties; the money-lender Fascination Fledgeby and his Jewish landlord Riah, through whom Dickens answered criticism of Fagin by creating a portrait of conspicuous Jewish benevolence; the schoolmaster Bradley Headstone's murderous obsession with Lizzie Hexam and his rivalry with the gentlemanly Eugene Wrayburn; and the waterside world of the Hexams and the crippled dolls' dressmaker Jenny Wren.

Themes

The novel is dominated by money and its corrupting power, by dust and refuse as literal and metaphorical currency, and by the River Thames as a source of both death and renewal. Identity, disguise and resurrection recur throughout: Harmon, Wrayburn and others are figuratively or literally reborn from the river.

Reception and adaptations

Victorian critics were divided, but the novel's reputation rose steadily in the 20th century; it is now often ranked among Dickens's greatest works. It has been adapted repeatedly for radio, stage and television, notably in BBC serials of 1976 and 1998. Dickens survived the Staplehurst rail crash of 9 June 1865 while travelling with the manuscript of an instalment, an experience he described in a postscript to the published novel.
