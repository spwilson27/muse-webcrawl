In Greek mythology, Echo (Greek: Ἠχώ, Ēkhṓ, "echo" or "sound") was an Oread — a mountain nymph — who loved her own voice. Her story, best known from Ovid's Metamorphoses, is an aetiological myth explaining the origin of the echo: cursed to be able only to repeat the last words spoken to her, Echo wasted away for love of Narcissus until nothing remained but her voice.

The curse

Zeus, fond of visiting the nymphs of Mount Cithaeron, employed Echo's gift of the gab to detain his wife Hera in conversation while he pursued them. When Hera discovered the trick, she punished Echo by taking away her power of speech except for repetition: Echo could never speak first, only answer by repeating the ends of others' sentences.

Echo and Narcissus

Echo fell in love with the beautiful youth Narcissus. Following him through the woods, she could not declare herself; when he called out "Who's here?" she could only repeat his words, until she rushed out to embrace him and he cruelly rejected her. Echo hid in caves and glens, pining away until her body withered entirely and only her voice survived. In Ovid's telling, Nemesis answered Echo's prayer for vengeance by making Narcissus fall in love with his own reflection in a pool, where he died and became the narcissus flower — while Echo's voice repeated his final farewell.

Echo and Pan

In the later pastoral romance Daphnis and Chloe by Longus, Echo is a musical nymph, daughter of a mortal man and a nymph, loved by the god Pan. Rejecting him, she was driven by his spite into a frenzy of herdsmen who tore her apart and scattered her limbs, which the Earth received; her buried remains still sing, imitating every sound — another origin of the echo phenomenon.

Legacy

Echo's name gives English the word echo itself, and she recurs throughout Western art and literature, from medieval Roman de la Rose to Waterhouse's painting and modern treatments of the Narcissus myth. The astronomical and acoustic uses of "echo" — and medical terms such as echolalia — descend from her story.
