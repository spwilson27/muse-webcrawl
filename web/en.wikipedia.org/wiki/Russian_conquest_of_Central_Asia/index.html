Russian conquest of Central Asia

The Russian conquest of Central Asia took place over several decades in the second half of the nineteenth century. Between the 1840s and the 1890s the Russian Empire absorbed the Kazakh steppe and the three Uzbek khanates of Bukhara, Khiva and Kokand, bringing Russian rule to the borders of Persia, Afghanistan and the Chinese Empire. The advance formed the Asian theatre of the "Great Game", the strategic rivalry between Russia and British India. The conquered territories were administered as the Governor-Generalship of Russian Turkestan, with its capital at Tashkent.

Advance across the steppe

Russian expansion began with lines of forts along the Kazakh steppe, from Orenburg toward the Syr Darya. In 1853 Russian troops seized the Kokand fortress of Ak-Mechet (later Perovsk) on the lower Syr Darya, gaining a foothold on the river line. The Kazakh khanates' earlier submission to Russia in the eighteenth century was converted into direct administration through the Steppe Statute and the abolition of the khanates of the Middle and Great Hordes.

Kokand and Tashkent

In 1864 Colonel Mikhail Chernyayev captured Chimkent, and in June 1865 — famously exceeding his instructions — stormed Tashkent, the largest city of the region with some 100,000 inhabitants. In 1867 Tashkent became the capital of the new Governor-Generalship of Turkestan under General Konstantin Petrovich von Kaufman. The Khanate of Kokand, weakened by civil war, was annexed outright in 1876 after the uprising of Pulat Khan, becoming the Fergana Oblast.

Bukhara and Khiva

War with the Emirate of Bukhara in 1866–1868 ended with the fall of Samarkand and a treaty that made the emir a Russian vassal while leaving him his throne. In 1873 a campaign under Kaufman crossed the Kyzyl Kum desert and compelled the Khanate of Khiva to accept protectorate status and cede territory on the right bank of the Amu Darya. Both states survived as protectorates until 1920.

Turkmenistan and the Panjdeh incident

The final stage was the subjugation of the Turkmen tribes of the Transcaspian region. General Mikhail Skobelev stormed the fortress of Geok Tepe in January 1881, and Merv submitted in 1884. Russian expansion toward the Afghan frontier produced the Panjdeh incident of March 1885, a clash with Afghan troops that nearly caused war with Britain; a subsequent boundary commission delimited the Afghan northern frontier, and the Pamir boundary was settled in 1895.

Consequences

The conquest added millions of new subjects and turned Central Asia into a cotton-producing hinterland for Russian industry, especially after the American Civil War disrupted supplies. Railways — the Trans-Caspian line reaching Samarkand by 1888 — bound the region to the empire. Russian and Ukrainian settlers moved into the steppe in large numbers, and resentment at land and conscription policies erupted in the Central Asian revolt of 1916, a precursor of the Basmachi movement after the Russian Revolution. The protectorates of Bukhara and Khiva were absorbed into the Soviet system in 1920, and the national delimitation of 1924 drew the republic borders that survive today.
