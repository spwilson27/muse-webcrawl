Alcoholics Anonymous

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A circle of chairs in a church hall, set out for an AA meeting]
A circle of chairs in a church hall, set out for an AA meeting

Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) is an international mutual-aid fellowship of people who share experience, strength and hope to recover from Alcoholism. Founded in Akron, Ohio, in 1935 by Bill Wilson and Dr Bob Smith, it is organised around the Twelve Steps and Twelve Traditions, is free, non-professional and deliberately non-hierarchical, and claims some two million members in about 180 countries. Its model has been adapted by dozens of fellowships (Narcotics Anonymous, Gamblers Anonymous, Overeaters Anonymous).

Programme

The Twelve Steps take members through admitting powerlessness over alcohol, turning to a 'Power greater than ourselves' (interpreted freely — AA describes itself as spiritual, not religious), moral inventory, amends, and carrying the message to other alcoholics. Sponsorship (an experienced member guiding a newcomer), regular meetings built on anonymity, and the Big Book (Alcoholics Anonymous, 1939) are its core institutions. The only requirement for membership is a desire to stop drinking.

Effectiveness and criticism

For decades evidence was anecdotal; a 2020 Cochrane review found AA and Twelve-Step Facilitation at least as effective as other treatments for abstinence, and cheaper. Critics question the disease model's universality, variability of meeting culture, the spiritual framing (US courts have ruled coerced AA attendance unconstitutional where secular alternatives are refused), and higher attrition among some groups.

Influence

AA transformed alcoholism's public understanding from moral failing to treatable condition, seeded the Minnesota Model of residential treatment, and normalised peer-support recovery worldwide. Its anonymity tradition shapes media coverage of addiction to this day.
