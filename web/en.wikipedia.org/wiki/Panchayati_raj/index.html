Panchayati raj (from panchayat, "assembly of five") is the system of local self-government in rural India. It consists of elected councils — panchayats — at the village, block (intermediate) and district levels, responsible for local development and administration.

History

Village councils are an ancient institution in India, praised by Mahatma Gandhi, who advocated gram swaraj (village self-rule). After independence, the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) recommended a three-tier structure, first implemented in Rajasthan in 1959. Early panchayats, however, suffered from irregular elections and weak finances.

The 73rd Amendment

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act of 1992 gave panchayats constitutional status. It mandated regular elections every five years supervised by State Election Commissions; reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to population; reservation of not less than one-third of seats (later raised to one-half in many states) for women; and devolution of functions over 29 subjects listed in the Eleventh Schedule, including agriculture, drinking water, primary education and rural health. The gram sabha — the assembly of all voters in a village — became the foundation of the system.

Functioning and criticism

India today has over 260,000 gram panchayats and about three million elected representatives, making it the largest experiment in grassroots democracy in the world. Critics point to uneven devolution of funds and functionaries, corruption, elite capture, and the phenomenon of sarpanch pati — husbands exercising the power of elected women sarpanches. Supporters note measurable gains in local infrastructure and women's political participation.
