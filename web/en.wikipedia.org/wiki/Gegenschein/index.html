Gegenschein

Gegenschein
TypeOptical phenomenon
LocationAntisolar point
CauseSunlight backscattered by interplanetary dust
First described1854 (Theodor Brorsen)

The gegenschein (German for "counter-glow") is a faint, diffuse brightening of the night sky centred on the antisolar point — the point in the sky directly opposite the Sun. It is caused by sunlight backscattered from dust particles in the interplanetary medium, the same dust responsible for the zodiacal light.

The gegenschein appears as a faint oval patch around the antisolar point.

Explanation

Interplanetary dust particles orbiting the Sun scatter sunlight in all directions. Particles lying near the antisolar point are seen fully illuminated, in the same way the Moon appears full when opposite the Sun; in addition, a phenomenon known as the opposition effect brightens them further because their shadows are hidden behind them from the observer's viewpoint. The combined result is a subtle oval glow some 8–10 degrees across, spanning the ecliptic at the antisolar point.

Observation

The gegenschein is extremely faint — fainter than the Milky Way — and can only be seen from very dark sites free of light pollution, on moonless nights, when the antisolar point is high in the sky and away from the galactic plane. It is most often observed in the hours around midnight, when the antisolar point is highest. The Danish astronomer Theodor Brorsen published the first detailed description in 1854; the phenomenon is sometimes called Brorsen's zodiacal counter-glow.
