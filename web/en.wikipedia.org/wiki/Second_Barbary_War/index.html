Second Barbary War

Date17–19 June 1815 (combat); treaties concluded 1815–1816
LocationMediterranean Sea, off Algeria
BelligerentsUnited States vs. Regency of Algiers
ResultAmerican victory; end of U.S. tribute payments

The Second Barbary War (1815) was a brief naval conflict between the United States and the Regency of Algiers, one of the Barbary states of North Africa. After the end of the War of 1812 freed American naval forces, President James Madison asked Congress for war against Algiers, whose corsairs had resumed capturing American merchant ships and enslaving their crews while the United States was distracted by Britain. Congress authorised action in March 1815, and a squadron under Commodore Stephen Decatur sailed for the Mediterranean.

Course of the war

Decatur's ten-ship squadron reached the Mediterranean in June 1815. On 17 June, off Cape Palos, Spain, his flagship Guerriere and consorts engaged and captured the Algerine flagship frigate Mashuda, killing its commander, Rais Hamidou, the most famous corsair captain of Algiers. Two days later the squadron captured the Algerine brig Estedio off Cape Palmones. Decatur then sailed directly to Algiers and, with his squadron anchored under the city's guns, dictated peace terms.

Treaty and aftermath

The treaty signed on 30 June 1815 ended all American tribute payments to Algiers, secured the release of American captives without ransom, provided compensation for seized American property, and guaranteed American shipping against future attack. A second American squadron under William Bainbridge arrived to display the flag along the Barbary coast, and similar treaties were confirmed with Tunis and Tripoli. When the Dey of Algiers later attempted to repudiate the treaty, an Anglo-Dutch fleet under Britain's Lord Exmouth bombarded Algiers in August 1816, effectively ending large-scale Barbary corsairing against European and American commerce.

In American memory the war is closely associated with Decatur, whose toast — "Our country! In her intercourse with foreign nations may she always be in the right; but our country, right or wrong" — dates from the celebrations of his return.
