Tufted capuchin

The tufted capuchin (Sapajus apella), also known as the brown capuchin, black-capped capuchin, or pin monkey, is a New World primate from South America and the Caribbean islands of Trinidad and Margarita. It is one of the most widespread and adaptable of the capuchin monkeys, and among the most widely studied Neotropical primates.

Description

The tufted capuchin is a robust monkey with a body length of roughly 32–57 cm and a long, semi-prehensile tail. Its fur ranges from light brown to black, with a darker cap on the head and characteristic tufts of hair above the ears. Adults weigh about 1.9–4.8 kg, males being larger than females.

Distribution and habitat

The species inhabits much of northern and central South America, including Colombia, Venezuela, the Guianas, Brazil, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia. It occupies a wide variety of forest types, from lowland rainforest to dry forest and mangrove.

Behaviour and intelligence

Tufted capuchins live in multi-male, multi-female groups of roughly 8–15 individuals. They are omnivorous, feeding on fruit, seeds, invertebrates, eggs and small vertebrates. The species is famous for its dexterity and problem-solving: wild tufted capuchins use stones as hammers and anvils to crack hard nuts, one of the few documented cases of habitual tool use among monkeys, and captive individuals have been subjects of extensive research on cognition, cooperation and inequity aversion.

Taxonomy

The tufted capuchin was long classified as Cebus apella; the robust capuchins were subsequently separated into the genus Sapajus. Several former subspecies have since been elevated to full species.
