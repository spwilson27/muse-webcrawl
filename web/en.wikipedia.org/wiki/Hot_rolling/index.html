Hot rolling

In metalworking, hot rolling is a process in which metal is passed through pairs of rolls at a temperature above the material's recrystallisation temperature — for steel, typically above 925 °C (1,700 °F). Because the metal is soft and its grains recrystallise as it is deformed, large reductions in thickness can be achieved with relatively low forces.

Process

Steel slabs or blooms are reheated in a furnace, descaled with high-pressure water jets, and passed through roughing and finishing mill stands that progressively reduce thickness. The strip is then coiled or cut to length. Hot-rolled products are often further processed by cold rolling, pickling, or coating.

Products

Hot rolling produces plate, sheet, rail, structural sections such as I-beams, and bar. Hot-rolled steel is recognisable by its bluish-grey mill scale and slightly rounded edges, and is used where precise tolerances and surface finish are less critical.

Comparison with cold rolling

Cold rolling, performed below the recrystallisation temperature, gives tighter tolerances, a smoother finish and higher strength through work hardening, but requires greater forces and additional annealing steps.
