Lokono

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Lokono, historically known as the Arawak (or Lokono Arawak), are an Indigenous people of the coastal regions of northern South America—in present-day Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, and Venezuela. Their endonym Lokono means "the people" (from loko, "person/people"); the name "Arawak" derives from a word for cassava flour, a staple food. The Lokono speak, or historically spoke, Lokono, the language that gives its name to the wider Arawakan language family, the largest language family of Indigenous South America.

History

The Lokono were among the first Indigenous peoples encountered by Europeans in the Americas: Arawakan-speaking peoples of the Caribbean islands (the Taíno) were close cultural and linguistic relatives, and Lokono on the mainland traded with the Spanish, Dutch, French, and British from the 16th century onward. Renowned as traders, canoeists, and farmers of cassava, they occupied the riverbanks and coasts of the Guianas, exchanging goods between the interior peoples and the coast. Colonial warfare, disease, and enslavement drastically reduced their population, and many communities were displaced inland. Today Lokono communities number in the tens of thousands, principally in Guyana (where Amerindian peoples make up roughly a tenth of the population) and Suriname.

Culture and language

Traditional Lokono society is organised in villages led by a chief (kakidu or captain), with matrilineal elements in kinship and a subsistence base of shifting cultivation, fishing, and hunting. Cassava processing—grating, pressing out the poisonous juice with a woven matapi press, and baking cassava bread—remains culturally central, as does the fermented drink piwari. The Lokono language has become severely endangered, with fluent speakers reduced to a few hundred elders, but revitalisation programmes in Guyana and Suriname teach it in schools and have produced dictionaries and recordings. Lokono leaders have been active in Indigenous land-rights movements, including disputes over logging, mining, and protected areas on ancestral lands.
