Palynology

Palynology is the study of microscopic particles called palynomorphs — principally pollen grains and spores, but also dinoflagellate cysts, acritarchs, and other organic microfossils — in both living and fossil form. The term was introduced in 1944 by Hyde and Williams, from the Greek palynō, "to scatter". Because pollen and spores are produced in vast quantities, widely dispersed, and highly resistant to decay, their fossil record provides a powerful record of past vegetation and climate.

Methods

Samples from lake sediments, peat bogs, ice cores, or rock strata are chemically treated — typically with hydrofluoric and hydrochloric acids and acetolysis — to concentrate the palynomorphs, which are then identified and counted under a microscope. The resulting pollen diagram shows changing percentages of taxa through depth, and hence through time.

Applications

Pollen analysis, pioneered by Lennart von Post in 1916, is central to reconstructing Quaternary climates and environments. In petroleum geology, fossil palynomorphs date and correlate sedimentary rocks where other fossils are scarce. Forensic palynology uses the pollen "fingerprint" of a place to link suspects, objects, or bodies to crime scenes, while melissopalynology identifies the floral sources of honey, and aerobiology tracks airborne pollen relevant to allergy sufferers.
