The Teletype Corporation, a part of American Telephone and Telegraph Company's Western Electric manufacturing arm from 1930, came to be a specialist in data and telecommunications equipment. Founded from the Morkrum-Kleinschmidt Company, which had developed mechanical teleprinters in the early 20th century, the corporation produced the machines that defined the teletypewriter — popularly shortened to "teletype" — for more than six decades.

History

The company's origins lie in the work of Charles Krum and his son Howard, and in Edward Kleinschmidt's inventions of printing telegraph equipment; their firms merged in the 1920s as the Morkrum-Kleinschmidt Corporation and was renamed Teletype Corporation in 1928. In 1930 AT&T acquired the company and made it a subsidiary of Western Electric. Teletype machines became the standard terminals for the worldwide telex network, for military communications, for news wire services, and for the TWX network in the United States.

Products and computing

Well-known models include the Model 15, the workhorse printing telegraph of the Second World War era; the Model 28 family; and the Model 33 ASR, introduced in 1963. The Model 33 — inexpensive, rugged, and equipped with a paper-tape reader and punch — became one of the most influential computer terminals ever made: it was the standard input/output device for early minicomputers and time-sharing systems and was instrumental in popularising the ASCII code. Model 35 and Model 37 machines served heavier-duty and higher-speed roles.

Teletype's influence persists in computing conventions: the abbreviation TTY for terminals, the CRLF line-ending sequence, and the layout of early terminal protocols all derive from its electromechanical machines. Following the 1984 breakup of AT&T, the company was renamed AT&T Teletype Corporation and then folded into AT&T's computer and information systems businesses; the Teletype name disappeared around 1990 as electronic video terminals displaced electromechanical printers.
