Gout

Gout is a form of inflammatory arthritis characterized by recurrent attacks of a red, tender, hot and swollen joint, caused by the deposition of monosodium urate crystals when blood uric acid levels are persistently elevated. The joint at the base of the big toe is affected in about half of cases ("podagra"), but gout can involve any joint, and chronic disease may produce firm crystal deposits called tophi.

Causes and pathophysiology

Uric acid is the end product of purine metabolism in humans, who lack the enzyme uricase. Hyperuricemia results mostly from reduced renal excretion of urate, sometimes from overproduction; diet (red meat, seafood, alcohol, fructose-sweetened drinks), obesity, certain diuretics and genetic variants in urate transporters all contribute. When urate crystallizes in joints, the crystals trigger an intense inflammasome-mediated inflammatory response, producing the abrupt, exquisitely painful flare.

Diagnosis and treatment

Definitive diagnosis is by identification of needle-shaped, negatively birefringent urate crystals in aspirated joint fluid. Acute flares are treated with NSAIDs, colchicine or glucocorticoids. Long-term management aims to lower serum urate below saturation — usually below 6 mg/dL — with xanthine oxidase inhibitors such as allopurinol or febuxostat, plus dietary moderation. Historically called "the disease of kings" for its association with rich food and drink, gout was described in antiquity and its crystals identified by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in 1679.
