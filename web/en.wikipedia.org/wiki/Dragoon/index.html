Dragoon

Dragoons were originally a class of mounted infantry, who used horses for mobility, but dismounted to fight on foot. From the early modern period onward, dragoons increasingly evolved into conventional light or heavy cavalry units, retaining their historic title. The name is said to derive from their weapon, a short musket or carbine called a "dragon" because it was said to breathe fire.

Origins

The first dragoons appeared in the mid-16th century. Marshal de Brissac is credited with organizing dragoons in Piedmont in the 1550s; the French army of the Thirty Years' War and later armies across Europe adopted the arm. Early dragoons were essentially infantry provided with horses — often inferior mounts — so commanders could move musketeers rapidly to seize bridges, defiles, and other key points.

Evolution into cavalry

During the 17th and 18th centuries the mounted role grew: by the era of Frederick the Great and Napoleon, dragoons in most armies fought primarily as medium or heavy cavalry, armed with sabres and carbines, while still trained to fight on foot when required. Nations raised celebrated dragoon regiments, such as the French dragons, the British Royal Dragoons and Scots Greys (Royal Scots Dragoon Guards), and Spain's and Austria's dragoon regiments.

19th and 20th centuries

Imperial Germany, Russia, and other powers maintained large dragoon establishments into the First World War. Mechanization transformed most surviving dragoon regiments into armoured or mechanized units, though the name survives in regimental titles in several countries, including the Royal Canadian Dragoons, the Royal Scots Dragoon Guards, and France's parachute and armoured dragoon regiments.

Verb usage

The verb "to dragoon" — to compel or coerce someone into doing something — derives from the use of dragoons by Louis XIV in the dragonnades (1681 onward), the billeting of dragoons in Huguenot households to force conversions to Catholicism.
