Rocksteady

Rocksteady is a music genre that originated in Jamaica around 1966. A successor to ska and a precursor to reggae, rocksteady was performed by Jamaican vocal harmony groups influenced by American soul music. Its slower tempo, prominent basslines, and romantic lyrics defined Jamaican popular music for roughly two years before it evolved into reggae around 1968.

Characteristics

Rocksteady slowed ska's brisk pace to a more relaxed groove, allowing singers room for expressive, soul-influenced phrasing modelled on artists such as Curtis Mayfield and the Impressions, Sam Cooke, and Motown acts. The rhythm guitar's offbeat "skank" remained, but the walking basslines of ska gave way to heavier, more melodic, broken bass patterns played high in the mix. The large horn sections of ska were reduced or dropped, with piano, organ, and guitar taking more prominent roles. Drumming emphasised the "one drop" pattern in embryonic form — an accent on the third beat — that would become central to reggae.

The name is generally traced to Alton Ellis's 1967 hit "Rock Steady", though other songs also claimed the term. A very hot summer in 1966 is often cited as one reason dancers preferred a slower beat.

Artists and songs

Leading rocksteady performers included Alton Ellis, Ken Boothe, Delroy Wilson, Phyllis Dillon, John Holt and the Paragons, the Techniques, the Melodians, the Heptones, Hopeton Lewis, and Desmond Dekker. Classic recordings include "Rock Steady" and "Girl I've Got a Date" by Alton Ellis, "The Tide Is High" by the Paragons (later a hit for Blondie), "007 (Shanty Town)" by Desmond Dekker, "Queen Majesty" by the Techniques, and "Rivers of Babylon" by the Melodians, later famous through Boney M.

Producers such as Duke Reid at Treasure Isle, Coxsone Dodd at Studio One, and Bunny Lee shaped the sound, with session bands drawn from Kingston's pool of musicians.

Legacy

Although its commercial lifespan was short, rocksteady established the vocal-group style, the bass-led mix, and much of the songwriting repertoire that reggae inherited. Many rocksteady songs were repeatedly re-versioned in reggae, dub, and dancehall, and the era remains a touchstone for Jamaican music connoisseurs. The "rude boy" culture of Kingston's dancehalls provided much of rocksteady's subject matter, in songs alternately celebrating and lamenting the rude boys' outlaw image.
