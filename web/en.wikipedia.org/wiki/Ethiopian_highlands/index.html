The Ethiopian Highlands (Amharic: የኢትዮጵያ ደጋ) are a rugged massif of mountains in Ethiopia, extending into northern Eritrea. Sometimes called the "Roof of Africa" for their height and large area, they form the largest continuous area of their elevation in the whole continent — a giant dome of ancient lava flows bisected from northeast to southwest by the Main Ethiopian Rift, part of the East African Rift system.

Geography

The highlands are divided by the Rift into northwestern and southeastern portions; the northwestern part includes the Simien Mountains, home to Ethiopia's highest summit, Ras Dashen (about 4,550 m), while the southeastern part culminates in the Bale Mountains. Elevations mostly range from about 1,500 m to over 4,000 m. Rivers radiating from the highlands include the Blue Nile, which rises in Lake Tana and carves deep gorges through the plateau.

Ecology

Because altitude strongly moderates the tropical climate, the highlands pass through vegetation zones known locally as kolla (hot lowland), woina dega (temperate) and dega (cool highland), rising to afroalpine moorland above the treeline. The isolation of the highlands has produced many endemic species, including the Ethiopian wolf, gelada, walia ibex and mountain nyala. The highlands are also the centre of origin of Coffea arabica (coffee) and of crops such as teff and enset.

History

The well-watered, defensible highlands formed the heartland of successive Ethiopian states, from the Kingdom of Aksum through the medieval solomonic empire to the modern state, and long served as a refuge preserving Ethiopian Christianity and literacy in Ge'ez.
