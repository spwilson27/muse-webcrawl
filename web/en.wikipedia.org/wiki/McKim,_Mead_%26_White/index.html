McKim, Mead & White was an American architectural firm based in New York City. Founded in 1879 by Charles Follen McKim, William Rutherford Mead, and Stanford White, it became one of the most prominent and influential architecture firms in the United States during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, defining the Beaux-Arts style in America.

History

The three partners met while working in the office of Henry Hobson Richardson. McKim and Mead established their practice in 1879, and Stanford White joined shortly afterward, bringing considerable design talent and social connections. The firm grew rapidly, eventually employing hundreds of architects and producing more than a thousand commissions.

The firm's work was central to the American Renaissance movement and the City Beautiful movement, popularized by the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 in Chicago, for which McKim designed the Agricultural Building.

Notable works

Major commissions include the Boston Public Library (1895), the Morgan Library in New York, the original Pennsylvania Station (1910, demolished 1963), the Washington Square Arch, Columbia University's Low Memorial Library, and the Rhode Island State House. The firm also designed numerous country houses, university buildings, and clubs.

Legacy

Stanford White was murdered in 1906 by Harry Kendall Thaw, a scandal that rocked the firm. McKim died in 1909 and Mead in 1928. The practice continued under successor partners for decades, and its archives are held by the New-York Historical Society. McKim, Mead & White is widely regarded as having set the standard for American classical architecture in the Beaux-Arts era.
