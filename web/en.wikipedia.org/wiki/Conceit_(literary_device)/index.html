In literature, a conceit is an extended metaphor with a complex logic that governs a poetic passage or entire poem. By juxtaposing, usurping and manipulating images and ideas in surprising ways, a conceit invites the reader into a more sophisticated understanding of an object of comparison. Conceits are most associated with the seventeenth-century metaphysical poets of England, though they were also used by other Renaissance poets, notably in the Italian and Spanish Petrarchan traditions from which the English poets drew.

Metaphysical conceits

John Donne's "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" compares two lovers to the two legs of a drawing compass: one leg stays fixed while the other roams, leaning toward the centre, and the circle it draws ends where it began. The conceit is elaborated over several stanzas with argumentative rigour, turning a mathematical instrument into an emblem of faithful love. Donne's "The Flea" similarly builds an entire seduction argument on the mingling of blood in a flea's bite.

Petrarchan conceits

Petrarchan conceits, by contrast, tend to be more conventional and hyperbolic: the mistress's eyes are stars, her lips coral, her cheeks roses. Elizabethan sonneteers adopted and sometimes parodied these formulas; Shakespeare's Sonnet 130 ("My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun") pointedly inverts them.

Critical history

Samuel Johnson criticised the metaphysical poets for yoking "the most heterogeneous ideas together by violence". In the twentieth century T. S. Eliot's essay "The Metaphysical Poets" (1921) rehabilitated the conceit as the mark of a unified sensibility, and conceits remain a standard topic in the study of Renaissance poetry.
