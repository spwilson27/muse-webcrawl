Callisthenes

Callisthenes accompanied Alexander as the expedition's official historian.

Callisthenes of Olynthus (c. 360 – c. 327 BC) was a Greek historian, a relative and pupil of Aristotle, who accompanied Alexander the Great on his Asian expedition as the official historian of the campaign. His history, the Deeds of Alexander, is lost but was a major source for later writers.

Life

Callisthenes came from Olynthus in Chalcidice and was related to Aristotle — his mother Hero was Aristotle's niece — under whom he studied. He had already written a history of Greece covering the years 387–356 BC and a work on the Third Sacred War before Aristotle recommended him to Alexander.

With Alexander

As court historian Callisthenes praised Alexander lavishly, portraying him as a heroic, Panhellenic conqueror and even associating him with divine honours. Relations deteriorated, however, over the king's adoption of Persian court customs. Callisthenes famously opposed the introduction of proskynesis — prostration before the king — arguing that such honours were fit for gods, not men. His witty refusal to perform the ritual offended Alexander.

Downfall and death

In 327 BC Callisthenes was implicated, probably unjustly, in the conspiracy of the Royal Pages. According to differing ancient accounts he was tortured and hanged, crucified, or carried about in chains until he died of disease or obesity. His fate became a favourite theme for rhetorical exercises and for philosophers who contrasted the frank speech of the scholar with the tyranny of the conqueror.

Works and legacy

None of Callisthenes' writings survive complete. The romanticised Alexander Romance was falsely attributed to him in antiquity, giving rise to the name "Pseudo-Callisthenes" for its anonymous author.
