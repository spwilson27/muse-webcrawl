The Roche limit, also called the Roche radius, is the distance from a celestial body within which a second body, held together only by its own gravity, will disintegrate because the first body's tidal forces exceed the second body's self-gravity. It is named after the French astronomer Édouard Roche, who first calculated it in 1848.

Explanation

For a rigid satellite of the same density as its primary, the limit is about 1.26 times the primary's radius; for a completely fluid satellite it is about 2.44 radii. Real bodies fall between these values depending on their material strength and density ratio. Inside the Roche limit, orbiting material cannot coalesce under its own gravity, which explains why planetary rings — such as Saturn's, which lie largely within the limit — remain rings rather than forming a moon.

Examples

Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 passed within Jupiter's Roche limit in 1992 and was torn into a chain of fragments that collided with the planet in 1994. The Martian moon Phobos orbits inside Mars's fluid Roche limit and is slowly spiralling inward; it is expected eventually to break up and form a ring. Some close-in exoplanets and binary stars are also shaped or disrupted by Roche-lobe overflow.
