Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act

The Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, commonly referred to as Dodd–Frank, is a United States federal law enacted on 21 July 2010 in response to the financial crisis of 2007–2008. Named after sponsors Senator Chris Dodd and Representative Barney Frank, it made sweeping changes to financial regulation.

Dodd–Frank Act
Enacted: 21 July 2010
Signed by: Barack Obama
Purpose: Financial regulation after 2008 crisis

Provisions

  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) — new agency protecting consumers in financial markets.
  • Financial Stability Oversight Council — monitors systemic risk; "too big to fail" institutions face higher capital and "living will" requirements.
  • Volcker Rule — restricts banks' proprietary trading and investments in hedge/private equity funds.
  • Derivatives — swaps moved to exchanges/clearinghouses under CFTC and SEC oversight.
  • Orderly liquidation authority for failing financial firms, plus mortgage lending and executive compensation reforms.

Effects and criticism

Supporters credit Dodd–Frank with making the banking system safer; critics argue it burdens community banks and entrenches large institutions. The Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act of 2018 rolled back some provisions for mid-sized banks, and debates over its scope continue.
