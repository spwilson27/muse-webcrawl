Himyarite Kingdom

The Himyarite Kingdom was a polity in the southern highlands and coastal plains of the Arabian Peninsula, in present-day Yemen, which dominated South Arabia for centuries. Emerging around 110 BC from the Qatabanian sphere, Himyar conquered the neighbouring kingdoms of Saba, Qataban, and Hadhramaut and by the early fourth century AD ruled a unified South Arabian state, until it fell to the Ethiopian kingdom of Aksum around 525 AD and then to Sasanian Persia.

History

The Himyarites first appear in inscriptions as a tribe of the southern highlands, with their capital at Zafar near modern Yarim. In the late second and third centuries AD their kings — adopting the title "King of Saba and Raydan" — absorbed Qataban and then, after long wars, defeated Saba itself around AD 275–300 and conquered Hadhramaut, unifying the ancient South Arabian kingdoms for the first time. Himyar controlled the frankincense and myrrh trade and the Red Sea ports through which goods from India and East Africa passed to the Mediterranean world.

Religion

From the late fourth century, Himyarite royal inscriptions abandon the old polytheistic pantheon and invoke a single high god, Rahmanan, "the Merciful, Lord of heaven and earth". In the fifth and early sixth centuries the kingdom's rulers adopted Judaism, most famously Yusuf As'ar Yath'ar, known in Arabic tradition as Dhu Nuwas, whose persecution of the Christians of Najran in 523 provoked invasion by the Christian kingdom of Aksum.

Fall

An Aksumite army conquered Himyar around 525 and installed a client king; the subsequent rule of the Aksumite general Abraha, and his campaigns into central Arabia, are remembered in Arabic tradition. Around 570–578 the Himyarite prince Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan obtained Sasanian Persian help to expel the Aksumites, but Yemen then became a Persian satrapy, ending the ancient South Arabian kingdoms shortly before the rise of Islam.
