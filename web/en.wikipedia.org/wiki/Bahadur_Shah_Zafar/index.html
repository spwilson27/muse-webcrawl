Bahadur Shah Zafar

Bahadur Shah II, better known by his pen name Zafar (24 October 1775 – 7 November 1862), was the last Mughal emperor of India, reigning nominally from 1837 to 1857. By his time the empire had shrunk to little more than the city of Delhi and he ruled as a pensioner of the British East India Company, but he remained a revered symbol of sovereignty and a noted Urdu poet.

Reign

Zafar succeeded his father Akbar II at the age of 62. His court at the Red Fort was a centre of Urdu literary culture, hosting poets such as Mirza Ghalib, Zauq, and Momin. Politically powerless, forbidden an army, and watched closely by the British Resident, Zafar occupied himself with poetry, calligraphy, Sufism, and the gardens and palaces of Delhi.

The rebellion of 1857

When mutinous sepoys reached Delhi in May 1857, they proclaimed the reluctant 81-year-old emperor the leader of the Indian Rebellion of 1857, making him a rallying symbol across northern India. After British forces retook Delhi in September 1857, Zafar took refuge at Humayun's Tomb, where he was captured by Captain William Hodson; his sons Mirza Mughal and Mirza Khizr Sultan and a grandson were shot by Hodson at the Khooni Darwaza. Tried by a military court for treason, Zafar was exiled to Rangoon (Yangon) in British Burma with his wife Zeenat Mahal.

Death and legacy

Zafar died in Rangoon on 7 November 1862 and was buried there; his grave became a shrine. His own poetry expressed his sorrow in exile — the famous couplet in which he laments that he did not get "two yards of land for burial in the street of the beloved" (in Delhi) is attributed to him. He is remembered as a symbol of Hindu-Muslim unity and of the end of the Mughal dynasty.
