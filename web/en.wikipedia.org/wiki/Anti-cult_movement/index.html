Anti-cult movement

The anti-cult movement (ACM) is a social and political movement that emerged in the 1970s, principally in the United States, in opposition to groups it considers to be cults, often new religious movements. The movement is largely composed of the relatives of cult members, former members, and mental-health professionals, and it characterises targeted groups as manipulative, authoritarian and harmful.

History

Grassroots anti-cult groups formed in the early 1970s, such as FREECOG (Parents' Committee to Free Our Sons and Daughters from the Children of God). Concerns intensified after the 1978 Jonestown mass murder-suicide. Organisations including the Cult Awareness Network (CAN) and the American Family Foundation coordinated information and support for families. Some movement figures practised "deprogramming" — the forcible removal and persuasion of cult members — which generated kidnapping controversies and lawsuits.

Criticism

Scholars of new religious movements and civil-liberties advocates have criticised the ACM for relying on disputed theories of "brainwashing", for stigmatising minority religions, and for the coercive methods of some deprogrammers. In the 1990s CAN was bankrupted by litigation, and its name and assets were purchased by a Scientology-affiliated buyer, an episode emblematic of the movement's later decline in the US, though anti-cult activity continues, notably in France through agencies such as MIVILUDES.
