Grandfather paradox

The grandfather paradox is a paradox of time travel in which inconsistencies can arise through changing the past. The name comes from the paradox's common description: a person travels to the past and kills their own grandfather before the conception of their father or mother, which prevents the time traveller's existence.

Despite this title, the grandfather paradox does not exclusively regard the contradiction of killing one's own grandfather to prevent one's birth. Rather, the paradox regards any action that makes the travel to the past in which it was made, impossible. Example paradoxes include the time traveller killing a younger version of themselves before becoming the traveller, or the bootstrap paradox.

History

An early example of the paradox is found in the French writer René Barjavel's 1943 novel Le Voyageur Imprudent (Future Times Three), where the protagonist travels back in time and accidentally kills one of his ancestors. The paradox was popularised in science fiction during the mid-20th century and has since become a staple of discussions about the possibility of time travel.

Proposed resolutions

Several approaches have been proposed to resolve the paradox:

  • Self-consistency principle: Novikov's self-consistency principle states that any attempt to change the past is doomed to fail in a way consistent with history; the probability of an event creating a paradox is zero.
  • Parallel universes: In the many-worlds interpretation, travelling to the past creates or enters a different branch of the universe, so changes there do not affect the traveller's original timeline.
  • No time travel: The paradox may simply demonstrate that backwards time travel is physically impossible.

Physicists including Stephen Hawking, who proposed the chronology protection conjecture, have suggested that the laws of physics conspire to prevent the formation of closed timelike curves that would permit paradoxes. See also time travel, causality, and Novikov self-consistency principle.
