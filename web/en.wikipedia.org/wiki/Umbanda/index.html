Umbanda

Umbanda (Portuguese pronunciation: [ũˈbɐ̃dɐ]) is a syncretic Afro-Brazilian religion that emerged in Brazil in the early 20th century, blending African (especially Yoruba and Bantu) spiritual traditions with Roman Catholicism, French Kardecist Spiritism, and Indigenous Brazilian beliefs. Its traditional founding date is 15 November 1908, when the young medium Zélio Fernandino de Moraes, in Niterói, Rio de Janeiro, manifested the spirit known as the Caboclo das Sete Encruzilhadas and announced a new religion in which humble spirits could work for charity.

Founded: 1908, Niterói, Brazil
Founder (trad.): Zélio Fernandino de Moraes
Adherents: ~400,000+ (Brazilian census, 2010; widely considered undercounted)
Elements: African religions, Catholicism, Spiritism, Indigenous traditions

Beliefs and practice

Umbanda worships a supreme God (Olorum/Zambi) and reveres the orixás — divine forces of nature, syncretised with Catholic saints. Rituals take place in temples called terreiros or tendas, guided by a pai or mãe de santo. Mediums incorporate spirit guides belonging to lines such as the caboclos (Indigenous spirits), pretos velhos (elderly enslaved Africans, symbols of wisdom and humility), crianças (child spirits), baianos, marinheiros and, in some houses, exus and pombagiras, who give consultations, healing and advice free of charge — the religion's ethical core is charity. Singing points (pontos cantados), drumming with atabaques, and offerings structure the ceremonies.

History and diversity

Umbanda grew rapidly in urban Brazil, organising federations from the 1940s and influencing Brazilian culture, music and ideas of national identity ("the only genuinely Brazilian religion"). Variants range from "White Umbanda" (closer to Kardecism) to Umbanda Omolocô and other forms closer to Candomblé. Umbandists have faced historical persecution and, more recently, intolerance from some neo-Pentecostal groups; the 2010 census counted roughly 407,000 adherents, though academics believe the true number is much higher.
