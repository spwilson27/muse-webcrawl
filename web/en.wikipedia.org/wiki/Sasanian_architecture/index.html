Sasanian architecture

Sasanian architecture refers to the building style of the Sasanian Empire (224–651 CE), the last pre-Islamic Persian empire. Drawing on Achaemenid and Parthian precedents, Sasanian architects developed monumental palaces and fire temples characterised by massive brick vaults, domes raised on squinches, and grand open halls called iwans. Their innovations profoundly influenced the architecture of the Islamic world.

The Taq Kasra at Ctesiphon — the largest single-span brick vault of the ancient world.

Features

Signature elements of Sasanian building include:

  • The iwan — a barrel-vaulted hall open on one side to a courtyard, perfected at palaces such as Firuzabad and Ctesiphon.
  • Domes on squinches — the device of placing a dome over a square chamber by bridging the corners, seen at the palace of Ardashir and in the chahar taq fire temples.
  • Massive use of rubble masonry and fired brick with rapid-setting gypsum mortar, permitting daring spans such as the great vault of the Taq Kasra (Ayvan-e Kesra) at Ctesiphon, some 25 metres wide.
  • Stucco decoration of extraordinary richness — carved and moulded plaster with vegetal, geometric and figural motifs — and palace programmes combining audience halls, domed throne rooms and garden pavilions.

Major monuments

Early Sasanian foundations include Ardashir I's circular city of Firuzabad with its palace and fire temple, and Shapur I's city of Bishapur with its cruciform hall. At Ctesiphon the Taq Kasra became the emblem of Sasanian kingship. Later rulers built at Sarvestan, Qasr-e Shirin (associated with Khosrow II and Shirin) and Takht-e Soleyman, a fortified fire-temple sanctuary around a sacred lake.

Influence

After the Arab conquest, Sasanian forms — the iwan plan, the squinch dome, stucco revetment, and the four-iwan courtyard — were absorbed into Islamic architecture across Persia, Mesopotamia and beyond, shaping everything from mosques and madrasas to caravanserais.
