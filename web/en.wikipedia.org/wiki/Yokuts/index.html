The Yokuts (previously spelled Yokut) are an ethnic group of Native Americans native to inland central California, principally the San Joaquin Valley and the western foothills of the Sierra Nevada. Before European contact they comprised dozens of autonomous tribelets speaking closely related Yokutsan languages, and were among the most populous Indigenous peoples of California.

Society and subsistence

Yokuts communities — including the Valley Yokuts of the valley floor and the Foothill Yokuts along the rivers of the Sierra foothills — lived in permanent villages, some numbering several hundred inhabitants, organized under hereditary chiefs (tiya). Their diet centered on acorns, seeds, roots, fish, waterfowl, and game from the rich wetlands around Tulare Lake, then one of the largest freshwater lakes in the western United States. Yokuts artisans were known for elaborate coiled and twined basketry, and the groups traded extensively with coastal and mountain peoples.

History

Spanish expeditions entered Yokuts territory in the early nineteenth century, and some Yokuts were drawn or forced into the coastal missions, though their inland homeland long remained a refuge. The California Gold Rush brought catastrophic disruption: disease, violence, and the destruction of food resources, including the draining of Tulare Lake for agriculture, reduced the Yokuts population drastically. Survivors regrouped on reservations and rancherias, notably the Tule River Reservation established in the 1870s after the Tule River War.

Today

Descendant communities include the Tule River Indian Tribe, the Santa Rosa Rancheria (Tachi Yokut), and the Picayune Rancheria of Chukchansi Indians, among others. Yokuts language revitalization programs work to sustain the surviving languages, most of which are severely endangered.
