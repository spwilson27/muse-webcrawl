Fortepiano

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fortepiano is an early piano, roughly the type built from the mid-18th century to the mid-19th century, distinguished from the modern piano by its lighter construction, wooden frame, leather- or felt-covered hammers, thinner strings at lower tension, and a clearer, more transparent tone with faster decay. The name derives from the instrument's ability to play forte and piano — loud and soft — according to the player's touch, a dynamic flexibility impossible on the harpsichord.

Development

Bartolomeo Cristofori invented the piano action in Florence around 1700. By the later eighteenth century two schools dominated: the Viennese action, light and responsive, favoured by Mozart and built by makers such as Johann Andreas Stein and Anton Walter; and the heavier English action of Broadwood, used by Beethoven. Beethoven's successive pianos — including the Érard of 1803 and the Broadwood of 1817 — trace the instrument's rapid evolution in range and power through his lifetime. Composers from Haydn and Mozart to Schubert and Chopin wrote for fortepianos whose compass and sound differ markedly from modern instruments.

Modern revival

From the 1970s the historically informed performance movement revived the fortepiano. Performers such as Malcolm Bilson demonstrated that articulation, balance and phrasing in Classical repertoire make new sense on period instruments, and modern builders now copy historic fortepianos for concert and recording use.
