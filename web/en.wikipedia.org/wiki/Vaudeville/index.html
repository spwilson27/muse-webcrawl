Vaudeville was a theatrical genre of variety entertainment popular chiefly in the United States and Canada from the early 1880s until the early 1930s. A vaudeville performance was a series of separate, unrelated acts grouped together on a common bill — singers, dancers, comedians, acrobats, jugglers, animal acts, magicians, and short sketches. At its height it was the dominant mass entertainment industry in North America, and many of its stars (the Marx Brothers, W. C. Fields, Mae West, Harry Houdini) went on to film and radio careers.

Origins

The word derives from French vau de ville, possibly from the valley of Vire in Normandy, whose work songs were a popular comic genre; the phrase acquired its entertainment meaning in France for light theatrical pieces with songs. In America, theatre manager Tony Pastor is credited in the 1870s–80s with making variety respectable — banning liquor and vulgar acts — to attract women and family audiences.

The big time

Entrepreneurs B. F. Keith and Edward Albee built chains of lavish theatres and a centralized booking system, the United Booking Office (1906), which controlled acts' contracts across much of the country; the rival Orpheum Circuit dominated the West. Performers travelled the "circuits" in a strict hierarchy from small-time theatres to the pinnacle, the Palace Theatre in New York (opened 1913). A typical bill ran about two hours with eight acts, deliberately ordered so any single act could be dropped without breaking the show.

Decline

The spread of motion pictures, then radio, and the economic shock of the Great Depression ended vaudeville. The Palace played its last vaudeville bill in 1932 and converted to films. Many theatres became cinemas; the Keith-Albee-Orpheum chain merged into RKO Pictures.

Legacy

Vaudeville's act structure shaped radio variety, television (especially The Ed Sullivan Show), stand-up comedy, and much of American popular song and dance. Its rehearsed variety format remains the template of the modern talent show.
