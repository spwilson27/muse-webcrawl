Deva (Hinduism)

Deva (Sanskrit: देव, deva) means "shining", "exalted", or "heavenly being" in Indian religions. In Hinduism, devas are benevolent supernatural beings and deities who inhabit the celestial realms (Svarga), preside over forces of nature and aspects of life, and stand in opposition to the asuras. The feminine form is devi (goddess).

The word derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *deiwós, "celestial" or "shining", cognate with Latin deus, Greek Zeus (gen. Dios), and the Old Norse Týr.

Etymology

Monier-Williams traces deva to the root div, "to shine", so that a deva is a "shining one". The term in the Rigveda can denote gods, divine powers, and exalted natural phenomena such as the dawn (Ushas) and fire (Agni).

Vedic devas

The Rigveda praises 33 principal devas, organized later tradition into groups of Vasus, Rudras, and Adityas. Prominent Vedic devas include Indra, king of the devas and god of storms; Agni, fire and sacrificial mediator; Surya and Savitr, solar deities; Varuna, guardian of cosmic order (rita); Vayu, wind; and Soma, both a god and the sacred ritual drink. Devas were sustained by yajna (sacrifice), through which humans and gods mutually upheld the cosmos.

Classical Hinduism

In later Hinduism the devas are subordinate to the supreme reality Brahman and to the great gods of the Trimurti — Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva — in sectarian traditions often regarded as forms or functions of the one God (Ishvara). Devas are powerful but not immortal in an absolute sense: they live for cosmic ages within samsara, dwell in Svarga under Indra, and can fall from heaven when their merit is exhausted.

Devas and asuras

Epic and Puranic literature narrates the perpetual rivalry of devas and asuras, children (in some accounts) of Kashyapa by different wives. Famous episodes include the churning of the Ocean of Milk (Samudra Manthana), in which both parties cooperated to obtain amrita, the nectar of immortality.

Number and worship

Popular tradition speaks of 33 koti devas — interpreted as 33 "types" or, in folk usage, 330 million deities — expressing the innumerability of the divine. Temples and household shrines honor devas as approachable presences, while philosophical schools interpret them variously as literal beings, cosmic functions, or symbols of the one Reality.
