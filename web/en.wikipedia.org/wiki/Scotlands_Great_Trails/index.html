Scotland's Great Trails

Scotland's Great Trails are long-distance "people-powered" trails (predominantly used by walkers, but also in part by cyclists and horse-riders) in Scotland. The network, managed and promoted by NatureScot, comprises 29 routes totalling around 3,000 kilometres (1,900 miles) in the network, running through Scotland's varied landscapes from the Southern Uplands to the Highlands and Islands.

The trails were formerly known as Long Distance Routes; they were rebranded as Scotland's Great Trails in 2010, with new waymarking using a stylised thistle within a hexagon.

History

The idea for long-distance routes in Scotland grew out of the Countryside (Scotland) Act 1967, which empowered the Countryside Commission for Scotland to approve long-distance routes. The first route to open was the West Highland Way in 1980, running 154 km (96 miles) from Milngavie to Fort William; it remains the most popular of the trails, attracting tens of thousands of walkers each year. Many routes follow historic rights of way, drove roads, military roads built after the Jacobite risings, canals and disused railway lines.

Principal trails

  • West Highland Way — Milngavie to Fort William, 154 km.
  • Great Glen Way — Fort William to Inverness along the Caledonian Canal and Loch Ness, 127 km.
  • Southern Upland Way — Portpatrick to Cockburnspath, coast to coast, 344 km — the longest of the trails.
  • Speyside Way — Buckie to Aviemore through whisky country, about 137 km with spurs.
  • Rob Roy Way — Drymen to Pitlochry, 127 km.
  • John Muir Way — Helensburgh to Dunbar, 215 km, named for the Scottish-born conservationist.
  • Hebridean Way — a walking and cycling route through the Outer Hebrides, 247 km walking.
  • St Cuthbert's Way, Fife Coastal Path, Moray Coast Trail, Kintyre Way, Clyde Walkway, and others.

Use and management

Unlike England and Wales, Scotland has no National Trails designated under the same statutory scheme; the Great Trails are instead coordinated by NatureScot and maintained by local authorities, landowners and partner bodies. Walkers benefit from Scotland's statutory right of responsible access under the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003, which the trail network complements with waymarked, managed paths.
