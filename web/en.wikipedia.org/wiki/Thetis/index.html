Thetis

Thetis
Greek: Θέτις
Abode: the sea
Parents: Nereus and Doris
Consort: Peleus
Children: Achilles
Group: the Nereids (fifty sea nymphs)
Epithet: "silver-footed"

Thetis (Greek: Θέτις) is a sea goddess in Greek mythology, one of the fifty Nereids, the daughters of the ancient sea god Nereus and the Oceanid Doris. She is best known as the mother of the hero Achilles, whose fate dominates her story: a goddess compelled to marry a mortal, and a mother who knows that her son is fated to die young at Troy.

Mythology

Both Zeus and Poseidon desired Thetis, but they withdrew when the Titan Prometheus (or the prophecy of Themis) revealed that she was destined to bear a son mightier than his father — a son who would have threatened the rule of the gods themselves. Zeus therefore decreed that she marry the mortal hero Peleus. Thetis, unwilling, transformed herself in succession into fire, water, wind, a tree, a bird, and various beasts as Peleus grappled with her, but he held on — advised by the centaur Chiron — until she resumed her form and consented.

The wedding of Peleus and Thetis, held on Mount Pelion and attended by the gods, was one of the most celebrated events of the mythological age: it was there that Eris threw down the golden Apple of Discord inscribed "to the fairest", provoking the Judgement of Paris and ultimately the Trojan War.

Mother of Achilles

Thetis bore Peleus a son, Achilles. In a later and famous version of the story, she attempted to make the infant invulnerable by dipping him into the river Styx, holding him by the heel — the one part left dry and thus the hero's single vulnerable spot, the origin of the expression "Achilles' heel". Other traditions say she tried to burn away his mortality in fire and was interrupted by Peleus.

In the Iliad, Thetis' grief and foreknowledge pervade the poem. She intercedes with Zeus at Achilles' request, asking that the Trojans prevail until the Achaeans honour her son; she laments that his life will be short; and after Patroclus' death she brings Achilles new armour forged by Hephaestus — a divine favour she could call upon because she had once sheltered the smith god. After Achilles' death she mourned him with the other Nereids.

Other tales and cult

Thetis also aided the gods themselves: when Zeus was once threatened with binding by the other Olympians, she summoned the hundred-handed Briareos to his side. She received the fugitive Dionysus into the sea. Though she had no great temples, she was honoured with local cults in Thessaly — the region of Peleus' kingdom — and appears throughout Greek vase painting, often at the moment of her wrestling match with Peleus or rising from the sea with Achilles' armour.
