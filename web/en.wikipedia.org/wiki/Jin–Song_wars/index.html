Jin–Song wars

The Jin–Song wars were a series of conflicts between the Jurchen-led Jin dynasty and the Han Chinese Song dynasty in the 12th and 13th centuries. Beginning in 1125, the Jin conquered northern China, extinguishing the Northern Song, and for more than a century the two states contested the Huai River frontier before both fell in turn to the Mongol Empire.

Conquest of the north (1125–1127)

The Song had allied with the rising Jurchen Jin against their common enemy, the Khitan Liao dynasty — the "Alliance Conducted at Sea" (1120) — expecting to recover the Sixteen Prefectures around modern Beijing. When Song armies performed poorly and the Jin took the Liao territories themselves, relations collapsed. In 1125 Jin armies invaded, and in the Jingkang incident of 1127 they captured the Song capital Bianjing (Kaifeng) and abducted the emperors Huizong and Qinzong along with thousands of members of the court, ending the Northern Song.

Southern Song resistance

A surviving prince re-established the dynasty as the Southern Song at Lin'an (Hangzhou). Jin forces pursued him south of the Yangtze but were checked by Song generals, most famously Yue Fei, whose campaigns recovered territory before he was recalled and executed in 1142 amid peace negotiations. The Treaty of Shaoxing (1141) fixed the frontier along the Huai River, with the Song paying annual tribute. Later Song attempts to recover the north (notably in 1206–08) failed, as did Jin invasions in reply.

Mongol era

Weakened by the Mongol conquests after 1211, the Jin succumbed in 1234 — ironically in a final joint operation with the Song, who then fought the Mongols alone until the last Song emperor died at the Battle of Yamen in 1279, completing the Mongol unification of China under the Yuan dynasty.
