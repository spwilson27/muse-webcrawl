Price elasticity of supply

The price elasticity of supply (PES or Es) is a measure used in economics to show the responsiveness, or elasticity, of the quantity supplied of a good or service to a change in its price. It is the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price:

Es = (% change in quantity supplied) / (% change in price)

Because the law of supply states that quantity supplied rises with price, the price elasticity of supply is normally positive. When Es > 1 supply is "elastic" — quantity supplied is more than proportionately responsive to price; when Es < 1 supply is "inelastic".

Types

Es = 0Perfectly inelastic — quantity does not changeA fixed seat capacity in the very short run
0 < Es < 1Relatively inelasticAgricultural output within a season
Es = 1Unit elastic—
Es > 1Relatively elasticManufactured goods with spare capacity
Es = ∞Perfectly elasticPrice-taking firms facing a market price

Determinants

The most important determinant is time: supply is usually more elastic in the long run than in the short run, because producers can build capacity, enter or exit. Other factors include the availability of spare capacity, the ease of switching production between products, the mobility of factors of production, the ability to store output (storable goods have more elastic supply), and the length and complexity of the production process. The supply of housing in a city with strict land-use limits and scarce land, for example, is notably inelastic, whereas the supply of simple manufactured goods can be highly elastic.

Applications

Elasticity of supply, together with price elasticity of demand, determines who bears the burden (tax incidence) of an indirect tax: the relatively inelastic side of the market bears more of the tax. It also shapes the effect of subsidies, price spikes in commodity markets — oil supply is very inelastic in the short run, amplifying price volatility — and the response of labour supply to wage changes.
