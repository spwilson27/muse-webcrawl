Shutter (photography)

Shutter
FunctionControls the duration of light exposure (shutter speed)
TypesFocal-plane, leaf (central), electronic/rolling
Typical speeds30 s to 1/8000 s (mechanical/electronic)
Related measuresExposure value; reciprocal of shutter time

In photography, a shutter is a device that allows light to pass for a determined period, exposing photographic film or a light-sensitive electronic sensor to light in order to capture a permanent image of a scene. The duration for which the shutter remains open — the shutter speed or exposure time — together with the aperture and sensor sensitivity (ISO), determines the total exposure, and also controls how motion is rendered in the image.

Types

A focal-plane shutter, located immediately in front of the film or sensor, uses two travelling curtains: the first opens to begin the exposure and the second closes to end it. At fast speeds the second curtain begins to close before the first has finished, so the image is exposed through a moving slit — which sets a limit (the flash sync speed, typically around 1/200 s) above which a flash cannot illuminate the whole frame at once. A leaf shutter, built into the lens, opens and closes with a set of overlapping blades; because the whole frame is exposed simultaneously it can sync with flash at any speed, and is favoured in many medium- and large-format and compact cameras. Many digital cameras additionally offer a fully electronic shutter, in which the sensor is read out line by line ("rolling shutter"), silently but with the risk of skewing fast-moving subjects.

Shutter speed and motion

Long exposures blur or erase moving subjects and are used deliberately for effects such as silky waterfalls, light trails and night photography; very fast speeds freeze motion, from a sprinter's stride to a water droplet. Hand-holding a camera at slow speeds introduces camera shake, countered by stabilisation and the rough rule of thumb that shutter speed should not be slower than the reciprocal of the focal length.

History

The earliest photographers simply removed and replaced a lens cap, exposures lasting seconds or minutes. Mechanical shutters developed in the late 19th century as emulsions grew faster; by the early 20th century pneumatic and clockwork leaf and focal-plane designs offered timed speeds down to fractions of a second, and the focal-plane shutter of the Leica made fast 35 mm photography practical.
