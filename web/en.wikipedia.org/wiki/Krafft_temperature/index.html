Krafft temperature

The Krafft temperature (also known as the Krafft point) is the minimum temperature at which surfactants form micelles. It is named after the German chemist Friedrich Krafft, who described the phenomenon in 1895. Below the Krafft temperature, the solubility of the surfactant is too low for micellisation to occur, no matter how much surfactant is added; above it, solubility rises sharply and micelles form once the concentration exceeds the critical micelle concentration (CMC).

Definition

At the Krafft temperature the solubility curve of the surfactant intersects the CMC-versus-temperature curve. Below this temperature the equilibrium surfactant species in water is the crystalline (hydrated) solid, whose solubility is lower than the CMC, so saturation is reached before micelles can form; added surfactant simply precipitates. Above the Krafft temperature the monomer solubility surpasses the CMC, excess surfactant aggregates into micelles, and the apparent solubility increases dramatically.

Factors affecting the Krafft temperature

The Krafft temperature depends strongly on surfactant structure. It generally increases with the length of the hydrophobic alkyl chain (for sodium alkyl sulfates it rises steeply with chain length), and is affected by the counterion — changing the counterion can shift the Krafft point by tens of degrees, a fact exploited in detergent formulation. Branching, unsaturation or a bulky headgroup tends to lower the Krafft point by disrupting crystal packing. Added electrolytes usually raise the Krafft temperature of ionic surfactants, whereas additives such as urea or alcohols may lower it. Nonionic surfactants typically have Krafft points below 0 °C and instead exhibit a cloud point on heating.

Significance

Detergents and other surfactant products must function at their use temperature: an ionic surfactant is useless as a micelle-forming cleaner below its Krafft point. Cold-water laundry detergents therefore favour surfactants and counterions with low Krafft temperatures, or nonionic surfactants, and builders and co-surfactants are used to suppress crystallisation. The Krafft temperature also matters in pharmaceutical solubilisation, enhanced oil recovery and the processing of soaps — traditional sodium soaps of long-chain fatty acids have high Krafft points, which is why soap performs poorly in cold, hard water.
