Boulonnais horse

The Boulonnais, also known as the "white marble horse", is a French breed of draught horse, developed in the Boulogne-sur-Mer district of the Pas-de-Calais département in north-western France. It is renowned for its elegant conformation — unusual among heavy horses — its predominantly grey coat, and its energetic, free action, which allowed it to trot loads of fresh fish rapidly from the Channel ports to Paris.

History

The breed's origins are traditionally traced to horses brought to the region before the Crusades, with later infusions of Spanish, Arabian and Andalusian blood during Spanish occupation, and of Mecklenburg and other draught stock. In the nineteenth century the Boulonnais was selected for size and power for farm and haulage work; the celebrated route du poisson teams carried fish from Boulogne to Paris in under 18 hours. Numbers collapsed after the Second World War with mechanisation, and the breed survived mainly because it was prized for the production of horse meat. The stud book, first established in the late nineteenth century, continues under French national supervision.

Characteristics

The Boulonnais stands roughly 155–180 cm and weighs 600–900 kg. The head is fine and expressive with a straight profile; the neck is arched, the body deep and muscular, and the movement notably lively for so large a horse. Coats are mostly shades of grey, with chestnut and black also accepted. Two types are recognised: the large draught type and the smaller, lighter mareyeur or "fish cart" type.
