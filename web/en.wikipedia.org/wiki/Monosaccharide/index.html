Monosaccharides (from Greek monos: single, sacchar: sugar), also called simple sugars, are the simplest forms of sugar and the most basic units (monomers) from which all carbohydrates are built. They are usually colourless, water-soluble, crystalline solids. Some monosaccharides have a sweet taste. Most monosaccharides have the formula (CH2O)x where x is usually between 3 and 7, although deoxyribose (C5H10O4) is an exception.

Structure and nomenclature

With few exceptions (e.g., deoxyribose), monosaccharides have the chemical formula (CH2O)n. They are classified both by the number of carbon atoms they contain and by their carbonyl group:

  • Trioses (3 carbons), tetroses (4), pentoses (5), hexoses (6), and heptoses (7).
  • Aldoses bear an aldehyde group at carbon 1 (e.g., glucose, ribose); ketoses bear a ketone group, usually at carbon 2 (e.g., fructose, ribulose).

Because each monosaccharide contains multiple chiral centres, many stereoisomers exist. The D/L system, based on the configuration of the chiral centre furthest from the carbonyl group, distinguishes the two mirror-image families; most naturally occurring sugars are D-sugars. In aqueous solution, monosaccharides with five or more carbons cyclise: the carbonyl carbon reacts with a hydroxyl group to form a hemiacetal (or hemiketal) ring, most often a six-membered pyranose or five-membered furanose ring. Cyclisation creates a new stereocentre at the anomeric carbon, giving α and β anomers that interconvert by mutarotation.

Reactions and derivatives

Monosaccharides are reducing sugars: their free aldehyde or ketone (or the open-chain form in equilibrium with the ring) can reduce reagents such as Benedict's and Fehling's solutions. Glycosidic bonds join monosaccharides into disaccharides (e.g., sucrose, lactose, maltose), oligosaccharides and polysaccharides, with the release of water. Oxidation of glucose yields gluconic acid; reduction yields sugar alcohols such as sorbitol. Amino sugars, such as glucosamine, replace a hydroxyl group with an amino group and occur in cartilage and chitin.

Role in biology

Glucose is the central fuel of cellular respiration in most organisms, and monosaccharides serve as building blocks for nucleic acids (ribose, deoxyribose), structural polymers (cellulose, chitin) and energy stores (starch, glycogen). In blood, glucose concentration is tightly regulated by the hormones insulin and glucagon; failure of this regulation underlies diabetes mellitus.
