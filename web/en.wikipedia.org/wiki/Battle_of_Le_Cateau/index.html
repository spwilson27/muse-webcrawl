Battle of Le Cateau

The Battle of Le Cateau was fought on 26 August 1914 during the Great Retreat of the First World War. The British Expeditionary Force's II Corps, commanded by General Sir Horace Smith-Dorrien, stood and fought the pursuing German First Army around Le Cateau-Cambrésis in northern France rather than continue retreating, buying time for the rest of the BEF to escape at the cost of heavy casualties.

Background

After the Battle of Mons on 23 August, the BEF retreated south in extreme heat. By the evening of 25 August, II Corps was exhausted, strung out and closely pressed by von Kluck's First Army; Smith-Dorrien judged that his men could not outmarch the Germans and, contrary to the spirit of Sir John French's orders to continue the retreat, decided to turn and fight a delaying action on the ridge west of Le Cateau.

The battle

The British deployed some 40,000 men and 246 guns in exposed positions, with little time to dig in. German infantry and massed artillery attacked from dawn; the British right flank was turned when the neighbouring French cavalry withdrew, and German troops entered Le Cateau itself. Rapid rifle fire — the famous "mad minute" standard of the pre-war regulars — inflicted heavy losses on the attackers, but by afternoon the position was untenable and Smith-Dorrien ordered a withdrawal, covered gallantly by the artillery and rear guards. Many guns had to be abandoned, and stragglers were rounded up in the night march that followed.

Aftermath

British casualties were about 7,800 killed, wounded and missing, with 38 guns lost — the BEF's costliest day since Mons. German losses were comparable or somewhat lower. Smith-Dorrien's stand is generally judged to have saved II Corps from destruction and delayed the German pursuit, allowing the BEF to retreat to the Marne, where it joined the Allied counter-offensive in September.
