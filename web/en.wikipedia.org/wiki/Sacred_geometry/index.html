Sacred geometry

Sacred geometry ascribes symbolic and sacred meanings to certain geometric shapes and proportions. It is associated with the belief that a god or gods created the universe according to a geometric plan, so that the geometry of nature and of sacred architecture reveals divine order. The study of sacred geometry has roots in the study of nature and the mathematical principles at work therein, and it has been practised in the design of temples, churches, mosques, altars, and monuments in many cultures.

History

The four elements and their associated solids in Plato's philosophy gave early expression to the idea that geometry underlies creation; the so-called Platonic solids were later linked by Kepler to the structure of the solar system. In ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, India, and Greece, religious buildings were laid out using proportions held to be harmonious and cosmically significant. Medieval cathedral builders employed geometric schemes such as ad quadratum and ad triangulum, and Islamic art, forbidden from depicting living beings in religious contexts in many traditions, developed elaborate geometric ornament.

Key figures and proportions

  • The golden ratio (φ ≈ 1.618) is said to recur in nature, art, and architecture, including the Parthenon and the Great Pyramid of Giza; many such claims are disputed by mathematicians.
  • The Flower of Life, a pattern of overlapping circles, and its derivatives the Seed of Life and Metatron's cube, from which the Platonic solids can be drawn.
  • Mandalas and yantras in Hindu and Buddhist practice, used as aids to meditation and as maps of the cosmos.
  • The vesica piscis, formed by two overlapping circles, a symbol of fertility and, in Christian iconography, of Christ.
  • Labyrinths, such as the pavement labyrinth at Chartres Cathedral, used for contemplative walking.

In architecture and art

Sacred geometry is invoked in accounts of the pyramids of Egypt, Stonehenge, the Parthenon, Hindu temples laid out according to Vastu shastra, the Borobudur stupa-mandala, and Renaissance works shaped by the rediscovery of classical proportion, such as those of Leonardo da Vinci, whose Vitruvian Man links the human figure to the circle and square. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the concept has been popularised by New Age writers and artists, and geometric patterns attributed with sacred significance are widely reproduced in jewellery, tattoos, and music and festival art.

Criticism

Mathematicians and historians frequently note that claims about sacred proportions in famous buildings rest on selective measurement, and that recurrence of simple ratios may reflect practical building constraints rather than cosmic symbolism. The subject nevertheless remains influential in religious art and architecture and in popular spirituality.
