Othniel Charles Marsh (October 29, 1831 – March 18, 1899) was an American paleontologist and professor at Yale University. One of the preeminent scientists of nineteenth-century America, he named and described dozens of dinosaur genera and assembled enormous fossil collections. His bitter rivalry with Edward Drinker Cope — the "Bone Wars" — drove the rapid discovery of dinosaurs in the American West.

Early life and education

Marsh was born in Lockport, New York, and raised in modest circumstances. His education was transformed by the support of his uncle, the banker and philanthropist George Peabody, whose funding later established Yale's Peabody Museum of Natural History. Marsh graduated from Yale in 1860 and studied geology and mineralogy in Germany. In 1866 he became professor of paleontology at Yale — the first such chair in the United States — a post he held for the rest of his life.

Western expeditions and discoveries

Between 1870 and 1873 Marsh led Yale student expeditions across the Great Plains and Rocky Mountains, collecting under military escort amid conflict on the frontier. He publicised the fossil sequence of horse evolution in North America, which Thomas Henry Huxley famously used as evidence in lectures supporting Darwin. From 1882 to 1892 Marsh served as vertebrate paleontologist of the U.S. Geological Survey, allowing him to employ collectors at rich sites such as Como Bluff, Wyoming, and the Morrison Formation quarries of Colorado.

Marsh's teams recovered such dinosaurs as Allosaurus, Stegosaurus, Triceratops, Diplodocus, and Apatosaurus (his "Brontosaurus"), and he also described toothed birds such as Hesperornis and Ichthyornis from the Kansas chalk. In all, he named some 500 new fossil species.

The Bone Wars

Marsh's feud with Cope began in friendship and degenerated into accusations of theft, spying, bribery of quarrymen, and the destruction of fossils to keep them from the rival camp. Both men rushed into print with hasty, sometimes erroneous descriptions, and both damaged their fortunes and reputations; yet the competition produced a flood of discoveries that defined classical dinosaur science. Their public quarrel, aired in the New York Herald in 1890, embarrassed American science.

Legacy

Marsh never married and left his collections, and eventually his house, to Yale. The Peabody Museum's Great Hall displays many of his finds. Though later research synonymised some of his taxa — and corrected errors such as the wrong skull placed on his Apatosaurus mount — his role in founding American vertebrate paleontology is undisputed.
