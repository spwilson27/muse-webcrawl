Community of practice

A community of practice (CoP) is a group of people who share a concern or a passion for something they do and learn how to do it better as they interact regularly. The concept was introduced by cognitive anthropologist Jean Lave and educational theorist Étienne Wenger in their 1991 book Situated Learning, which described learning as legitimate peripheral participation in social practice, and was developed further by Wenger in Communities of Practice: Learning, Meaning, and Identity (1998).

Characteristics

Wenger identifies three components that characterise a community of practice: mutual engagement (members interact and build relationships), a joint enterprise (a shared understanding of what the community is about, negotiated by its members), and a shared repertoire (common language, tools, stories, and ways of doing things developed over time). Membership typically moves from the periphery toward full participation as newcomers learn from old-timers — the process of legitimate peripheral participation.

Applications

The concept has been widely adopted in organisational theory, education, and knowledge management. Companies and agencies deliberately cultivate communities of practice to share expertise across departmental boundaries, onboard new employees, and sustain innovation; the World Bank and other large organisations popularised the model in the late 1990s. Online communities, professional societies, and hobbyist groups can all be analysed as communities of practice, though critics note the term is sometimes applied so loosely that it loses analytical force, and that power relations within communities deserve more attention than the original model gave them.
