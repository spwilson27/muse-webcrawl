Lead chamber process

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The lead chamber process was an industrial method used to produce sulfuric acid in large quantities. Patented by John Roebuck in 1746, it replaced earlier small-scale glass-vessel methods and supplied the growing acid demand of the Industrial Revolution for more than a century.

The process

Sulfur (or sulfur-bearing pyrite) is burned with saltpetre to produce sulfur dioxide, which is led, together with oxides of nitrogen, steam, and air, into large chambers lined with sheet lead — a material resistant to the acid formed. In the chamber, nitrogen oxides catalyse the oxidation of sulfur dioxide; the chemistry proceeds in part through the intermediate compound nitrosylsulfuric acid ("chamber crystals"). Dilute "chamber acid" (around 60–70% H2SO4) collects on the chamber floor.

Later improvements included the Glover tower (1859), which recovered nitrogen oxides and concentrated the acid, and the Gay-Lussac tower, which absorbed escaping nitrous gases so the catalyst could be recycled, greatly reducing saltpetre consumption.

Decline

The chamber process produced only moderately concentrated acid and was progressively displaced in the 20th century by the contact process, which yields concentrated acid and oleum. A small number of chamber plants survived into the late 20th century for specialty production.
