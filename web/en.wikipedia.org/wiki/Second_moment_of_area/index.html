Second moment of area

The second moment of area, also known as the area moment of inertia, is a geometrical property of a cross-section that reflects how its points are distributed with respect to an axis. It is used in structural and mechanical engineering to predict a beam's resistance to bending and deflection: the greater the second moment of area about the bending axis, the stiffer the beam. It has units of length to the fourth power (e.g. m4 or in4). Despite the alternative name "moment of inertia", it is not the mass moment of inertia and does not describe rotational dynamics.

Definition

For a plane area, the second moment about an axis x in its plane is defined as Ix = ∫ y² dA, where y is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the element of area dA. Analogous definitions hold about other axes, and the polar second moment of area, J = ∫ r² dA = Ix + Iy, about an axis perpendicular to the plane, is used in torsion calculations.

Common cross-sections

For a rectangle of width b and height h, the second moment about its horizontal centroidal axis is I = bh³/12. For a solid circle of radius r, I = πr⁴/4 about a diameter. For a hollow circular tube, I = π(R⁴ − r⁴)/4 from the outer and inner radii. Standard formulae are tabulated for structural shapes such as I-beams, channels, and angles; the familiar efficiency of the I-beam arises directly from placing most material in the flanges, far from the neutral axis, where the y² weighting makes it count most.

Parallel axis theorem

The second moment about any axis can be found from that about a parallel centroidal axis by the parallel axis (Steiner) theorem: I = Ic + A d², where A is the area and d the distance between the axes. This makes it easy to compute properties of composite sections by summing the contributions of their parts.

Applications

In Euler–Bernoulli beam theory the curvature of a bent beam is proportional to the bending moment divided by the product EI, where E is Young's modulus and I the second moment of area; the same product governs deflection formulae and Euler's buckling load for columns, Pcr = π²EI/(KL)². Bending stress is computed as σ = My/I, so for a given moment the stress is lowest where I — and the distance to material — is arranged most favourably.
