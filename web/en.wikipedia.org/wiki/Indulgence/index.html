Indulgence

In the teaching of the Catholic Church, an indulgence (Latin: indulgentia) is "a remission before God of the temporal punishment due to sins whose guilt has already been forgiven, which the faithful Christian who is duly disposed gains under certain prescribed conditions through the action of the Church". An indulgence is plenary if it removes all temporal punishment, or partial if it removes only part. Indulgences may be applied to the living or, by way of suffrage, to the dead in purgatory.

Theological basis

Catholic doctrine distinguishes the guilt of sin and its eternal punishment — remitted in the sacrament of Penance — from the temporal punishment that remains, which is satisfied by works of penance in this life or in purgatory. Indulgences draw on the "treasury of merit" — the infinite merits of Christ and the superabundant merits of the saints — which the Church, by the power of binding and loosing (Matthew 16:19), dispenses to the faithful under conditions that normally include confession, communion, prayer for the pope's intentions and detachment from sin.

History

Indulgences developed from the early Church's practice of relaxing the rigours of canonical penance, and from the commutation of penances into prayers, fasting or almsgiving. From the 11th century, popes granted plenary indulgences to participants in the Crusades. Gradually the granting of indulgences became associated with monetary offerings, and by the late Middle Ages professional sellers (quaestores, "pardoners") marketed them aggressively.

Abuses and the Reformation

The campaign of the Dominican friar Johann Tetzel, who sold indulgences to finance the rebuilding of St Peter's Basilica in Rome, provoked Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses of 1517 — the opening act of the Protestant Reformation. The Council of Trent (1563) upheld indulgences but condemned abuses, and Pope Pius V abolished their sale in 1567.

Modern practice

Pope Paul VI's apostolic constitution Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1967) reformed the system, simplifying partial indulgences and emphasising conversion of heart. Plenary indulgences are prominent in Holy Years (Jubilees), when pilgrims pass through a Holy Door, and are attached to acts such as praying the Rosary in common or adoring the Eucharist for half an hour.
