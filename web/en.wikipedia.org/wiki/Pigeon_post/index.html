Pigeon post

A homing pigeon with a message capsule

Pigeon post is the use of homing pigeons to carry messages. Pigeons have an innate homing ability: when transported away from their home loft, they will fly back to it over long distances. By attaching a small message to a bird released far from home, correspondents could send information quickly and reliably for centuries before the telegraph and telephone.

History

The use of pigeons as messengers is ancient. The Egyptians and Persians used them more than 3,000 years ago, and the outcomes of the Olympic Games were reportedly carried to Athens by pigeon. In the Islamic world, Sultan Nur ad-Din established a formal pigeon-post service in the twelfth century, and Genghis Khan maintained a pigeon relay across his empire. In the nineteenth century, Paul Julius Reuter used pigeons to carry stock prices between Aachen and Brussels, bridging a gap in the telegraph network and founding his news agency on the service.

Military use

During the Siege of Paris (1870–71) in the Franco-Prussian War, pigeons carried microfilmed dispatches into the besieged city, the only link with the outside world. In the First World War both sides used hundreds of thousands of pigeons; the American bird Cher Ami famously delivered a message that saved the "Lost Battalion" despite being badly wounded. Pigeons were used again in the Second World War, and some, such as G.I. Joe and Paddy, received the Dickin Medal for gallantry.

Method

Messages were written on thin, light paper, rolled into a small tube or capsule, and attached to the bird's leg or back. Later microfilm allowed thousands of messages to be carried on a single flight. Because a pigeon flies only towards its home loft, two-way communication required lofts at both ends, each stocking birds homed to the other.

Decline

Electronic communication largely ended organised pigeon post in the twentieth century, though the birds retained niche military and ceremonial roles, and pigeon racing developed as a popular sport from the same homing instinct.
