Pseudogene

Pseudogenes are nonfunctional segments of DNA that resemble functional genes. Pseudogenes can be formed from both protein-coding genes and RNA genes. Originally thought of as "junk DNA", they are now known in some cases to regulate their parent genes or produce functional RNAs. The human genome contains roughly 15,000 pseudogenes, a number comparable to its count of functional protein-coding genes.

Types

  • Processed (retrotransposed) pseudogenes — created when an mRNA is reverse-transcribed and reinserted into the genome; they lack introns and promoters and carry a poly-A tail remnant.
  • Non-processed (duplicated) pseudogenes — arise by gene duplication followed by accumulation of disabling mutations (stop codons, frameshifts) in one copy; they retain exon–intron structure.
  • Unitary pseudogenes — single-copy genes disabled by mutation without duplication, such as the human GULO gene, whose loss explains why humans cannot synthesise vitamin C.

Evolutionary significance

Because pseudogenes are freed from selective constraint, they accumulate mutations at a steady neutral rate, acting as "molecular fossils" that record a genome's history. Comparing pseudogenes across species reveals genes that were active in ancestors — for example, numerous olfactory receptor pseudogenes in primates and dolphins reflect reduced reliance on smell.

Function

Although most pseudogenes are not translated, some are transcribed and play regulatory roles: pseudogene RNAs can act as microRNA sponges (competing endogenous RNAs), as in the regulation of the tumour suppressor PTEN by its pseudogene PTENP1. Errors in distinguishing pseudogenes from genes can also complicate disease-genetic studies, for example in pharmacogenes with close pseudogene copies.
