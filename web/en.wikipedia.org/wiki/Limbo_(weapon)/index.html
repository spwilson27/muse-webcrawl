Limbo, or the Anti Submarine Mortar Mark 10 (A/S Mk.10), was the final development of the forward-throwing anti-submarine weapon Squid, designed during the Second World War and developed during the 1950s by the Admiralty Underwater Weapons Establishment in Portland. It was operated by the Royal Navy and exported to allied navies including those of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, the Netherlands and Sweden.

Description

Limbo consisted of three 12-inch (305 mm) mortar barrels mounted on a stabilised, trainable platform, usually at the stern of an escort vessel. It fired 350-pound (159 kg) projectiles containing about 200 lb (91 kg) of minol explosive in a pattern ahead of or around the ship. The range was controlled by adjusting gas vents — varying between roughly 400 and 1,000 yards — and the mounting was fully automatic: loading, training and firing were controlled from the sonar operations room, with the firing solution derived from the ship's Type 170 attack sonar.

Service

Fitted to Royal Navy frigates from the mid-1950s, including the Whitby, Rothesay, Leander and Tribal classes, Limbo remained the principal shipboard anti-submarine weapon of the Royal Navy until replaced in some roles by the Ikara missile and shipborne helicopters carrying torpedoes. A notable action occurred during the Falklands War of 1982, when Limbo mortars from HMS Plymouth and other escorts were used against Argentine submarine contacts; the weapon is also credited in attacks connected with the loss of the submarine ARA Santa Fe's contemporaries. Several Limbo mountings survive as museum exhibits.
