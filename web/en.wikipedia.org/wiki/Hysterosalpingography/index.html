Hysterosalpingography

Hysterosalpingography (HSG), also known as uterosalpingography, is a radiologic procedure used to investigate the shape of the uterine cavity and the patency (openness) of the fallopian tubes. It is a key investigation in the evaluation of female infertility. The resulting image is called a hysterosalpingogram.

Procedure

The test is usually performed in the first part of the menstrual cycle, after menstruation has finished and before ovulation, to avoid irradiating an early pregnancy. A cannula is placed in the cervix and a water-based or oil-based contrast medium is injected under fluoroscopic guidance. X-ray images are taken as the contrast outlines the uterine cavity and passes through the tubes; free spill of contrast into the peritoneal cavity demonstrates tubal patency. The procedure commonly causes cramping, for which analgesia may be given.

Findings and significance

HSG can reveal congenital uterine anomalies (such as a septate or bicornuate uterus), intrauterine adhesions (Asherman's syndrome), polyps and fibroids distorting the cavity, and tubal blockage, including the dilated, blocked tube of a hydrosalpinx. Interestingly, the procedure may itself improve subsequent pregnancy rates, possibly by flushing debris from the tubes; evidence suggests this effect is greater with oil-based contrast. Risks include infection (a particular concern with dilated tubes), allergic reaction to contrast, and a small radiation dose. Alternatives and complementary investigations include hysteroscopy, sonohysterography with saline or contrast, and laparoscopy with dye testing.
