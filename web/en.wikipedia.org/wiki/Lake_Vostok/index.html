Lake Vostok (Russian: Озеро Восток, Ozero Vostok) is the largest of Antarctica's subglacial lakes, buried beneath about four kilometres of ice beneath Russia's Vostok Station in East Antarctica. It is one of the largest lakes in the world by surface area and volume, yet its liquid water has been isolated from the atmosphere for millions of years, warmed from below by geothermal heat and kept liquid under immense pressure.

Discovery

The existence of a lake under the ice was suspected from seismic soundings in the 1960s–70s and confirmed in the 1990s by ice-penetrating radar and satellite altimetry, which revealed the strikingly flat ice surface above it. The lake lies in a rift valley and may be divided into two deep basins by a ridge.

Exploration

The Vostok ice core, drilled from the station above the lake, provided a climate record extending back more than 400,000 years before drilling was halted short of the lake to avoid contamination. In February 2012 Russian scientists completed a borehole that reached the lake surface, allowing lake water to rise and freeze in the hole for sampling; later expeditions recovered samples of this refrozen water and of accretion ice — lake water frozen onto the underside of the ice sheet. Genetic studies of accretion ice have reported DNA sequences suggesting a complex ecosystem may exist in the dark, nutrient-poor lake, possibly sustained by chemosynthesis and hydrothermal activity, making Vostok an analogue for icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus. The findings remain debated because of contamination concerns.
