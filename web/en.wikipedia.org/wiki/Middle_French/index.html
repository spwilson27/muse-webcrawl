Middle French (moyen français)
Era: c. 1340 – c. 1611
Region: France
Family: Indo-European → Romance → Gallo-Romance → French

Middle French (French: moyen français) is a historical division of the French language spanning roughly the period from 1340 to 1611, between Old French and Modern French. It was the language of the French Renaissance, of François Rabelais, Michel de Montaigne, Pierre de Ronsard and the poets of the Pléiade.

Changes from Old French

During the Middle French period the language lost the two-case declension system of Old French, so word order and prepositions became the principal markers of grammatical function. Phonological changes included the loss of final consonants in many contexts, the denasalisation of some nasal vowels, and the development of new diphthongs. The vocabulary expanded enormously through learned borrowings from Latin and Greek and from Italian during the Italian Wars and the Renaissance.

Standardisation

The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) required French to be used in royal administration and law, displacing Latin. Writers and grammarians such as Joachim du Bellay, whose Défense et illustration de la langue française (1549) argued for enriching French, campaigned to make it a language worthy of literature, while printers such as Robert Estienne produced the first French dictionaries. By the early 17th century the further codification of usage by the Académie française (founded 1635) ushered in the Classical and Modern French period.
