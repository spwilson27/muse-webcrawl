Mitral valve prolapse (MVP), also known as Barlow's syndrome, is a valvular heart disease characterised by the displacement of an abnormally thickened mitral valve leaflet back into the left atrium during systole (contraction of the heart). It is the most common cause of severe non-ischaemic mitral regurgitation requiring surgery in developed countries, and affects an estimated 2–3% of the population under modern echocardiographic criteria.

In MVP one or both leaflets bulge ("prolapse") into the left atrium during systole. (Labelled placeholder image.)

Signs and causes

Many people with MVP have no symptoms. The characteristic auscultatory finding is a mid-systolic click, sometimes followed by a late systolic murmur of mitral regurgitation. Underlying the prolapse is usually myxomatous degeneration of the leaflets and chordae — the valve tissue becomes redundant and stretchy, as in Barlow's disease — while a milder form, fibroelastic deficiency, affects thinner leaflets in older patients. MVP is associated with heritable connective-tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, and it sometimes clusters in families with autosomal dominant inheritance.

Course and treatment

Most cases are benign and require only periodic echocardiographic surveillance. Complications in a minority include progressive mitral regurgitation leading to heart failure, infective endocarditis, arrhythmias and, rarely, sudden cardiac death — particularly in "malignant" bileaflet prolapse with ventricular ectopy. Significant regurgitation is treated by mitral valve repair (preferred) or replacement; asymptomatic patients need reassurance and management of palpitations or anxiety, and antibiotic prophylaxis is no longer routinely recommended solely for MVP.
