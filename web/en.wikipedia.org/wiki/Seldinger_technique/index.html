Seldinger technique

A guidewire passed through a needle allows safe catheter placement

The Seldinger technique, also known as Seldinger wire technique, is a medical procedure to obtain safe access to blood vessels and other hollow organs. It is named after Swedish radiologist Sven-Ivar Seldinger (1921–1998), who introduced the procedure in 1953. It revolutionised angiography by allowing a catheter to be introduced through a needle puncture alone, replacing surgical cut-down and rigid trocars.

Procedure

  1. The vessel or cavity is punctured with a hollow needle.
  2. A flexible guidewire with a soft, rounded tip is advanced through the needle into the lumen.
  3. The needle is withdrawn, leaving the guidewire in place.
  4. A dilator and/or the catheter itself is passed over the guidewire into position.
  5. The guidewire (and dilator) is removed, leaving the catheter in situ.

Uses

The technique underlies most modern vascular and interventional practice: diagnostic and interventional angiography and angioplasty, central venous catheterisation, percutaneous drainage of fluid collections and abscesses, gastrostomy placement, nephrostomy, and many interventional radiology and cardiology procedures. A modified Seldinger technique, using a small initial needle and micropuncture set, is common for difficult access. Ultrasound guidance is now standard for many applications.

Complications

Possible complications include bleeding or haematoma at the puncture site, infection, arterial dissection, air embolism and misplacement of the catheter, mitigated by imaging guidance and careful technique.
