Father of the House (United Kingdom)

The Father of the House is a title in the House of Commons of the United Kingdom given to the member of Parliament (MP) with the longest continuous service. The title is purely honorary and confers no formal powers, though the Father of the House traditionally presides over the election of a new Speaker following a general election, and is often called upon to speak on ceremonial occasions.

History

The title came into use in the nineteenth century, at first informally. It is distinct from the title "Father of the House of Commons" sometimes used historically for other senior figures. Since the late twentieth century the convention has been that the title belongs strictly to the MP with the longest unbroken service, regardless of age.

Role

The Father of the House's one formal duty arises when the Commons elects its Speaker at the start of a new Parliament or after a vacancy: the Father of the House takes the chair and presides over the proceedings. Fathers of the House also commonly make the first speech of congratulation to a newly elected Speaker and are consulted on matters of parliamentary custom.

Notable holders

Long-serving Fathers of the House have included Winston Churchill, who held the title from 1959 until his retirement in 1964; Tam Dalyell, the longest-serving Scottish MP; Edward Heath; and Kenneth Clarke, Father of the House from 2017 to 2019. The equivalent title in the House of Lords is not formally used, though the longest-serving peer is sometimes informally described in similar terms. The longest continuous Commons service of the modern era was that of several members exceeding forty years.
