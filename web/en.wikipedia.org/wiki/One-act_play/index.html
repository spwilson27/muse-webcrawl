One-act play

A one-act play is a play that has only one act, as distinct from plays that occur over several acts. One-act plays may consist of one or more scenes. The 20–50 minute plays are usually paired with other plays in an evening of theatre, or staged at festivals and competitions dedicated to short works.

History

The form has roots in ancient and medieval drama, but the modern one-act play flourished at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th century, when little theatre movements and groups such as the Abbey Theatre in Ireland and the Provincetown Players in the United States championed short new works. Anton Chekhov's farces, August Strindberg's chamber plays, and the early works of Eugene O'Neill and Samuel Beckett include notable one-act plays.

Characteristics

Because of their brevity, one-act plays usually focus on a single situation or episode, a small cast, and a concentrated effect. They are widely used in education and amateur theatre, and competitions for one-act plays are common in schools and community drama festivals.
