Rebound effect (conservation)

Rebound effect
FieldEnergy economics, environmental economics
DefinitionShare of expected energy savings offset by increased use
Extreme formJevons paradox ("backfire")
Named originKhazzoom–Brookes postulate (1980s–90s)
Rebound: part of the engineering saving from higher efficiency is taken back as increased use.

In energy economics, the rebound effect (or take-back effect) is the reduction in expected gains from a new technology that increases the efficiency of resource use, because of behavioral or other systemic responses. These responses usually counteract the beneficial effects of the new technology or other measure. For example, a driver who replaces a car with a more fuel-efficient model may drive more, because the cost per kilometre has fallen; a household that installs insulation may keep rooms warmer. If the responses are large enough that total resource use actually rises, this is called backfire, of which the classic statement is the Jevons paradox.

Mechanisms

  • Direct rebound — increased consumption of the same good or service whose efficiency improved (driving more in an efficient car).
  • Indirect rebound — money saved is spent on other goods and services that themselves use energy (flying on holiday with the fuel savings).
  • Economy-wide effects — efficiency lowers the effective price of energy services throughout the economy, stimulating growth and further energy demand (the Khazzoom–Brookes postulate).

Evidence and magnitude

Empirical estimates for household energy services in developed countries commonly place direct rebound in the range of roughly 10–30% for space heating, cooling, and personal transport, though estimates vary widely by sector and country, and are generally larger in developing economies where demand for energy services is far from saturated. Because of rebound, engineering projections of savings from efficiency standards systematically overstate realized energy and emissions reductions, and economists argue that efficiency policy is best combined with carbon pricing or caps, which prevent the savings from being fully spent on more energy use.

History

The idea originates with William Stanley Jevons, who argued in The Coal Question (1865) that more efficient steam engines increased, rather than decreased, Britain's coal consumption. The modern formulation followed work by Daniel Khazzoom (1980) and Len Brookes, and the term was popularized in the 1990s debates over the energy savings from lighting and appliance efficiency.
