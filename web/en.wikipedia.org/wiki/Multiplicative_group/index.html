Multiplicative group

In group theory, the multiplicative group is a group whose operation is multiplication. Many familiar algebraic structures become groups when equipped with multiplication rather than addition: the nonzero real numbers, the nonzero complex numbers, and the units of a ring are all examples.

Definition

A multiplicative group is a set G together with a binary operation, written as juxtaposition or with a centered dot, satisfying the group axioms: associativity, the existence of an identity element (usually denoted 1), and the existence of an inverse for every element. The identity element of a multiplicative group is called the one or unit element, and the inverse of an element a is written a−1.

The terminology contrasts with an additive group, in which the operation is written as addition, the identity is denoted 0, and inverses are written as negatives.

Examples

  • The set of nonzero rational numbers under multiplication.
  • The set of positive real numbers under multiplication, isomorphic to the additive group of all real numbers via the logarithm.
  • The group of units of a ring, often denoted R×; for a field this is the set of all nonzero elements.
  • The multiplicative group of integers modulo n, consisting of the residue classes coprime to n, of order φ(n) given by Euler's totient function.
  • The circle group of complex numbers of absolute value 1 under multiplication.

Group of units

For any ring R, the set of units (elements with a multiplicative inverse) forms a group under multiplication, called the group of units or multiplicative group of R. For a finite field with q elements, this group is cyclic of order q − 1.
