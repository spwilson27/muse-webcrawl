Rhynia is a genus of extinct single-species vascular plant, Rhynia gwynne-vaughanii, from the Early Devonian, preserved in the famous Rhynie chert of Aberdeenshire, Scotland. Described by Robert Kidston and William Lang in 1920, it is one of the earliest and best-understood land plants, its tissues preserved in cellular detail by siliceous hot-spring deposits.

Description

The sporophyte of Rhynia consisted of simple, leafless, dichotomously branching aerial stems up to about 20–30 cm tall, rising from a creeping rhizome anchored by rhizoids rather than true roots. The stems had a central strand of xylem — making it a true vascular plant — a cortex, and an epidermis with stomata. Branch tips bore oval sporangia that released spores. The plant lacked leaves, and photosynthesis was carried out by the green stems themselves.

Significance

Rhynia gives its name to the rhyniophytes, a grade of early vascular plants central to understanding how plants colonized land. Its gametophyte generation, known as Remyophyton delicatum, is also preserved in the Rhynie chert. The exceptional preservation of the site records an entire Early Devonian ecosystem, including some of the earliest known plant–fungus (mycorrhizal-like) associations.
