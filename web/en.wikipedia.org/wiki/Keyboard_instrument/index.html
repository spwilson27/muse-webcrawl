Keyboard instrument

A keyboard instrument is a musical instrument played using a keyboard, a row of levers that are pressed by the fingers. The most common of these are the piano, organ, and various electronic keyboards, including synthesizers and digital pianos. Other keyboard instruments include celestas, which are struck idiophones operated by a keyboard, and carillons, which are usually housed in bell towers or belfries of churches or municipal buildings.

Today, the term keyboard instrument is often used mainly to refer to keyboard-style synthesizers. Under the fingers of a sensitive performer, the keyboard may also be used to control dynamics, phrasing, shading, articulation, and other elements of expression—depending on the design and inherent capabilities of the instrument.

History

The earliest known keyboard instrument was the ancient Greek hydraulis, a type of pipe organ invented in the third century BC. The keys were likely balanced and could be played with a light touch, as is clear from the reference in a Latin poem by Claudian (late 4th century), who says magna levi detrudens murmura tactu . . . intonet, that is "let him thunder forth as he presses out mighty roarings with a light touch" (Paneg. Manlio Theodoro, 324–25).

From its invention until the fourteenth century, the organ remained the only keyboard instrument. Often, the organ did not feature a keyboard at all, but rather buttons or large levers operated by a whole hand, much less by a finger. Almost every keyboard until the fifteenth century had seven naturals to each octave.

The clavichord and the harpsichord appeared during the fourteenth century—the clavichord probably earlier. The harpsichord and clavichord were both common until the widespread adoption of the piano in the eighteenth century, after which their popularity decreased. The piano was revolutionary because a pianist could vary the volume (or dynamics) of the sound by varying the vigour with which each key was struck. The piano's full name is gravicembalo con piano e forte meaning "harpsichord with soft and loud" but can be shortened to piano-forte, which means "soft-loud" in Italian, then just piano. In its current form, the piano is a product of the late 19th century, and is far removed in both sound and appearance from the "pianos" known to Mozart, Haydn, or Beethoven. In fact, the modern piano is significantly different from even the pianos of the 19th century used by Liszt, Chopin, and Brahms.

Keyboard instruments were further developed in the 20th century. Early electromechanical instruments, such as the Ondes Martenot, appeared early in the century. A very important development in the 20th century was the electronic organ and the synthesizer, which allowed keyboard instruments to produce an enormous variety of sounds.

Modern keyboard instruments

  • Piano
  • Harpsichord
  • Clavichord
  • Pipe organ
  • Electronic organ, including the Hammond organ
  • Electric piano
  • Synthesizer
  • Celesta
  • Accordion (partially; some have keyboards)

Considerable effort has been put into creating an instrument that sounds like the piano but lacks its size and weight. The electric piano and electronic piano were early efforts that, while useful instruments in their own right, did not convincingly reproduce the timbre of the piano. Electric and electronic organs were developed during the same period. More recent electronic keyboard designs strive to emulate the sound of specific brand-name pianos using extensive digital sampling and computer models.
