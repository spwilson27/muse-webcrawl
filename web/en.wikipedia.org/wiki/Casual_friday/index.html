Casual Friday (also called Dress-down Friday) is a workplace dress-code convention under which employees may wear casual clothing on Fridays, traditionally the last working day of the week, in place of formal business attire such as suits and ties.

Origins

The custom derives from Hawaii's "Aloha Friday", promoted from the 1960s, when employers allowed staff to wear aloha (Hawaiian) shirts on Fridays. During the early 1990s, amid the rise of the casual technology industry of Silicon Valley, mainland US companies adopted casual Fridays to boost morale and compete for staff. The menswear industry — notably Dockers, which distributed a "Guide to Casual Business Wear" to thousands of human-resources departments in 1992 — actively promoted the trend, helping define the khakis-and-polo-shirt "business casual" uniform.

Development

Casual Friday contributed to the broader relaxation of office dress codes, and in many companies business casual became acceptable all week. Practice varies by industry: finance and law remained more formal, while creative and technology firms adopted fully casual dress. Variants include "casual Friday" fundraising, where employees pay a small donation to charity for the privilege of dressing down.
