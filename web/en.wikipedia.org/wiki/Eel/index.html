Eel

Eels

Order: Anguilliformes

Families: About 20

Species: More than 800

Habitat: Marine and fresh waters worldwide

Eels are ray-finned fish belonging to the order Anguilliformes, which contains eight suborders, 20 families, 164 genera and more than 800 species. Eels undergo considerable development from the early larval stage to the eventual adult stage and are usually predators. The term "eel" also refers to other eel-like fish, such as electric eels and spiny eels, which are not true eels.

Description

Eels are elongated fish, ranging in length from 5 cm (2 in) in the one-jawed eel to 4 m (13 ft) in the slender giant moray. Adults range in weight from 30 g (1 oz) to well over 25 kg (55 lb). They possess no pelvic fins, and many species lack pectoral fins as well. The dorsal and anal fins are fused with the caudal fin, forming a single ribbon running along much of the length of the animal. Eels swim by generating waves that travel the length of their bodies.

Life cycle

Most eels begin life in the ocean as flat, transparent larvae called leptocephali, which drift with currents before metamorphosing into glass eels and then elvers. Many species are catadromous, living in fresh water and returning to the ocean to spawn; the European and American eels famously migrate to the Sargasso Sea to breed.

Distribution and habitat

Most eels live in the shallow waters of the ocean and burrow into sand, mud, or amongst rocks. The majority of eel species are nocturnal. A very small number of species, in the family Anguillidae, live in fresh water but return to the sea to breed.

Use by humans

Freshwater eels (unagi) and marine eels (anago) are commonly used in Japanese cuisine, and eels are also eaten in European, American and other cuisines, including jellied eels in England and smoked eel in northern Europe. Several species, notably the European eel, are now critically endangered, and international trade is regulated under CITES.
