Moral development is the process through which children and adults acquire attitudes, reasoning, and behaviour concerning right and wrong, fairness, and the rights and welfare of others. It is a major topic in developmental psychology, studied through frameworks emphasising moral reasoning, moral emotions, social learning, and the distinction between moral rules and social conventions.

Piaget's theory

Jean Piaget proposed two broad phases. In heteronomous morality (roughly under age 9–10), children regard rules as fixed, sacred, and handed down by authority, judging wrongness mainly by consequences. In autonomous morality, emerging through peer cooperation, children see rules as agreements that can be changed and judge acts by intentions.

Kohlberg's stages

Lawrence Kohlberg extended Piaget's approach with a six-stage model across three levels, assessed with moral dilemmas such as the "Heinz dilemma":

  • Pre-conventional — obedience and punishment; self-interest and exchange.
  • Conventional — interpersonal approval and conformity; maintaining law and social order.
  • Post-conventional — social contract and individual rights; universal ethical principles.

Criticisms and alternatives

Carol Gilligan argued that Kohlberg's justice-centred model, derived from an all-male sample, undervalued an "ethics of care" more prominent in women's reasoning. Social-domain theory (Elliot Turiel) showed that even young children distinguish moral rules (about harm and fairness) from mere conventions. Other research stresses moral emotions such as empathy, guilt, and shame; socialisation by parents and peers; temperament; and cross-cultural variation in values such as autonomy and community.
