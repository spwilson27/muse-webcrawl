Atmospheric science is the study of the Earth's atmosphere and its various inner-working physical processes. It is an umbrella term encompassing meteorology (the study of weather), climatology (long-term patterns), atmospheric physics and atmospheric chemistry, and is sometimes extended to the atmospheres of other planets.

Scope

Atmospheric science examines the structure and composition of the atmosphere, the circulation of air, cloud and precipitation processes, the transfer of radiation, the interactions between the atmosphere and oceans, land and ice, and the chemical cycles of gases and aerosols. Phenomena studied range in scale from centimetre-scale turbulence to global climate change, and in time from seconds to millennia.

Methods

Observations come from surface weather stations, weather balloons (radiosondes), aircraft, Doppler radar and satellite remote sensing. Theory and simulation rely on numerical weather prediction and climate models run on supercomputers, solving the equations of fluid motion and thermodynamics on three-dimensional grids.

History

The field's roots lie in 17th-century instruments — the barometer and thermometer — and 19th-century weather maps and telegraph networks. The Bergen school's air-mass and frontal theory, the discovery of the jet stream, and Vilhelm Bjerknes's vision of physics-based forecasting laid the foundations of modern practice; the satellite era from 1960 transformed observation, and atmospheric chemistry gained prominence with the discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole in 1985.
