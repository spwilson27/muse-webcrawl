Reaction–diffusion system

A reaction–diffusion system is a mathematical model in which substances spread through space by diffusion while simultaneously undergoing chemical reactions that transform them into one another. Such systems occur naturally in chemistry and biology, and mathematically they are described by parabolic partial differential equations. They are famous for generating striking spatial patterns — spots, stripes, spirals, and travelling waves — from initially nearly uniform conditions.

A Turing pattern simulated from a two-component reaction–diffusion model.

Equations

The general two-component system for concentrations u(x, t) and v(x, t) takes the form

∂u/∂t = Du∇²u + f(u, v),   ∂v/∂t = Dv∇²v + g(u, v)

where Du and Dv are diffusion coefficients and f and g describe the local reaction kinetics. Well-known examples include the FitzHugh–Nagumo model of neuronal excitation, the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, and the Gray–Scott model.

Turing patterns

In his 1952 paper "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis," Alan Turing showed that a system of two reacting and diffusing chemicals ("morphogens") can spontaneously form stable spatial patterns when one substance (the activator) diffuses much more slowly than the other (the inhibitor). This diffusion-driven instability — now called a Turing instability — was proposed as a mechanism for biological pattern formation, and has since been implicated in animal coat markings, digit patterning, and feather and hair follicle spacing.

Applications

Reaction–diffusion models are used in developmental biology, epidemiology, ecology (for example vegetation patterns in arid landscapes), cardiology (spiral waves in cardiac tissue), computer graphics (procedural texture generation), and chemistry. Related formulations include systems with three or more components, systems on growing domains, and lattice-based cellular models.
