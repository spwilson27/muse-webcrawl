Color space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram with the triangular gamut of the sRGB color space.

A color space is a specific organization of colors. In combination with color profiling supported by various physical devices, it supports reproducible representations of color — whether such representation entails an analog or digital representation. A color space may be arbitrary, i.e. with physically realized colors assigned to a set of physical color swatches with assigned color names (as in the Pantone collection), or structured with mathematical precision (as with the NCS System, Adobe RGB, and sRGB).

Color models and spaces

A color model is an abstract mathematical framework describing the way colors can be represented as tuples of numbers — for example RGB (red, green, blue) for additive mixing on displays, CMYK (cyan, magenta, yellow, black) for subtractive printing, and HSV/HSL using hue, saturation, and value or lightness. A color space adds a precise mapping of those numbers to real, measurable colors, together with a defined white point and transfer function.

The CIE 1931 XYZ color space, derived from experiments on human color perception, was the first mathematically defined space and remains the reference against which others are specified. Device-independent spaces such as CIELAB and CIELUV are designed so that equal distances approximate equal perceived differences. Practical working spaces include sRGB (the default for the web and consumer devices), Adobe RGB, and DCI-P3, used in digital cinema and modern wide-gamut displays.

Conversion and gamut

Converting between color spaces requires color management, typically using ICC profiles that map colors through a profile connection space. The set of colors a space or device can represent is its gamut; colors outside a destination gamut must be clipped or compressed, which is why prints can look duller than the screen image for highly saturated colors.
