The choroid is the vascular layer of the eye lying between the retina and the sclera. Together with the ciliary body and the iris, it forms the uvea, the pigmented middle coat of the eyeball. The choroid is rich in blood vessels and dark melanin pigment, and it nourishes the outer layers of the retina while absorbing scattered light so that it does not degrade the visual image.

Structure

From outside to inside, the choroid is commonly described in layers: the suprachoroidal layer against the sclera; the vascular stroma (Haller's layer of large vessels and Sattler's layer of smaller vessels); the choriocapillaris, a dense sheet of capillaries that supplies the retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptors; and Bruch's membrane, a thin basement-membrane complex bordering the retina. Choroidal blood flow per unit weight is among the highest of any tissue in the body.

Function

The choroid provides oxygen and nutrients to the outer retina, which lacks its own blood supply in that region, and it helps regulate the temperature of the eye. Its heavy pigmentation darkens the interior of the eyeball, limiting internal reflections. Choroidal thickness changes measurably over the day and is implicated in the regulation of eye growth and refractive development.

Clinical significance

Disorders involving the choroid include choroiditis and the chorioretinopathies (often grouped under uveitis), choroidal melanoma — the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults — and degenerative changes in high myopia. Thinning of the choroid and breakdown of Bruch's membrane contribute to age-related macular degeneration. Imaging of the choroid is performed with optical coherence tomography (OCT).
