Gene flow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gene flow (also gene migration) is the transfer of genetic material from one population to another of the same species, through the movement of individuals or their gametes — for plants, often by wind- or animal-borne pollen and seeds. If migrants reproduce, their alleles enter the recipient gene pool, tending to make populations more similar; restricted gene flow lets populations diverge and can lead to speciation.

Effects

Gene flow counteracts genetic drift and local adaptation: even small amounts can prevent populations from differentiating, while strong flow can swamp locally favoured traits or, conversely, rescue inbred populations from harmful mutations (genetic rescue). In conservation it informs decisions about wildlife corridors and translocations. In agriculture, gene flow between crops and wild relatives, or from genetically modified varieties, is closely monitored.

Measurement

Population geneticists infer gene flow from allele-frequency differences (for example F-statistics) and, increasingly, directly from genomic data, which can also reveal historic hybridisation — such as gene flow between early modern humans and Neanderthals.
