Caroline minuscule or Carolingian minuscule is a script developed as a calligraphic standard in the monasteries of the Carolingian Empire so that the Latin alphabet could be easily and uniformly read across Europe. It was the product of the Carolingian Renaissance under Charlemagne and his scholarly court, particularly the circle of Alcuin of York, abbot of Saint Martin's at Tours.

Development

Before its introduction, writing in Latin Europe used a confusing variety of regional and often barely legible hands—Merovingian, Visigothic, Beneventan, Insular—derived from late Roman cursive. Around 780, scribes at the palace school and at monasteries such as Corbie, and then Tours, refined older half-uncial letterforms into a clear, rounded, disciplined script: letters stand upright with generous spacing, word separation is regular, ascenders and descenders are modest, and abbreviations are restrained. The reform was practical as well as aesthetic—it served Charlemagne's program of standardizing liturgy, law, and education throughout his empire.

Features and legacy

Caroline minuscule is the principal ancestor of the modern lowercase Latin alphabet. Its letterforms—a, d, g and others in essentially their modern shapes—were revived by Renaissance humanists such as Poggio Bracciolini and Niccolò Niccoli, who mistook Carolingian manuscripts for authentic Roman ones and adapted the script into the humanist minuscule from which the first roman typefaces of the printing era were cut. The script dominated Western European book production from the 9th to the 12th century before giving way to the compressed Gothic hands of the later Middle Ages.
