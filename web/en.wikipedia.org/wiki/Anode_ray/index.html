Anode ray

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An anode ray (also positive ray or canal ray) is a beam of positive ions that is created by certain types of gas-discharge tubes. They were first observed in Crookes tubes during experiments by the German scientist Eugen Goldstein in 1886. Later work by Wilhelm Wien and J. J. Thomson on anode rays led to the development of mass spectrometry.

History

Goldstein used a discharge tube with a perforated cathode; when a high electrical potential was applied, a faint flickering glow appeared behind the cathode, streaming through the holes ("canals") in the opposite direction to cathode rays. Because the rays passed through the canals, Goldstein called them Kanalstrahlen ("canal rays"). In 1907, deflection experiments showed the particles in the beam had masses far larger than electrons and depended on the residual gas in the tube — they were positive ions of the gas.

Applications

Wien measured the charge-to-mass ratio of canal ray particles, and Thomson's parabola method of 1913 separated ions by mass, revealing for the first time two isotopes of neon — the first evidence of stable isotopes in a non-radioactive element. Anode ray tubes were also adapted as ion sources and, in the form of canal ray spectroscopy, for studying atomic spectra.
