United States Merchant Marine

The United States Merchant Marine consists of US-flagged, privately and government-owned merchant vessels and the civilian mariners who crew them. In peacetime it carries American waterborne commerce, especially in the domestic ("Jones Act") trades between US ports; in war or national emergency it serves as a naval auxiliary, delivering supplies and equipment to US forces overseas. Its members are licensed and documented by the US Coast Guard, and officers are trained at the US Merchant Marine Academy at Kings Point and six state maritime academies.

History

American merchant shipping dates to the colonial era. It reached its greatest size during the Second World War, when the United States built more than 5,000 merchant ships — including 2,710 Liberty ships — and some 243,000 mariners served; about 8,300 merchant seamen died, a casualty rate among the highest of any US service branch, though veteran status was not formally recognised until 1988. Merchant mariners have since supported operations in Korea, Vietnam — where civilian-crewed ships carried the great majority of supplies — and the 1990–91 Gulf War and post-2001 campaigns.

The modern fleet

Since the war the US-flag oceangoing fleet has shrunk dramatically as owners "flagged out" to open registries; fewer than 200 large US-flag vessels trade internationally, supplemented by a Jones Act fleet of roughly a hundred oceangoing ships, plus thousands of tugs and barges. Federal programmes — the Maritime Security Program, cargo preference laws and the Ready Reserve Force — sustain a core fleet for defence sealift, and a Tanker Security Program was created in the 2020s to expand US-flag tanker capacity.

Merchant Marine service is commemorated by the Merchant Marine Act of 1936, the US Merchant Marine Academy (dedicated 1943), and National Maritime Day on 22 May.
