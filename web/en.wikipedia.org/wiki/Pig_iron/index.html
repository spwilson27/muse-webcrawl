Pig iron, also known as crude iron, is an intermediate industrial product obtained by smelting iron ore in a blast furnace. It has a very high carbon content, typically 3.8–4.7%, along with silica and other constituents of slag, which makes it very brittle and not directly useful as a structural material. Pig iron is the feedstock for steelmaking and for the production of cast iron.

Name and form

Traditionally, molten iron from the furnace was run into a branching mould of sand channels: a central runner (the "sow") with many smaller ingots at right angles (the "pigs"), a layout said to resemble piglets suckling a sow — hence the name. Modern pigs are cast by machine into small, uniform ingots, though most pig iron today is transferred molten directly to steelmaking vessels as "hot metal".

Production and use

In the blast furnace, iron ore, coke, and limestone flux are reacted at high temperature; the reduced iron absorbs carbon from the coke. The tapped metal is charged, usually with scrap, into basic oxygen or electric arc furnaces, where excess carbon and impurities are oxidised away to make steel, or remelted in cupolas and dosed with alloys to produce cast irons. Grades include basic pig iron for steelmaking, foundry (haematite) pig iron for castings, and nodular grades with low sulfur and phosphorus for ductile iron.

History

Cast pig iron was produced in China by the early centuries AD, and in Europe from the late Middle Ages as the blast furnace spread from the 14th–15th centuries; Abraham Darby's coke smelting at Coalbrookdale (1709) made pig iron cheap and plentiful, fuelling the Industrial Revolution.
