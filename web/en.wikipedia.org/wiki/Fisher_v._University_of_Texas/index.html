Fisher v. University of Texas

Court: Supreme Court of the United States
Cases: 570 U.S. 297 (2013) (Fisher I); 579 U.S. 365 (2016) (Fisher II)
Issue: Affirmative action in university admissions

Fisher v. University of Texas refers to two decisions of the Supreme Court of the United States concerning the constitutionality of affirmative action in undergraduate admissions at the University of Texas at Austin (UT Austin). Abigail Fisher, a white applicant denied admission in 2008, challenged the university's race-conscious admissions programme under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Background

After the Fifth Circuit's Hopwood decision barred affirmative action in Texas, the state legislature enacted the Top Ten Percent Plan, guaranteeing admission to Texas public universities for students graduating in the top ten percent of their high-school class. Following the Supreme Court's decision in Grutter v. Bollinger (2003) upholding race-conscious admissions in the interest of diversity, UT Austin added race as one factor in a holistic review for applicants not admitted under the percentage plan. Fisher, who was not in the top ten percent of her class, was denied admission and sued.

Fisher I (2013)

In Fisher v. University of Texas, 570 U.S. 297 (2013), the Court, in an opinion by Justice Kennedy, vacated the Fifth Circuit's judgment upholding the programme and remanded, holding that the lower court had been too deferential: a university receives some deference on the educational judgement that diversity is essential, but courts must apply strict scrutiny independently to whether the admissions process is narrowly tailored, and the university bears the burden of proving that race-neutral alternatives would not achieve the same benefits.

Fisher II (2016)

On remand the Fifth Circuit again upheld the programme, and in Fisher v. University of Texas, 579 U.S. 365 (2016), the Supreme Court affirmed, 4–3, in another opinion by Justice Kennedy. The Court held that UT Austin's limited consideration of race, supplementing the Top Ten Percent Plan, satisfied strict scrutiny, given the university's studied evidence that the percentage plan alone did not produce the educational benefits of diversity. Justice Alito's dissent argued the programme was unconstitutional. The Fisher decisions were later effectively superseded by Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023), which held race-conscious admissions programmes at Harvard and UNC unconstitutional.
