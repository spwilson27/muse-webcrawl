Torpedo

A torpedo is a ranged underwater weapon launched above or below the water surface, self-propelled towards a target, carrying an explosive warhead designed to detonate either on contact with the target or in proximity to it. Modern torpedoes are among the most complex and effective naval weapons, used by submarines, surface ships, and aircraft against ships and other submarines.

History

The word "torpedo" originally referred to any explosive naval device, including what are now called naval mines. The first self-propelled torpedo was developed in the 1860s by Giovanni Luppis and manufactured by Robert Whitehead in Fiume; Whitehead's design, driven by a compressed-air engine and able to maintain a set depth, was adopted by navies worldwide. Torpedoes proved decisive in the Russo-Japanese War and both World Wars, when German U-boats and American submarines used them to sink millions of tons of shipping.

Design

A torpedo consists of a warhead, an energy source and propulsion system (electric batteries, monopropellants, or stored chemical energy), guidance electronics, and control surfaces. Guidance methods include straight-running, pattern-running, wire guidance, and active or passive acoustic homing; some wake-homing torpedoes track the turbulence behind a ship. Heavyweight torpedoes such as the American Mark 48 and British Spearfish are launched from submarine tubes, while lightweight torpedoes are dropped by aircraft and helicopters against submarines.

Countermeasures

Ships defend against torpedoes with towed decoys, acoustic jammers, manoeuvre, and hard-kill anti-torpedo torpedoes.
