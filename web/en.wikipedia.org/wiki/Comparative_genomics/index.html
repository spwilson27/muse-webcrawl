Comparative genomics

[Image: Genome alignment and synteny]

Comparative genomics is a branch of biological research in which the genome sequences of different species are compared. By comparing the finished genomic sequences of different organisms, researchers are able to identify regions of similarity and difference, pinpoint genes and other functional elements, and infer the evolutionary relationships and history of the organisms.

Principles

The field rests on the observation that features common to two organisms will often be encoded within DNA that is conserved between them, whereas differences reflect their distinct adaptations and phylogeny. Comparing genomes reveals protein-coding genes (through open reading frames and conserved coding sequences), non-coding regulatory elements (which are often more conserved than neutral DNA), synteny — the conserved ordering of genes along chromosomes — and events such as gene duplication, horizontal gene transfer, whole-genome duplication and genome reduction.

History

Comparative genomics began with the first complete viral and bacterial genome sequences in the late 1970s and 1990s, including Haemophilus influenzae in 1995 and yeast in 1996. The publication of the human genome (2001) and the mouse genome (2002) made large-scale vertebrate comparison practical: the finding that about 5% of the human genome is under purifying selection revealed far more functional sequence than the protein-coding fraction alone. Landmark projects include the sequencing of multiple Drosophila species, the ENCODE project, and the Zoonomia consortium's alignment of hundreds of mammalian genomes.

Applications

Comparative genomics is used to annotate new genomes, identify disease genes by cross-species conservation, trace pathogen outbreaks and virulence factors, improve crops and livestock, and reconstruct the tree of life. In medicine, comparing tumour genomes with matched normal genomes underlies cancer genomics.
