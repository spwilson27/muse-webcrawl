Grimm's law

Grimm's law, also known as the First Germanic Sound Shift, is a set of systematic sound changes that affected the consonants of Proto-Indo-European as it developed into Proto-Germanic, in roughly the first millennium BC. It was formulated by Jacob Grimm in the 1822 edition of his Deutsche Grammatik, building on observations by Rasmus Rask.

The changes

The shift operated in a chain: voiceless stops (p, t, k, kʷ) became fricatives (f, þ, x/h, xʷ/hw); voiced stops (b, d, g, gʷ) became voiceless stops (p, t, k, kʷ); and voiced fricatives (β, ð, ɣ, ɣʷ) became voiced stops. Examples include Latin piscis versus English fish, and Latin tres versus English three.

Exceptions and Verner's law

Apparent exceptions in which voiceless fricatives appear voiced were explained by Karl Verner in 1875: the fricatives were voiced when the Indo-European accent did not fall on the immediately preceding syllable. Verner's law was a triumph of the Neogrammarian doctrine of exceptionless sound laws.
