Serendipity

The Three Princes of Serendip, source of the word.

Serendipity is the occurrence and development of events by chance in a happy or beneficial way — the finding of valuable or agreeable things not originally sought. The word was coined by the English writer and politician Horace Walpole in a letter of 28 January 1754 to Horace Mann, inspired by the Persian fairy tale The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes "were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things they were not in quest of." Serendip is an old name for Sri Lanka (Ceylon), from the Arabic Sarandib.

Meaning and nuance

Walpole's original sense emphasized not mere luck but the combination of accident with sagacity — the prepared, observant mind recognizing the significance of an unexpected observation. Sociologist Robert K. Merton, who with Elinor Barber studied the history of the word (published posthumously as The Travels and Adventures of Serendipity), distinguished serendipity in science as the experience of observing an unanticipated, anomalous, and strategic datum that becomes the occasion for developing a new theory or extending an existing one. In common usage the term has broadened to cover any fortunate chance happening, from meeting a friend in a foreign city to stumbling upon a useful website.

In science and invention

Many famous discoveries are described as serendipitous: Alexander Fleming's notice of penicillin's antibacterial effect in 1928; Wilhelm Röntgen's discovery of X-rays; the identification of the cosmic microwave background radiation by Penzias and Wilson, who initially treated the signal as noise; Percy Spencer's observation that a chocolate bar melted near radar equipment, leading to the microwave oven; and the discovery of vulcanized rubber, Teflon, Post-it notes, and Viagra's repurposing from a failed angina drug. Historians caution that such stories often simplify long processes of work, but the pattern — anomaly noticed by someone prepared to value it — recurs. Louis Pasteur's dictum that "chance favors the prepared mind" is frequently quoted in this context.

In information science and the arts

In library and information science, serendipity describes the incidental discovery of information while browsing — a rationale for open stacks, and a quality that digital environments both threaten (through algorithmic filtering) and enhance (through hyperlinks and recommendation systems). The concept also names a romantic comedy film (2001) and appears widely in popular culture as a term for happy accidents in love and life.

Serendipity and related terms

The British chemist and science writer Royston Roberts distinguished true serendipity from "pseudoserendipity," in which a discovery is made by accident while seeking something else by a different route. The negative counterpart — finding something by accident that one did not want — is sometimes called zemblanity, a term coined by William Boyd after the frigid island of Novaya Zemlya, in pointed contrast to warm Serendip.
