Psychodrama

Psychodrama is a method of psychotherapy in which clients use guided dramatic action to examine problems in their lives. Developed by Jacob L. Moreno in the early 20th century, it combines elements of theatre, group therapy and role theory: instead of merely talking about difficulties, participants enact scenes — past, present or anticipated — to gain insight, rehearse new behaviour and achieve emotional release (catharsis).

Structure

A classical session has three phases: warm-up, action and sharing. In the action phase the director (therapist) helps the protagonist stage a scene. Other group members act as "auxiliary egos" playing significant people in the protagonist's life; a member may also serve as a "double", voicing the protagonist's unspoken feelings. The audience witnesses and later shares resonances from their own lives without analysing the protagonist.

Techniques

Signature techniques include role reversal (the protagonist swaps places with another person in the scene), doubling, mirroring (watching someone else replay one's own behaviour), the empty chair, and soliloquy. Moreno also coined the terms "group therapy" and developed sociometry, the measurement of interpersonal relations, alongside psychodrama.

Applications

Psychodrama is used in mental-health treatment, addiction recovery, trauma work, education, organisational training and conflict resolution. While evidence is less extensive than for cognitive-behavioural therapies, studies suggest benefits for self-esteem, interpersonal functioning and symptom reduction.
