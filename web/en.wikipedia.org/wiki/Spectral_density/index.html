Spectral density

The power spectral density (PSD) of a signal describes the distribution of the signal's power over frequency. Formally, the PSD is the Fourier transform of the signal's autocorrelation function — a result known as the Wiener–Khinchin theorem. Integrating the PSD over a frequency band gives the average power in that band. The related energy spectral density applies to finite-energy transient signals.

An estimated power spectral density, showing signal peaks above a coloured-noise floor.

Definition

For a wide-sense stationary random process x(t) with autocorrelation R(τ), the two-sided PSD is S(f) = ∫ R(τ) e−i2πfτ dτ. For a deterministic finite-energy signal, the energy spectral density is |X(f)|², the squared magnitude of its Fourier transform. One-sided spectra, defined for f ≥ 0, double the two-sided values except at DC.

Estimation

Estimating the PSD from finite records is a central task in signal processing. The basic periodogram is noisy (inconsistent), so methods reduce variance at the price of frequency resolution: Bartlett's and Welch's methods average periodograms of windowed segments; multitaper methods use orthogonal tapers; parametric methods (autoregressive models, Yule–Walker, MUSIC) fit a model and can resolve closely spaced lines. PSD estimation underlies spectrum monitoring, vibration analysis, seismology, EEG analysis, and radio astronomy.

Units and interpretation

Units are power per unit frequency, e.g. V²/Hz for a voltage signal, often expressed in dB relative units such as dBm/Hz. Noise floors are quoted as spectral densities — for instance, thermal noise at room temperature is −174 dBm/Hz. Colours of noise (white, pink 1/f, brown) are defined by their spectral slopes.
