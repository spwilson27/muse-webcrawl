Warburg effect

In oncology, the Warburg effect is the observation that most cancer cells preferentially produce energy by a high rate of glycolysis followed by lactic acid fermentation in the cytosol, rather than by a comparatively low rate of glycolysis followed by oxidation of pyruvate in mitochondria, as in most normal cells. The effect was first described by the German physiologist Otto Warburg in the 1920s, and is also known as aerobic glycolysis.

Warburg effect
Named after: Otto Warburg
Field: Oncology, metabolism
Key feature: Aerobic glycolysis

Mechanism

Normal differentiated cells primarily metabolise glucose to carbon dioxide by oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria. Cancer cells, by contrast, convert much of their glucose to lactate even when oxygen is abundant. This is far less efficient per molecule of glucose — roughly 2 ATP versus about 36 ATP — but glycolysis can run much faster, and its intermediates feed biosynthetic pathways that a dividing cell needs: nucleotides, amino acids and lipids.

Possible explanations

Several explanations have been proposed: mitochondrial damage (Warburg's original hypothesis), adaptation to hypoxic tumour microenvironments, oncogenic signalling through pathways such as PI3K/Akt and Myc that upregulate glucose transporters and glycolytic enzymes, and the need to maintain redox balance. Modern research treats the effect as one facet of the metabolic reprogramming that is now considered a hallmark of cancer.

Clinical significance

The Warburg effect underlies positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG): tumours take up the glucose analogue avidly, allowing them to be visualised. It is also a target of experimental therapies that seek to inhibit glycolysis or to exploit the metabolic dependencies of cancer cells.
