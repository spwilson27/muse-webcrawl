Slovak national movement

The Slovak national movement (Slovak: Slovenské národné hnutie) was the process of national awakening and political mobilisation of Slovaks within the Kingdom of Hungary from the late 18th century through the creation of Czechoslovakia in 1918. Like other Central European national revivals, it proceeded through language codification, cultural institution-building, and eventually mass politics.

Language codification

In 1787 the Catholic priest Anton Bernolák codified the first literary Slovak, based on western dialects. The decisive codification came from the Lutheran Ľudovít Štúr and his circle, who in 1843 adopted the central Slovak dialect as the basis of the standard language, publishing its grammar in 1846. Štúr's newspaper Slovenské národné noviny gave the movement a public voice.

The revolutionary years and the Memorandum

In 1848–49, during the Hungarian Revolution, Štúr, Jozef Miloslav Hurban and Michal Miloslav Hodža raised Slovak volunteer corps fighting alongside Vienna against the Hungarian government, and presented Slovak demands at the Slavic Congress in Prague. After the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867, Magyarisation intensified. The 1861 Memorandum of the Slovak Nation demanded a Slovak autonomous district and language rights; the cultural foundation Matica slovenská (founded 1863) was suppressed by the Hungarian authorities in 1875, as were the Slovak gymnasia.

Toward 1918

Mass parties emerged around 1900 — the Slovak National Party and the Slovak People's Party of Andrej Hlinka. The 1907 Černová tragedy, in which gendarmes shot 15 Slovak villagers at a church consecration, drew international attention. In 1918 Slovak leaders issued the Martin Declaration (30 October 1918) joining the new Czechoslovak state.
