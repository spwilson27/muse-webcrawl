Cranial capacity

Cranial capacity is a measure of the volume of the interior of the cranium (skull), used as a rough indicator of the size of the brain. It is measured in cubic centimetres, historically by filling the skull with seed, shot or water, and today more accurately from imaging and endocasts.

Cranial capacity (typical)
Modern humans~1,200–1,500 cm³
Chimpanzees~350–400 cm³
Australopithecus~400–550 cm³

In human evolution

The fossil record shows a broad increase in cranial capacity over the course of hominin evolution: Homo habilis averaged roughly 600 cm³, Homo erectus about 900–1,100 cm³, and Neanderthals equalled or slightly exceeded modern human averages. The trend is not linear and varies among populations, and brain organisation matters as well as size.

History and criticism

Nineteenth-century craniometry, practised by figures such as Paul Broca and Samuel George Morton, linked cranial capacity to race and intelligence in ways now discredited as scientific racism; Stephen Jay Gould's The Mismeasure of Man (1981) criticised Morton's measurements, though later re-analyses disputed some of Gould's claims. Within species, correlations between cranial capacity and measured intelligence are weak to modest, and the measure is used mainly in palaeoanthropology.
