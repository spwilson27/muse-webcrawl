Corm

A corm, bulbo-tuber, or bulbotuber is a short, vertical, swollen underground plant stem that serves as a storage organ for some plants to survive winter or other adverse conditions such as drought and heat (perennation). The word comes from the Greek kormos, meaning a trunk or stump. Plants producing corms include crocus, gladiolus, freesia, montbretia (Crocosmia), taro, and some irises and orchids.

Structure

A corm consists of one or more internodes of solid stem tissue with at least one growing point, protected by modified scale leaves called the tunic. This distinguishes it from a true bulb, which is made mainly of fleshy scale leaves surrounding a much smaller stem plate: a corm is mostly stem, a bulb is mostly leaf. Nodal rings around the corm mark the bases of the dry scale leaves, and roots emerge from a basal plate at the bottom. Inside, the stored reserves are primarily starch.

Each season the corm produces a new corm on top of the old one, while the old corm withers; in plants such as gladiolus a large number of small offsets called cormels (cormlets) form around the base and can be grown on to flowering size over one or more seasons. Some species, such as Colocasia (taro), produce long corms that are major staple foods in the Pacific and Asia; others, like the autumn crocus, are toxic.

Cormels

Cormels are miniature corms produced vegetatively around the mother corm. Gardeners separate and store them over winter, planting them out to bulk up; for gladiolus a cormel typically needs two seasons to reach flowering size. Vegetative propagation by corms and cormels preserves cultivar characteristics exactly, which is how ornamental varieties are maintained.
