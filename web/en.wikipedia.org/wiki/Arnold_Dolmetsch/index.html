Eugène Arnold Dolmetsch (24 February 1858 – 28 February 1940) was a French-born musician and instrument maker who spent most of his working life in England and founded the modern revival of interest in early music and historical instruments. He built and restored harpsichords, clavichords, viols, lutes and recorders, performed the music of the 16th–18th centuries on them, and trained a generation of makers and performers.

Life and work

Dolmetsch was born in Le Mans into a family of Swiss-French organ and piano makers, and trained as a violinist at the Brussels Conservatoire and at the Royal College of Music in London. Fascinated by old instruments, he began restoring viols and keyboard instruments and building copies. In the 1890s he worked for the Boston piano firm Chickering, building harpsichords and clavichords in the United States, before returning to Europe and working for Gaveau in Paris.

In 1914 he settled at Haslemere in Surrey, where his workshop became the centre of the Dolmetsch enterprise. There he famously re-developed the modern recorder, reconstructing a lost consort instrument and establishing the recorder designs later mass-produced for schools. His 1915 book The Interpretation of the Music of the XVIIth and XVIIIth Centuries was a landmark of historically informed performance scholarship, though some of its conclusions were later challenged.

Legacy

Dolmetsch founded the Haslemere Festival of early music in 1925, which continues today. His children — including Carl Dolmetsch, a noted recorder virtuoso — and later descendants continued the workshop, and the Dolmetsch name remains associated with historically inspired instrument making. He was appointed CBE in 1938 (though he could not accept as a French citizen at first) and awarded the Légion d'honneur.
