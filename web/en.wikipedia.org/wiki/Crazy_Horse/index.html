Crazy Horse (Lakota: Tȟašúŋke Witkó, literally "His-Horse-Is-Crazy"; c. 1840 – September 5, 1877) was a Lakota war leader of the Oglala band in the 19th century. He took up arms against the United States federal government to fight against encroachment by white American settlers on Native American territory and to preserve the traditional way of life of the Lakota people. His participation in several famous battles of the Great Sioux War of 1876 on the northern Great Plains — above all the Battle of the Little Bighorn, where the Lakota and Cheyenne destroyed five companies of the U.S. 7th Cavalry — earned him great respect from both his enemies and his own people.

A famously reticent man, Crazy Horse never allowed his photograph to be taken and his burial place remains unknown. He surrendered to U.S. troops in May 1877 and was mortally wounded a few months later, bayoneted while resisting imprisonment at Fort Robinson.

Early life

Crazy Horse was born near Rapid Creek in the Black Hills country, the son of a holy man, also named Crazy Horse (later called Worm), and Rattling Blanket Woman. As a boy he was called Light Hair for his light, curly hair. In his youth he had a vision — after fasting in the manner of the hanbleceya (vision quest) — of a horseman riding through a storm, which his father interpreted as foretelling that he would be a great warrior who could not be killed by bullets. He took his father's name after proving himself in battle, and his exploits as a decoy in ambushes and raids against enemy tribes and, later, U.S. soldiers, made him a shirt wearer (a recognised war leader) by his mid-twenties.

War leader

Crazy Horse fought in Red Cloud's War (1866–1868), serving as a decoy leader in the Fetterman Fight of December 1866, in which Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors annihilated a detachment of 81 U.S. soldiers near Fort Phil Kearny — the army's worst defeat on the northern plains until Little Bighorn. He opposed the sale of the Black Hills, guaranteed to the Lakota by the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868), after gold was discovered there in 1874, and refused to move his band to an agency.

The Great Sioux War

In 1876 the government ordered all Lakota bands onto reservations and launched converging columns against those who refused. On 17 June 1876, Crazy Horse checked General George Crook's advance at the Battle of the Rosebud. Eight days later, joining the great encampment on the Little Bighorn River, he led the counterattack that overwhelmed and destroyed Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer's battalion at the Battle of the Little Bighorn (25–26 June 1876), the most famous Native American victory of the Plains wars.

Surrender and death

Relentless winter campaigning, the destruction of villages and the collapse of the buffalo herds broke Lakota resistance. On 6 May 1877 Crazy Horse led nearly 900 followers into Camp Robinson, Nebraska, and surrendered. Tensions followed — over a promised buffalo hunt, a promised agency in the Powder River country, and garbled translations suggesting he intended to fight again. On 5 September 1877, soldiers attempted to confine him in the guardhouse; in the struggle that followed he was bayoneted, reportedly by Private William Gentles, and died that night, attended by his father and the post surgeon, Dr. Valentine McGillycuddy. His parents buried him at a secret location, traditionally along Wounded Knee Creek.

Legacy

Crazy Horse is remembered as an emblem of Lakota resistance and humility; he is said to have fought with a single hawk feather, a sacred stone behind his ear and a handful of dust, according to his vision. Near Mount Rushmore, the Crazy Horse Memorial — a mountain carving begun in 1948 by Korczak Ziolkowski at the invitation of Lakota elders — seeks to depict him at a scale dwarfing the presidential faces; it remains unfinished and, among some Lakota, controversial.
