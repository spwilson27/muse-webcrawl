Holyrood Abbey is a ruined abbey of the Canons Regular in Edinburgh, Scotland. Founded in 1128 by King David I, it stands at the eastern end of the Royal Mile, adjacent to the Palace of Holyroodhouse. During the later Middle Ages the abbey guest house developed into a royal residence, and after the Scottish Reformation the abbey church was used as a parish church until the 17th century; it has been a ruin since the 18th century.

Foundation legend

According to legend, David I was hunting in the forest east of Edinburgh Castle on the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross (Rood Day) in 1127 when he was thrown from his horse and attacked by a stag. In the king's vision, a cross appeared between the stag's antlers, and grasping it he was saved; in gratitude he founded the abbey of the Holy Rood (rood meaning cross) on the spot. A stag's head with a cross between its antlers remains the abbey's emblem.

History

The abbey was colonised by Augustinian canons from Merton Priory. Its church, largely built in the 13th century, was one of the grandest in Scotland, and the abbey hosted meetings of the Parliament of Scotland and royal ceremonies. Several kings were crowned or married there, and James II, James III and James IV were among the monarchs buried in the royal vault. Mary, Queen of Scots married both Henry Stuart, Lord Darnley (1565) and James Hepburn, Earl of Bothwell (1567) at Holyrood. The church was damaged during the Rough Wooing in the 1540s and despoiled at the Reformation. Charles I had it remodelled for his Scottish coronation in 1633. In 1688 a mob wrecked the interior and royal tombs; the roof collapsed in 1768 after ill-judged repairs, leaving the nave roofless.

The abbey today

The surviving ruin comprises the roofless nave, the twin western towers and the royal vault. It forms part of the Holyrood Palace complex and is cared for by Historic Environment Scotland. The name Holyrood is also applied to the surrounding district, including the Scottish Parliament Building, opened in 2004.
