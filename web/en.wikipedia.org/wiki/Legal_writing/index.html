Legal writing

Legal writing involves the analysis of fact patterns and presentation of arguments in documents such as legal memoranda, briefs, contracts, and judicial opinions. It is a type of technical writing used by lawyers, judges, legislators, and others in law to express legal analysis, rights, and duties. Legal writing is typically divided into two broad categories: legal analysis writing, which is predictive or persuasive, and legal drafting, which creates binding legal texts such as statutes, contracts, and wills.

Legal writing covers everything from office memoranda to appellate briefs and legislation.

Categories

Predictive analysis

Predictive writing — most commonly the office memorandum — analyzes the law objectively to forecast how a court is likely to rule on a client's facts. The writer must present both favorable and unfavorable authority candidly, because the purpose is advice rather than advocacy.

Persuasive analysis

Persuasive writing, such as a trial or appellate brief or a motion, argues for a particular outcome. While it must remain accurate and within rules of professional conduct, it selects, orders, and frames authority to convince the reader.

Legal drafting

Drafting produces the legal instruments themselves: contracts, deeds, wills, regulations, and statutes. It demands extreme precision, because the words chosen will be construed as binding, often years later and in disputes the drafter never anticipated.

Structure and method

Legal analysis in the United States is commonly taught through frameworks such as IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) and its variants (CRAC, CREAC), which organize analysis of a legal question. Writers support argument with citations to primary authority — constitutions, statutes, regulations, and case law — in standardized citation formats, notably The Bluebook in the United States.

Style and the plain language movement

Traditional legal writing is notorious for "legalese": archaic terms (hereinafter, witnesseth), Latinisms, redundant doublets ("null and void", "cease and desist"), and long sentences. Since the late twentieth century, the plain language movement — championed by writers such as Bryan A. Garner and embodied in measures like the U.S. Plain Writing Act of 2010 — has pushed for clarity, shorter sentences, and ordinary words in legal documents, arguing that precision and readability are compatible.

Education

Legal writing is a required, graded component of the first year of law school in the United States, and many schools require advanced courses in brief writing, drafting, or scholarship. Student-edited law reviews publish doctrinal legal writing, while practitioners develop skills through continuing practice.
