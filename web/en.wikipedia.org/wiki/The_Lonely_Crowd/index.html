The Lonely Crowd: A Study of the Changing American Character is a 1950 sociological study by David Riesman, Nathan Glazer, and Reuel Denney. It is considered, along with works such as White Collar by C. Wright Mills, a landmark study of American character and one of the best-selling works of sociology ever published in the United States.

Argument

The book proposes a typology of social character correlated with stages of population growth and social organisation:

  • Tradition-directed people, characteristic of pre-modern societies, conform to long-established customs and draw their direction from inherited norms.
  • Inner-directed people, characteristic of emerging industrial societies, internalise goals early in life—like a gyroscope—and act on them regardless of others' approval.
  • Other-directed people, characteristic of modern, affluent, bureaucratic societies, take their direction from contemporaries and the mass media—like radar—constantly adjusting to the expectations and signals of peers.

Riesman argued that mid-twentieth-century America was shifting from inner-direction to other-direction, producing a character type that is cosmopolitan, approval-seeking, and anxious—even in the midst of crowds, "lonely". The book explored consequences for work, leisure, consumption, politics, and child-rearing.

Reception

The Lonely Crowd became a bestseller, unusual for an academic monograph, and its terms—especially "inner-directed" and "other-directed"—entered general usage. It was debated by sociologists, some of whom criticised its broad characterisations and nostalgic undertones, but it remains a canonical work in American sociology and cultural criticism. An abridged edition appeared in 1953, and Riesman published a retrospective preface in later editions reflecting on its reception.
