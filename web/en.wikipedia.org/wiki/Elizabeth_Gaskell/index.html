Elizabeth Cleghorn Gaskell (née Stevenson; 29 September 1810 – 12 November 1865), often referred to as Mrs Gaskell, was an English novelist, biographer, and short story writer. Her novels offer a detailed portrait of the lives of many strata of Victorian society, including the very poor. Her first novel, Mary Barton (1848), drew on her experience of industrial Manchester; she is also known for Cranford (1853), North and South (1855), and the unfinished Wives and Daughters (1866), and for her controversial biography of her friend Charlotte Brontë.

Biography

Elizabeth Stevenson was born in Chelsea, London. Her mother died when she was an infant, and she was raised largely by an aunt in Knutsford, Cheshire — the town later fictionalised as Cranford and Hollingford. In 1832 she married William Gaskell, a Unitarian minister at Cross Street Chapel in Manchester, and settled in the rapidly industrialising city. The death of her infant son in 1845 spurred her to write Mary Barton as a distraction from grief.

Works

Mary Barton: A Tale of Manchester Life (1848), published anonymously, depicted the hardships of the working class so vividly that some mill owners accused her of unfairness. Charles Dickens admired the book and invited her to contribute to his magazine Household Words, in which Cranford and North and South were serialised. North and South contrasts the industrial North of England with the rural South through the eyes of Margaret Hale. Her Life of Charlotte Brontë (1857), the first biography of Brontë, was frank about its subject's hardships and attracted threats of legal action that forced revisions. Her later novels include Sylvia's Lovers (1863) and Wives and Daughters (1864–66), widely considered her masterpiece, left unfinished at her sudden death from a heart attack in 1865.

Style and reputation

Gaskell's style is marked by close observation of dialect and domestic detail, sympathy for the marginalised, and a Unitarian moral seriousness. Although long overshadowed by her male contemporaries, her reputation rose steadily through the twentieth century, and her novels have been widely adapted for television.
