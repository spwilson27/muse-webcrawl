Eye chart

An eye chart is a chart used to measure visual acuity. Eye charts are often used by health care professionals, such as optometrists, physicians, or nurses, to screen persons for vision impairment. Ophthalmologists, physicians who specialize in the eye, also use eye charts to monitor the visual acuity of their patients in response to various therapies such as medications or surgery.

Types

The best-known chart is the Snellen chart, developed by Dutch ophthalmologist Herman Snellen in 1862, with rows of capital letters ("optotypes") decreasing in size; acuity is recorded as a fraction such as 20/20 (6/6 in metres). Alternatives include the logMAR chart, designed for greater precision and used in research; the Landolt C chart, using a ring with a gap; and the tumbling E chart, useful for children and people unfamiliar with the Latin alphabet. Charts for young children use pictures, such as the Lea test symbols.

Procedure

The chart is placed at a standardized distance — 20 feet (6 metres) for a full-size Snellen chart — and the patient reads the smallest line of letters they can identify, one eye at a time, with and without corrective lenses. Results guide refraction and prescription of glasses or contact lenses.
