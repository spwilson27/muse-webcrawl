Bauhaus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Bauhaus building at Dessau (1925–26) by Walter Gropius: glass curtain-wall workshop wing, balconies and the vertical Bauhaus sign]

The Staatliches Bauhaus, commonly known as the Bauhaus, was a German art school operational from 1919 to 1933 that combined crafts and fine arts, and became the most influential school of modernist design. Founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar, it moved to Dessau in 1925 and to Berlin in 1932, before its masters, under Nazi pressure, dissolved it in 1933.

The school's preliminary course — taught first by Johannes Itten, then Josef Albers and László Moholy-Nagy — broke students of academic habit through exercises in materials, form and colour. Workshops in metal, weaving, pottery, typography and stage produced prototypes for industry: Marcel Breuer's tubular-steel chairs, Marianne Brandt's lamps, and the Dessau building itself, an icon of the International Style.

Directors and development

Gropius directed until 1928, preaching first Expressionist craft unity ('a new guild of craftsmen') and from 1923 'art and technology — a new unity'. Hannes Meyer (1928–30) pushed social, functional design; Ludwig Mies van der Rohe (1930–33) steered the school toward architecture before closing it in Berlin. Teachers included Paul Klee, Wassily Kandinsky, Lyonel Feininger and Oskar Schlemmer, whose Triadic Ballet embodied Bauhaus stage experiment.

Legacy

Driven abroad, Bauhaus masters remade design education worldwide — Albers at Black Mountain College and Yale, Gropius and Breuer at Harvard, Moholy-Nagy in Chicago, Mies at IIT. 'Bauhaus' remains shorthand for functional modernism in architecture, typography and product design, and Dessau and Weimar's Bauhaus sites are UNESCO World Heritage.
