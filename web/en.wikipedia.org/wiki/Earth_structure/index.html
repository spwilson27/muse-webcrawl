Structure of Earth

Earth's layers by depth
Crust0–35 km (oceanic 5–10 km, continental 30–70 km)
Mantle35–2,890 km

Inner core5,150–6,371 km (solid)

The structure of Earth is divided into layers defined by chemical composition and by mechanical properties. Chemically, Earth consists of the crust, the mantle, and the iron-rich core. Mechanically, it is divided into the rigid lithosphere, the ductile asthenosphere, the mesospheric mantle, the liquid outer core, and the solid inner core. Knowledge of the interior comes almost entirely from seismology — the study of how earthquake waves refract and reflect at layer boundaries.

Crust and mantle

The crust is Earth's thin outer shell: oceanic crust is basaltic and young, while continental crust is granitic, thicker, and much older. Below the crust lies the mantle, composed mainly of silicate rocks rich in magnesium and iron (peridotite). The Mohorovičić discontinuity (the Moho) marks the crust–mantle boundary. The upper mantle includes the lithosphere — crust plus rigid uppermost mantle — which is broken into tectonic plates, and the weaker asthenosphere over which plates move.

Core

At about 2,890 km depth the Gutenberg discontinuity separates the mantle from the core. The outer core is a liquid layer of iron and nickel whose convection generates Earth's magnetic field via the geodynamo. Inge Lehmann showed in 1936 that a solid inner core exists within the liquid; it is an iron–nickel alloy at temperatures comparable to the Sun's surface, kept solid by immense pressure.
