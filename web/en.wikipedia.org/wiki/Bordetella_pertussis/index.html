Bordetella pertussis is a Gram-negative, aerobic, pathogenic, encapsulated coccobacillus of the genus Bordetella. It is the causative agent of pertussis, commonly known as whooping cough. The bacterium is spread through airborne droplets from the respiratory tract of infected individuals and infects the ciliated epithelial cells of the human respiratory system.

History

The bacterium was first isolated in 1906 by Jules Bordet and Octave Gengou, who developed a culture medium — Bordet–Gengou agar — to grow the fastidious organism. The disease itself had been described centuries earlier, but identifying the causative agent paved the way for the development of vaccines in the 1940s.

Virulence factors

B. pertussis produces several virulence factors, including pertussis toxin, an AB5-type exotoxin that modifies host G proteins and interferes with immune signaling. Other factors include filamentous hemagglutinin, which mediates attachment to respiratory cells, tracheal cytotoxin, which damages ciliated cells, and adenylate cyclase toxin.

Disease

Pertussis typically begins with mild cold-like symptoms (the catarrhal stage), followed by severe paroxysmal coughing fits that may end in a characteristic high-pitched "whoop" as the patient gasps for air. The disease is most dangerous in infants, in whom it can cause pneumonia, seizures, and death.

Prevention and treatment

Vaccination is the primary means of prevention. Whole-cell pertussis vaccines were widely used from the 1940s; acellular vaccines, containing purified antigens, are now standard in most developed countries as components of DTaP and Tdap combination vaccines. Macrolide antibiotics such as azithromycin are used for treatment and post-exposure prophylaxis.
