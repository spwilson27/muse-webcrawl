The River Dane is a river in North West England, a tributary of the River Weaver. It rises on Axe Edge Moor near Buxton in Derbyshire/Staffordshire, close to the source of the River Goyt, and flows roughly westwards for about 47 kilometres (29 mi) through Congleton, Holmes Chapel and Middlewich, joining the River Weaver at Northwich in Cheshire.

Course

Near its source the Dane forms the Three Shire Heads, the meeting point of Derbyshire, Staffordshire and Cheshire, where it is crossed by an old packhorse bridge below a series of small waterfalls and pools. The river then descends through the Dane Valley, passing Danebridge and the Staffordshire village of Gradbach, before entering Cheshire near Congleton. Its main tributary is the River Wheelock, which joins it near Middlewich.

History

The name "Dane" is thought to derive from a Celtic word meaning a slow or trespassing stream. The Dane formed part of the boundary of the historic earldom of Mercia, and tradition holds that the Vikings ascended the river in the 10th century; the name of the Danish settlement at Danebridge survives in the upper valley. In its lower valley around Middlewich, the river's brine springs supported the Cheshire salt industry, and the surrounding area is prone to subsidence caused by brine extraction.

Geology and flooding

The lower Dane meanders across the Cheshire Plain through a wide floodplain. Like other rivers draining the western Pennines it can rise quickly after heavy rain; the valley bottom has flooded at Congleton and Northwich. Near Rudheath the river is crossed by the Trent and Mersey Canal on the Dane Aqueduct.
