Maximus the Confessor (Greek: Μάξιμος ὁ Ὁμολογητής, romanized: Maximos ho Homologētēs), also spelled Maximos, otherwise known as Maximus the Theologian and Maximus of Constantinople (c. 580 – 13 August 662), was a Christian monk, theologian and scholar. In his early life, he was a civil servant, and an aide to the Byzantine Emperor Heraclius. He gave up this life in the political sphere to enter the monastic life.

Maximus is venerated in both the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Churches. He was instrumental in the fight against Monothelitism, the doctrine that Christ had only one will. For his refusal to recant, he was tried in Constantinople, his tongue and right hand were cut out, and he died in exile in Georgia. He is the most recent saint to be recognised as a Doctor-adjacent figure of undivided Christianity and is one of the few figures to be venerated by both East and West after the schisms of his era.

Life

Maximus was born in Constantinople around 580. He received an excellent education and served as first secretary to Emperor Heraclius. Around 614 he retired to a monastery at Chrysopolis, later moving to a monastery near Carthage. There he studied the writings of Gregory of Nazianzus and Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite and became a leading spokesman for Dyothelitism — the doctrine that Christ has two wills, human and divine.

The Monothelite controversy

When Emperor Constans II issued the Typos (648), forbidding discussion of whether Christ had one or two wills, Maximus refused to comply. He persuaded the Lateran Synod of 649, under Pope Martin I, to condemn Monothelitism. Arrested and brought to Constantinople, he was tried twice for heresy and treason. After his second trial in 662, his tongue was cut out so he could no longer preach, and his right hand was cut off so he could no longer write. He died in exile at a fort in Lazica on 13 August 662.

Theology

Maximus developed a comprehensive cosmic theology centred on the logoi — the divine intentions for all created beings, grounded in the Logos, Christ. His major works include the Ambigua (difficult passages in Gregory of Nazianzus), the Quaestiones ad Thalassium, the Mystagogy, and the Chapters on Charity. His synthesis reconciled the ascetic tradition of Evagrius with Chalcedonian Christology and Pseudo-Dionysian cosmology.

Legacy

The Sixth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople III, 680–681) vindicated Maximus posthumously, affirming two wills in Christ. His thought influenced John of Damascus, and in the West, John Scotus Eriugena translated his Ambigua into Latin. Interest in Maximus revived in the 20th century through the work of Hans Urs von Balthasar.
