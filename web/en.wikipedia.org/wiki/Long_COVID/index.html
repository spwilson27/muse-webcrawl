Long COVID

Protesters calling for research into long COVID

Long COVID, also known as post-COVID-19 condition or post-acute sequelae of COVID-19, is a condition characterised by continuing or new symptoms that develop after an initial SARS-CoV-2 infection. The World Health Organization defines post-COVID-19 condition as symptoms usually beginning within three months of infection, lasting at least two months, and not explained by an alternative diagnosis. The term "long COVID" was coined by patients in 2020, and patient-led research played a major role in defining the condition.

Symptoms

More than 200 symptoms have been reported across multiple organ systems. The most common include profound fatigue, post-exertional malaise (a worsening of symptoms after physical or mental effort), shortness of breath, cognitive dysfunction often described as "brain fog", chest pain, palpitations, muscle and joint pain, sleep disturbance, and loss or distortion of smell and taste. Symptoms may persist continuously, relapse and remit, or appear after recovery from the acute illness.

Causes and mechanisms

The mechanisms are not fully understood. Hypotheses supported by varying degrees of evidence include viral persistence in tissue reservoirs, immune dysregulation and autoimmunity, reactivation of latent viruses such as Epstein–Barr virus, endothelial dysfunction and microclots, and damage to organs sustained during acute illness. Risk factors include female sex, severity of the initial infection, and pre-existing conditions; the condition also occurs after mild infections and in children. Vaccination reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk.

Epidemiology and treatment

Estimates vary with definitions and study design, but a significant minority of infections — commonly estimated at around 5–10% in the post-Omicron era — result in long-lasting symptoms, amounting to tens of millions of people worldwide. As of the mid-2020s there is no single approved curative treatment; care focuses on symptom management, rehabilitation adapted to post-exertional malaise, and treatment of identified sub-conditions, while clinical trials test antivirals, immunomodulators and other candidates.
