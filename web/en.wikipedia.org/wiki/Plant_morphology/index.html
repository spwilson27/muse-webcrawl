Plant morphology

Plant morphology is the study of the form and structure of plants — their external appearance, organs, and the developmental processes by which those organs arise. Along with plant anatomy (the study of internal tissues and cells), it is one of the oldest branches of botany, and its descriptive vocabulary underpins plant identification, taxonomy, and evolutionary biology.

Scope

Morphologists describe the vegetative organs of vascular plants — roots, stems, and leaves — and the reproductive structures: flowers, fruits, and seeds in angiosperms; cones in gymnosperms; and spores and sporangia in ferns and other seedless plants. The typical flowering plant body consists of a root system below ground and a shoot system of stems bearing leaves and buds, growing from meristems — regions of dividing cells at the shoot and root tips. Goethe's Metamorphosis of Plants (1790) proposed that all aerial organs are modifications of a single archetypal leaf, an idea that anticipated modern concepts of homology.

Development and variation

Modern plant morphology studies how form changes over the life cycle (developmental morphology), how meristems lay down leaves in regular patterns (phyllotaxis), and how organs may be transformed into spines, tendrils, traps, or storage structures. Because plant form responds strongly to environment — light, water, herbivory — morphologists distinguish inherited structure from plastic response, and compare forms across species to infer evolutionary relationships (comparative morphology). Molecular developmental genetics, especially the ABC model of flower development, now complements the classical descriptive tradition.
