Whig history

Whig history (or Whig historiography) is an approach to historiography that presents history as a journey from an oppressive and benighted past to a "glorious present", where the passage of events is treated as inevitable progress towards liberty and enlightenment. The term was coined by the British historian Herbert Butterfield in his influential book The Whig Interpretation of History (1931), in which he criticised historians who wrote history as though the present—particularly parliamentary democracy and constitutional monarchy—were its natural destination.

Origins of the term

Butterfield named the approach after the British Whigs, the political faction that championed parliamentary supremacy against absolute monarchy. In the nineteenth century, historians such as Thomas Babington Macaulay, Henry Thomas Buckle, and William Lecky wrote narratives of English (and more broadly European) history centred on the steady expansion of freedom, the decline of superstition, and the triumph of reason. Macaulay's History of England (1848 onwards) was the most popular exemplar, tracing a line from Magna Carta through the Reformation and the Glorious Revolution of 1688 to Victorian constitutional government.

Butterfield's critique

Butterfield argued that Whig historians studied the past "with reference to the present", dividing historical actors into heroes who advanced progress and villains who obstructed it. This, he held, produced distortions: abridgements that simplified complex events, moral judgements applied anachronistically, and a neglect of the genuine otherness of past ages. Butterfield insisted that the historian's task is to understand the past in its own terms, not to ratify the present. His famous summary was that the Whig interpretation tends "to emphasise certain principles of progress in the past and to produce a story which is the ratification if not the glorification of the present."

Broader applications

The label has since been applied well beyond British political history. "Whig history of science" describes accounts—criticised by historians of science—that treat past science chiefly as a forerunner of modern knowledge, celebrating figures who were "right" and dismissing those who were "wrong". Similar criticisms have been made of triumphalist narratives in the histories of technology, medicine, and economics. The concept overlaps with presentism and teleology: reading ends back into beginnings, and treating outcomes as inevitable.

Criticism and defence

Some historians have defended elements of the Whig tradition, arguing that narrative history cannot wholly avoid the present, and that Butterfield himself, in works such as The Englishman and His History (1944), partly rehabilitated the Whig tradition as a useful political myth. Others note that all historical storytelling selects and orders material, so the relevant question is how self-consciously it does so. Nevertheless, "Whig history" remains a standard term of criticism in historiography seminars, used to warn against progress narratives that flatter the present.
