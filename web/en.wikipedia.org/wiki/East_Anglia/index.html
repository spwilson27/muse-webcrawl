East Anglia

East Anglia is an area in the East of England, often defined as the area covered by the counties of Norfolk, Suffolk and Cambridgeshire, with Essex sometimes included. The name derives from the Anglo-Saxon Kingdom of East Anglia, whose territory roughly matched Norfolk and Suffolk. The region's major cities are Norwich, Cambridge, Peterborough and Ipswich.

History

The area was home to the Iceni tribe, famous for Boudica's revolt against Rome (AD 60/61). In the early medieval period the Kingdom of the East Angles was one of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms; it was conquered by the Danes in 869 (when King Edmund was killed, becoming St Edmund) and later incorporated into England. In the Middle Ages, East Anglia grew rich on the wool trade, building many great "wool churches", and Norwich was England's second city.

Geography

East Anglia is one of the driest and flattest parts of Britain, formed largely of low-lying chalk and glacial deposits. The Fens in the west were drained in the 17th century (by engineers including Cornelius Vermuyden) to create rich agricultural land, much of it below sea level. The Norfolk Broads form a network of navigable rivers and lakes in the east, and the coast includes long shingle and sand shores subject to rapid erosion.

Economy and culture

Agriculture — wheat, sugar beet, vegetables and pigs — remains important, alongside the Cambridge technology and research cluster ("Silicon Fen"), the port of Felixstowe (Britain's busiest container port, in Suffolk), tourism, and offshore energy. The region is associated with painters John Constable ("Constable Country" on the Essex–Suffolk border) and the Norwich School, and with the University of East Anglia and University of Cambridge.
