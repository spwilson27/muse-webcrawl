Heinrich Eduard Heine (March 16, 1821 – October 21, 1881) was a German mathematician. He is known for the Heine–Borel theorem and for introducing, in 1861, the concept of uniform continuity of functions. He worked on Legendre polynomials, Lamé functions, Bessel functions, potential theory, and the theory of basic hypergeometric series, whose functions are still called Heine functions.

Life and career

Heine was born in Berlin and studied mathematics at the universities of Göttingen, Berlin and Königsberg, attending lectures by Gauss, Dirichlet and others. He took his doctorate at Berlin in 1842 with a thesis on linear differential equations. In 1848 he became a professor at the University of Bonn, and in 1856 he moved to the University of Halle, where he remained for the rest of his career, serving as rector in 1864–65. He was elected to several academies, including the Prussian Academy of Sciences.

Work

Heine–Borel theorem

In an 1872 paper on trigonometric series, Heine stated and used the result now known (with Émile Borel's later 1895 contribution) as the Heine–Borel theorem: a subset of the real line is compact if and only if every open cover of it has a finite subcover — equivalently, if it is closed and bounded. The theorem is foundational in real analysis.

Uniform continuity

In the same period Heine gave the first explicit definition of uniform continuity and proved that a continuous function on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous (the Heine–Cantor theorem). His work helped rigorise analysis, contributing to the arithmetisation of the subject associated with Weierstrass and Cantor, the latter a colleague at Halle.

Special functions

Heine systematically developed the theory of basic hypergeometric series (q-series), publishing the fundamental transformations and summations now associated with his name, work that later became central to combinatorics and number theory.
