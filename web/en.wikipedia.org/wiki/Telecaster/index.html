Telecaster

The Fender Telecaster, colloquially known as the Tele, is an electric guitar produced by Fender. Together with its sister model, the Esquire, it was the world's first mass-produced, commercially successful solid-body electric guitar. Introduced for national distribution as the Broadcaster in autumn 1950, it became the first guitar of its kind manufactured on a substantial scale and has been in continuous production since.

Design

Designed by Leo Fender in Fullerton, California, the Telecaster has a flat, single-cutaway solid body, generally made of ash or alder, with a bolt-on maple neck. Its simplicity of construction — likened by Fender to the Model T Ford — made it cheap and quick to manufacture and easy to repair. The original design has two single-coil pickups: a bright, cutting bridge pickup mounted in a steel plate bridge, and a softer neck pickup with a metal cover. Controls consist of a three-way pickup selector switch and master volume and tone knobs.

History

Fender first marketed the single-pickup Esquire in 1950, followed by the two-pickup Broadcaster later that year. After Gretsch objected that the name infringed its Broadkaster drum line, Fender removed the model name from the headstock (creating the collectible "Nocaster") and renamed the guitar the Telecaster in 1951, a reference to the new medium of television. The later dual-cutaway Stratocaster (1954) was developed partly from players' feedback on the Telecaster.

Players and influence

The Telecaster's bright, twangy sound made it a staple of country music, but it has been used in rock, blues, jazz, punk and indie music. Notable players include James Burton, Albert Collins, Muddy Waters, Bruce Springsteen, Keith Richards, Chrissie Hynde and Jonny Greenwood.
