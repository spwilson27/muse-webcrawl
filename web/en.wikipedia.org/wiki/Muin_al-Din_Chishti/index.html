Mu'in al-Din Chishti

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ajmer Sharif Dargah in Rajasthan, tomb shrine of Mu'in al-Din Chishti.

Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti Sijzi (Persian: معین الدین چشتی; 1 February 1143 – 15 March 1236), commonly known as Moinuddin Chishti and reverently as Khwaja Gharib Nawaz ("Benefactor of the Poor"), was a Persian Islamic scholar and Sufi saint who introduced and established the Chishti order of Sufism in the Indian subcontinent. He is one of the most venerated figures in South Asian Islam, and his shrine at Ajmer in Rajasthan remains one of the subcontinent's most visited pilgrimage sites, drawing devotees of many faiths.

Life

Born in Sijistan (Sistan) in eastern Persia, Mu'in al-Din was orphaned in his teens and gave away his inheritance — a mill and an orchard — to the poor before setting out in search of religious knowledge. He studied in Bukhara and Samarkand, travelled to Baghdad and other centres, and became a disciple of the Sufi master Usman Harooni, whom he served for about twenty years. After journeys that tradition says included Medina and Mecca, he travelled east through Lahore and Delhi and settled at Ajmer around 1191–92, in the years when Muhammad of Ghor's armies were conquering northern India.

Teaching and legacy

At Ajmer, Mu'in al-Din taught a path of devotion, generosity, and service, famously holding that the highest form of worship is to relieve the distressed, feed the hungry, and help the afflicted. He kept aloof from royal courts and political power, a hallmark of the early Chishti order in India. His principal disciples — Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki in Delhi, Hamiduddin Nagori, and through them Nizamuddin Auliya — spread the Chishtiyya across South Asia, where it became one of the most influential Sufi orders.

He died at Ajmer in 1236. His tomb (dargah) was later monumentalized under the Delhi sultans and the Mughals; the emperor Akbar made repeated pilgrimages to it on foot. The annual urs festival commemorating his death anniversary draws hundreds of thousands of pilgrims, and the shrine remains an emblem of composite, cross-communal devotion in India.
