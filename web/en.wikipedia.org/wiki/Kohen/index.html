Kohen

A kohen (Hebrew: כֹּהֵן, plural kohanim) is a Jewish priest, a patrilineal descendant of Aaron, the brother of Moses, who served in the Tabernacle and the Temple in Jerusalem. The status is hereditary through the male line and, although the Temple sacrifices ended in 70 AD, kohanim retain certain honours, restrictions and ritual roles in Judaism today.

Biblical origins

According to the Torah, Aaron and his sons were consecrated as priests during the Exodus, and the priesthood was restricted to Aaron's descendants, while the wider tribe of Levi assisted them. Kohanim performed the sacrificial service, burned incense, maintained the menorah and pronounced the priestly blessing. They received tithes and portions of offerings in lieu of a territorial inheritance.

The High Priest

The Kohen Gadol (High Priest) stood at the head of the hierarchy, uniquely permitted to enter the Holy of Holies on Yom Kippur and distinguished by special vestments, including the breastplate bearing the names of the twelve tribes.

Modern practice

Today, a kohen is called first to the Torah reading (aliyah), performs the redemption of the firstborn (pidyon haben), and in Orthodox and many Conservative services delivers the priestly blessing with hands raised in the distinctive split-finger position. Traditional law imposes extra restrictions on kohanim, including prohibitions on contact with the dead (with exceptions for close relatives) and on marrying a divorcée or convert. Surnames such as Cohen, Kahn, Katz and Kagan often — though not always — indicate kohen descent. Genetic studies of the Y-chromosome "Cohen modal haplotype" have been widely discussed in relation to the tradition of a common patrilineal ancestor.
