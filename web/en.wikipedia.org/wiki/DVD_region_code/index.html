DVD region codes are a digital rights management technique introduced in 1997 to allow film studios to control the release, price and content of DVDs in different parts of the world. A DVD-Video disc carries one or more region codes, stored as a byte of flags in its control data; a commercial DVD player checks the disc's codes against its own region setting and refuses playback if there is no match. The system divides the world into six numbered regions, plus special codes for aircraft and other uses, and code 0, meaning playable in all regions.

Rationale

Studios used region coding to stagger release dates (a film might open theatrically abroad months after its US home-video release), to set different prices in different markets, to comply with local censorship and classification requirements, and to respect differing distribution contracts. The practice was widely criticized by consumers, travellers and expatriates; in some jurisdictions, such as Australia, competition regulators objected to region coding as contrary to free-trade principles, and region-free players were openly sold there.

Enforcement and circumvention

Complementing the disc-side check, the Regional Playback Control (RPC) system governs players: early drives (RPC-1) had no hardware region checking, while RPC-2 drives enforce the region in firmware, typically allowing the user to change region only five times before the setting locks permanently. "Multi-region" or "region-free" players, firmware modifications, and computer software players that ignore the disc flags have long circumvented the system. Airlines and cruise ships commonly used region 8 discs. The successor format Blu-ray simplified the scheme to three regions (A, B, C), and many Blu-ray discs are released region-free; Ultra HD Blu-ray abolished region coding entirely.
