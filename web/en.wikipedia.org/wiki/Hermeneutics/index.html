Hermeneutics

Hermeneutics
FieldPhilosophy, theology, literary theory, law
Key figuresFriedrich Schleiermacher, Wilhelm Dilthey, Martin Heidegger, Hans-Georg Gadamer, Paul Ricoeur
Key conceptsHermeneutic circle, horizon of understanding, exegesis

Hermeneutics is the theory and methodology of interpretation, especially the interpretation of biblical texts, wisdom literature, and philosophical texts. The word derives from the Greek god Hermes, the messenger who made the messages of the gods intelligible to humans. Modern hermeneutics includes both the theory of textual interpretation and broader questions about how human beings understand meaning in language, art, history, and experience.

The interpretation of ancient texts is the historical core of hermeneutics.

History

Hermeneutics began as the theory of biblical exegesis. In the ancient and medieval worlds, interpreters distinguished between literal, allegorical, moral, and anagogical senses of Scripture. The Protestant Reformation raised the stakes of interpretation, as reformers such as Martin Luther argued that Scripture should be accessible to ordinary readers, while figures such as Matthias Flacius Illyricus developed systematic principles of interpretation.

In the nineteenth century, Friedrich Schleiermacher generalised hermeneutics into a universal art of understanding, arguing that misunderstanding is the default and that interpretation requires both grammatical and psychological reconstruction of an author's meaning. Wilhelm Dilthey extended hermeneutics into a methodology for the human sciences (Geisteswissenschaften), distinguishing understanding (Verstehen) from the explanation used in the natural sciences.

Philosophical hermeneutics

Martin Heidegger transformed hermeneutics into fundamental ontology in Being and Time (1927), arguing that interpretation is not a specialised method but the basic structure of human existence (Dasein). His student Hans-Georg Gadamer developed this into philosophical hermeneutics in Truth and Method (1960), emphasising the role of tradition, prejudice (pre-judgement), and the "fusion of horizons" between text and reader.

Paul Ricoeur broadened the field to include the interpretation of symbols, metaphors, narratives, and human action itself. Hermeneutics has since influenced literary theory, legal interpretation, historiography, and the philosophy of social science.

The hermeneutic circle

A central concept is the hermeneutic circle: the idea that a text can only be understood through its parts, while the parts can only be understood in relation to the whole. Interpretation therefore proceeds by a back-and-forth movement, revising one's understanding of the whole as the parts become clearer and vice versa. Later thinkers extended the circle beyond texts to the relation between a reader's prior understanding and any new experience.
