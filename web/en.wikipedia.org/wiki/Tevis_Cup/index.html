The Tevis Cup, formally the Western States Trail Ride, is a 100-mile endurance horse ride held annually in California. It is widely regarded as the founding event of modern endurance riding and one of the most difficult equestrian competitions in the world. The winner of the ride receives the Tevis Cup, while the horse judged best conditioned among the top finishers receives the Haggin Cup.

History

The ride was founded in 1955 by Wendell Robie, an Auburn lumberman and horseman, who wagered that a modern horse could still cross the Sierra Nevada in a single day as horses had in the era of the Pony Express and Wells Fargo. Robie and five companions completed the first ride, and the event has been run nearly every year since, following the Western States Trail over rugged mountain and canyon terrain between Lake Tahoe and Auburn.

The course

Riders start before dawn near Robie Park, east of Truckee, climb over the Sierra crest near Squaw Valley (Palisades Tahoe), then descend through a series of steep canyons — including the notorious Cougar Rock section, El Dorado Canyon, and Volcano Canyon — with cumulative elevation changes exceeding 40,000 feet of combined climbing and descent. Horses must pass veterinary checks at designated stops; completion rates are typically around 50–60 percent. The runner's race, the Western States Endurance Run, follows essentially the same trail and was inspired by the ride after Gordy Ainsleigh completed the course on foot in 1974.

Rules and awards

To finish, horse and rider must complete the 100 miles within 24 hours, including mandatory rest stops, and the horse must be judged "fit to continue" at the finish. Riders who complete the ride earn a silver completion buckle; milestones such as 1,000 miles are specially recognised. The Tevis Cup is awarded to the first horse and rider to finish whose horse is judged fit, and the Haggin Cup to the best-conditioned horse among the top ten finishers.
