The Israeli West Bank barrier, comprising the West Bank Wall and the West Bank Fence, is a separation barrier built by Israel along and within the West Bank. Construction began in 2002 during the Second Intifada; the approved route totals about 708 kilometres (440 mi). Most of the barrier consists of a multi-layer fence system with electronic sensors, patrol roads, and ditches; in urban areas, notably around Jerusalem, it takes the form of a concrete wall up to 8 metres high.

Supporters in Israel describe the barrier as a necessary security measure that sharply reduced suicide bombings and other attacks from the West Bank after 2002. Palestinians and many international critics describe it as an "apartheid wall" and a land grab, because roughly 85% of its route runs inside the West Bank rather than along the 1949 Green Line, incorporating major settlement blocs on the western side and separating many Palestinian communities from their farmland, schools, and services.

Route and construction

The route's deviations from the Green Line create the "seam zone" between the barrier and the Green Line, in which Palestinian residents require permits to live in their own homes and to cross through designated gates and checkpoints. Sections near Qalqilya and Tulkarm entirely surround Palestinian towns. The Israeli Supreme Court, sitting as the High Court of Justice, ordered route changes in several rulings (notably Beit Sourik, 2004, and Mara'abe, 2005) on proportionality grounds.

International legal response

In July 2004 the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion holding that the construction of the wall in the Occupied Palestinian Territory, including in and around East Jerusalem, was contrary to international law, and that Israel was obliged to dismantle it and make reparations. The UN General Assembly demanded compliance; Israel rejected the opinion, while continuing to adjust parts of the route pursuant to its own court rulings. Construction proceeded slowly and substantial sections remained unfinished two decades after approval.

Effects

Suicide attacks originating from the West Bank fell dramatically during and after construction, a change attributed by the Israeli government to the barrier, and by other analysts also to Operation Defensive Shield, intelligence penetration, and Palestinian political decisions. Studies and UN OCHA reporting document extensive Palestinian economic and humanitarian impacts, including fragmented access to agricultural land and the isolation of East Jerusalem.
