Textiles of Mexico

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The textiles of Mexico have a long history. The making of fibers, cloth, and other textile goods has existed in Mexico for at least 7,000 years, beginning with fibers from yucca, palm, maguey (ixtle), and later cotton. Indigenous weaving traditions survived the Spanish conquest and today blend pre-Hispanic techniques with European materials and designs.

History

Pre-Hispanic peoples wove cotton and agave fibers on the backstrap loom, reserving fine cotton garments for nobility; dyes came from cochineal, indigo, and the sea snail púrpura. The Spanish introduced sheep's wool, silk, the treadle (pedal) loom, and European embroidery stitches, which artisans in regions such as Oaxaca, Chiapas, Puebla, and Michoacán adapted to local garment types.

Traditional garments and products

Signature items include the huipil (a tunic-like garment), the embroidered Puebla dress (china poblana), rebozos (shawls, notably the jaspe ikat rebozos of Santa María del Río and Tenancingo), sarapes from Saltillo and Oaxaca, and Zapotec rugs from Teotitlán del Valle woven on pedal looms with natural dyes. Techniques include brocade, embroidery, ikat (jaspe), and waist-loom weaving whose patterns encode community identity and cosmology.

Modern era

Mexican textiles are an important part of the country's folk-art economy and cultural heritage; museums such as the Museo Textil de Oaxaca preserve historic pieces, and government programs (e.g., FONART) promote artisan work, though hand production competes with industrial imitations and imported copies.
