Copal

Copal is a name given to tree resins that are intermediate in hardness and maturity between freshly exuded ("gummy") resins and fully fossilized amber. The word derives from the Nahuatl copalli, meaning "incense." Copals are produced by various tropical trees, including species of Protium, Bursera, Hymenaea, and Agathis (kauri copal).

Types and sources

Commercial copals have historically come from East Africa (Zanzibar and Mozambique copal), West Africa (Congo and Sierra Leone copal), New Zealand (kauri gum, dug in great quantities from ancient swamps in the 19th century), and the Americas (including the courbaril resin of Hymenaea courbaril). Colombian copal, much of it subfossil material from Santander and Boyacá, is widely sold and frequently used in palaeontology because, like young amber, it can contain insect inclusions, typically thousands rather than millions of years old.

Uses

Copal was a valued ingredient in varnishes and lacquers before synthetic resins displaced it, owing to its hardness and pale color. In Mesoamerica it has been burned as ritual incense since pre-Columbian times and remains central to ceremonies such as Day of the Dead observances. Because copal is softer, more soluble, and chemically less polymerized than amber, the two can be distinguished by tests involving solvents, hardness, and fluorescence.
