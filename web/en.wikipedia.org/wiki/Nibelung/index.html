Nibelung

The term Nibelung (German: Nibelungen) refers to figures in Germanic heroic legend, above all in the Middle High German epic the Nibelungenlied and in Richard Wagner's opera cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen. The meaning of the name shifts between sources: it can denote a legendary clan of dwarves or supernatural beings, the possessors of a fabulous treasure hoard, or the Burgundian royal house itself.

In the Nibelungenlied

In the Nibelungenlied (c. 1200), the Nibelungs are originally the owners of a great treasure guarded in Norway, connected with the dwarf-like king Nibelung and his sons. The hero Siegfried wins the hoard, along with the magic sword Balmung and a cloak of invisibility, and thereafter he and then the Burgundian kings — Gunther, Gernot and Giselher — are themselves called "Nibelungs". After Siegfried's murder by Hagen, Hagen sinks the treasure in the Rhine, and Siegfried's widow Kriemhild takes a terrible revenge that destroys the Burgundian court at the hall of Etzel (Attila).

In Norse tradition

The cognate Old Norse form is Niflungar, found in the Poetic Edda and the Völsunga saga, where the Niflungs are the family of Gunnar (Gunther), descendants of Gjúki, locked in feud over the cursed gold of Andvari.

In Wagner

In Wagner's Ring, "Nibelungs" are a race of dwarves living in Nibelheim beneath the earth, ruled by Alberich, who forges the ring of power from the Rhinegold. The etymology of the name is usually connected with Germanic words for "mist" or "fog" (Old High German nebul, "mist"), suggesting "children of the mist".
