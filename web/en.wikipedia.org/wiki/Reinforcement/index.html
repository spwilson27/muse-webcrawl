Reinforcement

In behavioural psychology, reinforcement refers to consequences that increase the likelihood of an organism's future behaviour, typically by being contingent on that organism's behaviour. It is a core concept of operant conditioning, formalised by B. F. Skinner: a reinforcer is any stimulus which, when delivered following a response, strengthens that response. Reinforcement differs from punishment, which is a consequence intended to decrease behaviour, and from rewards, which are defined by being pleasurable rather than by their effect on behaviour.

Types

  • Positive reinforcement — presenting a desirable stimulus after a behaviour (e.g. praise, food, a token), increasing the behaviour.
  • Negative reinforcement — removing an aversive stimulus after a behaviour (e.g. a seat-belt buzzer stopping when the belt is fastened), also increasing the behaviour. Negative reinforcement is frequently confused with punishment, but the two have opposite effects.
  • Primary reinforcers satisfy biological needs (food, water, warmth); secondary (conditioned) reinforcers gain power by association, including money and tokens in a token economy.

Schedules of reinforcement

Skinner and his collaborators showed that the pattern (schedule) by which reinforcers are delivered strongly shapes response rates and resistance to extinction. Continuous reinforcement delivers a reinforcer after every response; intermittent schedules are fixed-ratio (after a set number of responses), variable-ratio (after an unpredictable number — as in gambling), fixed-interval (after a set time — producing a scalloped pattern of responding) or variable-interval. Variable-ratio schedules produce the highest, steadiest response rates. Partial reinforcement also makes behaviour more resistant to extinction than continuous reinforcement (the partial reinforcement effect).

Shaping and applications

Shaping (method of successive approximations) reinforces progressively closer approximations to a target behaviour and is the basis of much animal training and of applied behavior analysis used in education and autism therapy. Reinforcement principles underlie token economies in psychiatric and classroom settings, contingency management in addiction treatment, clicker training of animals, and — by deliberate design or emergent effect — engagement features of games and social media. Critics, notably Alfie Kohn in Punished by Rewards (1993), argue that extrinsic reinforcement can undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks, a position supported in part by the overjustification effect literature.

Neural basis

Many reinforcers engage the brain's reward circuitry, especially dopaminergic pathways from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens; dopamine signals are now commonly modelled as reward prediction errors, a bridge between behaviour analysis and computational neuroscience and the inspiration for reinforcement learning in machine learning.
