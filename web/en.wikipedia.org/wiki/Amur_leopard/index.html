Amur leopard

Scientific name: Panthera pardus orientalis

Conservation status: Critically Endangered (IUCN)

Range: Russian Far East and northeastern China

The Amur leopard (Panthera pardus orientalis) is a leopard subspecies native to the Primorye region of southeastern Russia and northern China. It is considered one of the rarest big cats in the world and is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List. It is also known as the Far Eastern leopard, the Manchurian leopard, and the Korean leopard.

Description

Adapted to the cold, snowy climate of the temperate forests of the Russian Far East, the Amur leopard has a thick coat that varies in length with the season, growing up to 7.5 cm long in winter. Its coat is pale and cream-coloured in winter and more reddish-yellow in summer, with widely spaced rosettes. Males weigh between 32 and 48 kg and females between 25 and 43 kg.

Distribution and habitat

The subspecies once ranged across the Korean Peninsula, northeast China, and the Russian Far East. Today it survives mainly in the Land of the Leopard National Park in Primorsky Krai, Russia, and in adjacent parts of Jilin and Heilongjiang provinces in China. It lives in temperate forests where wild ungulates such as roe deer, sika deer, and wild boar are present.

Conservation

Threats include habitat loss and fragmentation, depletion of prey, poaching for fur and bones, and inbreeding within the tiny population. Camera-trap censuses have indicated a slow recovery: estimates in the 2010s suggested roughly 60 individuals in Russia and a growing number in China, with later estimates exceeding 100 across the transboundary population. Conservation measures include anti-poaching patrols, protected-area expansion, and reintroduction planning.

In captivity

Amur leopards are kept in zoos worldwide as part of coordinated breeding programmes, though concerns exist about hybridisation in some captive lineages descended from animals crossed with other subspecies.
