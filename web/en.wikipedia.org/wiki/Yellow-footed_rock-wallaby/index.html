Yellow-footed rock-wallaby

Yellow-footed rock-wallaby
Petrogale xanthopus
Family: Macropodidae
Status: Near Threatened

The yellow-footed rock-wallaby (Petrogale xanthopus) is a member of the macropod family, which includes kangaroos and wallabies. It is a strikingly marked rock-wallaby, once found across the rocky ranges of inland eastern Australia and now restricted to fragments of its former range in South Australia, New South Wales and Queensland.

Description

The yellow-footed rock-wallaby is one of the most brightly coloured macropods. Its fur is grey-brown above and paler below, with distinctive white cheek stripes, dark dorsal markings, orange-yellow ears, forelimbs and hind feet, and a long tail ringed with alternating light and dark bands. Adults weigh roughly 6 to 11 kilograms. The rough, heavily textured soles of its hind feet give exceptional grip on rock surfaces, and it is an agile climber and leaper among cliffs and boulder piles.

Habitat and behaviour

Rock-wallabies shelter by day in caves and crevices among rocky outcrops, emerging at dusk to graze on grasses, forbs and browse, and to drink where water is available. They live in colonies that may number from a few individuals to around a hundred. Breeding can occur throughout the year when conditions are favourable; a single joey is born and develops in the mother's pouch.

Conservation

The species declined severely through hunting for its pelt in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and through competition and habitat degradation caused by introduced goats and predation by foxes. It is listed as Near Threatened, and protected populations survive in places such as the Flinders Ranges and Mutawintji National Park. Control of goats and foxes and captive breeding and reintroduction programmes have supported local recoveries. See also rock-wallaby.
