Carotid stenosis

Carotid stenosis
SpecialtyCardiology, vascular surgery, neurology
CauseAtherosclerosis
Risk factorsSmoking, hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, age
ComplicationsStroke, transient ischaemic attack
TreatmentLifestyle change, antiplatelets, statins, endarterectomy, stenting

Carotid stenosis is a narrowing of the carotid arteries, usually caused by atherosclerosis, that increases the risk of stroke. Plaque builds up where the common carotid artery divides into the internal and external carotids; fragments can break off and travel to the brain (embolism), or the artery can block completely.

Signs and symptoms

Many people have no symptoms. Symptomatic stenosis may present with a transient ischaemic attack (TIA) or stroke — weakness or numbness on one side of the body, speech disturbance, or temporary loss of vision in one eye (amaurosis fugax). A whooshing sound (bruit) may be heard over the neck with a stethoscope.

Diagnosis

Duplex ultrasound is the usual first test, measuring blood-flow velocity to estimate the degree of narrowing; CT or MR angiography can confirm the severity. Stenosis is often graded by the NASCET method as mild (<50%), moderate (50–69%) or severe (70–99%).

Treatment

All patients benefit from risk-factor control: smoking cessation, antiplatelet drugs, statins and blood-pressure management. For symptomatic severe stenosis, carotid endarterectomy — surgical removal of plaque — reduces stroke risk, as shown in the NASCET and ECST trials; carotid artery stenting is an alternative for selected patients at high surgical risk. The benefit in asymptomatic stenosis is smaller and remains debated as medical therapy improves.
