Spina bifida is a birth defect in which there is incomplete closing of the spine and the membranes around the spinal cord during early development in pregnancy. It is one of the most common neural tube defects. The name is Latin for "split spine."

Types

Three main types are recognized. In spina bifida occulta, the mildest form, one or more vertebrae are not properly formed but the spinal cord and meninges remain in place, often with no symptoms; it may be marked only by a dimple or tuft of hair on the back. In a meningocele, the protective membranes (meninges) push out through the opening in the vertebrae into a sac, but the spinal cord itself stays in position, so nerve damage is usually limited. In myelomeningocele, the most severe form, the spinal cord and nerves also protrude into the sac, causing significant disability below the level of the lesion.

Signs and complications

Myelomeningocele can cause weakness or paralysis of the legs, loss of sensation, bladder and bowel dysfunction, orthopaedic deformities such as clubfoot and scoliosis, and sexual dysfunction. It is commonly associated with the Chiari II malformation of the brain and hydrocephalus (build-up of cerebrospinal fluid), which may require a shunt. Learning difficulties can occur but intelligence is often normal. People with spina bifida also have an unusually high rate of latex allergy.

Causes and prevention

Spina bifida results from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors. The best-established risk factor is insufficient folate (vitamin B9) in the mother around conception and in the first weeks of pregnancy; certain medications such as valproic acid, poorly controlled diabetes, and obesity also increase risk. Taking folic acid supplements before conception and during early pregnancy greatly reduces the risk, and many countries fortify flour or other staple foods with folic acid for this reason.

Diagnosis

The condition can often be detected before birth through maternal blood screening for alpha-fetoprotein and by ultrasound examination; open spina bifida is usually visible on a detailed anomaly scan. After birth, diagnosis is by physical examination and imaging of the spine.

Treatment

There is no cure, but treatment can greatly improve outcomes. Babies with myelomeningocele usually undergo surgery within the first days of life to close the opening and reduce the risk of infection; a landmark clinical trial (the MOMS trial, published 2011) showed that surgery before birth can reduce the need for shunting and improve motor outcomes, though it carries risks of premature delivery. Ongoing care is multidisciplinary, involving neurosurgery, urology, orthopaedics, physiotherapy, and mobility aids such as braces and wheelchairs. Many adults with spina bifida live independently.

Epidemiology

Rates vary widely by region and have fallen in many countries following folic acid fortification and supplementation. In the United States the condition occurs in roughly 1 in 2,800 births.
