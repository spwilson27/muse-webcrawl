Metamorphism is the solid-state alteration of pre-existing rocks (protoliths) — igneous, sedimentary, or older metamorphic rocks — in response to changes in temperature, pressure and chemical environment, without the rock melting completely. The word comes from the Greek meta ("change") and morphē ("form"). Metamorphism produces the metamorphic rocks, one of the three main rock types, and is a central process in the rock cycle.

Agents of metamorphism

  • Heat — from burial, nearby magma intrusions, or geothermal gradients — accelerates chemical reactions and recrystallisation.
  • Pressure — uniform (lithostatic) pressure from burial compacts rock, while directed (differential) stress deforms it and produces aligned, foliated textures.
  • Fluids — hot, chemically active water and other fluids transport ions, promote reactions and can change a rock's composition, a process called metasomatism.

Types of metamorphism

Regional metamorphism

Regional metamorphism affects large volumes of rock during mountain-building (orogenic) events, where heat and directed pressure act together. It produces foliated rocks such as slate, schist and gneiss, over belts that may extend for hundreds of kilometres.

Contact metamorphism

Contact (thermal) metamorphism occurs in a baked zone, or aureole, around an igneous intrusion. Temperatures are high but pressure is low, producing non-foliated rocks such as hornfels and marble.

Other types

Burial metamorphism results from deep burial alone; dynamic (cataclastic) metamorphism is localised along fault zones; hydrothermal metamorphism is driven by hot fluids; and shock metamorphism results from meteorite impacts or explosions.

Grade, facies and index minerals

Metamorphic grade describes the intensity of metamorphism, from low grade (e.g. slate) to high grade (e.g. granulite). Sets of mineral assemblages that form under particular pressure–temperature conditions define metamorphic facies such as greenschist, amphibolite, blueschist and eclogite facies. Certain minerals — chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite and sillimanite — appear in sequence as grade increases in pelitic rocks and serve as index minerals mapping the pattern of metamorphism.

Textures

Metamorphic rocks may be foliated — showing a layered or banded arrangement of minerals caused by directed pressure, as in slaty cleavage, schistosity and gneissic banding — or non-foliated, as in marble and quartzite. Large crystals grown within a finer matrix during metamorphism are called porphyroblasts; garnet is a common example.
