Cauchy's integral formula

FieldComplex analysis
Named afterAugustin-Louis Cauchy
First stated1825 (Memoir on definite integrals)
Key consequenceHolomorphic functions are analytic

Cauchy's integral formula, named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy, is a central statement in complex analysis. It expresses the fact that a holomorphic function defined on a disk is completely determined by its values on the boundary of the disk, and it provides integral formulas for all derivatives of a holomorphic function. Cauchy's formula shows that, in complex analysis, "differentiation is equivalent to integration": complex differentiation, like integration, behaves in ways not seen in real differentiation.

Statement

Let U be an open subset of the complex plane C, and suppose the closed disk D = {z : |z − z0| ≤ r} is completely contained in U. Let f : U → C be a holomorphic function, and let γ be the circle forming the boundary of D, oriented positively (counterclockwise). Then for every a in the interior of D:

f(a) = (1/2πi) ∮γ f(z)/(z − a) dz

The proof of this statement uses the Cauchy integral theorem and only requires the function to be complex differentiable. Since the integrand's denominator is the "kernel" of the integral, the formula can be differentiated under the integral sign to obtain formulas for every derivative.

Derivatives

Because the denominator can be differentiated with respect to a, the formula implies that every derivative of f exists and is given by

f(n)(a) = (n!/2πi) ∮γ f(z)/(z − a)n+1 dz

for all n ≥ 0. It follows that holomorphic functions are analytic: they can be expanded in power series around any point of their domain. A direct consequence is Cauchy's estimates for the derivatives, which in turn yield Liouville's theorem — a bounded entire function must be constant — and a proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra.

Consequences

  • Morera's theorem, a converse criterion for holomorphicity.
  • The mean value property: the value of a holomorphic function at the centre of a disk equals the average of its values on the boundary circle.
  • The maximum modulus principle.
  • Proofs that holomorphic functions are infinitely differentiable and equal to their Taylor series.

Generalizations

The formula generalizes to multiply connected domains via the residue theorem: the integral around a general closed contour equals 2πi times the sum of the residues of the integrand inside the contour. Versions of the formula also exist for several complex variables (the Bochner–Martinelli formula) and in the theory of differential forms.
