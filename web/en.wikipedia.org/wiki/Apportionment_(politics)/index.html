Apportionment (politics)

Apportionment is the process by which seats in a legislative body are distributed among administrative divisions, such as states or parties, entitled to representation. It is typically done on the basis of population, so that each representative speaks for approximately the same number of people, though historical and federal considerations often modify strict proportionality.

Methods

Because seats are indivisible, exact proportional division is impossible, and many divisor and quota methods have been devised. The largest remainder method allocates seats by quota and then distributes leftovers; divisor methods such as D'Hondt (used in many party-list systems) and the Huntington–Hill method (used for the U.S. House of Representatives since 1941) divide populations by successive divisors. Different methods produce different results from the same data, giving rise to paradoxes such as the Alabama paradox, in which increasing the total number of seats can cost a state a seat.

United States

Following each decennial census, the 435 seats of the U.S. House of Representatives are reapportioned among the states; states then redraw congressional districts, a process subject to constitutional equal-population requirements established in Wesberry v. Sanders and Reynolds v. Sims and to controversies over gerrymandering.
