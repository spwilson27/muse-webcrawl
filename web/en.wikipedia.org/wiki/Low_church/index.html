Low church

In Anglican Christianity, low church refers to those who give little emphasis to ritual, sacraments, clergy and church tradition, stressing instead the authority of Scripture, preaching, personal faith and the Reformed character of Anglicanism. The term is used in contrast to high church, and is often, though not exactly, interchangeable with evangelical Anglicanism.

History

The terms "high" and "low" church emerged in the late 17th and early 18th centuries in the Church of England, originally in connection with attitudes to the authority of the Church, the monarchy and nonjurors after the Glorious Revolution. In the 19th century, the low church party stood in opposition to the Tractarian (Oxford) Movement and Anglo-Catholic ritualism, defending a more Protestant understanding of the Church of England: plain worship, the surplice rather than elaborate vestments, the communion table rather than an altar in the east end, and a low view of sacramental regeneration and apostolic succession.

Characteristics

Present-day low church parishes typically feature simpler services with greater emphasis on Bible teaching and preaching, congregational singing (often with contemporary music), extemporary prayer, and an informal style of dress and ceremony. The Lord's Supper may be celebrated less frequently and understood in more memorialist or receptionist terms. Theologically, most low church Anglicans are evangelical, holding a high view of Scripture and of justification by faith.

The term is also used analogically for similar tendencies in other traditions, such as "low" Lutheranism or Presbyterianism, in contrast to more liturgical "high church" forms.
