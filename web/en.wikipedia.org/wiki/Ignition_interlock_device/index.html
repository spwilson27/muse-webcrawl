Ignition interlock device

An ignition interlock device (IID) is a breathalyzer installed in a motor vehicle that prevents the engine from starting unless the driver provides a breath sample below a preset blood alcohol concentration (BAC) limit. The device is wired into the vehicle's ignition system and requires the driver to blow into a mouthpiece before the starter will engage. Most jurisdictions that mandate the devices do so as a condition of regaining driving privileges after a conviction for driving under the influence (DUI).

A typical ignition interlock handset installed in a car.

How it works

Before starting the vehicle, the driver blows into the device. A fuel-cell or semiconductor sensor measures the alcohol content of the breath sample. If the measured BAC exceeds the programmed limit — commonly 0.02% in the United States — the ignition is disabled for a lockout period. Once the vehicle is running, the device typically requires periodic "rolling retests" to confirm that the driver remains sober and that a passenger did not provide the initial sample.

Modern interlocks are equipped with cameras, GPS, and cellular reporting so that test results, failed attempts, and tampering events are logged and transmitted to the supervising authority, such as a probation department or motor vehicle agency.

History

Interlock technology dates to the late 1960s and 1970s, when early performance-based devices required drivers to complete a reaction-time or coordination test. The first breath-alcohol interlocks were developed in the 1980s, and pilot programs in the United States and Canada showed significant reductions in repeat DUI offenses while the devices were installed. Legislation mandating interlocks for convicted drunk drivers spread through U.S. states during the 1990s and 2000s; many states now require them even for first-time offenders.

Effectiveness

Research by traffic-safety organizations, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, has found that ignition interlocks reduce repeat DUI offenses by roughly two-thirds while installed. Critics note that the benefit often diminishes after removal, leading some researchers to argue for longer installation periods combined with alcohol treatment.

Costs and requirements

Offenders usually bear the cost of installation and monthly leasing, which in the United States commonly totals between $70 and $150 per month, plus periodic calibration visits — typically every 30 to 60 days — at a service center. Attempting to circumvent the device, or having another person blow into it, is itself a violation that can extend the restriction period.
