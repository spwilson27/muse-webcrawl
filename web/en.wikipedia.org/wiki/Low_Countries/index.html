Low Countries

The Low Countries are a coastal lowland region in northwestern Europe, corresponding roughly to the modern countries of the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg — together often called the Benelux — plus parts of northern France and western Germany near the deltas of the Rhine, Meuse and Scheldt rivers.

Geography

Much of the region lies at or below sea level and has historically been protected by dikes and reclaimed through polders. The great river deltas made the Low Countries a natural crossroads for trade between the North Sea, the German hinterland and France.

History

In the Middle Ages the region consisted of numerous counties, duchies and bishoprics, many of them prosperous centres of cloth trade and finance, such as Bruges, Ghent, Antwerp and Utrecht. The Burgundian dukes gradually unified many of these territories in the 15th century, and they later passed to the Habsburgs.

During the Eighty Years' War, the northern provinces broke away from Spanish rule to form the Dutch Republic, while the southern provinces remained under Habsburg control, eventually becoming Belgium. Luxembourg survived as a grand duchy. The three modern states formalised their cooperation in the Benelux union in the 20th century, a forerunner of European integration.

Name

The name reflects the region's low elevation. The word "Netherlands" itself derives from the Dutch neder, meaning "low".
