Anaconda Copper

IndustryMining, smelting
Founded1881 (as Anaconda Gold and Silver Mining Company)
HeadquartersButte, Montana, United States
FateAcquired by ARCO in 1977; closed 1983

The Anaconda Copper Mining Company, later the Anaconda Company, was one of the largest mining corporations in the United States and, for much of the twentieth century, one of the dominant copper producers in the world. Centered on the copper mines of Butte, Montana, the company grew into a diversified conglomerate with operations across the Americas and wielded enormous political influence in Montana.

The Anaconda Smelter Stack, completed in 1918, is among the tallest masonry structures in the world

History

The company originated in 1881, when Marcus Daly purchased the Anaconda silver mine near Butte. Digging deeper, Daly's miners struck an enormous vein of copper ore at a moment when electrification was driving demand for copper wiring. The Hill at Butte became known as "the richest hill on earth." Daly, one of Montana's "Copper Kings," feuded bitterly with rivals William A. Clark and F. Augustus Heinze.

Amalgamated and Standard Oil era

In 1899 the enterprise was folded into the Amalgamated Copper Company, backed by Standard Oil interests including Henry H. Rogers and William Rockefeller. The company consolidated control of Butte's mines, built the great smelter town of Anaconda, Montana, and operated the Washoe smelter, whose 585-foot stack remains a landmark. In 1915 the corporation took the name Anaconda Copper Mining Company; "Anaconda" became its short name in 1955.

Foreign operations and nationalization

Anaconda developed huge mines in Chile, including Chuquicamata and El Salvador, which by the mid-twentieth century supplied a large share of company earnings. In 1971 the Chilean government of Salvador Allende nationalized the Chilean copper industry, a devastating blow. The company was acquired by the Atlantic Richfield Company (ARCO) in 1977. Falling prices and the exhaustion of Butte's underground mines led to the end of smelting in 1980 and the closure of the Berkeley Pit operations in 1982–83.

Environmental legacy

A century of mining and smelting left the Upper Clark Fork River basin one of the largest Superfund sites in the United States. The Berkeley Pit, a flooded former open-pit mine in Butte, contains heavily acidic, metal-laden water and remains under long-term remediation.
