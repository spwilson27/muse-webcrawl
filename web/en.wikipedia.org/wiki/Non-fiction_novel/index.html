Non-fiction novel

The non-fiction novel is a literary genre that portrays actual events using the techniques of novel writing — scene, dialogue, characterisation and narrative structure — while remaining, in principle, factually accurate. The term was popularised by Truman Capote, who described his 1966 book In Cold Blood, about the 1959 Clutter family murders in Kansas, as a non-fiction novel.

Precursors and development

Earlier works often classed with the genre include Rodolfo Walsh's Operación Masacre (1957) in Argentina and journalistic book-length narratives that blurred reportage and the novel. Capote's success encouraged the related New Journalism of the 1960s and 1970s, including Norman Mailer's The Armies of the Night (1968) and The Executioner's Song (1979).

Criticism

The genre attracts debate about how much invention is permissible: reconstructed dialogue, compressed timelines and interior thoughts attributed to real people test the boundary between fact and fiction, and Capote's claims of total accuracy for In Cold Blood have been questioned. Modern equivalents are often shelved as narrative nonfiction or creative nonfiction.
