Tallow

Tallow is a rendered form of beef or mutton suet, primarily made up of triglycerides. Industrially, tallow is defined as animal fat with a melting point above 40 °C. Unlike suet, which is the raw fat around the kidneys and loins, tallow is the purified product obtained by rendering — melting the fat and removing impurities and moisture.

Composition

Tallow consists mainly of saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids: roughly 50% saturated fat, 42% monounsaturated, and a small proportion of polyunsaturated fat. The principal fatty acids are palmitic, stearic, and oleic acids. Properly rendered tallow is stable at room temperature for long periods without refrigeration.

Uses

Historically tallow was the primary material for candles ("tallow candles" were the cheap alternative to beeswax) and remains an ingredient in traditional soap-making via saponification. It is used as a cooking fat — notably in British suet puddings, traditional fish-and-chip frying, and Pemmican — as a lubricant, in leatherworking, and increasingly as a feedstock for biodiesel and renewable diesel.
