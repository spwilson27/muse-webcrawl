Müllerian mimicry

Müllerian mimicry is a form of biological mimicry in which two or more species that are genuinely well-defended — for example toxic, unpalatable, or equipped with stings — evolve to share the same warning signals (aposematic colouration). Because predators learn to avoid the common pattern, all the participating species benefit: the cost of teaching each predator is shared among them, so fewer individuals of each species are sacrificed during predator education.

History

The phenomenon was described by the German naturalist Fritz Müller in 1878, working in Brazil. Müller gave one of the first mathematical arguments in support of natural selection, showing that if two common defended species share a pattern, predators need to sample only a fixed number of individuals to learn avoidance, reducing the per-species mortality. His explanation gave early support to Charles Darwin's theory and is often cited as a classic example of the power of Darwinian reasoning.

Examples

The classic example is a ring of butterfly species: many Heliconius species in Central and South America share orange-and-black wing patterns despite each being genuinely unpalatable. Stinging hymenopterans — bees, wasps and ants — also share yellow-and-black banding, as do poison dart frogs in some regions and the monarch and viceroy butterflies (though the latter pair has been debated because the viceroy is itself mildly unpalatable, making it a borderline case between Müllerian and Batesian mimicry).

Contrast with Batesian mimicry

In Batesian mimicry, a harmless species mimics a defended model and gains protection without paying the cost of producing toxins. Batesian mimics are parasites on the signal: if they become too common, predators learn the signal is unreliable. In Müllerian mimicry, all parties are honest signallers, so the resemblance is mutually beneficial and tends to strengthen rather than erode the warning signal. The two forms are endpoints of a continuum, since species vary in how strongly they are defended.
