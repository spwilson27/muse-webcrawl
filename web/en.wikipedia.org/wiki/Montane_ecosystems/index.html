Montane ecosystems

Montane ecosystems are ecosystems found on mountains. They are characterised by decreasing temperature with increasing elevation, which produces a sequence of vegetation zones over a short horizontal distance, analogous to moving poleward across latitudes. Montane environments are often rich in endemic species, because isolated mountain ranges act as "sky islands", and they are especially vulnerable to climate change as warming pushes species upslope toward summits.

Zones

Ascending a mountain, one typically passes from lowland forest through the montane zone proper — cool, moist forest, in the tropics often cloud forest — to the subalpine zone of krummholz and stunted conifers, then above the tree line into the alpine zone of tussock grasses, cushion plants, and meadows, and finally the nival zone of rock, snow, and ice. Elevation ranges differ with latitude: near the equator montane forest may begin above 1,000 m, while at higher latitudes the zones compress downward.

Climate

Air temperature falls with altitude at an average environmental lapse rate of about 6.5 °C per kilometre. Mountains force rising air to cool and condense, giving windward slopes high precipitation while leeward slopes lie in rain shadow. Thin air, intense ultraviolet radiation, strong winds, and short growing seasons select for slow-growing, ground-hugging plants and animals with large lungs or efficient oxygen use.

Biodiversity and conservation

Mountain regions cover about a quarter of Earth's land surface and harbour a disproportionate share of its species. Threats include deforestation, overgrazing, mining, tourism infrastructure, and climate-driven range shifts; because montane species have nowhere higher to go, mountaintop extinctions are a recognised risk.
