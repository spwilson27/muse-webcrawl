Makhtesh Ramon

Makhtesh Ramon (Hebrew: מכתש רמון) is a geological feature of Israel's Negev desert, located at the peak of Mount Negev about 85 km south of Beersheba near the town of Mitzpe Ramon. It is the world's largest "makhtesh" — an erosion cirque — measuring roughly 40 km long, 2–10 km wide, and up to 450 metres deep.

Formation

A makhtesh is not an impact crater or volcanic caldera. It formed as a mountain of hard sedimentary rock (limestone and dolomite) was uplifted over a softer sandstone core; erosion stripped the hard cap from the crest, and a river — here Nahal Ramon — carved out the exposed soft interior, leaving steep walls around a deep valley. Makhteshim are rare worldwide; the few known examples are concentrated in the Negev and Sinai.

Geology and nature

The crater walls expose a remarkable stratigraphic sequence, including Jurassic and Triassic formations, fossilised trees, ammonite beds, ancient volcanic dikes, and colourful sandstone and clay hills such as the "Carpentry Shop" (HaMinsara), a hill of prismatic sandstone columns. The area is protected as part of Israel's largest nature reserve. Wildlife includes Nubian ibex, onagers reintroduced to the region, hyraxes, and desert birds. In 2017 the area around Mitzpe Ramon was designated an International Dark Sky Park, the first in the Middle East.

History

The ancient Nabataean Incense Route passed along the crater's northern rim, with the way station of Avdat nearby. The town of Mitzpe Ramon was founded in 1951 as a camp for road builders and later became a development town; today it serves as the gateway for visitors to the crater.
