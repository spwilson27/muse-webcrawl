The World Hockey Association (WHA) was a major professional ice hockey league that operated in North America from 1972 to 1979. It was the first league to challenge successfully the monopoly of the National Hockey League (NHL) since the 1920s, and its competition for players dramatically raised salaries and undermined the NHL's reserve clause. Four of its teams joined the NHL when the leagues merged in 1979.

History

The WHA was founded by Gary Davidson and Dennis Murphy, who had earlier co-founded the American Basketball Association. Announced in 1971, the league began play in October 1972 with twelve teams, many in markets the NHL had ignored. Its decisive early coup was signing Bobby Hull away from the Chicago Black Hawks: the Winnipeg Jets gave him a contract including a $1 million signing bonus, then the largest in hockey history. Other stars followed, including Gordie Howe, who came out of retirement to play with his sons Mark and Marty on the Houston Aeros, and goaltender Gerry Cheevers.

Crucially, the WHA refused to honour the standard NHL player contract's reserve clause, and a 1972 court decision prevented the NHL from enforcing it against defectors, opening a free market in players. The league also innovated with coloured pucks (blue, then red), overtime formats and aggressive expansion into the American Sun Belt. Chronic financial instability, however, caused franchises to fold or relocate frequently, and by 1978 the league had shrunk to seven teams.

Merger with the NHL

After years of negotiations, a merger agreement was approved in 1979. The Edmonton Oilers, Winnipeg Jets, Quebec Nordiques and Hartford Whalers were admitted to the NHL as expansion teams, while the remaining WHA clubs folded. The WHA championship trophy was the Avco World Trophy; the Oilers and Jets dynasties of the 1980s grew directly out of WHA rosters. The league also launched the career of Wayne Gretzky, who turned professional at seventeen with the Indianapolis Racers in 1978 before being sold to Edmonton.

Legacy

The WHA is credited with opening major-league hockey to new regions, European players and younger talent (it signed teenagers the NHL would not draft), and with breaking the reserve system that had bound players to one club indefinitely. Two surviving franchises from the merger, the Oilers and the franchise now known as the Carolina Hurricanes (formerly Hartford), remain in the NHL; the Jets and Nordiques later relocated to Phoenix and Colorado respectively, though a new Winnipeg Jets returned in 2011.
