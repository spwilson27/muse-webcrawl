Shōjo manga (少女漫画, "girls' comics") is an editorial category of Japanese comics (manga) marketed to an audience of adolescent girls and young adult women. It is one of the major demographic categories of manga, alongside shōnen manga (for boys), seinen (for adult men), and josei (for adult women).

Shōjo manga covers a wide range of genres — romance, fantasy, science fiction, horror, comedy, and historical drama — and is not defined by content but by its target readership and the magazines in which it is serialized. Visually it is often associated with elaborate page layouts, decorative screentones, flowers and sparkles, and heroines drawn with large, expressive eyes.

History

Manga for girls appeared in Japanese girls' magazines in the early 20th century. In the postwar era, male artists such as Osamu Tezuka, whose Princess Knight (1953) is regarded as a foundational work, initially dominated the field. From the late 1960s and 1970s, women artists — most notably the loosely affiliated "Year 24 Group" including Moto Hagio and Keiko Takemiya — transformed the genre, introducing psychological depth, complex narratives, and themes such as gender ambiguity and same-sex romance.

In the 1990s, series such as Naoko Takeuchi's Sailor Moon and CLAMP's Cardcaptor Sakura achieved international success and helped popularize manga among female readers worldwide.

Characteristics

Shōjo manga frequently emphasizes characters' emotions and relationships, using visual devices such as floating backgrounds, superimposed imagery, and non-rectangular panel arrangements to depict interior states. Romance is a common but by no means universal focus.
