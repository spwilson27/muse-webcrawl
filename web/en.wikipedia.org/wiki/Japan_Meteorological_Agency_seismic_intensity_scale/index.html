The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) seismic intensity scale (known in Japanese simply as Shindo, "degree of shaking") is a seismic intensity scale used in Japan to measure the intensity of ground shaking at a given location during an earthquake. Unlike magnitude scales such as the Richter or moment magnitude scale, which express the energy released by an earthquake as a single value, Shindo values vary from place to place for the same event, depending on distance from the epicentre, local geology and building quality.

The scale

The modern instrumental scale has ten grades:

GradeTypical effects
1Felt by only some people indoors.
2Felt by most people indoors; hanging objects sway.
3Dishes rattle; most people indoors feel shaking.
4Objects may fall; some people are frightened; sleepers wake.
5-lower (5弱)Books and dishes fall from shelves; unsecured furniture may move.
5-upper (5強)Heavy furniture may topple; walls may crack; difficult to walk without holding on.
6-lower (6弱)Impossible to stand unaided; many unreinforced walls collapse.
6-upper (6強)Movement only possible by crawling; landslides and ground fissures possible.
7Most buildings suffer damage; even reinforced structures may tilt or be destroyed.

History

The ancestor of the scale dates to 1884, when the first Japanese seismic intensity classes were drawn up. The scale was repeatedly revised (notably in 1949, when grade 7 was added after the 1948 Fukui earthquake). Until 1996, intensities were assigned by JMA observers based on felt reports and damage surveys; since then, values are computed automatically from accelerometer measurements of ground acceleration at more than 4,000 observation stations, allowing them to be broadcast within minutes of an earthquake.

Use

Shindo readings dominate Japanese earthquake reporting and disaster response: television bulletins, mobile-phone alerts and the JMA's Earthquake Early Warning system all announce expected and observed intensities by municipality. A maximum intensity of 7 has been recorded for several destructive earthquakes, including the 1995 Great Hanshin (Kobe) earthquake, the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (in part of Miyagi Prefecture), and the 2016 Kumamoto earthquakes.
