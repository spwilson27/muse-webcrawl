Lynch syndrome

Lynch syndrome
Also called: Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC)
Inheritance: Autosomal dominant
Genes: MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM
Key risk: Colorectal and endometrial cancer

Lynch syndrome, historically known as hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), is an inherited condition that greatly increases the risk of colorectal cancer and several other cancers. It is caused by germline mutations in DNA mismatch repair genes — chiefly MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 — or by deletions in EPCAM affecting MSH2 expression.

Cancer risks

People with Lynch syndrome have a lifetime colorectal cancer risk that can reach roughly 50–80% depending on the gene, often at a younger age than typical and predominantly in the right colon. Endometrial cancer risk in women is also substantially elevated, along with increased risks of ovarian, gastric, urinary tract, small bowel, pancreatic, and sebaceous skin tumours.

Diagnosis

Tumours in Lynch syndrome typically show microsatellite instability and loss of mismatch repair protein staining on immunohistochemistry, which are used as screening tests before confirmatory germline genetic testing. Clinical criteria such as the Amsterdam criteria and revised Bethesda guidelines historically guided testing, though universal tumour screening is now widely recommended.

Management

Management centres on intensive surveillance, including colonoscopy every one to two years beginning in early adulthood, consideration of risk-reducing hysterectomy after childbearing, and aspirin chemoprevention, which the CAPP2 trial showed reduces colorectal cancer incidence in Lynch syndrome carriers.
