Strain hardening

Strain hardening, also known as work hardening or cold working, is the strengthening of a metal or polymer by plastic deformation. The strengthening occurs because of dislocation movements and dislocation generation within the crystal structure of the material: as the material is deformed, dislocations multiply and entangle, impeding further dislocation motion and thereby increasing the yield strength while reducing ductility.

Mechanism

When a crystalline material is plastically deformed, the density of dislocations rises dramatically — from roughly 106–108 per square metre in an annealed metal to as much as 1015 per square metre in a heavily cold-worked one. The stress required to move a dislocation through this dense "forest" of other dislocations increases with the square root of the dislocation density.

Description

The plastic region of the engineering stress–strain curve of many metals is often modelled by a power law, σ = Kεn, where n is the strain-hardening exponent and K the strength coefficient (the Hollomon equation). Strain hardening delays the onset of necking in tension, described by the Considère criterion.

Applications and removal

Cold-rolling, drawing, bending, and shot peening exploit work hardening to strengthen components. Annealing reverses the effect through recovery, recrystallisation, and grain growth, restoring ductility.
