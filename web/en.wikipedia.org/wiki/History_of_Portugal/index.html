History of Portugal

The history of Portugal traces one of Europe's oldest nation-states, established in the 12th century, which pioneered the Age of Discovery and built the first global maritime empire, endured union with Spain and a devastating earthquake, and became a democratic republic after the Carnation Revolution of 1974.

Formation of the kingdom

The territory was inhabited by Lusitanians, conquered by Rome, and later ruled by Visigoths and then Moors after 711. A County of Portugal emerged in the 9th century during the Reconquista. Afonso Henriques defeated the Moors at Ourique (1139), proclaimed himself king, and secured recognition of Portuguese independence from León (1143) and the papacy (1179). His successors completed the reconquest with the capture of the Algarve in 1249, fixing mainland borders that are among Europe's oldest.

Age of Discovery and empire

Under the Avis dynasty, Portugal led European maritime exploration: Ceuta (1415), Madeira and the Azores, the rounding of the Cape of Good Hope by Bartolomeu Dias (1488), Vasco da Gama's sea route to India (1498), and Cabral's landfall in Brazil (1500). The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the non-European world between Portugal and Spain. Lisbon grew rich on the spice trade, and the Portuguese empire extended to Africa, India, Southeast Asia, China (Macau), and Brazil.

Union, earthquake and decline

The death of King Sebastian at Alcácer Quibir (1578) precipitated a succession crisis and the Iberian Union under the Spanish Habsburgs (1580–1640), ended by the Restoration War. The catastrophic Lisbon earthquake of 1755 devastated the capital and was followed by the Marquis of Pombal's authoritarian modernisation. Brazil's independence in 1822 ended Portugal's largest colony.

Republic, dictatorship and democracy

The monarchy fell in 1910; the unstable First Republic gave way to military rule and then António de Oliveira Salazar's Estado Novo dictatorship (1932–1968), which fought costly colonial wars in Africa from 1961. On 25 April 1974 the Carnation Revolution — a nearly bloodless military coup — restored democracy; Portugal decolonised rapidly, adopted a democratic constitution in 1976, and joined the European Community in 1986.
