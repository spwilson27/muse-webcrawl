August Strindberg

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Self-portrait photograph of August Strindberg in the 1890s: the playwright glaring into his own camera, wild-haired]

Johan August Strindberg (22 January 1849 – 14 May 1912) was a Swedish playwright, novelist, poet and painter, one of the most influential and tormented figures of modern literature. His naturalist dramas of the 1880s — The Father (1887), Miss Julie (1888), Creditors (1889) — savaged marriage and class with a psychological violence new to the stage; after the spiritual crisis of his 'Inferno' years he pioneered a dream-logic expressionism in To Damascus (1898–1904), A Dream Play (1902) and The Ghost Sonata (1907).

Strindberg wrote over 60 plays and more than 30 works of fiction, autobiography and history, including the novel The Red Room (1879), which founded the modern Swedish novel, and the confessional Inferno (1897). Three marriages, paranoia, alchemy experiments and occult studies mark his life as luridly as his art.

Miss Julie and naturalism

Miss Julie — the count's daughter who seduces her father's valet on Midsummer Eve and is destroyed by class, sex and heredity, prefaced by a manifesto of naturalist drama — was banned, privately printed and destined to become his most performed play. His prefaces announced a theatre of Darwinian struggle and complex, 'characterless' characters, a generation ahead of its staging.

After the Inferno

In 1910 Strindberg's final polemics (the 'Strindberg feud') split Swedish letters; Stockholm's workers marched for his 63rd birthday months before his death. His late chamber plays, written for his own Intimate Theatre, and his expressionist dramaturgy shaped German and American theatre — O'Neill acknowledged him as his master and dedicated his Nobel speech partly to the debt.
