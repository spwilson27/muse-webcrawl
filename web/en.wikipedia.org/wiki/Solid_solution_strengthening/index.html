Solid solution strengthening

Solid solution strengthening is a type of alloying that can be used to improve the strength of a pure metal. The technique involves adding atoms of one element (the alloying element) to the crystalline lattice of another element (the base metal), forming a solid solution. The local nonuniformity in the lattice due to the alloying element makes plastic deformation more difficult by impeding dislocation motion.

Types

In a substitutional solid solution, solute atoms replace solvent atoms on lattice sites. This is favoured when the two atoms are similar in size (differing by less than about 15 per cent, per the Hume-Rothery rules), share similar electronegativity, and have the same crystal structure. In an interstitial solid solution, small solute atoms — such as carbon in iron — occupy the spaces between solvent atoms, producing strong local distortions even at low concentrations.

Mechanism

Solute atoms distort the surrounding lattice, creating strain fields that interact with the stress fields of dislocations. A solute atom larger than the host compresses the lattice nearby; a smaller one stretches it. Dislocations are attracted to or repelled by these strained regions and must be driven harder to move past them, raising the yield strength. The strengthening increment increases with solute concentration and with the size misfit between solute and solvent atoms.

Effects and examples

Solid solution strengthening typically raises strength and hardness while slightly reducing ductility, and it avoids the loss of strength at elevated temperature that can affect precipitation-hardened alloys. Classic examples include brass (zinc in copper), bronze (tin in copper), carbon steel (interstitial carbon in iron), and nickel-based superalloys strengthened by chromium, molybdenum and other substitutional solutes.
