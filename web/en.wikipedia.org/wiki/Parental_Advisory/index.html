The Parental Advisory label is a warning notification applied to audio recordings in the United States (and adopted in various forms elsewhere) indicating that the recording contains explicit content, such as profanity, violence, or sexual references. The black-and-white label, reading "Parental Advisory: Explicit Content," is administered through a programme of the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA), though it is applied voluntarily by record labels rather than assigned by an independent ratings board.

The Parental Advisory label as it appears on album covers.

History

The label originated in 1985 amid controversy over explicit lyrics in popular music. The Parents Music Resource Center (PMRC), co-founded by Tipper Gore, campaigned against violent and sexually explicit lyrics and published a list of songs it called the "Filthy Fifteen." The Senate held hearings on "porn rock" in September 1985. In November 1985, the RIAA agreed that member labels would either affix warning labels to recordings with explicit lyrics or reprint the lyrics on the packaging. In 1990 the industry standardised the now-familiar black-and-white logo; in 1996 the wording was changed from "Explicit Lyrics" to "Explicit Content."

Use and effect

Labels apply the sticker at their own discretion, and edited ("clean") versions of albums are commonly produced for retailers that decline to stock labelled recordings — Walmart's refusal to sell stickered albums made clean versions commercially significant. The label has frequently been criticised as ineffective or counterproductive: it is popularly said to increase an album's appeal to young buyers (the "forbidden fruit" effect), and artists have reproduced the label in artwork as a badge of honour; it has become a widely recognised piece of graphic design, parodied and merchandised in its own right. With digital distribution, streaming services and download stores display an "explicit" tag alongside tracks instead of the physical sticker.
