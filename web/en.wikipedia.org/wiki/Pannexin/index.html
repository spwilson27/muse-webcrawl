Pannexin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pannexins are a family of vertebrate membrane channel proteins, homologous to the invertebrate innexins that form gap junctions. Humans and other mammals have three pannexin genes: PANX1, PANX2, and PANX3. Pannexin subunits assemble into large-pore channels called pannexons — most evidence indicates a heptameric (seven-subunit) structure for pannexin-1 — that connect the cytoplasm to the extracellular space.

Function

Unlike connexins, pannexins in vivo appear to function mainly as single-membrane channels rather than as intercellular gap junctions. Pannexin-1 channels are best known for releasing signalling molecules, above all ATP, as well as ions and metabolites. ATP released through pannexin-1 during apoptosis acts as a "find-me" signal attracting phagocytes; in neurons and astrocytes the channels participate in purinergic signalling, seizure activity, and responses to ischemia. Pannexin channels can be opened by membrane depolarization, mechanical stretch, increased extracellular potassium, and caspase cleavage during cell death, and are blocked by drugs such as probenecid and carbenoxolone.

Expression and disease

Pannexin-1 is widely expressed in brain, vasculature, immune cells, and many other tissues; pannexin-2 is concentrated in the nervous system; pannexin-3 is found in skin, bone, and cartilage. Genetic variants in PANX1 have been linked to rare multisystem disorders, and channel dysfunction is implicated in inflammation (via the inflammasome), migraine, hypertension, stroke, and tumour biology, making pannexins an active drug target — though no pannexin-specific therapy is yet in clinical use.

History

The family was identified in 2000 by Panchin and colleagues, who named the proteins from the Latin pan ("all, throughout"), reflecting their wide distribution, combined with nexus, for their relation to junction proteins.
