Terrestrial television

Terrestrial television is a type of television broadcasting in which the signal is transmitted by radio waves from the terrestrial (Earth-based) transmitter of a television station to a receiver (an antenna) attached to a television set. The term terrestrial is more common in Europe and Latin America, while in North America it is usually called over-the-air (OTA) or broadcast television. The term "terrestrial" is used to distinguish this type from newer technologies such as satellite television (direct broadcast satellite or DBS), in which the television signal is transmitted to the receiver from an orbiting satellite; cable television, in which the signal is carried to the receiver through a coaxial or fibre-optic cable; and internet streaming media.

History

Terrestrial television was the first technology used for television broadcasting. Regular services began in the 1930s and 1940s, with networks such as the BBC in the United Kingdom and NBC and CBS in the United States using transmitters in the VHF and later UHF bands. It remained the only means of receiving television until the 1950s, when cable television began to develop, initially as community antenna systems serving areas with poor reception.

Analogue and digital

Analogue terrestrial broadcasts used systems such as NTSC, PAL and SECAM. From the late 1990s and 2000s, countries transitioned to digital terrestrial television (DTT), using standards such as DVB-T and DVB-T2 in most of the world, ATSC in North America, ISDB-T in Japan and parts of Latin America, and DTMB in China. Digital broadcasting allows more channels, high-definition pictures and additional services within the same spectrum, and most countries have now switched off analogue terrestrial transmissions. In many countries, terrestrial broadcasting survives as a free-to-air platform competing with cable, satellite and streaming services.
