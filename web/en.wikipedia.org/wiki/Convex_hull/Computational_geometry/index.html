Computational geometry

Computational geometry is a branch of computer science devoted to the study of algorithms for problems stated in terms of geometry. Typical problems include computing the convex hull of a point set, finding intersections of line segments, constructing Delaunay triangulations and Voronoi diagrams, motion planning for robots, and nearest-neighbour search.

History

The field emerged in the 1970s, with Michael Shamos's 1975 doctoral thesis usually credited as founding its modern algorithmic study. It grew with the demands of computer graphics, computer-aided design, geographic information systems and robotics.

Core problems and techniques

  • Convex hulls — Graham scan, Jarvis march, and Chan's algorithm compute hulls in O(n log n) or output-sensitive time.
  • Triangulations — Delaunay triangulations maximize the minimum angle and underlie mesh generation.
  • Arrangements and duality — point–line duality transforms incidence problems into equivalent forms.
  • Sweep line algorithms — a moving line processes events in sorted order, as in Fortune's algorithm for Voronoi diagrams.
  • Spatial data structures — kd-trees, R-trees and range trees support geometric queries.

Applications

Computational geometry underpins computer graphics and vision, CAD/CAM, robotics, GIS, astronomy, molecular biology, and the finite element meshing used in engineering simulation. Libraries such as CGAL provide robust implementations of its algorithms.
