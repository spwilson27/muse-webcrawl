Prince-bishop

A prince-bishop is a bishop who is also the civil ruler of some secular principality and sovereignty. The principality or Hochstift was ruled politically by a prince of the church, as either a prince-elector, prince-archbishop, or prince-bishop, and was generally distinct from the larger diocese he administered spiritually.

Holy Roman Empire

Prince-bishoprics were most numerous in the Holy Roman Empire, where bishops received grants of land and regalian rights from the emperors beginning in the Ottonian period. The three most powerful were the prince-archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier, who were also prince-electors with a vote in imperial elections. Other major prince-bishoprics included Salzburg, Würzburg, Bamberg, Münster, Liège, and Breslau.

These ecclesiastical states endured until the great secularisation of 1803, when the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss mediatised nearly all of them, transferring their territories to secular princes.

Elsewhere

Comparable institutions existed elsewhere in Europe: the bishops of Durham in England exercised palatine powers on the Scottish border; the bishop of Urgell remains co-prince of Andorra to this day, sharing sovereignty with the president of France; and the archbishops of Montenegro and the prince-bishops of the Montenegro metropolitanate ruled a de facto theocratic state until the mid-19th century. The Bishop of Rome, the Pope, ruled the Papal States until 1870 and today remains sovereign of Vatican City.
