Kufic

Kufic is the oldest calligraphic form of the various Arabic scripts. It consists of a modified form of the old Nabataean script and was developed in the late 7th century, named after the city of Kufa in Iraq, though it did not necessarily originate there. Kufic is characterised by angular, rectilinear letterforms with extended horizontal strokes, and it was the principal script used for copying Qur'ans from the 8th to the 10th century.


A folio of the "Blue Qur'an" (9th–10th century), written in gold Kufic on indigo-dyed vellum.

Development and uses

Early Qur'an manuscripts (such as the Birmingham and Sanaa manuscripts) were written in the related Hijazi script; the more formal Kufic then became standard for deluxe codices, usually on parchment. Kufic was also used for inscriptions on buildings, tombstones, coins and pottery across the Islamic world. Because its letterforms can be difficult to read, it gradually gave way to the cursive naskh scripts for book hands after the 10th–12th centuries, but survived in decorative and epigraphic use.

Variants

Regional and decorative variants include Maghrebi Kufic in North Africa and al-Andalus; "floriated" and "foliated" Kufic, in which strokes sprout leaves and tendrils; plaited or knotted Kufic; and square Kufic (banna'i), a highly geometric form built on a square grid, common in tilework and architecture, in which words such as "Allah" and "Muhammad" are repeated in maze-like patterns. Pseudo-Kufic — imitation Arabic lettering used decoratively in medieval and Renaissance European art — is sometimes called Kufesque.
