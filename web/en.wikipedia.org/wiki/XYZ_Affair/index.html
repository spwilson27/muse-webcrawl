The XYZ Affair was a political and diplomatic episode in 1797 and 1798, early in the administration of President John Adams, involving a confrontation between the United States and Revolutionary France that led to the Quasi-War, an undeclared naval war. French agents demanded bribes and a loan before they would permit American diplomats to begin negotiations to end French seizures of American shipping; when the correspondence was published in the United States — with the French agents' names replaced by the letters X, Y and Z — it provoked public outrage and a wave of patriotic, anti-French sentiment.

Background

France, angered by the Jay Treaty of 1794 between the United States and Britain, and by American neutrality in the French Revolutionary Wars, had begun seizing American merchant ships bound for Britain. Adams sent a three-man commission — Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, John Marshall and Elbridge Gerry — to Paris in 1797 to negotiate. Foreign minister Charles Maurice de Talleyrand refused formal reception and instead sent intermediaries.

The demands

Talleyrand's agents informed the commissioners that negotiations required a substantial bribe (a douceur of about $250,000) for Talleyrand personally, a large loan to the French government, and an apology for remarks by Adams critical of France. The Americans refused the bribe; the slogan "Millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute!" became a rallying cry in the United States (often misattributed to Pinckney's sharper reply, "No, no, not a sixpence"). Gerry remained in Paris for a time trying to avert war, to the frustration of his colleagues and the administration.

Consequences

The publication of the dispatches in April 1798 transformed American politics. Congress authorised military preparations, created the Department of the Navy, and abrogated the Franco-American treaties; the Quasi-War followed at sea from 1798 to 1800, ending with the Convention of 1800. Domestically the affair strengthened Adams's Federalists in the short term, contributed to the passage of the Alien and Sedition Acts, and deepened the partisan divide with Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans — many of whom had initially suspected the dispatches were fabricated.
