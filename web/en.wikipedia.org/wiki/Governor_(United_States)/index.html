Governor (United States)

In the United States, a governor serves as the chief executive and head of state of a U.S. state or territory. Governors are responsible for implementing state laws and overseeing the operation of the state executive branch. As state leaders, governors advance and pursue new and revised policies and programs using a variety of tools, among them executive orders, executive budgets, and legislative proposals and vetoes.

A U.S. state capitol building with a governor's mansion nearby.

Election and terms

Governors in all states are directly elected by the voters of the state. Most serve four-year terms; New Hampshire and Vermont elect governors to two-year terms. Many states impose term limits, commonly two consecutive four-year terms. Candidates must generally meet age, citizenship, and residency requirements set by state constitutions. If a governorship falls vacant, it typically passes to the lieutenant governor or another designated official in the line of succession.

Powers

Gubernatorial powers vary by state — some constitutions create strong governors, others disperse executive authority among separately elected officials (the "plural executive"). Common powers include: preparing the state budget; signing or vetoing legislation (many governors hold a line-item veto over appropriations); appointing agency heads and judges; granting pardons and commutations; commanding the state National Guard when not in federal service; and delivering an annual State of the State address. Governors also play a central role in emergencies, issuing declarations and coordinating disaster response.
