Separate system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The separate system, also known as the Pennsylvania system, was a form of prison management based on the principle of keeping prisoners in complete solitary confinement — separate from one another at all times — so that, isolated with their conscience and a Bible, they would reflect on their crimes and become penitent. Along with the rival silent system of Auburn Prison, it dominated 19th-century penology.

Origins

The system was developed under the influence of the Philadelphia Society for Alleviating the Miseries of Public Prisons and Quaker reformers, and first fully applied at the Eastern State Penitentiary in Philadelphia, which opened in 1829. Prisoners there lived, worked, and exercised alone in individual cells and yards, hooded when moved, and identified by number rather than name.

Spread and influence

The separate system influenced prison design across Europe. In Britain it was adopted at Pentonville Prison in London (1842), which became a model for dozens of radial-plan prisons, and sepia commissions promoted the system abroad. Charles Dickens, visiting Eastern State in 1842, condemned the regime in American Notes as a cruel psychological punishment.

Decline

Critics argued that prolonged isolation caused mental illness and that the system was expensive to build and administer. By the late 19th and 20th centuries most jurisdictions abandoned strict separation in favour of congregate labour and classification systems, though solitary confinement continues to be debated in modern corrections.
