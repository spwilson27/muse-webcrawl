The Book of Wisdom, also called the Wisdom of Solomon, is a deuterocanonical work of Jewish wisdom literature written in Greek, probably in Alexandria, Egypt, in the late 1st century BCE. Although the speaker purports to be King Solomon, the book's Greek language, philosophical vocabulary, and references to conditions under Roman rule show that it was composed many centuries after Solomon's time. It is included in the canons of the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches; Protestants classify it among the Apocrypha.

The book addresses rulers, exhorting them to love justice and seek Wisdom, whom it praises as the fashioner and guide of all things. It contrasts the fates of the righteous and the ungodly, develops an unusually explicit affirmation of the immortality of the soul, and closes with a long retelling of the Exodus in which Wisdom is the agent of Israel's deliverance. Its description of Wisdom as "a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God" (7:26) had great influence on later Jewish and Christian thought.

Structure

Scholars commonly divide the book into three parts. Chapters 1–5 contrast the righteous and the wicked, depicting the ungodly as materialists who reason that life is short and chance-governed, and who persecute the just man — a passage (2:12–20) later read by Christians as a prophecy of Christ's passion. Chapters 6–9 praise Wisdom and contain Solomon's prayer for her (ch. 9), modelled on Solomon's prayer at Gibeon. Chapters 10–19 review sacred history from Adam to the Exodus, pairing acts of deliverance for the righteous with punishments for their enemies, and include a long digression against idolatry (13–15).

Authorship and date

The author was a Greek-speaking Jew, probably of Alexandria, steeped in Hellenistic philosophy: the text employs concepts and terms drawn from Platonism and Stoicism, such as the four cardinal virtues (8:7) and the incorruptibility of the soul. Allusions to persecution and to Roman-era conditions lead most scholars to date it between about 50 BCE and the early 1st century CE. The Muratorian Fragment lists it among accepted writings "of Solomon," though Jerome and others recognised its pseudepigraphal character.

Main ideas

"For God created us for incorruption, and made us in the image of his own eternity, but through the devil's envy death entered the world." (2:23–24)

Key themes include the immortality and pre-existence of the soul, retribution after death, Wisdom as a divine attribute almost personified into a separate entity, God's providential care for Israel amid the nations, and a philosophical critique of idolatry as the origin of moral corruption. Notably, the book denies that God made death (1:13), attributing evil to human choice and demonic envy.

Influence

The Wisdom of Solomon influenced early Christian writers profoundly; its portrayal of the righteous sufferer and its language about Wisdom fed into New Testament Christology (compare Hebrews 1:3 with Wisdom 7:26). Philosophically, it is a key witness to the encounter between biblical faith and Hellenistic thought in Second Temple Judaism.
