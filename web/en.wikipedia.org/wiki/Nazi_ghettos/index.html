Nazi ghettos

The Warsaw Ghetto wall, 1941

During the Holocaust, Nazi ghettos were segregated districts of cities in German-occupied Europe in which Jews were forced to live under miserable, overcrowded conditions. The Nazi regime established at least 1,143 ghettos in occupied eastern Europe, beginning in occupied Poland in 1939–1940. Though German officials initially described them as a temporary measure, ghettos served to isolate, exploit and ultimately facilitate the deportation of Jews to concentration and extermination camps.

Establishment and conditions

The first ghetto was established at Piotrków Trybunalski in October 1939. The largest were the Warsaw Ghetto, which at its peak held about 460,000 people in 3.4 km², and the Łódź Ghetto, which held about 200,000. Ghettos were sealed with walls, fences or barbed wire and guarded; leaving without permission was punishable by death. Inside, starvation rations (in Warsaw officially under 200 calories a day), disease — especially typhus — and forced labour killed tens of thousands even before deportations began. Jewish councils (Judenräte) and Jewish ghetto police were compelled to administer internal affairs under German orders.

Liquidation and resistance

From 1942, as the "Final Solution" proceeded, ghettos were progressively liquidated: inhabitants were deported to extermination camps such as Treblinka, Bełżec and Auschwitz, or murdered in mass shootings. Armed resistance culminated in the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943, the largest single revolt by Jews during the war, and uprisings also occurred in Białystok and elsewhere. The last major ghetto, Łódź, was liquidated in August 1944.
