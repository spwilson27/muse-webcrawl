Aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDH) are a family of enzymes that catalyse the oxidation of aldehydes to carboxylic acids, using NAD+ or NADP+ as a cofactor. They occur in virtually all organisms and play central roles in detoxification and intermediary metabolism.

Function

The best-known role of ALDH enzymes is in alcohol metabolism: ethanol is first converted by alcohol dehydrogenase to toxic acetaldehyde, which mitochondrial ALDH2 then oxidises to harmless acetate. ALDH enzymes also process retinaldehyde in vitamin A signalling, neurotransmitter-derived aldehydes, and lipid-peroxidation products.

Human genetics

Humans have 19 functional ALDH genes. A common polymorphism in ALDH2 (the ALDH2*2 allele), prevalent in East Asian populations, greatly reduces enzyme activity and causes the alcohol flush reaction — facial flushing, nausea and tachycardia after drinking — because acetaldehyde accumulates. The drug disulfiram, used to treat alcohol dependence, works by inhibiting aldehyde dehydrogenase.

Clinical significance

Defects in various ALDH genes cause metabolic disorders, including Sjögren–Larsson syndrome (ALDH3A2) and type II hyperprolinemia. Elevated ALDH activity is also a marker of certain cancer stem cells and contributes to resistance to some chemotherapy drugs such as cyclophosphamide.

Classes

ALDH enzymes are grouped into families (ALDH1, ALDH2, ALDH3 and so on) based on amino-acid sequence similarity, with members found in the cytosol, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum.
