Tissue engineering

Tissue engineering is a biomedical engineering discipline that combines cells, engineering, materials methods, and suitable biochemical and physicochemical factors to restore, maintain, improve, or replace different types of biological tissues. It most frequently involves the use of cells placed on scaffolds — tissue-supporting structures made of natural or synthetic biomaterials — which guide the formation of new viable tissue for a medical purpose. The term is closely related to, and often used interchangeably with, regenerative medicine.


Cells seeded onto a scaffold in tissue engineering

Overview

The classic strategy, articulated by Robert Langer and Joseph Vacanti in a widely cited 1993 paper, is to isolate cells from a patient, seed them onto a biodegradable scaffold shaped like the desired tissue, allow the construct to mature in a bioreactor, and implant it so that the scaffold is gradually replaced by natural tissue. Stem cells, including embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells, are attractive cell sources because of their capacity to proliferate and differentiate.

Scaffolds and biomaterials

Scaffolds may be made from natural materials such as collagen, alginate and decellularised extracellular matrix, or from synthetic polymers such as polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA) and polycaprolactone. Fabrication methods include electrospinning, freeze-drying, and 3D bioprinting, in which cells and hydrogels ("bioinks") are deposited layer by layer.

Applications

Clinically established tissue-engineered products include artificial skin for burns and chronic wounds, cartilage repair constructs, and engineered bladder tissue. Research targets include heart valves, blood vessels, liver, bone, cornea and whole organs. Major challenges include achieving adequate vascularisation of thick tissues, immune compatibility, and scalable, reproducible manufacture.
