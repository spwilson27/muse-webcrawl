Reginald Punnett

Reginald Crundall Punnett FRS (20 June 1875 – 3 January 1967) was a British geneticist who, together with William Bateson, helped establish the new science of genetics at the University of Cambridge following the rediscovery of Mendel's laws in 1900. Punnett devised the Punnett square, the grid diagram still used in every introductory genetics course to predict the outcomes of genetic crosses, and wrote Mendelism (1905), the first textbook of the subject.

Life and career

Educated at Clifton College and Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, where he read natural sciences, Punnett began as a zoologist, working on nemertean worms and holding a post at St Andrews before returning to Cambridge in 1902. There he joined Bateson's circle of early Mendelians. With Bateson and others he reported some of the earliest evidence of genetic linkage (in sweet peas, 1905–1906) and of gene interaction (comb shape in chickens, explained by epistasis). In 1910 he succeeded Bateson in the quickening institutionalisation of genetics, becoming the first Arthur Balfour Professor of Genetics in 1912, a chair he held until 1940. He co-founded the Journal of Genetics with Bateson in 1910.

Contributions

The Punnett square

The square first appeared in the second edition of Mendelism (1907): the gametes of each parent are arrayed along two sides of a grid, and the cells of the grid give every combination in its expected proportion, making segregation and independent assortment visually immediate.

Hardy–Weinberg and poultry genetics

At a famous 1908 meeting, Punnett posed the puzzle of why a dominant trait (brachydactyly) does not drive out its recessive counterpart; the mathematician G. H. Hardy, prompted by the conversation, published the celebrated letter deriving the stable genotype frequencies now known as the Hardy–Weinberg principle. Punnett's applied work produced auto-sexing chicken breeds — the Cambar (1929), created with Michael Pease, and later the Legbar — in which day-old chicks can be sexed by down colour, of great value to poultry farmers. He also applied Mendelian methods to butterflies, including mimicry in Papilio.

Later life

Punnett retired to Somerset in 1940 and died in 1967 at the age of 91. His genetics library and papers are held at Cambridge. The Punnett square and his textbooks — Mendelism ran to many editions — remain his most visible legacy.
