Poetic realism

Poetic realism (French: réalisme poétique) was a film movement in French cinema of the 1930s and early 1940s. More a tendency than a codified school, it combined working-class subject matter and fatalistic, often tragic storylines with a lyrical, studio-crafted visual style. Its films typically followed doomed lovers, criminals, deserters, and other marginal figures, rendered with atmospheric sets, fog, and an elegiac mood that reflected the anxieties of France in the years before the Second World War.

Characteristics

Poetic realist films emphasized mood and atmosphere through elaborate set design — notably the studio-built streets and docks of production designer Alexandre Trauner — and the diffuse cinematography of cameramen such as Eugen Schüfftan and Curt Courant. Rather than the documentary location shooting of later neorealism, poetic realism stylized reality: its Parisian faubourgs, ports, and cheap hotels were lovingly recreated in the studio and suffused with a sense of inevitable doom. Themes included fatal love, betrayal, nostalgia for an unattainable elsewhere, and the crushing weight of fate.

Key figures and films

The movement is associated above all with director Jean Renoir (La Bête humaine, 1938), Marcel Carné (Port of Shadows / Le Quai des brumes, 1938; Le Jour se lève, 1939; Children of Paradise, 1945), and Julien Duvivier (Pépé le Moko, 1937). The screenwriter Jacques Prévert supplied many of its defining scripts, and actor Jean Gabin became its emblematic star, often playing the trapped, doomed working-class hero. Other contributors included Jean Vigo (L'Atalante, 1934), whose work anticipated the movement.

Legacy

Poetic realism influenced American film noir, with its fatalism, shadowy urban settings, and antiheroes, and it helped shape Italian neorealism, whose directors admired its focus on ordinary people despite its studio artifice. Several poetic realist films, including Children of Paradise, are routinely ranked among the greatest films ever made.
