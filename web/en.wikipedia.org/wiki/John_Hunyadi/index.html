John Hunyadi (Hungarian: Hunyadi János; Romanian: Ioan de Hunedoara; c. 1406 – 11 August 1456) was a leading Hungarian military and political figure during the 15th century. As voivode of Transylvania, regent-governor of Hungary, and captain-general of the kingdom's forces, he led a series of campaigns against the expanding Ottoman Empire and became the most celebrated Christian commander of his age in southeastern Europe.

Early life and rise

Hunyadi was the son of Voyk, a knight of Wallachian origin whom King Sigismund granted the estate of Hunyad (Hunedoara) in 1409. He spent his youth in the service of several magnates and rulers, learning the art of war in Italy and in Sigismund's wars against the Hussites and the Ottomans. By the early 1440s he had become voivode of Transylvania and count of the Székelys, building a power base on the kingdom's threatened southern frontier.

Wars against the Ottomans

Beginning in 1441, Hunyadi won a string of victories over Ottoman raiding forces, raiding deep into Serbia and Bulgaria during the "Long Campaign" of 1443–44. He fought at the catastrophic Battle of Varna (1444), where King Władysław III of Poland and Hungary was killed, and at the Second Battle of Kosovo (1448), which ended in defeat. After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, Sultan Mehmed II turned against Belgrade, the key fortress guarding Hungary. In July 1456, Hunyadi, reinforced by crusaders preached by the Franciscan friar John of Capistrano, decisively repelled the Ottoman siege at the Siege of Belgrade — a victory Pope Callixtus III commemorated by ordering the ringing of church bells at noon, a custom that survives today.

Political career

In 1446 the Diet elected Hunyadi governor (regent) of Hungary during the minority of King Ladislaus the Posthumous, a post he held until 1453. He struggled against rival baronial leagues, particularly the counts of Celje, while continuing to direct the kingdom's defense. His immense wealth — he was Hungary's largest landowner — funded a standing force of mercenaries that formed the nucleus of his son's later Black Army.

Death and legacy

Weeks after his triumph at Belgrade, Hunyadi died of plague in the crusader camp at Zimony on 11 August 1456. He was buried in Alba Iulia Cathedral. His younger son, Matthias Corvinus, was elected king of Hungary in 1458. Hunyadi remains a national hero in both Hungary and Romania; statues of him stand in Budapest's Heroes' Square, at Hunedoara Castle, and in Belgrade (Zemun).
