Artificial ventilation

A bag valve mask used for manual artificial ventilation

Artificial ventilation, also called artificial respiration, is the act of assisting or stimulating respiration — moving air (or oxygen-enriched gas) into and out of the lungs of a person who is not breathing, or not breathing adequately. It may be performed manually, as in mouth-to-mouth resuscitation or with a bag valve mask, or mechanically by a ventilator, and it is a core component of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and of intensive care medicine.

Manual methods

First-aid techniques include mouth-to-mouth and mouth-to-nose ventilation, in which the rescuer exhales into the casualty's airway, and mechanical manual devices: the bag valve mask (BVM), squeezed by hand, and pocket masks with one-way valves that protect the rescuer. Historically, methods such as the Silvester and Holger Nielsen techniques manipulated the arms and chest; these are now obsolete in favour of positive-pressure methods combined with chest compressions.

Mechanical ventilation

In hospitals, ventilators deliver measured breaths through an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy (invasive ventilation) or through a tight-fitting mask (non-invasive ventilation, such as CPAP and BiPAP). Modern machines offer many modes — volume-controlled, pressure-controlled, pressure-support and others — and clinicians balance oxygenation against the risks of ventilator-induced lung injury, barotrauma and infection. Negative-pressure devices such as the iron lung, once vital in polio epidemics, enclose the body and cyclically lower the pressure around the chest.

Indications

Artificial ventilation is used in respiratory failure, cardiac arrest, general anaesthesia, severe head injury, drug overdose and neuromuscular disease, among many other conditions. Weaning — gradually withdrawing support as the patient's own breathing recovers — is an important part of care.
