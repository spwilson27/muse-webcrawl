Nanquan (martial art)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nanquan (Chinese: 南拳; pinyin: Nánquán; lit. 'southern fist') refers to a family of Chinese martial arts that originated and developed south of the Yangtze River in China, and the modern competitive wushu discipline derived from them. It is contrasted with the northern styles (Beiquan, "northern fist") celebrated in the saying "Southern fists and Northern legs" (南拳北腿, Nánquán Běituǐ).

Characteristics

Southern styles are known for low, stable stances, powerful short-range arm techniques, intricate hand methods, and the deliberate vocalisation of shouts (fasheng) to coordinate breath with strikes. Because southern practitioners historically fought in confined spaces such as boats and narrow alleys — and in the humid rice-paddy country of Fujian, Guangdong, and Guangxi — the styles emphasise a solid root, a tight guard, and economical footwork rather than the high kicks and long leaps of northern schools.

Styles

Major traditional Nanquan styles include the five great family styles of Guangdong: Hung Ga (Hongjia), Lau Ga, Choy Gar, Lee Gar, and Mok Gar, along with Choy Li Fut, Wing Chun, Fujian White Crane, Five Ancestors (Wuzuquan), Hakka styles such as Southern Praying Mantis, and Fujian dog boxing (Dishuquanfa).

Modern wushu Nanquan

In contemporary sport wushu, Nanquan is one of the principal taolu (forms) events, codified from the traditional southern styles, along with compulsory routines for the southern broadsword (Nandao) and southern staff (Nangun) added in the 1990s. Its competition choreography features low stances, tiger claw hands, bridge hands, loud shouts, and powerful, compact hand strikes. Nanquan is contested at the Asian Games and the World Wushu Championships.
