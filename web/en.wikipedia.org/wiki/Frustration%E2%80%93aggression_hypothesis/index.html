Frustration–aggression hypothesis

The frustration–aggression hypothesis, also called the frustration–aggression–displacement theory, is a theory of aggression proposed in 1939 by John Dollard, Neal Miller, Leonard Doob, Orval Mowrer and Robert Sears of Yale University. It holds that aggression is the result of frustration — the blocking of an individual's efforts to attain a goal.

Original formulation

The original monograph Frustration and Aggression (1939) made the strong claims that frustration always leads to some form of aggression and that aggression always presupposes frustration. When the source of frustration cannot be challenged directly, aggression may be displaced onto an innocent target, a mechanism invoked in explanations of scapegoating.

Revisions

Later research softened the theory: Miller himself noted in 1941 that frustration produces instigations to several kinds of behaviour, not only aggression. Leonard Berkowitz's reformulation proposed that frustration creates a readiness for aggression through negative affect, which aggressive cues can then trigger. The hypothesis remains influential in social psychology despite criticism of its original strong form.
