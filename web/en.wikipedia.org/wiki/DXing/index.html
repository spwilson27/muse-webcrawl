DXing

DXing, taken from DX, the telegraphic shorthand for "distance" or "distant", is the hobby of receiving and identifying distant radio or television signals, or making two-way radio contact with distant stations in amateur radio, citizens band radio, or other two-way radio communications. Many DXers also attempt to obtain written verifications of reception or contact, known as QSL cards. The practice arose in the early days of radio broadcasting.

History

Early radio listeners in the 1920s competed to log distant AM broadcast stations, mailing reception reports to stations in exchange for verification cards. Shortwave listening grew between the world wars as international broadcasters such as the BBC World Service and Voice of America filled the high-frequency bands. The hobby expanded after the Second World War with surplus receivers and the growth of amateur radio, and clubs and magazines devoted to propagation forecasts and station schedules flourished.

Types of DXing

  • Medium wave (AM) DXing exploits night-time skywave propagation to hear stations thousands of kilometres away.
  • Shortwave DXing targets international broadcasters and utility stations on the HF bands.
  • FM and TV DXing relies on sporadic E, tropospheric ducting, and meteor scatter to receive VHF signals far beyond line of sight.
  • Amateur radio DXing involves contacting distant stations, often to earn awards such as the DX Century Club (DXCC) for confirmed contact with at least 100 countries or territories.
  • Weather satellite and scanner listening are related pursuits pursued by some DXers.

Equipment and technique

Successful DXing depends on sensitive receivers, directional antennas such as Beverage and loop antennas for medium wave, and a knowledge of radio propagation, including the effects of the ionosphere, solar activity, and the seasons. Listeners keep logbooks recording frequency, time (usually in UTC), signal quality using the SINPO code, and programme details to support reception reports. Software-defined radio has transformed the hobby, allowing wideband recording of entire bands for later analysis.
