Tankette

A tankette is a tracked armoured fighting vehicle that resembles a small tank, roughly the size of a car, mostly intended for light infantry support and scouting. It is mainly designed for portability and produces a short stature and cannot be fitted with a large-calibre gun. Tankettes were common in the interwar period and saw limited combat use in World War II.

History

The concept arose after World War I from designs such as the British Carden Loyd tankette, developed by Major Giffard Le Quesne Martel and the Carden-Loyd company (later Vickers-Armstrongs). The Carden Loyd Mark VI of 1928 was exported widely and inspired foreign copies and derivatives, including the Soviet T-27, the Polish TKS, the Italian CV-33 and CV-35 (L3 series), the Japanese Type 94, and the French Renault UE, though the UE was used chiefly as an armoured tractor.

Characteristics

Tankettes typically carried a crew of one or two, armour of only a few millimetres to about 15 mm, and armament of one or two machine guns (or, in upgraded variants, a 20 mm cannon). They were cheap to produce in large numbers, which attracted armies with limited budgets, but combat in Spain, Poland, and elsewhere in 1939–1941 demonstrated their vulnerability to anti-tank weapons, mines, and even heavy machine guns.

Decline

By the mid-war years the tankette concept was largely abandoned in favour of light tanks, armoured cars, and universal carriers. Nevertheless, small tracked reconnaissance vehicles with a similar philosophy reappeared later, such as the German Wiesel of the Cold War era.
