Bilateral filter

A bilateral filter is a non-linear, edge-preserving smoothing filter for images. It replaces each pixel's intensity with a weighted average of nearby pixels, where the weights depend not only on spatial distance (as in Gaussian blur) but also on the difference in intensity or colour. Consequently, pixels across a sharp edge contribute little to one another, so noise is reduced while edges remain crisp. The filter was popularised by Carlo Tomasi and Roberto Manduchi in 1998.

Bilateral filtering smooths flat regions but keeps edges sharp.

Definition

The filtered output at a pixel is a normalised weighted sum of neighbouring intensities. A spatial Gaussian kernel supplies weights decreasing with distance, and a range Gaussian supplies weights decreasing with photometric difference. Two parameters — the spatial and range standard deviations — control the amount of smoothing and the sensitivity to edges.

Properties and applications

The bilateral filter is simple to implement and is related to anisotropic diffusion, robust estimation and non-local means. It is used in denoising, HDR tone mapping (as a base/detail decomposition), texture removal, flash/no-flash photography, and medical imaging. Its main drawback is computational cost, which has motivated many fast approximations, including the bilateral grid and permutohedral lattice.
