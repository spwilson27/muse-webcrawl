The Mosin–Nagant is a five-shot, bolt-action, internal magazine–fed military rifle developed from 1882 to 1891 in the Russian Empire and adopted as the 3-line rifle M1891 (its calibre, three "lines", being .30 calibre, 7.62 mm). It combined the design of Russian officer Sergei Ivanovich Mosin with features of the Belgian designer Léon Nagant's entry, after trials of both. It went on to serve the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union, and many other states through both world wars, Korea, Vietnam, and beyond, and remains in use in conflicts in the 21st century.

Design

The rifle fires the rimmed 7.62×54mmR cartridge — among the oldest military cartridges still in service — from a five-round fixed magazine loaded by stripper clips or single rounds. Its bolt uses a two-piece design with a separate bolt head; the safety is engaged by pulling back and rotating the cocking piece. A socket bayonet was standard, and the rifles were sighted with the bayonet fitted.

Variants

The Soviet Model 91/30, simplified for mass production, became the standard Red Army rifle of the Second World War, produced in the tens of millions; a sniper version with a turned-down bolt handle and PU or PEM scope was used by famous snipers including Vasily Zaitsev and Lyudmila Pavlichenko. Short carbines (M38, M44 — the latter with a permanently attached folding bayonet) followed. Licensed or copied versions were made in Finland (including the refined M39), China (Type 53), and other Eastern Bloc states. Early production shortfalls led Imperial Russia to contract American firms Remington and Westinghouse to build over a million rifles during the First World War.

Legacy

With total production around 37 million, the Mosin–Nagant is among the most mass-produced military bolt-action rifles in history. Vast surplus stocks made it a staple of the civilian collectors' and hunting markets, and its 7.62×54mmR cartridge continues in service in machine guns and sniper rifles such as the Dragunov SVD.
