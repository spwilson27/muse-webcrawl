Gram stain

Gram stain (Gram staining or Gram's method) is a method of staining used to classify bacterial species into two large groups: Gram-positive bacteria and Gram-negative bacteria. It is named after the Danish bacteriologist Hans Christian Gram, who devised the technique in 1884.

Gram stain
InventorHans Christian Gram (1884)
Primary stainCrystal violet
CounterstainSafranin
UseBacterial classification and diagnosis

Procedure

A bacterial smear is heat-fixed, flooded with crystal violet, then treated with Gram's iodine as a mordant. A decoloriser such as ethanol or acetone is applied briefly, and the slide is counterstained with safranin. Gram-positive cells, whose thick peptidoglycan wall retains the crystal violet–iodine complex, appear purple; Gram-negative cells lose the primary stain and take up the pink counterstain.

Interpretation

The Gram stain is usually the first test performed on clinical specimens and bacterial cultures. Combined with cell shape (cocci, bacilli, and so on), it guides early empirical antibiotic therapy while definitive identification is pending. Some organisms, such as Mycobacterium, stain poorly by this method and require acid-fast techniques instead.
