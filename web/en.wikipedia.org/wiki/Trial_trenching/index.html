Trial trenching

Trial trenching is a method of archaeological evaluation used to assess the presence, character, extent, and significance of buried archaeological remains on a site, most often in advance of proposed development. It is one of the standard intrusive techniques of archaeological field evaluation, alongside test pits and boreholes.

Method

A trial trenching programme typically excavates a sample of trenches — commonly around 2–5 percent of the development area — laid out to test both areas of known potential and apparently blank zones. Trenches are usually opened by machine under archaeological supervision to the top of the archaeological horizon or natural geology, then cleaned, planned, and sampled by hand. Features are excavated sufficiently to establish their date and nature without being fully dug out.

Purpose

The results inform planning decisions: whether significant remains can be preserved in situ, whether further excavation (such as a strip, map and record exercise or a full excavation) is required, or whether no further archaeological work is needed. Trial trenching is widely used in commercial archaeology in the United Kingdom and elsewhere as part of the planning process.
