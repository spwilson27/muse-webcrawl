Neo-Luddism

Neo-Luddites take inspiration from the 19th-century Luddites who destroyed textile machinery.

Neo-Luddism or new Luddism is a philosophy opposing many forms of modern technology, or fearing its societal impact. Taking its name from the English Luddites of 1811–1816, who destroyed mechanized looms that threatened their livelihoods, neo-Luddism is generally critical of the effects of technology on individuals, communities, and the environment, and advocates a skeptical, precautionary approach to technological change.

Origins and ideas

The modern movement coalesced in the late twentieth century. Chellis Glendinning's 1990 "Notes toward a Neo-Luddite Manifesto" argued that new technologies, created and controlled by elites, serve their creators more than the public and cause social and ecological harm. Kirkpatrick Sale's Rebels Against the Future (1995) reappraised the original Luddites as defenders of community against industrialization. Related ideas appear in the work of critics such as Theodore Roszak, Jerry Mander, and Neil Postman, who questioned the assumption that technological progress is automatically beneficial.

Neo-Luddite critiques commonly target computers and the internet, nuclear energy, genetic engineering, nanotechnology, and artificial intelligence, focusing on issues such as job displacement, surveillance, deskilling, environmental damage, and the concentration of power in technology corporations. Some neo-Luddites advocate minimal technology use or withdrawal from technological society; others support democratic assessment and regulation of new technologies rather than total rejection.

Distinctions

Neo-Luddism overlaps with anarcho-primitivism, deep ecology, and degrowth, but is broader and generally less doctrinaire. The term "Luddite" is also used pejoratively to describe anyone resistant to new technology, a usage neo-Luddites dispute, pointing out that the historical Luddites opposed specific machinery used to degrade labor rather than technology in itself. The violent anti-technology campaign of Theodore Kaczynski (the Unabomber) is generally disavowed by neo-Luddite writers, though his manifesto Industrial Society and Its Future (1995) is sometimes discussed in this context.
