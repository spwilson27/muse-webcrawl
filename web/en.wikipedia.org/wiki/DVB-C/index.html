DVB-C

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

DVB-C is the DVB European consortium standard for the broadcast transmission of digital television over cable. The standard, formally designated EN 300 429 by the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI), describes a system which uses the same transmission infrastructure as analogue cable television but carries digitally modulated video, audio and data services, principally in MPEG transport streams.

DVB-C is part of the Digital Video Broadcasting family of standards, alongside DVB-S for satellite and DVB-T for terrestrial transmission. Although technically similar in many respects, the cable variant was designed for the comparatively clean, interference-free environment of a hybrid fibre-coaxial (HFC) cable network, allowing it to use higher-order modulation schemes than would be practical over the air.

Technical description

The DVB-C system adapts the MPEG-2 (and later MPEG-4/HEVC) transport stream to the characteristics of a cable channel. Key features of the physical layer include:

  • Modulation: Quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM), typically 64-QAM or 256-QAM, with optional 16-QAM and 32-QAM modes.
  • Channel bandwidth: Designed for 8 MHz channels in Europe (with 6 MHz variants used elsewhere), fitting the existing cable channel raster.
  • Error correction: Reed–Solomon forward error correction inherited from the common DVB framing structure.
  • Symbol rates: Variable, commonly up to about 6.9 megabaud in an 8 MHz channel, giving net data rates approaching 38–50 Mbit/s with 256-QAM.

Because cable networks provide a high signal-to-noise ratio and negligible multipath propagation, DVB-C dispenses with the complex guard intervals and robust low-order modulation used by terrestrial DVB-T.

Deployment

DVB-C has been widely adopted by cable operators in Europe, Asia and parts of Latin America, either as the sole digital standard or alongside proprietary and DOCSIS-based services. It is received by set-top boxes and by television sets with integrated DVB-C tuners, often in combination with a conditional-access module (CAM) and smart card for encrypted pay-television channels.

DVB-C2

A second-generation successor, DVB-C2 (EN 302 769), was published in 2009. It introduces LDPC forward error correction and OFDM-like techniques adapted to cable, offering up to roughly 80% greater capacity than DVB-C under equal conditions. Adoption of DVB-C2 has been slower than that of its terrestrial and satellite counterparts, in part because the capacity of existing DVB-C networks remained sufficient for many operators.
