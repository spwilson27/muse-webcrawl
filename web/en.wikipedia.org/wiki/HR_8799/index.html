HR 8799 is a roughly 30-million-year-old main-sequence star located 133 light-years away from Earth in the constellation of Pegasus. It has roughly 1.5 times the Sun's mass. HR 8799 is famous as the first star around which multiple exoplanets were directly imaged: in 2008 astronomers announced the direct images of three massive planets (b, c and d), and a fourth, HR 8799 e, closer to the star, followed in 2010. The system also hosts a debris disk, and an additional planet candidate orbiting interior to e has been reported.

The planets

The four confirmed planets are gas giants of roughly 5–10 Jupiter masses, orbiting at projected separations of about 15 to 70 astronomical units. Their masses make them near the boundary between planets and brown dwarfs, and their formation — whether by core accretion or gravitational instability — remains debated. HR 8799 b, c and d were announced in November 2008 by a team led by Christian Marois, using images from the Keck and Gemini North telescopes; the discovery was announced simultaneously with the direct image of Fomalhaut b. High-contrast imaging instruments including the Gemini Planet Imager, SPHERE, and the JWST have since characterised the planets' atmospheres, which show clouds and disequilibrium chemistry; traces of carbon monoxide, water and silicate clouds have been reported.

The star and disk

HR 8799 is a Lambda Boötis star, showing unusual surface abundances, and is a variable star of the Gamma Doradus type. Infrared observations with the Spitzer and Herschel space telescopes revealed a complex debris disk system: a warm inner belt of planetesimals, a broad cold outer disk extending to roughly 2,000 AU, and an extended halo.

Significance

The HR 8799 system remains one of the most important laboratories for the direct study of exoplanet atmospheres and system architectures resembling a scaled-up version of the outer Solar System. It demonstrated that direct imaging could open exoplanets to spectroscopic study.
