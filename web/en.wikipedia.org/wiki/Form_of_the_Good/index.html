Form of the Good

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Form of the Good (Ancient Greek: ἡ τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ ἰδέα) is the highest of the Forms in the philosophy of Plato, introduced principally in the Republic. In Plato's theory of Forms, everything that exists participates in eternal, unchanging archetypes; the Form of the Good stands above all others, including the Forms of Justice and Beauty, and is the ultimate source of truth, intelligibility and value. Plato compares it to the sun: just as the sun makes visible things visible and gives them growth and nourishment, the Good makes intelligible things knowable and confers being upon them.

In the Republic

In Book VI of the Republic, Socrates declines to define the Good directly and instead offers the analogy of the sun, which is elaborated in the allegory of the cave and the divided line in Books VI and VII. The Good is described as "beyond being" (epekeina tēs ousias), exceeding it in dignity and power. Knowledge of the Good is the culmination of the education of the philosopher-rulers, who must ascend from the cave of appearances to contemplate it and then return to govern the city in its light.

Interpretation

Scholars disagree about the precise nature of the Form of the Good: whether it is a metaphysical entity, a principle of order and intelligibility, or something approaching a god. Aristotle criticised the Platonic separation of the Good from particular goods, arguing that "good" is said in many ways. Neoplatonists such as Plotinus developed the idea into the doctrine of the One, the ineffable first principle from which all reality emanates, profoundly influencing later Christian, Jewish and Islamic theology.
