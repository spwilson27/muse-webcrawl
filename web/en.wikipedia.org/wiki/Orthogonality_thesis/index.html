Orthogonality thesis

The orthogonality thesis is a proposition in the philosophy of artificial intelligence, articulated most prominently by philosopher Nick Bostrom, holding that an agent's intelligence and its final goals can vary independently of each other: an agent can in principle be arbitrarily intelligent while pursuing almost any goal, including trivial or destructive ones. Intelligence and goals are "orthogonal axes" along which a mind can be freely varied.

Relation to AI safety

Together with the instrumental convergence thesis — that agents with very different final goals will predictably pursue shared instrumental goals such as self-preservation and resource acquisition — the orthogonality thesis is used to argue that an advanced artificial general intelligence cannot be assumed to share human values, and that aligning AI systems' goals with human interests is a distinct technical problem that does not solve itself as systems become more capable.

Criticism

Critics argue that certain goals may be incoherent or self-defeating for sufficiently intelligent agents, or that moral truths, if cognitively accessible, might constrain what a highly intelligent agent pursues. Defenders respond that the thesis concerns the space of possible minds in principle rather than the likely design of any particular system.
