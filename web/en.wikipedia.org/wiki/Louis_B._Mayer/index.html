Louis B. Mayer

Louis Burt Mayer (born Lazar Meir; July 12, 1884 – October 29, 1957) was a Canadian-American film producer and co-founder of Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer (MGM) studios. Generally cited as the creator of the "star system" within the movie industry, Mayer was the highest-paid man in America during the late 1930s and the dominant figure of MGM's golden age, overseeing a studio famed for prestige pictures and the slogan "more stars than there are in the heavens."

Early life

Mayer was born to a Jewish family in Minsk, then part of the Russian Empire. The family emigrated to Saint John, New Brunswick, Canada, when he was a child. He grew up in poverty, left school young, and worked in his father's scrap metal business. In 1904 he moved to Massachusetts, where he continued in the scrap trade before entering show business.

Early film career

In 1907 Mayer bought and renovated a run-down burlesque theatre in Haverhill, Massachusetts, turning it into a successful nickelodeon. He expanded into a chain of theatres across New England and, in 1916, entered film distribution and production. Moving to Los Angeles in 1918, he founded Louis B. Mayer Pictures and produced popular serials and melodramas, signing stars such as Anita Stewart.

Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer

In 1924 theatre magnate Marcus Loew merged Metro Pictures, Goldwyn Pictures and Louis B. Mayer Pictures to form Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, with Mayer as studio head and Irving Thalberg as head of production. Under Mayer's management MGM became the most profitable and glamorous of the Hollywood studios, home to stars including Greta Garbo, Clark Gable, Spencer Tracy, Katharine Hepburn, Judy Garland and Mickey Rooney. Mayer championed wholesome, family-oriented entertainment, famously saying that he wanted films that would not embarrass a mother or a child.

Mayer was a co-founder of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences in 1927. He was also a powerful figure in the Republican Party in California. After a long power struggle, and as the studio system declined under pressure from television and antitrust rulings, Mayer was forced out of MGM in 1951 and replaced by Dore Schary.

Death and legacy

Mayer died of leukemia in Los Angeles on October 29, 1957. He is widely regarded as the archetype of the Hollywood studio mogul: autocratic, sentimental, and shrewd, he built MGM into the premier studio of Hollywood's golden age. His daughter Irene Mayer Selznick became a noted theatrical producer.
