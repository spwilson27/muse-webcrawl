Silicification

In geology, silicification is the process by which organic or mineral material is infiltrated, replaced, or encrusted by silica (silicon dioxide, SiO2), usually in the form of chalcedony, opal, or quartz. It is a common mode of fossilisation and of hydrothermal alteration of rocks.

Process

Silica-rich waters — derived from volcanic ash, hot springs, groundwater, or the dissolution of siliceous organisms such as sponges and diatoms — percolate through sediments or buried organisms. As conditions change, silica precipitates, filling pore spaces (permineralisation) and progressively replacing the original material molecule by molecule. Because precipitation can be extremely fine-grained, silicified fossils often preserve cellular detail.

Examples

Famous silicified deposits include the petrified forests of Arizona, where logs were replaced by colourful chalcedony and jasper; the Rhynie chert of Scotland, which preserves an Early Devonian land ecosystem in exquisite detail; and silicified limestones and chert nodules in flint-bearing chalks. Silicification also produces jasper, agate, and silicified ("opalised") fossils such as those from the Australian opal fields.
