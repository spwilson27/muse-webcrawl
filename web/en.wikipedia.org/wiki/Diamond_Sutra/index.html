The Diamond Sutra (Sanskrit: Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra) is a Mahayana Buddhist sutra from the genre of Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom") sutras. Translated into a variety of languages over a broad geographic range, the Diamond Sutra is one of the most influential Mahayana scriptures in East Asia, and it is particularly prominent within Zen Buddhism.

Contents

The sutra takes the form of a dialogue between the Buddha and the monk Subhuti in the Jetavana gardens at Sravasti. Its central themes are emptiness (śūnyatā), non-attachment, and the illusory nature of perceived reality: the Buddha teaches that one should develop a mind that abides nowhere, cultivate compassion without clinging to notions of self, other, or being, and understand that all conditioned phenomena are "like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow."

The Dunhuang copy

A copy of the Diamond Sutra printed in Tang dynasty China is the world's earliest complete survival of a dated printed book, dated to 11 May 868 CE. It was discovered in 1900 by the Daoist monk Wang Yuanlu among the Dunhuang manuscripts in the Mogao Caves and was acquired by the archaeologist Aurel Stein in 1907; it is now held by the British Library. The colophon records that it was printed by one Wang Jie for free distribution on behalf of his parents.

Title

The Sanskrit title, Vajracchedikā, means "the diamond that cuts through illusion" — the vajra (diamond/thunderbolt) symbolising a wisdom that cuts through delusion and leads to the other shore of enlightenment.
