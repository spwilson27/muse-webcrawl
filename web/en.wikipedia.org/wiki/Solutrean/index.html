Solutrean

The Solutrean industry is a relatively advanced flint tool-making style of the European Upper Paleolithic, dating to roughly 22,000 to 17,000 years ago, during and after the Last Glacial Maximum. Named after the type site of Solutré near Mâcon in eastern France, the culture is found mainly in France and the Iberian Peninsula.

Technology

The Solutrean is famous for its finely made bifacial points, including the elegant "laurel leaf" and "willow leaf" points produced by pressure flaking — among the most accomplished stone tools ever made. Characteristic artifacts also include shouldered points, burins, end scrapers, and some of the earliest eyed needles, which enabled tailored clothing. Solutrean groups produced noteworthy cave art, such as at Cosquer Cave, and likely used the spear-thrower (atlatl).

Context

The Solutrean follows the Gravettian and precedes the Magdalenian. It is often interpreted as an adaptation to the harsh, cold conditions of the glacial maximum, when human populations contracted into southwestern European refugia.

Solutrean hypothesis

A controversial hypothesis proposed that Solutrean seafarers crossed the Atlantic and contributed to the North American Clovis culture. This idea is rejected by most archaeologists and is unsupported by genetic evidence, which links Native American ancestry to Siberian populations.
