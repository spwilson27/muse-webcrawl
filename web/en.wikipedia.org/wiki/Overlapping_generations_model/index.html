Overlapping generations model

The overlapping generations (OLG) model is a framework in macroeconomics in which agents live a finite length of time and generations overlap: at any moment, young and old cohorts coexist and trade with one another. It contrasts with the infinitely-lived representative agent of the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model. The framework originates with Paul Samuelson's 1958 consumption-loan model and was developed extensively by Peter Diamond (1965), who added production and capital accumulation.

The model

In the canonical two-period model, each agent lives for two periods: young (working, saving) and old (retired, dissaving). The young supply labour, consume part of their wage and save the rest — in Samuelson's version, by acquiring money; in Diamond's, by accumulating capital — and consume their savings in old age. Because generations are finite-lived, the economy can have a steady state that is dynamically inefficient: it may over-accumulate capital relative to the golden rule.

Applications

OLG models are the standard tool for analysing pay-as-you-go pension systems and social security, government debt and intergenerational transfers, the role of money and asset-price bubbles, and bequests and education. Samuelson's version showed how fiat money can improve welfare by enabling trade between generations who never meet — a rationale for money that has no intrinsic use.

Key results

Famous results include the possibility of rational bubbles in dynamically inefficient economies, the indeterminacy of equilibrium in some specifications, and a welfare role for government debt: because the old cannot tax the unborn, debt and pay-as-you-go transfers can move the economy toward the golden rule — themes central to modern debates on Ricardian equivalence.
