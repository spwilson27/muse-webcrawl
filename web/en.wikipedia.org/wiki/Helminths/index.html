Helminths

Helminths (from Greek ἕλμινς, helmins, "worm"), also commonly known as parasitic worms, are a group of multicellular organisms that live as parasites within the bodies of other animals, including humans. They are not a single taxonomic group but a collection of unrelated worm-like organisms from several phyla, united by their parasitic lifestyle and similar body form. The study of helminths is called helminthology.

Helminth parasites are divided into three main groups:

  • Nematodes (roundworms), phylum Nematoda — including Ascaris, hookworms, pinworms, and filarial worms.
  • Cestodes (tapeworms), phylum Platyhelminthes — including Taenia and Echinococcus.
  • Trematodes (flukes), phylum Platyhelminthes — including Schistosoma and the liver flukes.

Acanthocephalans (thorny-headed worms) are also sometimes included among the helminths.

Helminthiasis

Infection with helminths is called helminthiasis. Soil-transmitted helminthiases (caused by Ascaris lumbricoides, whipworm, and hookworms) affect more than a billion people worldwide, mainly in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation. Schistosomiasis, caused by blood flukes of the genus Schistosoma, is considered the second most socioeconomically devastating parasitic disease after malaria. Many helminthiases are classified among the neglected tropical diseases.

Helminth infections often cause few symptoms in light cases but can cause malnutrition, anaemia, impaired growth and cognitive development in children, and organ damage in heavy infections. Diagnosis is usually by identification of eggs or larvae in stool or tissue samples. Treatment relies on anthelmintic drugs such as albendazole, mebendazole, and praziquantel, often given in mass drug administration campaigns.

Life cycles

Helminths have a wide variety of life cycles. Some, such as the pinworm Enterobius vermicularis, have direct life cycles transmitted from person to person. Others require one or more intermediate hosts: schistosomes develop in freshwater snails before infecting humans through the skin; tapeworms pass through livestock or fish; and filarial worms are transmitted by biting insects such as mosquitoes and black flies.

Immunology

Helminths are master manipulators of the host immune system, typically inducing a modified type 2 (Th2) immune response and regulatory mechanisms that allow them to persist for years. Chronic helminth infection has been associated with reduced incidence of allergic and autoimmune disease, an observation related to the "hygiene hypothesis" and sometimes called the "old friends" hypothesis. Experimental helminth therapy, using controlled infections with pig whipworm or hookworm, has been investigated for conditions such as Crohn's disease and multiple sclerosis.

Use in medicine and research

Beyond their role as pathogens, helminths and their products are studied as sources of immunomodulatory molecules. In ecology and evolution, helminths are also model organisms for studying host–parasite coevolution.
