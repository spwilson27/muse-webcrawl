The Three Little Pigs is a fable and fairy tale about three pigs who build houses of straw, sticks and bricks, and a wolf who tries to catch them. The tale was first published in print in 1843 by James Halliwell-Phillipps in his collection Nursery Rhymes and Nursery Tales, and reached its widest audience through Joseph Jacobs's retelling in English Fairy Tales (1890). Its moral — that foresight, diligence and hard work are rewarded — made it one of the best-known children's stories in the English-speaking world.

Plot

Three little pigs set out to build their own houses. The first pig builds quickly and cheaply out of straw and goes off to play. The second builds of sticks (or furze), little stronger. The third pig labours to build a solid house of bricks. A big bad wolf comes to the straw house and demands entry:

"Little pig, little pig, let me come in."
"Not by the hair on my chinny chin chin."
"Then I'll huff, and I'll puff, and I'll blow your house in!"

The wolf blows down the straw house and (in the older versions) eats the first pig; the same happens at the house of sticks. At the brick house his blowing fails. In Jacobs's version the wolf then tries to outwit the third pig with appointments to fetch turnips, apples and go to a fair, but the pig outwits him each time; finally the wolf climbs down the chimney and lands in a pot of boiling water, and the pig eats him for supper. Later, softened retellings allow the first two pigs to escape to their brother's house and let the wolf flee with a singed tail instead.

Origins and analysis

The story is classified in the Aarne–Thompson–Uther index as type 124, "Blowing the House In". Although its print history begins in the nineteenth century, the tale has the repetitive, formulaic structure of an orally transmitted folk tale, and some elements — such as the dialogue by the door — parallel older animal tales. The contrast between the three houses embodies a simple moral arithmetic: ease and haste end in disaster, while effort produces security.

In popular culture

Walt Disney's Silly Symphony cartoon Three Little Pigs (1933) became one of the most successful animated shorts of its era; its song "Who's Afraid of the Big Bad Wolf?" turned into a defiant anthem during the Great Depression. The characters went on to appear in further Disney shorts and comics. The phrase "by the hair of my chinny chin chin" and the wolf's "I'll huff and I'll puff" have passed into everyday idiom, and the story has been retold and parodied countless times, including versions told from the wolf's point of view.
