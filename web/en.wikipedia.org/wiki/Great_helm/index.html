Great helm

The great helm or heaume, also called pot helm, bucket helm and barrel helm, is a helmet of the High Middle Ages which arose in the late twelfth century, in the context of the Crusades, and remained in use until the fourteenth century. They were used by knights and heavy infantry in most European armies between about 1220 and 1540.

History

The great helm developed from earlier nasal helmets and the flat-topped helmets of the late 1100s. In its developed form it was a cylinder of steel plates, often reinforced with additional vertical bands, that completely enclosed the head, with narrow slits for the eyes and small holes for breathing. It was worn over a padded coif and often over a mail coif or a smaller inner helmet. Because it rested on the shoulders, it protected the wearer well against lance strikes and sword blows, but limited vision and ventilation.

By the fourteenth century the great helm was increasingly displaced on the battlefield by lighter, close-fitting helmets such as the bascinet, which offered better visibility and movement. The great helm survived in tournaments, where protection mattered more than vision, and in heraldry, where it became the conventional helmet shown above a knight's shield and crest — the helm of the "frog-mouth" type (Stechhelm) being reserved for the joust.

Heraldry

In heraldic depiction, the great helm is one of the most common helmets placed above the escutcheon, often adorned with mantling and a crest. In some heraldic traditions the type and orientation of the helm, and whether it is shown with bars or an open visor, have been used to indicate the rank of the armiger.
