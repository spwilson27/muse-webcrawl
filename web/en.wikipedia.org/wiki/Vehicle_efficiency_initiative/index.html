Vehicle efficiency initiative

A vehicle efficiency initiative is a government or industry programme intended to improve the fuel efficiency (or energy efficiency) of vehicles and thereby reduce fuel consumption, oil dependence and greenhouse gas emissions. Such initiatives combine regulatory standards, consumer information, research support and financial incentives.

Fuel-economy labelling is a common element of efficiency programmes, helping buyers compare vehicles.

Regulatory standards

The best-known example is the United States' Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) programme, created by Congress in 1975 after the 1973 oil crisis, which requires each manufacturer's fleet to meet an average fuel-economy standard. The European Union instead regulates CO2 emissions per kilometre for new cars and vans, with fleet-average targets and excess-emissions premiums; China, Japan, India, Canada and Australia operate similar systems. Heavy-duty vehicles are increasingly covered by separate standards.

Complementary measures

Efficiency initiatives typically also include: standardised test cycles and window-sticker labels (such as the U.S. Monroney label) that disclose consumption to buyers; feebates and tax incentives or penalties tied to efficiency; scrappage schemes that retire old, inefficient vehicles; government-funded research into lightweight materials, aerodynamics, low-rolling-resistance tyres, advanced combustion and electrification; and requirements for government fleets to purchase efficient vehicles.

Effects and debate

Evaluations generally find that standards have raised new-vehicle efficiency substantially, though part of the technical progress has been absorbed by increases in vehicle size, weight and performance. Economists debate the cost-effectiveness of mandates compared with fuel taxes, and the "rebound effect" — drivers travelling more when each mile becomes cheaper — which offsets a modest fraction of the savings. Efficiency programmes are also increasingly intertwined with electrification policy, as electric drivetrains convert energy far more efficiently than internal combustion engines.
