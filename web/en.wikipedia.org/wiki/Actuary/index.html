Actuary

An actuary is a professional who deals with the measurement and management of risk and uncertainty. Using mathematics, statistics, probability, and financial theory, actuaries assess the likelihood of future events and design ways to reduce the financial impact of undesirable events — most prominently in the insurance and pension industries.

Work

Actuaries price insurance policies, calculate the reserves insurers must hold to pay future claims, design pension and retirement plans, and model financial risks for banks, governments, and corporations. Their work involves analyzing historical data, building predictive models, and projecting demographic change, mortality, morbidity, investment returns, and catastrophe losses.

Specialties include life insurance, health insurance, property and casualty (general) insurance, pensions and retirement benefits, enterprise risk management, and increasingly data science and predictive analytics.

Qualification

Becoming a fully credentialed actuary typically requires passing a rigorous series of professional examinations administered by bodies such as the Society of Actuaries and the Casualty Actuarial Society (North America), the Institute and Faculty of Actuaries (United Kingdom), and equivalent organizations worldwide. Credentialing usually begins with exams in probability, financial mathematics, and statistics, followed by advanced fellowship-level examinations that take several years to complete while working.

History

The profession emerged with the development of life insurance in the 17th and 18th centuries, drawing on the mortality tables of John Graunt and Edmond Halley and the mathematics of compound interest. The Equitable Life Assurance Society (1762) employed the first person titled an "actuary," and professional bodies were founded in the 19th century.
