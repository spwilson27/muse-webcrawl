Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982

Section 35, Constitution Act, 1982
Enacted17 April 1982 (s. 35(2) definition amended 1983)
JurisdictionCanada
SubjectRecognition of Aboriginal and treaty rights
Key casesR v Sparrow (1990); Delgamuukw v British Columbia (1997); Haida Nation (2004); Tsilhqot'in Nation (2014)

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 provides constitutional protection to the Aboriginal and treaty rights of the Indigenous peoples of Canada. Its opening subsection states: "The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed." Section 35 falls outside the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms — it is in Part II of the Act — so the rights it protects are not subject to the Charter's section 1 limitations clause or the section 33 notwithstanding clause.

Text and definition

Section 35(2) defines "aboriginal peoples of Canada" as including the Indian (First Nations), Inuit and Métis peoples. A 1983 constitutional amendment added section 35(3), confirming that treaty rights include rights under modern land claims agreements, and section 35(4), guaranteeing the rights equally to male and female persons. The word "existing" was interpreted by the Supreme Court in R v Sparrow (1990) to mean rights not extinguished before 1982; such rights may still be infringed by the Crown, but only where the infringement can be justified under the Sparrow test (valid legislative objective, honour of the Crown, minimal impairment, consultation and compensation).

Interpretation

In R v Van der Peet (1996) the Court held that an Aboriginal right protects practices integral to the distinctive culture of the group prior to European contact. Delgamuukw v British Columbia (1997) recognised Aboriginal title as a right to the land itself. The duty to consult and accommodate, articulated in Haida Nation v British Columbia (2004), arises whenever the Crown contemplates conduct that might adversely affect asserted or established rights. In Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia (2014) the Court issued its first declaration of Aboriginal title. Because section 35 does not define the rights themselves, their content has been developed almost entirely through litigation and negotiation, including modern treaties and self-government agreements.
