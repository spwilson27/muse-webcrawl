Đổi Mới (Vietnamese: [ɗo᷉i mə̌ːi], "Renovation") is the name given to the economic reforms initiated in Vietnam in 1986 with the goal of creating a "socialist-oriented market economy". Formally adopted at the 6th National Congress of the Communist Party of Vietnam in December 1986, the reforms dismantled much of the centrally planned economy, legalised private enterprise, decollectivised agriculture, and opened the country to foreign trade and investment, while the Communist Party retained its monopoly on political power.

Background

After reunification in 1976, Vietnam extended the North's command economy to the South. By the mid-1980s the country faced hyperinflation (peaking above 700 percent), chronic food shortages, a costly occupation of Cambodia, and the collapse of Soviet aid. Local experiments with market mechanisms — "fence-breaking" by factories and farms producing above quota — demonstrated the potential of reform, and the death of conservative leader Lê Duẩn in 1986 opened the way for the reformist Nguyễn Văn Linh to become General Secretary.

Reforms

Key measures included Resolution 10 (1988), which returned land-use rights to farm households and ended collective farming in practice; price liberalisation and the abolition of most subsidies; unification and devaluation of the currency; the 1987 Foreign Investment Law, among the most liberal in Asia at the time; legal recognition of private businesses in the 1990 Company Law and 1992 Constitution; and banking and state-enterprise reform. Vietnam joined ASEAN in 1995, normalised relations with the United States the same year, and acceded to the WTO in 2007.

Results

The reforms transformed Vietnam from a rice importer into one of the world's largest rice exporters within a few years. GDP growth averaged roughly 6–8 percent annually for three decades, and the poverty rate fell from well over half the population in the early 1990s to low single digits, one of the fastest sustained reductions in world poverty on record — at the cost of rising inequality and persistent state-sector inefficiency.
