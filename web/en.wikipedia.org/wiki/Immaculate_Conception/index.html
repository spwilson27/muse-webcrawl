Immaculate Conception

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: The Immaculate Conception by Murillo]

The Immaculate Conception is the belief that the Virgin Mary was free of original sin from the moment of her conception. It is one of the four Marian dogmas of the Catholic Church, solemnly defined by Pope Pius IX in the apostolic constitution Ineffabilis Deus on 8 December 1854.

Doctrine

The dogma holds that Mary, "in view of the merits of Jesus Christ", was preserved exempt from every stain of original sin from the first instant of her conception — a unique grace, not earned by her own merit. It is frequently confused with the virginal conception of Jesus, but concerns Mary's own conception by her parents, traditionally named Joachim and Anne. The feast is celebrated on 8 December, nine months before the feast of Mary's nativity.

History

Devotion developed in the early medieval church and was championed by the Franciscans, notably Duns Scotus, who argued potuit, decuit, ergo fecit ("He could do it, it was fitting, therefore He did it"), against Dominican reservations. The Council of Trent left the question open; papal interventions progressively protected the belief until the 1854 definition. Four years later, the apparitions at Lourdes, where the vision identified herself as "the Immaculate Conception", were widely read as confirming the dogma.

Other traditions

The dogma is not accepted by most Protestant churches, and Eastern Orthodoxy, while venerating Mary as all-holy, rejects the Latin doctrine of original sin on which it rests.
