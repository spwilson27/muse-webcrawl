Cowboy

A cowboy is an animal herder who tends cattle on ranches in North America, traditionally on horseback, and often performs a multitude of other ranch-related tasks. The historic American cowboy of the late 19th century arose from the vaquero traditions of northern Mexico and the southwestern United States, and became a central figure of frontier folklore and the Western genre in literature and film.

History

Cattle ranching spread through Spanish colonial Mexico, where vaqueros developed the skills, tools and vocabulary — much of it borrowed into English, as with lariat (la reata), chaps (chaparreras) and rodeo — later used by Anglo-American cowboys. After the American Civil War, huge herds of Texas longhorns were driven north along trails such as the Chisholm Trail to railheads in Kansas. The era of the long drive ended in the 1880s and 1890s with barbed wire, railways and severe winters.

Modern cowboys and culture

Cowboys still work on ranches today, combining horsemanship with modern vehicles and equipment. The cowboy image — independent, rugged and self-reliant — became a powerful American cultural symbol through dime novels, Wild West shows, rodeo, country music and Hollywood westerns. Women who do the same work are called cowgirls, and analogous herding figures exist worldwide, including the Argentine gaucho, the Australian stockman and the Chilean huaso.
