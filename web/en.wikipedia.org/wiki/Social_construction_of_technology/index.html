Social construction of technology

The social construction of technology (SCOT) is a theory in science and technology studies arguing that technology does not determine human action; rather, human action and social groups shape technology. It was developed principally by Trevor Pinch and Wiebe Bijker in the 1980s, partly in reaction against technological determinism.

Key concepts

  • Relevant social groups — groups that share a meaning of an artefact (e.g., users, producers, opponents).
  • Interpretive flexibility — the same artefact can mean different things to different groups, so its design is open to negotiation.
  • Closure and stabilisation — over time, one interpretation comes to dominate and the design stabilises; closure can occur by redefining the problem the artefact solves.
  • Wider context — later versions of the theory embed the process in political, economic and cultural structures.

The bicycle case study

Pinch and Bijker's study of the bicycle showed that the high-wheeled "ordinary" and the safety bicycle meant different things to different groups — athletic young men, women, and anti-cyclists — and that the eventual dominance of the safety bicycle with pneumatic tyres resulted from social negotiation as much as technical superiority.

Criticism

Critics such as Langdon Winner argued SCOT ignores groups excluded from design and the political consequences of closure. SCOT is closely related to actor-network theory and the social shaping of technology approach.
