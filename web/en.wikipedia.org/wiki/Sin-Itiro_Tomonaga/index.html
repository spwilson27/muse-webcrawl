Tomonaga in the 1950s.

Sin-Itiro Tomonaga (31 March 1906 – 8 July 1979) was a Japanese theoretical physicist and Nobel laureate, a founder of Quantum electrodynamics in parallel with the American school. Working in wartime isolation, Tomonaga developed by 1943 a relativistically covariant formulation of quantum field theory (the Tomonaga–Schwinger equation) and, after the war, his Tokyo group applied renormalisation to QED, computing the Lamb shift consistently — work published in Japanese and little known in the West until Robert Oppenheimer brought it to American attention in 1948.

Tomonaga shared the 1965 Nobel Prize with Julian Schwinger and Richard Feynman. A Kyoto classmate of fellow laureate Hideki Yukawa and a student of Werner Heisenberg's in Leipzig, he also made founding contributions to the theory of one-dimensional quantum liquids (the Tomonaga–Luttinger liquid) and led Japanese physics' postwar reconstruction at the Tokyo University of Education. His lucid essays made him a national figure in Japan.

The covariant formulation

Tomonaga's 1943 paper generalised the Schrödinger equation to an arbitrary spacelike surface — the 'super-many-time theory' — freeing field theory from a preferred time slicing and making relativistic covariance manifest. When American physicists finally read the translated work, Schwinger acknowledged its priority in the formalism, and the synthesis that Freeman Dyson published in 1949 wove the three men's approaches into one.
