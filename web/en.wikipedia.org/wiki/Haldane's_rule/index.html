Haldane's rule

Haldane's rule is an observation in evolutionary biology, formulated in 1922 by the geneticist J. B. S. Haldane, concerning hybrid sterility and inviability: "When in the offspring of two different animal races one sex is absent, rare, or sterile, that sex is the heterozygous [heterogametic] sex." In mammals and fruit flies, where males are XY, hybrid males are affected first; in birds and butterflies, where females are ZW, hybrid females are affected first.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. The dominance theory holds that sterility and inviability factors are often recessive and located on the X (or Z) chromosome, so they are exposed in the heterogametic sex, which carries only one copy. The faster-male theory proposes that male fertility genes evolve rapidly under sexual selection, so hybrid males break down first in XY taxa. The faster-X theory notes that the X chromosome may accumulate hybrid incompatibilities faster than autosomes (the "large X effect"), and meiotic drive and genomic conflict may also contribute. Haldane's rule is now generally understood as a composite phenomenon with different causes in different taxa.

Significance

The rule is one of the most robust empirical patterns in speciation research and a key line of evidence for the role of the sex chromosomes and the Dobzhansky–Muller model of hybrid incompatibility in the origin of species.
