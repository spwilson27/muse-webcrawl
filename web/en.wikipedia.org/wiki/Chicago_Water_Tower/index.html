Chicago Water Tower

The Chicago Water Tower is a landmark tower at 806 North Michigan Avenue in Chicago, Illinois, built in 1869 to house a standpipe for the city's waterworks. One of the very few structures to survive the Great Chicago Fire of 1871, it has become a beloved symbol of the city's resilience and of old Chicago.

History

Designed by architect William W. Boyington in a castellated Gothic style, the tower concealed a 138-foot standpipe that regulated pressure for the adjacent Chicago Avenue Pumping Station, which drew Lake Michigan water through a tunnel. Oscar Wilde, visiting in 1882, mocked it as "a castellated monstrosity with pepper boxes stuck all over it", but Chicagoans embraced it. When the Great Fire swept the city in October 1871, the limestone tower and the pumping station across the street were among the only public buildings left standing in the burned district.

Present day

The standpipe was removed in 1911, and the tower now serves as a city gallery and visitor information centre (the City Gallery at the Historic Water Tower), displaying work by local artists and photographers. The square around it is named Jane Byrne Plaza. The tower anchors the north end of the Magnificent Mile shopping district, near the John Hancock Center.
