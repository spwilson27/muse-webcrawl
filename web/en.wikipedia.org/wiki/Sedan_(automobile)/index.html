Sedan (automobile)

A sedan (American English) or saloon (British English) is a passenger car body style with a three-box configuration: separate compartments for the engine, the passengers, and the cargo (trunk or boot). Sedans typically have four doors, a fixed roof, and seating for four or more occupants, with a B-pillar between the front and rear side windows.

The classic sedan profile: distinct hood, cabin, and trunk.

Etymology

The word "sedan" originally referred to a covered chair carried on poles by porters — the sedan chair — and was borrowed from Italian and ultimately Latin sedes ("seat"). It was applied to an enclosed automobile body by the 1910s; the Speedwell Motor Company advertised a "sedan" in 1912. "Saloon" in British usage derives from the spacious passenger saloon of railway coaches.

History

Early automobiles were mostly open; closed bodies became common in the 1920s as steel body construction improved. The sedan became the archetypal family car of the mid-twentieth century, produced in two-door and four-door forms and in sizes from subcompact to full-size luxury models such as the Cadillac Sedan de Ville. From the 2010s, sedan sales declined sharply in North America and Europe as buyers shifted to crossovers and SUVs, though sedans such as the Toyota Camry and Honda Accord remain major sellers and the style persists in executive and electric cars.

Variations

Notchback sedans have a sharply defined trunk; fastback sedans slope the roof continuously toward the rear; hardtop sedans of the 1950s–1970s omitted the B-pillar for a coupe-like look. Two-door sedans, once common economy models, largely disappeared as coupes took over that niche.
