Leasehold estate

A leasehold estate is an ownership interest in land or property in which a lessee or tenant holds rights of possession and use for a fixed period under a lease, after which the property reverts to the lessor (landlord). It is one of the four basic types of land estate in common-law jurisdictions, and is sometimes described as a "wasting asset" because the tenant's interest diminishes as the term runs down.

Types

  • Estate for years — a leasehold for a definite period, from days to centuries, which ends automatically at the expiry date.
  • Periodic tenancy — continues for successive periods (week to week, month to month, year to year) until proper notice is given.
  • Tenancy at will — possession with the landlord's consent but no fixed term; either party may end it at any time.
  • Tenancy at sufferance — a holdover tenant remains after the right to possession has ended.

Leasehold ownership of homes

In England and Wales, many flats and some houses are sold on long leasehold terms — originally often 99 or 999 years — under which the leaseholder owns the dwelling for the term but pays ground rent to the freeholder and service charges toward the upkeep of the building. Statutes since 1967 have given long leaseholders rights to enfranchisement (buying the freehold) or lease extension, and reforms in the 2020s addressed escalating ground rents and the terms of new leases. In other jurisdictions similar arrangements appear as ground leases, emphyteusis, or strata and cooperative structures.

Landlord and tenant obligations

The lease allocates obligations between the parties: the tenant covenants to pay rent, keep the property in repair, and use it only as permitted; the landlord covenants to allow quiet enjoyment and, in residential cases, to meet statutory repairing duties. Breach may lead to forfeiture of the lease, subject in many jurisdictions to relief granted by the courts.
