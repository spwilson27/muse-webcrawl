Persia

Persia (from Latin Persia, from Greek Persis, from Old Persian Pārsa) is the historical name long used in the West for the country now officially known as Iran. Strictly, the name derives from Persis (modern Fars province), the southwestern region that was the homeland of the Persians and the core of the Achaemenid Empire; by extension it came to denote the whole Iranian plateau and the successive empires and states centred on it.

History

The Persians, an Iranian people, rose to prominence under Cyrus the Great, who founded the Achaemenid Empire (c. 550–330 BC), the largest empire the world had yet seen, stretching from the Balkans to the Indus Valley. After its conquest by Alexander the Great, the region passed through Hellenistic, Parthian, and Sasanian rule; the Sasanian Empire (224–651 AD) was Rome's great eastern rival. Following the Muslim conquest in the 7th century, Persian culture, language, and administration profoundly shaped the wider Islamic world, and dynasties such as the Samanids, Safavids, and Qajars ruled the plateau in later centuries.

Name change

In 1935, the government of Reza Shah Pahlavi requested that foreign states use the name "Iran" — the country's own name for itself since antiquity, meaning "land of the Aryans" — in place of Persia. "Persia" remains in common use for the historical and cultural sphere, for the Persian language (Farsi), and for products and concepts such as Persian carpets, Persian cats, and Persian cuisine.

Culture

Persian civilisation is noted for its poetry (Rumi, Hafez, Saadi, Ferdowsi), miniature painting, carpets, gardens, and architecture, and for its contributions to mathematics, astronomy, and medicine in the medieval period.
