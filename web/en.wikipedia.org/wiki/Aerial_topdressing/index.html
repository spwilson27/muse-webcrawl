Aerial topdressing

Aerial topdressing is the aerial application of fertilisers, lime, or seed over farmland, developed to a high degree in New Zealand after the Second World War. It allowed steep hill country, inaccessible to ground-spreading vehicles, to be fertilised and developed into productive pasture.

History

Trials in 1948–49 using war-surplus aircraft such as the Grumman Avenger and Tiger Moth demonstrated that superphosphate could be sown from the air. Purpose-built aircraft followed, including the Fletcher FU-24 and the PAC Cresco, and aerial topdressing became a cornerstone of New Zealand hill-country farming, spreading millions of tonnes of fertiliser annually at its peak.

Technique

Aircraft load fertiliser from a truck-mounted hopper at a farm airstrip, often a short uphill strip on a ridge, then make repeated low passes guided today by GPS and flow-control systems that vary the rate across the block. The work is among the most demanding in civil aviation, with very high cycles of take-off, low-level manoeuvring, and landing.
