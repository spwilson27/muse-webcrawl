FamilyPapaveraceae

Papaver orientale, the Oriental poppy, is a perennial flowering plant native to the Caucasus, northeastern Turkey, and northern Iran. It is a classic plant of the herbaceous border, grown for its large, showy, cup-shaped flowers, which are typically a vivid scarlet-orange with a black blotch at the base of each petal and a boss of black stamens.

Description

Oriental poppies form clumps of coarsely hairy, pinnately divided grey-green leaves. In late spring and early summer, stout bristly stems rise to 60–90 cm (2–3 ft) and bear solitary flowers up to 15 cm (6 in) across, with four to six tissue-paper petals. After flowering, the plant produces a smooth, rounded seed capsule and the foliage typically dies back in midsummer, leaving a gap that can be filled by later-growing companions such as baby's breath. The plant re-emerges with fresh foliage in autumn.

Cultivation

Papaver orientale is hardy and long-lived, thriving in full sun and well-drained soil in USDA zones 3–7. It resents disturbance and is best propagated by root cuttings taken in late summer or by seed. Breeders have produced many cultivars expanding the colour range from the wild scarlet to white ('Perry's White'), pink ('Princess Victoria Louise'), coral, maroon, and near-plum shades, as well as fringed and double-flowered forms. The dried seed heads are valued in flower arrangements.

Relatives and chemistry

The Oriental poppy is closely related to the great scarlet poppy, Papaver bracteatum, which has been investigated as a commercial source of the alkaloid thebaine, a precursor that can be converted into codeine and other pharmaceuticals without producing the opium poppy's morphine-rich latex. P. orientale itself contains only small quantities of alkaloids, principally oripavine, and is grown purely as an ornamental.
