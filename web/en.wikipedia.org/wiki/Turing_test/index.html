Turing test

The Turing test, originally called the imitation game by Alan Turing in 1950, is a test of a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behaviour equivalent to, or indistinguishable from, that of a human. Turing proposed that a human evaluator would judge natural language conversations between a human and a machine designed to generate human-like responses. The evaluator would be aware that one of the two partners in conversation is a machine, and all participants would be separated from one another. If the evaluator could not reliably tell the machine from the human, the machine would be said to have passed the test.

History

Turing introduced the test in his 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in the journal Mind, as a way of replacing the question "Can machines think?" with an operational one. He predicted that by the year 2000, machines with sufficient storage would be able to fool 30% of human judges in a five-minute conversation. Early programs such as ELIZA (1966) and PARRY (1972) exploited conversational tricks; more recently, chatbots have periodically been claimed to pass restricted versions of the test, claims that remain contested.

Criticisms and variants

Philosophers and AI researchers have criticized the test from several directions: John Searle's Chinese room argument contends that symbol manipulation without understanding could pass it; others argue it rewards deceptive mimicry of human flaws rather than intelligence, or that it sets too narrow a behavioural criterion. Variants include the total Turing test (adding perception and manipulation of objects) and reverse Turing tests such as CAPTCHAs. The Loebner Prize, run annually from 1990, awarded prizes to the most human-like chatbots in restricted contests.
