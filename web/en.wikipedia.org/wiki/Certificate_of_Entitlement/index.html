Certificate of Entitlement

A Certificate of Entitlement (COE) is a licence required in Singapore to own and use a vehicle on public roads. Introduced on 1 May 1990 under the Vehicle Quota System, the COE gives its holder the right to register, own, and use a vehicle for a period of 10 years, after which the vehicle must be deregistered or the COE renewed. The system is a form of road pricing and congestion management intended to control the growth of the vehicle population on the island city-state's limited road network.

Operation

The government sets a quota of COEs for each vehicle category — broadly, small cars (Category A), larger cars (Category B), goods vehicles and buses (Category C), motorcycles (Category D), and an open category (Category E) — based on the number of vehicles deregistered and planned growth of the fleet. Certificates are allocated by competitive bidding in open auctions held twice a month, administered by the Land Transport Authority. Prices fluctuate with demand and have at times exceeded S$100,000 for cars, often making the COE more expensive than the vehicle itself.

Renewal and deregistration

At the end of 10 years, an owner may renew the COE for a further 5 or 10 years by paying the Prevailing Quota Premium, a moving average of recent auction prices, or deregister the vehicle, in which case the Preferential Additional Registration Fee (PARF) rebate may apply for younger deregistered cars.

Criticism and debate

Supporters credit the system with preventing severe traffic congestion and funding public transport. Critics argue that it makes car ownership a luxury, disadvantages middle-income households and businesses, and contributes to volatile vehicle prices. Nevertheless, Singapore's approach is frequently cited internationally as a leading example of demand-side transport management.
