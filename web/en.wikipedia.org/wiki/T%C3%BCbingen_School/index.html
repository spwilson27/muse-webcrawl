Tübingen School

The Tübingen School is the name given to groups of theologians associated with the University of Tübingen in Germany; the term is used for distinct Protestant and Catholic traditions. The Protestant Tübingen School of the nineteenth century, led by Ferdinand Christian Baur, pioneered the historical-critical study of the New Testament.

Baur and the Protestant school

Ferdinand Christian Baur (1792–1860), professor at Tübingen from 1826, applied Hegelian dialectics to early Christian history, arguing that primitive Christianity was shaped by a conflict between a Jewish-Christian ("Petrine") party led by Peter and a Gentile ("Pauline") party led by Paul, resolved in the later "early Catholic" church. Baur and his students, including Eduard Zeller and Albert Schwegler, dated most New Testament writings to the second century — conclusions later scholarship largely abandoned, though their historical approach permanently changed biblical studies.

Catholic Tübingen School

A contemporary Catholic Tübingen School, associated with Johann Sebastian Drey and Johann Adam Möhler, emphasised the historical development of doctrine and the church as a living organism, influencing later Catholic theology.
