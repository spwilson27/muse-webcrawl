Saltbox

A saltbox house is a traditional New England style of timber-framed house characterised by a long, pitched roof that slopes down low at the back, giving the building the shape of a wooden box once used for storing salt. The front is typically two storeys high while the rear is a single storey, beneath the extended roof slope often called a "catslide."

History and form

Saltboxes were built in colonial America from the mid-17th century into the early 19th century, and were especially common in Massachusetts, Connecticut and other New England colonies. The form commonly developed as houses were enlarged: a lean-to addition at the back was covered by extending the main roof down over it, producing the distinctive asymmetrical profile. A large central chimney served several fireplaces. Popular explanations — that the sloping roof shed snow efficiently or evaded a tax on two-storey houses — are considered folklore by historians. Notable surviving examples include the Thomas Lee House in Connecticut and houses preserved at museum villages such as Old Sturbridge Village. The style was revived in the 20th century during the Colonial Revival movement.
