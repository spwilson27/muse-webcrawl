Strategy (game theory)

A payoff matrix: rows and columns represent players' strategies

In game theory, a strategy is a complete plan of action for a player in a game: it specifies what the player will do in every situation that could arise, for every possible state of the game, even situations that the player's own earlier choices would have ruled out. A player's strategy set (or strategy space) is the set of all strategies available to that player.

Pure and mixed strategies

A pure strategy determines a single, definite move or action in each situation. A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies: the player randomises, choosing each pure strategy with some probability. Mixed strategies are essential because many games, such as matching pennies, possess no Nash equilibrium in pure strategies but do possess one in mixed strategies—as guaranteed by Nash's theorem for finite games. A behaviour strategy, used in extensive-form games, assigns probabilities independently at each information set; by Kuhn's theorem, under perfect recall behaviour and mixed strategies are equivalent.

Strategy profiles and equilibrium

A combination of one strategy for each player is called a strategy profile; a game's payoffs are determined by the profile played. A profile is a Nash equilibrium if no player can unilaterally deviate to another strategy and improve their own payoff, given the others' strategies. A strategy is (strictly or weakly) dominant if it performs at least as well as every alternative against all opposing strategies. Eliminating dominated strategies is a standard first step in solving games.

Examples

In the prisoner's dilemma, "defect" is a dominant strategy for both players. In rock paper scissors, the only equilibrium is the mixed strategy that plays each option with probability one-third.
