Morgoth Bauglir is a fictional character in J. R. R. Tolkien's legendarium. Originally named Melkor, he is the first and greatest of the Ainur created by Eru Ilúvatar, who rebelled against his creator and became the primordial source of evil in Arda. He is the main antagonist of The Silmarillion: the Dark Lord who steals the Silmarils, corrupts Elves into Orcs (in one of Tolkien's conceptions), breeds dragons, and wages war on Elves and Men throughout the First Age, from his fortresses of Utumno and Angband in the north of Middle-earth. His chief lieutenant, Sauron, becomes the Dark Lord of The Lord of the Rings after Morgoth's fall.

Conception and development

Melkor appears in Tolkien's writings from the earliest versions of the mythology in The Book of Lost Tales (from 1916–17). The name Morgoth ("Black Foe of the World," from Sindarin) is the name given him by the Elven prince Fëanor after the theft of the Silmarils; Bauglir means "the Constrainer" or "the Oppressor." Tolkien revised the character's cosmology and power repeatedly across the posthumously published History of Middle-earth volumes, including the essays of "Morgoth's Ring," which develop the idea that Morgoth dispersed his native power into the very matter of Arda, corrupting the whole world as Sauron later did with the One Ring.

Story

During the Music of the Ainur, Melkor introduced discord into the theme of Ilúvatar, seeking to impose his own designs. Entering Arda, he contested the other Valar for dominion of the world, raised the fortress of Utumno, and destroyed the Two Lamps. After a captivity in Valinor and a feigned repentance, he destroyed the Two Trees with the spider Ungoliant, murdered Fëanor's father Finwë, and stole the Silmarils, setting them in his Iron Crown. For over five centuries he warred against the Elves of Beleriand; the thief Beren and Lúthien recovered one Silmaril from his crown. At the end of the First Age, the Valar intervened in the War of Wrath, broke Angband, chained Morgoth, and thrust him through the Door of Night into the Timeless Void. Prophecy holds that he will return for the final battle, Dagor Dagorath.

Analysis

Scholars have compared Morgoth to Satan in the Christian tradition — a fallen angel of the highest order — while noting Tolkien's distinct treatment of evil as the corruption and marring of good rather than an independent creative power. Morgoth's progressive diminishment as he spends his power on domination is read as a commentary on the self-defeating nature of tyranny.
