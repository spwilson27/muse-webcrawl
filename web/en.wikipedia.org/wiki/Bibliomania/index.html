Bibliomania

Bibliomania is an obsessive-compulsive passion for collecting books, in which the desire to acquire books goes beyond any intention of reading them. The term, from the Greek biblion (book) and mania (madness), gained currency in the early nineteenth century, when book collecting among wealthy amateurs reached a fever pitch and the Reverend Thomas Frognall Dibdin published Bibliomania; or Book Madness: A Bibliographical Romance (1809), a playful dialogue examining the condition and its supposed symptoms and cures.

A bibliomaniac's library: acquisition outruns reading.

Symptoms

Dibdin listed characteristic symptoms including an insatiable appetite for first editions, large-paper copies, books printed on vellum, unique copies, and volumes with uncut pages or eccentric bindings. The bibliomaniac prizes rarity, condition and provenance over content, and may accumulate multiple copies of the same work.

History of the craze

The great age of bibliomania in England is often dated to the sale of the Duke of Roxburghe's library in 1812, whose forty-five-day auction produced record prices — most famously for a copy of Boccaccio's Decameron printed by Valdarfer in 1471 — and founded the Roxburghe Club of bibliophiles. Critics such as Holbrook Jackson, in The Anatomy of Bibliomania (1930), later treated the subject with affectionate erudition.

Modern usage

Today "bibliomania" is used loosely for any overwhelming enthusiasm for books, though clinicians sometimes relate extreme cases to hoarding disorder. The related Japanese word tsundoku describes acquiring books and letting them pile up unread.
