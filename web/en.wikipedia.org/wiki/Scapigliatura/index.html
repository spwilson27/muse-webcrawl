Scapigliatura

Scapigliatura
Years active: c. 1860s–1880s
Location: Milan, Italy
Fields: Literature, painting, music

Scapigliatura (from the Italian scapigliare, "to dishevel") was an artistic and literary movement that flourished in northern Italy, chiefly Milan, during the 1860s and 1870s. The term — roughly translatable as "bohemianism" or "the dishevelled ones" — was popularized by the writer Cletto Arrighi, whose 1862 novel La Scapigliatura e il 6 febbraio portrayed the group. The scapigliati rebelled against the conservative, romantic, and Catholic culture of the newly unified Italy, drawing inspiration from French and German models such as Baudelaire, Gautier, Heine, and Hoffmann.

Characteristics

The scapigliati cultivated an image of disorderly, unconventional lives — several died young of tuberculosis, alcoholism, or suicide — and championed artistic modernity, realism, and the exploration of the duality between beauty and ugliness, health and disease. Their works frequently attacked the Church, the bourgeoisie, and the official culture of the Risorgimento.

Figures

Leading writers included Arrigo Boito, Iginio Ugo Tarchetti (author of the novel Fosca), Giovanni Camerana, and Emilio Praga. In painting, Tranquillo Cremona, Daniele Ranzoni, and Giuseppe Grandi were associated with the movement, while Boito and Franco Faccio were central to its music. Boito's libretti for Verdi's late operas carried the movement's influence into mainstream Italian culture.

Legacy

Although brief, Scapigliatura introduced modern European literary currents into Italy and paved the way for verismo and decadentism.
