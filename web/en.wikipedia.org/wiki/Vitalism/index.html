Vitalism

Vitalism is a body of historical doctrines holding that living organisms are fundamentally different from non-living entities because they possess some non-physical element or are governed by principles distinct from those of inanimate matter — often a "vital force", "life spark", or élan vital. Vitalist ideas are ancient, appearing in Aristotelian concepts of the soul and in traditional medical systems, but the doctrine is principally associated with European science and philosophy from the seventeenth to the early twentieth century.

History

Chemists such as Jöns Jacob Berzelius held that a vital force was required to produce organic compounds. Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate is conventionally (if somewhat mythically) cited as the first blow to chemical vitalism, and nineteenth-century organic chemistry progressively explained "vital" products mechanistically. Paul Bert, Claude Bernard, and others founded experimental physiology on physico-chemical principles. Nevertheless vitalism enjoyed a philosophical revival: Hans Driesch argued from his sea-urchin embryo experiments (1890s) that development required a non-mechanical "entelechy", and Henri Bergson's Creative Evolution (1907) popularised the élan vital.

Decline

By the 1920s–30s vitalism had largely disappeared from professional biology as biochemistry, enzymology, and genetics explained heredity, metabolism, and development in molecular terms — a triumph of the mechanistic research programme, though philosophers note the borderline cases (self-organisation, emergence) that keep organicist ideas alive in systems biology. Echoes of vitalism persist in alternative medicine (for example the "vital force" of homeopathy and "energy" concepts in some traditional practices) and in ordinary language, but the doctrine has no standing in contemporary science.
