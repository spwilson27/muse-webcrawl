Antimicrobial resistance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites evolve so that the medicines designed to kill them no longer work, making infections harder or impossible to treat. It is driven by natural selection: microbes with resistance traits survive exposure to a drug and multiply, passing resistance genes to other microbes, including across species, by horizontal gene transfer. AMR is accelerated by the overuse and misuse of antimicrobial medicines in people and livestock.

Scale of the problem

A landmark study in The Lancet estimated that bacterial AMR was directly responsible for about 1.27 million deaths in 2019 and associated with nearly 5 million deaths, making it one of the leading causes of death globally. Notorious resistant pathogens include methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis, and carbapenem-resistant gut bacteria. The World Health Organization publishes a priority list of resistant bacteria for which new antibiotics are urgently needed, and warns of a "post-antibiotic era" in which common infections again become lethal.

Responses

Responses combine stewardship (prescribing antibiotics only when needed, at the right dose and duration), infection prevention and sanitation, vaccination, rapid diagnostics to distinguish bacterial from viral illness, surveillance of resistance patterns, and incentives to develop new drugs — since antibiotics are economically unattractive to develop compared with chronic-disease medicines. The 2016 O'Neill review for the UK government projected, in a widely cited scenario, up to 10 million deaths a year by 2050 if resistance goes unchecked — a projection intended as a warning rather than a precise forecast.
