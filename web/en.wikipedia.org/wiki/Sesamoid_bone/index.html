Sesamoid bone

In anatomy, a sesamoid bone is a bone embedded within a tendon or a muscle. Its name comes from the Greek sēsamoeidēs, "sesame-seed shaped", because many sesamoids are about that size. Sesamoids act like pulleys, providing a smooth surface over which tendons slide, increasing the leverage of the attached muscle and modifying pressure.

In humans

The largest sesamoid bone in the human body is the patella (kneecap), embedded in the quadriceps tendon. Two sesamoids are consistently found beneath the head of the first metatarsal bone in the foot (the tibial and fibular sesamoids of the big toe), where they bear weight and protect the tendon of flexor hallucis longus. Others commonly occur in the tendons of the hand, including two at the metacarpophalangeal joint of the thumb. The fabella is a sesamoid sometimes present in the lateral head of the gastrocnemius behind the knee. Development and number of sesamoids vary considerably between individuals.

Clinical significance

Sesamoids in the foot may be injured in turf toe, fractured, or become inflamed in sesamoiditis, a common complaint among dancers and runners. Because sesamoids have a limited blood supply, fractures may heal poorly and sometimes require surgical excision (sesamoidectomy).

In other animals

Sesamoid bones are found in many vertebrates; in horses, the paired proximal sesamoids at the fetlock are clinically important and prone to fracture in racing injuries.
