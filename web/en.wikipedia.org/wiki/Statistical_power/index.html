In statistics, the power of a binary hypothesis test is the probability that the test correctly rejects the null hypothesis when a specific alternative hypothesis is true. It is commonly denoted 1 − β, where β is the probability of a Type II error (failing to reject a false null hypothesis). Equivalently, statistical power is the probability of detecting an effect of a given size when it truly exists.

Factors affecting power

Power increases with:

  • a larger effect size (a bigger true difference is easier to detect);
  • a larger sample size;
  • a higher significance level α (at the cost of more Type I errors);
  • lower variability in the data, or designs (such as paired measurements) that reduce it.

For a given test, any one of power, sample size, effect size and α determines the others, which allows researchers to perform a power analysis before a study to choose a sample size that gives adequate power. Jacob Cohen popularised conventions for small, medium and large effect sizes and the convention of designing studies for 80% power (β = 0.20, four times the conventional α = 0.05).

Importance

Underpowered studies are a major concern in the replication crisis: a low-powered study is unlikely to detect a real effect, and the significant results it does produce tend to exaggerate the effect size (the "winner's curse") and are less likely to replicate. Post-hoc ("observed") power calculated from the observed effect is generally considered uninformative because it is a deterministic function of the p-value.
