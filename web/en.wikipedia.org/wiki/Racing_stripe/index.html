Racing stripe

Racing stripes, also called Le Mans stripes or rally stripes, are decorative stripes applied to the body of racing cars and sports cars, typically running in parallel bands from the front of the car over the bonnet, roof and rear. Though now primarily a styling feature, they began as a practical identification aid that helped pit crews and spectators distinguish cars at speed.

Origins

The stripes trace back to the early 1950s, when the American team of Briggs Cunningham applied distinctive blue stripes over white cars — the reverse of the traditional American racing colours assigned under the pre-war international colour code. The broad bands made Cunningham's cars instantly recognisable at events such as the 24 Hours of Le Mans. In the 1960s, Carroll Shelby's Ford GT40s and Shelby Mustangs made twin stripes iconic, and manufacturers began offering them as factory options on production sports cars.

Design

Traditional racing stripes are two parallel bands, sometimes of unequal width or paired with a pinstripe edge, in a colour contrasting with the body. They may be painted or applied as vinyl decals. In some racing series stripes also served a functional role for rookie drivers — for example, yellow stripes on the rear of the car used to mark novice competitors in open-wheel racing.

In popular culture

Racing stripes became a common visual shorthand for speed and performance, appearing on production cars, motorcycles, boats, clothing and even aircraft, regardless of any actual racing connection.
