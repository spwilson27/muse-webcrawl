The HIV/AIDS epidemic in the United States began in 1981, when the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported clusters of rare infections among young gay men in Los Angeles and New York. More than 700,000 people in the United States have died of HIV-related illness since then. In recent years an estimated 1.2 million people have been living with HIV in the country, of whom roughly one in eight were unaware of their infection.

History

The causative virus, HIV, was identified in 1983–84. In the 1980s AIDS was a leading cause of death among young adults in many American cities, and the epidemic provoked both fear and activism: organisations such as ACT UP pressed for faster drug approvals and funding. The Ryan White CARE Act of 1990 created the largest federal programme for people living with HIV. The introduction of combination antiretroviral therapy (HAART) in 1996 transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition for those with access to treatment, and AIDS deaths fell sharply.

Epidemiology

New infections are concentrated among gay and bisexual men, who account for the majority of diagnoses, and disproportionately affect Black and Latino Americans and residents of the South. Transmission occurs mainly through sexual contact and sharing of injection equipment. Public-health measures include routine testing, pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP, approved in 2012), syringe services programmes, and the "Undetectable = Untransmittable" (U=U) message, reflecting evidence that people with durably suppressed viral loads do not transmit the virus sexually.

Policy

Federal initiatives include the Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative, launched in 2019, which targets counties and states with the highest transmission with the aim of reducing new infections by 90 per cent by 2030. Despite progress, the US epidemic persists, shaped by stigma, unequal access to care and the intersection with the opioid crisis.
