Reverse transcriptase

FunctionRNA-dependent DNA polymerase
Discovery1970, Howard Temin and David Baltimore
Found inRetroviruses (including HIV), retrotransposons, telomerase

A reverse transcriptase (RT) is an enzyme used to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) from an RNA template, a process termed reverse transcription. Reverse transcriptases are used by viruses such as HIV and hepatitis B to replicate their genomes, by retrotransposon elements to proliferate within host genomes, and by eukaryotes in the form of telomerase to extend telomeres.

History

The enzyme was discovered independently in 1970 by Howard Temin at the University of Wisconsin and David Baltimore at MIT, overturning the simple one-way reading of the "central dogma" of molecular biology; the discovery was shared in the 1975 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Role in HIV

HIV reverse transcriptase converts the virus's single-stranded RNA genome into double-stranded DNA that can integrate into the host cell's chromosomes. The enzyme lacks proofreading activity and is highly error-prone, generating the rapid mutation that complicates vaccine development and drug resistance. Because of its central role, reverse transcriptase is the target of two major drug classes: nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) and non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs).

Applications

In the laboratory, reverse transcriptase is used in RT-PCR and RT-qPCR to convert RNA into DNA for amplification and quantification, a technique central to gene-expression studies and diagnostic tests for RNA viruses.
