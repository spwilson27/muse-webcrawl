Pink triangle

OriginNazi concentration camp badge for men imprisoned under Paragraph 175
Reclaimed1970s gay liberation movement; ACT UP from 1987
MeaningGay pride; memorial to victims of persecution

The pink triangle (German: Rosa Winkel) was a symbol used in Nazi concentration camps to identify male prisoners imprisoned for homosexuality under Paragraph 175 of the German penal code. Since the 1970s it has been reclaimed as a symbol of gay pride and of remembrance for the victims of anti-gay persecution.

Nazi-era use

Prisoners in the camps wore coloured triangular badges denoting the reason for their imprisonment. The pink triangle, larger than other badges to make its wearers conspicuous, marked men convicted of homosexual acts; an estimated 5,000 to 15,000 men were imprisoned in the camps on this ground, and many died there. After the war, men imprisoned under Paragraph 175 were not recognised as victims for decades, and the law remained in force in West Germany until 1969.

Reclamation

Gay liberation activists began wearing the pink triangle in the 1970s after memoirs such as Heinz Heger's The Men with the Pink Triangle (1972) drew attention to its history. In 1987 ACT UP adopted the symbol — pointing upward rather than downward as in the camps — above the slogan "SILENCE = DEATH", making it an international emblem of AIDS activism. Pink-triangle memorials stand in cities including San Francisco, Amsterdam, Berlin and Sydney.
