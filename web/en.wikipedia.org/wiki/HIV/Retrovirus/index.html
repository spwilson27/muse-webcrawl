Retrovirus

A retrovirus is a type of RNA virus that inserts a DNA copy of its genome into the host cell it invades in order to replicate. This is achieved with the enzyme reverse transcriptase, which transcribes the viral RNA into DNA — reversing the usual flow of genetic information described by the central dogma of molecular biology. The viral family is Retroviridae.

Replication cycle

After entering a host cell, the retrovirus uses reverse transcriptase to produce double-stranded DNA from its single-stranded RNA genome. Another viral enzyme, integrase, inserts this DNA into the host's chromosomes, where it is called a provirus. The host cell then transcribes and translates the proviral genes, producing new viral particles that bud from the cell. Because reverse transcriptase lacks proofreading, retroviruses mutate rapidly.

Classification and examples

Retroviruses are divided into subfamilies including the orthoretroviruses and spumaviruses, and into genera such as the lentiviruses. Notable examples include the human immunodeficiency viruses HIV-1 and HIV-2, which cause AIDS, and human T-lymphotropic viruses (HTLV), associated with adult T-cell leukaemia. Many animal retroviruses cause cancers in their hosts, and the study of retroviral oncogenes was central to understanding the genetic basis of cancer. Fragments of ancient retroviral infections, called endogenous retroviruses, make up a significant fraction of the human genome.

Medical significance

Drugs targeting reverse transcriptase, integrase and the viral protease form the backbone of antiretroviral therapy for HIV. Engineered retroviral and lentiviral vectors are also widely used as tools to deliver genes in research and in some approved gene therapies.
