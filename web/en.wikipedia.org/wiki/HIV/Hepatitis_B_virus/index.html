HIV/Hepatitis B virus

Co-infection with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and hepatitis B virus (HBV) is common because the two viruses share routes of transmission — sexual contact, injection drug use, and exposure to infected blood. Worldwide, an estimated 5–15% of people living with HIV are also chronically infected with HBV, and co-infection substantially increases the risk of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma and liver-related death compared with either infection alone.

VirusesHIV (retrovirus); HBV (hepadnavirus)
Shared transmissionSexual, percutaneous, perinatal
Main complicationAccelerated liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma

Clinical course

HIV-induced immunosuppression impairs control of HBV replication, leading to higher HBV DNA levels, lower rates of spontaneous HBeAg seroconversion and faster progression of liver disease. Conversely, immune reconstitution after starting antiretroviral therapy (ART) can provoke hepatic flares as the recovering immune system attacks infected hepatocytes. Withdrawal of HBV-active drugs can likewise trigger dangerous HBV reactivation.

Management

Guidelines recommend that all people with HIV/HBV co-infection receive ART containing two agents active against HBV — typically tenofovir (TDF or TAF) plus lamivudine or emtricitabine — regardless of CD4 count. Vaccination against hepatitis B is recommended for non-immune people with HIV, and patients should be monitored for hepatocellular carcinoma and tested for hepatitis D, which requires HBV to replicate.
