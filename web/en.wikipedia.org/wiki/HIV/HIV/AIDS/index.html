HIV/AIDS

HIV/AIDS is a spectrum of conditions caused by infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Following initial infection an individual may not notice symptoms, or may experience a brief period of influenza-like illness. Typically, this is followed by a prolonged incubation period with no symptoms. If the infection progresses, it interferes more with the immune system, increasing the risk of developing common infections such as tuberculosis, as well as other opportunistic infections, and tumors which are otherwise rare in people who have normal immune function. These late symptoms of infection are referred to as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).

Transmission

HIV is spread primarily by unprotected sex (including anal and vaginal sex), contaminated hypodermic needles or blood transfusions, and from mother to child during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding. Some bodily fluids, such as saliva, sweat and tears, do not transmit the virus. The virus is not spread by casual contact, insects, or the sharing of food and utensils.

Pathophysiology

HIV is a retrovirus that primarily infects components of the human immune system such as CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. It directly and indirectly destroys CD4+ T cells. When CD4+ T cell numbers decline below a critical level, cell-mediated immunity is lost and the body becomes progressively more susceptible to opportunistic infections, leading to the development of AIDS.

Diagnosis and treatment

HIV infection is diagnosed by testing blood or saliva for antibodies to the virus, for viral antigens such as p24, or for HIV RNA. There is no cure or vaccine; however, antiretroviral therapy (ART) can slow the course of the disease and may lead to a near-normal life expectancy. Treatment is recommended as soon as the diagnosis is made, as starting treatment early reduces the risk of death and complications. Without treatment, average survival time after infection is 9 to 11 years, depending on the HIV subtype. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) can prevent infection in exposed individuals.

Epidemiology and history

As of 2022, AIDS has caused an estimated 40 million deaths worldwide, and about 39 million people were living with HIV. The disease was first recognized by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 1981; its cause, HIV, was identified in the early 1980s by teams including those of Luc Montagnier and Robert Gallo. The virus is believed to have originated in non-human primates in West-central Africa and to have transferred to humans in the early-to-mid 20th century. Sub-Saharan Africa remains the most affected region, though access to antiretroviral treatment has expanded dramatically since the 2000s through programmes such as PEPFAR and the Global Fund.

Society and culture

The disease has had large impacts on society as an illness and as a source of discrimination. AIDS denialism has hindered treatment efforts in some countries. Religious attitudes, stigma surrounding sexuality and drug use, and the economics of pharmaceutical development have all shaped the global response. World AIDS Day is observed on 1 December each year.
