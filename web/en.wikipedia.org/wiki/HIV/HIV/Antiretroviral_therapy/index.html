Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is the use of combination drug regimens to treat infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Because HIV mutates rapidly and develops resistance to single drugs, standard therapy combines at least three antiretroviral agents — historically called highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) — typically two nucleoside reverse-transcriptase inhibitors forming a "backbone" plus an integrase inhibitor, a non-nucleoside reverse-transcriptase inhibitor, or a boosted protease inhibitor.

How it works

Antiretroviral drug classes attack different stages of the viral life cycle: entry and fusion inhibitors block the virus from entering CD4 cells; reverse-transcriptase inhibitors prevent copying of viral RNA into DNA; integrase inhibitors stop insertion of viral DNA into the host genome; and protease inhibitors prevent maturation of new viral particles. Used consistently, ART suppresses viral load to undetectable levels, allows CD4 counts to recover, and prevents progression to AIDS. A person with a durably undetectable viral load does not sexually transmit the virus ("U=U", undetectable = untransmittable).

History and impact

The first antiretroviral, zidovudine (AZT), was approved in 1987, but durable benefit arrived only with combination therapy in the mid-1990s, which transformed HIV infection in wealthy countries from a rapidly fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition. Global access expanded dramatically in the 2000s through generic fixed-dose combinations and programs such as PEPFAR and the Global Fund; tens of millions of people now receive ART. Current guidelines recommend starting treatment immediately after diagnosis regardless of CD4 count, and long-acting injectable regimens have joined daily oral therapy.
