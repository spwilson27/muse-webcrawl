HIV

The human immunodeficiency viruses (HIV) are two species of Lentivirus (a subgroup of retrovirus) that infect humans: HIV-1 and HIV-2. Over time, infection causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), a condition in which progressive failure of the immune system allows life-threatening opportunistic infections and cancers to thrive.

Transmission and course of infection

HIV is transmitted through contact with infected blood, semen, vaginal fluids, rectal fluids or breast milk, most commonly through sexual contact, shared injection equipment, or from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. It is not spread by casual contact. After an initial acute phase, infection typically enters a long period of clinical latency during which CD4+ T helper cells gradually decline; without treatment most people progress to AIDS within about a decade.

Treatment and prevention

There is no widely available cure or vaccine, but antiretroviral therapy (ART) suppresses viral replication, restores immune function and allows a near-normal life expectancy; a person with a durably undetectable viral load does not sexually transmit the virus. Prevention includes condoms, sterile injecting equipment, pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis, and treatment of pregnant women. HIV-1, discovered in 1983, originated from simian immunodeficiency viruses of Central African primates and is responsible for the global pandemic, while HIV-2 is largely confined to West Africa and progresses more slowly. An estimated 39 million people were living with HIV worldwide in the early 2020s.
