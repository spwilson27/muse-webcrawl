Antiretroviral drug

PurposeTreatment and prevention of HIV infection
ClassesNRTIs, NNRTIs, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors, entry inhibitors
First approvedZidovudine (AZT), 1987

Antiretroviral drugs are medications used to treat infection with retroviruses, primarily HIV. They do not cure HIV, but when used in combination — a regimen known as antiretroviral therapy (ART), formerly HAART — they suppress viral replication, allow the immune system to recover, prevent progression to AIDS and greatly reduce transmission.

Classes

Antiretrovirals are grouped by the stage of the viral life cycle they target: nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) such as tenofovir, emtricitabine and zidovudine; non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) such as efavirenz and doravirine; protease inhibitors such as darunavir; integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) such as dolutegravir and bictegravir; and entry/fusion inhibitors such as maraviroc and enfuvirtide.

Combination therapy

Standard ART combines three drugs, usually two NRTIs plus an INSTI, NNRTI or boosted protease inhibitor. Because HIV mutates rapidly, combination therapy is essential to prevent drug resistance. Modern single-tablet regimens have made once-daily dosing standard, and long-acting injectable combinations such as cabotegravir/rilpivirine are also available.

Prevention

Antiretrovirals are also used for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). Treatment that maintains an undetectable viral load eliminates the risk of sexual transmission, a principle summarised as "Undetectable = Untransmittable" (U=U).
