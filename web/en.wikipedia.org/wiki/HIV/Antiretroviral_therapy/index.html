Antiretroviral therapy

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is the use of combination drug regimens to treat infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Modern regimens typically combine two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors with a third agent — commonly an integrase strand transfer inhibitor, a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor, or a boosted protease inhibitor. When taken consistently, ART suppresses viral replication to undetectable levels, restores and preserves immune function, and prevents progression to AIDS.

History

The first antiretroviral, zidovudine (AZT), was approved in 1987, but single-drug therapy rapidly selected resistant virus. The introduction of combination therapy in the mid-1990s — often called highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) — transformed HIV from a usually fatal infection into a manageable chronic condition in settings with access to treatment, sharply reducing mortality.

Benefits and treatment as prevention

Current guidelines recommend starting ART for all people diagnosed with HIV, regardless of CD4 count. Sustained viral suppression also eliminates the risk of sexual transmission of HIV, a finding summarised by the slogan "Undetectable = Untransmittable" (U=U) and supported by large studies of serodifferent couples. Global access programmes have expanded ART to tens of millions of people, though gaps in testing, linkage to care and drug supply remain.

Challenges

ART must usually be taken for life. Challenges include drug resistance following poor adherence, side effects, interactions with other medicines, and the persistence of latent viral reservoirs that current therapy cannot eradicate, which is why stopping treatment normally leads to viral rebound.
