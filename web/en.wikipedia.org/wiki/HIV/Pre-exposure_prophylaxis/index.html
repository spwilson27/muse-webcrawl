Pre-exposure prophylaxis

Pre-exposure prophylaxis for HIV (PrEP) is the use of antiretroviral medication by people who do not have HIV in order to prevent acquiring it. When taken as prescribed, PrEP is highly effective — reducing the risk of sexual acquisition of HIV by about 99% — and is recommended by the World Health Organization and national guidelines for people at substantial risk of infection.

Regimens

The first regimen, approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2012, is a daily oral tablet combining tenofovir disoproxil fumarate with emtricitabine; a formulation based on tenofovir alafenamide followed in 2019. Long-acting injectable cabotegravir, given every two months, was approved from 2021 and showed superior effectiveness in trials, largely through removing the burden of daily adherence. Users undergo HIV testing before starting and at regular intervals, along with screening for other sexually transmitted infections and monitoring of kidney function for tenofovir-based regimens.

PrEP differs from post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), a 28-day course begun within 72 hours after a possible exposure. Uptake of PrEP has grown unevenly worldwide, constrained by cost, awareness and stigma, and it is credited with contributing to steep declines in new HIV diagnoses in cities with high coverage.
