Sir Archibald Edward Garrod (25 December 1857 – 28 March 1936) was an English physician who pioneered the field of inborn errors of metabolism. His work on alkaptonuria, published in the first decade of the twentieth century, provided some of the earliest evidence that inherited diseases follow Mendelian patterns and that genes act through biochemical pathways.

Life and career

Garrod was the son of Sir Alfred Baring Garrod, a noted physician. He studied natural sciences at Christ Church, Oxford, and medicine at St Bartholomew's Hospital, London, qualifying in 1885. He held posts at the Hospital for Sick Children, Great Ormond Street, and St Bartholomew's, and during the First World War served as a consultant physician to the army in Malta. In 1920 he succeeded William Osler as Regius Professor of Medicine at Oxford, a chair he held until 1927. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society and was knighted in 1915.

Inborn errors of metabolism

Studying families affected by alkaptonuria — a condition in which urine blackens on exposure to air — Garrod, working with the geneticist William Bateson, showed in 1902 that the disorder was inherited as a Mendelian recessive trait. He went on to identify albinism, cystinuria, and pentosuria as similar hereditary metabolic anomalies. In his 1908 Croonian Lectures and his 1909 book Inborn Errors of Metabolism, Garrod proposed that each condition resulted from the inherited absence or defect of a specific enzyme, an idea that anticipated the "one gene–one enzyme" hypothesis formulated decades later by Beadle and Tatum. He also articulated the concept of "chemical individuality", arguing that each person's biochemistry is unique.

Legacy

Garrod's insights were largely overlooked until the rise of biochemical genetics in the mid-twentieth century, when he came to be recognised as a founder of human genetics and of precision medicine.
