Arcus cloud

Arcus cloud
GenusAccessory cloud of cumulonimbus or cumulus
Altitude0–2 km (low level)
TypesShelf cloud; roll cloud
PrecipitationNone directly; associated with storms

An arcus cloud is a low, horizontal cloud formation, usually appearing as an accessory cloud to a cumulonimbus thunderstorm. Two principal types are recognized: the shelf cloud and the roll cloud. Arcus clouds form along the leading edge of a thunderstorm's outflow — its gust front — and their arrival typically heralds a sudden squall of wind and rain.

Shelf cloud

A shelf cloud is attached to the base of the parent storm cloud and forms where rain-cooled air spreads out at the surface and lifts warm, moist air ahead of the storm to its condensation level. The cloud has a wedge- or shelf-like leading edge, often dark and ominous, with a smooth laminar lower surface on the front side and a turbulent, rising zone behind. Shelf clouds mark the gust front itself and are followed by strong outflow winds and heavy precipitation.

Roll cloud

A roll cloud is a detached, tube-shaped cloud that appears to roll about its horizontal axis. It is not connected to the storm base and is not associated with a tornado. Roll clouds can form along thunderstorm outflows, cold fronts, or in stable air where undulations propagate along an inversion — most famously the Morning Glory clouds of Australia's Gulf of Carpentaria, which can extend for hundreds of kilometres and are used by glider pilots for soaring.

Significance

Although sometimes mistaken for a wall cloud, an arcus cloud does not itself produce tornadoes; its operational importance is as a visual warning of imminent severe outflow winds, downbursts, and heavy rain along the squall line.
