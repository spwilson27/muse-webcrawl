CMOS

Complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS, pronounced "see-moss") is a technology for constructing integrated circuits. CMOS technology is used for constructing integrated circuit components such as microprocessors, microcontrollers, memory chips (including CMOS image sensors), and other digital logic circuits. It is characterized by the use of complementary pairs of p-type and n-type MOSFETs, giving very low static power consumption.

Principle

CMOS circuits use pairs of p-type and n-type metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistors arranged so that, in a steady state, one network is always off: current flows only briefly while the output switches. This makes power dissipation mainly dynamic, proportional to switching frequency, which enabled the enormous transistor densities of modern chips.

History

The CMOS process was patented by Frank Wanlass in 1963 and demonstrated at Fairchild Semiconductor; RCA commercialized CMOS logic in its 4000-series chips from 1968. By the 1980s CMOS had displaced NMOS and TTL as the dominant logic technology, enabling the microprocessor revolution and portable battery-powered devices.

Other uses

The term "CMOS" is also commonly used for active-pixel image sensors ("CMOS sensors") in cameras, and, in older PC terminology, for the battery-backed memory holding BIOS settings.
