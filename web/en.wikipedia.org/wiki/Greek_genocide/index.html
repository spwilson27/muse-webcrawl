The Greek genocide (Greek: Γενοκτονία των Ελλήνων) was the systematic destruction of the Christian Greek population of the Ottoman Empire during and after the First World War (1914–1922). It was carried out through massacres, forced deportations into the Anatolian interior, labour battalions, death marches, and the burning of Christian towns and villages. The campaign formed part of a wider pattern of persecution that also swept up Armenians and Assyrians.

Background

Greeks had lived in Asia Minor and Pontus since antiquity. By 1914 the Ottoman Empire's Greek Orthodox population numbered roughly two million, concentrated in western Anatolia, the Pontus region along the Black Sea, Cappadocia, and Eastern Thrace. The rise of Turkish nationalism under the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), and the empire's losses in the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913, produced a drive to homogenise Anatolia religiously and ethnically. Greeks were accused collectively of disloyalty and of acting as a fifth column for Greece and the Allied powers.

Course of events

Persecution began before the war, with boycotts and expulsions of Greeks from Eastern Thrace and the Aegean coast in 1913–1914. During the war, Greek men were conscripted into unarmed labour battalions (amele taburları) in which mortality from exhaustion, starvation and disease was extremely high. Communities in Pontus and interior Anatolia were deported on forced marches to remote districts. After the Greek landing at Smyrna in 1919 and the ensuing Greco-Turkish War, violence intensified; the Turkish nationalist victory in 1922 culminated in the Great Fire of Smyrna and the flight or expulsion of the remaining Christian population.

Aftermath

The 1923 Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations, attached to the Treaty of Lausanne, formalised the removal of some 1.2–1.5 million Orthodox Christians from Turkey and around 400,000 Muslims from Greece, with exemptions for Constantinople and the islands of Imbros and Tenedos. Greece's population rose by roughly a quarter almost overnight, transforming its society and politics.

Recognition

Greece, Cyprus and Armenia have officially recognised the events as genocide, as have several other legislatures and the International Association of Genocide Scholars. Turkey rejects the term, maintaining that deaths resulted from wartime conditions, intercommunal violence and civil war rather than a planned extermination. Estimates of Greek deaths range from about 300,000 to 900,000.
