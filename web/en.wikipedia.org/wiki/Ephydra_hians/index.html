Ephydra hians

Ephydra hians, the alkali fly

Ephydra hians, commonly known as the alkali fly, is a species of shore fly in the family Ephydridae. It is famous for its ability to live, feed, and reproduce in the highly alkaline and saline waters of Mono Lake in California, where enormous numbers of the flies gather along the shoreline and dive beneath the surface encased in bubbles of air.

Biology

Adult alkali flies crawl underwater to feed on algae and to lay eggs, trapping a silvery film of air around their bodies and wings. This air bubble functions as a physical gill — a plastron — allowing the fly to breathe while submerged, and the fly's densely hairy, water-repellent body keeps the surrounding water from wetting it. The lake's water is so alkaline (about pH 10) and salty that the flies must overcome strong forces pulling them into the water; their hydrophobic hairs and claws let them walk down submerged tufa and vegetation and resurface unharmed. Larvae and pupae develop underwater, feeding on algae.

Ecological role

The vast populations of E. hians — together with the lake's brine shrimp (Artemia monica) — are a crucial food resource for migratory birds at Mono Lake, including eared grebes, phalaropes, and California gulls. Historically, the Indigenous Kutzadika'a people harvested the fly pupae (called kutsavi) as a rich food source. Mark Twain described the flies in Roughing It (1872), marvelling that "you can hold them under water as long as you please — they do not mind it — they are only proud of it."
