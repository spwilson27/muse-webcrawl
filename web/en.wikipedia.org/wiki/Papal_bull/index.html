A papal bull is a type of public decree, letters patent, or charter issued by a pope of the Catholic Church. It is named after the leaden seal (bulla) that was traditionally appended to the end in order to authenticate it. Bulls are traditionally identified by their Latin incipit — their opening words — such as Unam sanctam (1302) or Inter caetera (1493).

History and format

Papal bulls have been in use at least since the 6th century, but the elaborate form developed in the medieval papal chancery. The document was written in an ornate Latin hand on parchment; the first line displayed the pope's name with elongated lettering. The seal, stamped with the heads of Saints Peter and Paul on one side and the pope's name on the other, hung from hemp cords for documents of justice or silk cords for documents of grace. Since the late 19th century the leaden seal has been replaced in most cases by a red ink stamp, and Pope Benedict XVI and his successors have generally issued solemn acts in the form of apostolic constitutions or motu proprio documents; the bull survives for formal acts such as the proclamation of a holy year (e.g. Spes non confundit, 2024).

Notable bulls

  • Clericis laicos (1296) and Unam sanctam (1302) — Boniface VIII's assertions of papal supremacy.
  • Inter caetera (1493) — Alexander VI's division of the non-European world between Spain and Portugal.
  • Regnans in Excelsis (1570) — Pius V's excommunication of Elizabeth I of England.
  • Zelo Domus Dei (1648) — Innocent X's protest against the Peace of Westphalia.
  • Ineffabilis Deus (1854) — definition of the dogma of the Immaculate Conception.
  • Munificentissimus Deus (1950) — definition of the dogma of the Assumption of Mary.

Related documents

Bulls form the most solemn class of papal documents; less solemn instruments include apostolic letters, briefs (sealed with the Fisherman's Ring in wax), encyclicals and motu proprio letters.
