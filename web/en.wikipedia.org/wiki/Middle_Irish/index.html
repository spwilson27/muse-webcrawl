Middle Irish

Middle Irish (Irish: Meán-Ghaeilge) is the form of the Irish language spoken and written from about 900 to 1200 AD, in Ireland, Scotland, and the Isle of Man. It is the direct descendant of Old Irish and the ancestor of Early Modern Irish, from which the modern Goidelic languages — Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx — developed. Middle Irish is known from a rich body of literature preserved in great manuscript compilations.

Linguistic features

Middle Irish was a period of transition and simplification. The elaborate verbal system of Old Irish broke down: the distinction between absolute and conjunct verb endings eroded, deuterotonic and prototonic forms were levelled, and analytic constructions with independent pronouns began to supplement the older infixed pronouns. Spellings became less consistent than in the classical Old Irish period, partly reflecting genuine sound changes — including the loss or weakening of unstressed syllables — and partly scribal uncertainty as the spoken language drifted away from the written standard.

Literature

Much of Ireland's early saga literature survives in Middle Irish recensions, even when the tales themselves originated earlier. The great manuscripts of the period include the Book of Leinster (c. 1160), which preserves the fullest version of the Táin Bó Cúailnge (Cattle Raid of Cooley), and the Book of the Dun Cow (Lebor na hUidre, c. 1100), the oldest surviving manuscript written entirely in Irish. Middle Irish literature encompasses the Ulster and Fenian cycles, voyage tales (immrama), religious poetry, law texts, annals, genealogies, and translations and adaptations of classical epics such as the Togail Troí (Destruction of Troy).
