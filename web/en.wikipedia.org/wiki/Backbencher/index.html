Backbencher

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Westminster and other parliamentary systems, a backbencher is a member of parliament (MP) or a legislator who occupies the back benches and is not a government minister or a senior member of the opposition front bench. The term derives from the British House of Commons, where government and opposition leaders sit on the front benches, while other members of their parties sit behind them.

Role

Backbenchers typically comprise the great majority of MPs. Because they hold no ministerial or shadow-ministerial office, they are somewhat freer to express dissenting views and to represent constituency interests, although in most parliamentary parties they remain subject to the party whip when voting. Influential backbenchers may chair or sit on select committees, introduce private members' bills, and lead debates on issues outside the government's legislative programme.

In the United Kingdom, the Backbench Business Committee, established in 2010, allocates parliamentary time for debates chosen by backbench MPs. Groups such as the 1922 Committee of Conservative backbenchers can exert significant influence on party leadership.

Other uses

The term is also used in other countries with Westminster-derived legislatures, including Canada, Australia, New Zealand and India. By extension, "backbench" can refer in any organisation to members without leadership positions.
