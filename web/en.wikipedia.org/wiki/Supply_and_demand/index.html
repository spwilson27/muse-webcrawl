Supply and demand

[Image: a busy open-air market with stalls of produce and shoppers, illustrating buyers and sellers meeting]

Supply and demand is an economic model of price determination in a market. It states that, other things equal, the price of a good or service tends toward the level at which the quantity demanded by buyers equals the quantity supplied by sellers — the market equilibrium — at which the market "clears". The model, developed by writers including Adam Smith and given its standard form by Alfred Marshall in his Principles of Economics (1890), is the foundation of Microeconomics price theory.

The model

The law of demand states that quantity demanded falls as price rises: the demand curve slopes downward, reflecting diminishing marginal Utility and the substitution and income effects of a price change. The law of supply states that quantity supplied rises as price rises, because higher prices cover the rising marginal cost of production; the supply curve slopes upward. Marshall compared the two curves to the blades of a pair of scissors — neither alone determines price.

A change in price causes a movement along a curve; a change in other determinants (income, tastes, prices of substitutes and complements for demand; input prices, technology, taxes and the number of sellers for supply) shifts the whole curve, creating a shortage or surplus at the old price and a new equilibrium.

Elasticity and applications

Elasticity measures responsiveness: price elasticity of demand compares the percentage change in quantity demanded to the percentage change in price. Inelastic demand (as for fuel or cigarettes) means revenue rises with price; elastic demand the reverse. Governments use the model to analyse price controls (rent ceilings, minimum prices), taxes — whose burden is shared between buyers and sellers according to relative elasticities — and the Market that keeps many agricultural and commodity prices volatile.
