Kegel exercise

Named afterArnold Kegel
Developed1948
TargetsPelvic floor muscles (pubococcygeus)
UsesUrinary incontinence, pelvic organ prolapse prevention, post-prostatectomy recovery

Kegel exercises, also known as pelvic floor exercises, consist of repeatedly contracting and relaxing the muscles that form part of the pelvic floor. The exercises were described in 1948 by American gynaecologist Arnold Kegel, who published them as a non-surgical treatment for urinary stress incontinence and genital relaxation in women.

Method

The exercises target the pubococcygeus and surrounding pelvic floor muscles — the same muscles used to stop the flow of urine midstream. A typical regimen involves contracting the muscles for several seconds, then relaxing fully, repeated in sets throughout the day. Kegel also developed the perineometer, a device measuring the strength of voluntary pelvic floor contractions, which provided biofeedback to patients.

Benefits

Pelvic floor muscle training is a first-line conservative treatment for stress urinary incontinence in women and can help prevent or improve symptoms of pelvic organ prolapse. In men, Kegels are recommended after prostate surgery (prostatectomy) to speed recovery of continence, and may help with erectile dysfunction and premature ejaculation. The exercises are also commonly recommended during pregnancy and after childbirth.

Evidence

Systematic reviews, including Cochrane reviews, support pelvic floor muscle training as effective for female stress and mixed urinary incontinence compared with no treatment. Correct technique matters: contracting the wrong muscles (such as repeatedly stopping urination as a routine practice) is discouraged, and people with pelvic floor hypertonicity may be advised against Kegels in favour of relaxation training.
