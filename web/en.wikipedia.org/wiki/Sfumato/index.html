Sfumato

Sfumato (Italian: [sfuˈmaːto], "smoked off", from sfumare, "to vanish like smoke") is a painting technique in which subtle gradations of tone and colour are used to soften the transition between areas, without lines or hard borders. It is one of the four canonical painting modes of Renaissance art, alongside chiaroscuro, cangiante and unione.

Technique

The effect is achieved by applying numerous extremely thin, semi-transparent glazes of paint, so that forms emerge gradually from shadow. Leonardo da Vinci, the technique's most famous practitioner, described the aim as leaving "no edges or lines" — in his words, modelling should be "without lines or borders, in the manner of smoke". Analysis of the Mona Lisa has revealed glaze layers as thin as a few micrometres around the eyes and mouth, producing the painting's famously elusive expression.

Artists

Leonardo pioneered sfumato in works such as the Virgin of the Rocks and the Mona Lisa. The technique was adopted by his followers and adapted by Giorgione, Correggio and Raphael, and it became a defining quality of High Renaissance portraiture.
