Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov, father of the Soviet atomic bomb.

Igor Vasilyevich Kurchatov (12 January 1903 – 7 February 1960) was a Soviet physicist who played a central role in the development of his country's nuclear weapons programme and civilian nuclear power. He is known as the father of the Soviet atomic bomb and the founder and first director of the Institute of Atomic Energy (now the Kurchatov Institute) in Moscow.

Early career

Kurchatov studied physics at Crimea State University and worked at the Ioffe Institute in Leningrad, researching ferroelectricity and then nuclear physics. In 1932 his group was among the pioneers of nuclear research in the USSR; he built one of the first cyclotrons in Europe and studied neutron physics and artificial radioactivity.

The bomb project

In 1943, after intelligence reports on the Allied Manhattan Project, the Soviet government appointed Kurchatov scientific director of its small atomic programme, centred on the secret Laboratory No. 2. After 1945 the project received enormous resources under Lavrentiy Beria's overall administration. Kurchatov's team achieved the first Soviet chain reaction in the F-1 reactor in December 1946 — the first reactor in Europe — and tested the first Soviet atomic bomb, RDS-1, on 29 August 1949 at Semipalatinsk. He also oversaw the Soviet thermonuclear programme, which tested a boosted device in 1953 and a true two-stage hydrogen bomb (RDS-37) in 1955.

Civilian nuclear work and later life

Kurchatov pushed for peaceful uses: the Obninsk Nuclear Power Plant (1954), the world's first grid-connected nuclear power station, and the nuclear icebreaker Lenin (1957). From 1956 he spoke internationally for openness in fusion research and against nuclear testing. He died in 1960, reputedly worn out by overwork and radiation exposure. The synthetic element 104 was named kurchatovium in his honour in the Soviet Union (now rutherfordium), and the city of Kurchatov in Kazakhstan and the crater Kurchatov on the Moon bear his name.
