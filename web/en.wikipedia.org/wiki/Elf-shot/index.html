Elf-shot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Elf-shot (Old English ylfa gescot) is a medical condition described in Anglo-Saxon medical texts, believed to be caused by invisible elves shooting the victim with magical projectiles. Sudden, sharp internal pains in humans and livestock — of the kind now attributed to rheumatism, arthritis, muscle cramps or strokes — were explained as the wounds of elf arrows.

In Anglo-Saxon medicine

The Old English metrical charm Wið færstice ("Against a sudden stitch"), preserved in the Lacnunga manuscript, is a remedy against elf-shot and related afflictions attributed to elves, Æsir and witches; it invokes the smiths who forged the offending spear and calls for the weapon to "melt". Leechbooks prescribe herbal preparations, amulets and ritual actions against the condition.

Folklore

In Scottish and Scandinavian folklore the belief survived for centuries: Neolithic flint arrowheads found in fields were identified as elf-bolts or elf-arrows, and cattle that sickened suddenly were said to have been "elf-shot". Such arrowheads were kept as protective charms, sometimes set in silver and worn, or dipped in water given to afflicted animals as a cure.
