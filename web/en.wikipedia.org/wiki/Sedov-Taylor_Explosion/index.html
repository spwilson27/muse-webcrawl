Sedov–Taylor blast wave

The expanding fireball of the Trinity test (1945), from whose published photographs G. I. Taylor estimated the bomb's yield.

The Sedov–Taylor blast wave (also called the Sedov–Taylor explosion or Sedov solution) is a self-similar solution of the fluid equations describing the expansion of a strong spherical blast wave produced by the sudden release of a large amount of energy at a point. It was derived independently by Leonid Sedov in the Soviet Union and G. I. Taylor in Britain (and, in related form, by John von Neumann in the United States) during the Second World War, and published after the war — Taylor's analysis appearing in 1950.

The solution

For an explosion releasing energy E instantaneously in a uniform gas of density ρ1, dimensional analysis shows that the radius R of the shock front grows as

R = ξ ( E t2 / ρ1 )1/5

where t is the time since the explosion and ξ is a dimensionless constant of order unity determined by solving the self-similar flow equations (it depends on the gas's ratio of specific heats). The shock velocity therefore decreases as t−3/5. Behind the shock, the Sedov solution gives the spatial profiles of density, pressure and velocity in terms of a single similarity variable.

The Trinity estimate

Taylor famously applied the R ∝ t2/5 law to a sequence of declassified photographs of the 1945 Trinity nuclear test, whose time and length scales were visible in the frames. His simple analysis yielded an energy of roughly 20 kilotons of TNT — close to the then-secret official figure — and caused consternation that so sensitive a number could be extracted from public photographs plus elementary scaling.

Applications

In astrophysics, the Sedov–Taylor phase is the second, adiabatic stage of a supernova remnant's evolution, after free expansion and before radiative cooling becomes important; the same scaling describes the expansion of ionised gas regions and wind-blown bubbles. The solution is also a standard test problem in computational fluid dynamics.
