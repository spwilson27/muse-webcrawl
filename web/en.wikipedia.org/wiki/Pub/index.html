A pub (short for public house) is a licensed establishment for drinking alcohol, forming a distinctive social institution in the United Kingdom, Ireland and countries settled by the British and Irish, including Australia, New Zealand and Canada. Pubs are characterised by a bar at which customers order and pay for drinks, a comparatively informal atmosphere, a licensee known as the landlord or publican, and traditionally a focus on draught beer — particularly cask ale in Britain. Many pubs also serve food ("pub grub" and the gastropub), host darts, pool and quiz nights, and function as community hubs.

History

The pub descends from Roman taverns and Anglo-Saxon alehouses, through the coaching inns of the stagecoach era. The Gin Craze of the early 18th century prompted tighter licensing, and the Beerhouse Act 1830, intended to wean drinkers from spirits to beer, triggered a boom in beer houses. Victorian legislation and magistrates' licensing consolidated the trade; large breweries acquired estates of "tied houses" selling only their products, a structure partly dismantled by the Beer Orders of 1989. Pub numbers in the UK have declined steadily — from around 69,000 in 1980 to under 40,000 by the 2020s — under pressure from supermarket alcohol prices, smoking bans (2006–07), changing habits and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Types and customs

Traditional pubs often divide into a public bar (plain, for regulars and games) and a saloon or lounge bar (plusher, historically pricier). Customs include buying rounds, the "local" as social anchor, pub signs descended from a legal requirement to display a pictorial sign for illiterate customers, and last-orders calls governed since 2005 by the Licensing Act 2003's flexible hours in England and Wales. In Ireland, pubs likewise anchor community life and added significance as centres of traditional music sessions.
