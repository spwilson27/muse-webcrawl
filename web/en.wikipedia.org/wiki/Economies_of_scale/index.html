Economies of scale

In microeconomics, economies of scale are the cost advantages that enterprises obtain due to their scale of operation, typically measured by the amount of output produced. The cost per unit of output generally decreases with increasing scale as fixed costs are spread out over more units of output. Often operational efficiency increases as well. Conversely, beyond a certain point, diseconomies of scale — rising average costs from bureaucracy, coordination problems, and slower decision-making — can set in.

Sources

Economies of scale arise from several sources: spreading fixed and research costs; bulk purchasing of inputs at discounts; technical efficiencies of larger machinery (such as the engineering "two-thirds rule" for vessels and pipelines); specialisation and division of labour; cheaper financing for large firms; and marketing economies. Network effects and learning-by-doing can also lower unit costs as scale grows.

Internal and external economies

Internal economies come from the firm's own expansion, while external economies arise when an industry clusters geographically — giving access to skilled labour pools, specialist suppliers, and knowledge spillovers, as in industrial districts and technology hubs.

Long-run average cost

The long-run average cost curve is classically U-shaped: average cost falls as output rises toward the minimum efficient scale, then flattens and eventually rises with diseconomies of scale. The shape of this curve helps explain market structure — whether an industry tends toward many small firms or a few large ones, a natural monopoly being the extreme case where one firm can supply the market at lowest cost.
