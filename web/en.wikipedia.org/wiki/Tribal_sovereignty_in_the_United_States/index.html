Tribal sovereignty in the United States is the concept of the inherent authority of Indigenous tribes to govern themselves within the borders of the United States. The U.S. federal government recognises certain Native American tribes as having a government-to-government relationship, describing them as "domestic dependent nations" — a term coined by Chief Justice John Marshall in Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831).

Legal foundations

Tribal sovereignty predates the United States; it is inherent, not granted by the federal government. The Commerce Clause of the U.S. Constitution gives Congress power to regulate commerce "with the Indian Tribes", and hundreds of treaties were made between the United States and tribal nations. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832) the Supreme Court held that states have no authority in tribal territory.

Historical development

Federal policy swung between removal (1830s), allotment and assimilation under the Dawes Act (1887), reorganisation under the Indian Reorganization Act (1934), termination in the 1950s, and self-determination from the 1970s, reflected in the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975.

Modern scope

Federally recognised tribes exercise powers including defining membership, administering justice through tribal courts, taxation and zoning on tribal land, though these powers are limited by federal law and Supreme Court decisions. Landmark modern cases include McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), which affirmed that much of eastern Oklahoma remains reservation land for purposes of federal criminal law. The Trail of Tears and the Indian Removal Act remain central historical background to the modern doctrine.
