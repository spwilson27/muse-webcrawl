Immersion cooling

Immersion cooling is a method of cooling computer hardware — especially in data centers — by submerging components in a thermally conductive but electrically insulating liquid, such as mineral oil or engineered dielectric fluids. Heat passes directly from components to the liquid, which is then cooled by circulation through a heat exchanger or by evaporation and condensation in two-phase systems.

Types

Single-phase immersion keeps the coolant liquid; pumps circulate it to a heat exchanger. Two-phase immersion uses a low-boiling-point fluid that evaporates at the component surface and condenses on a coil above the bath, transferring heat very efficiently without pumps in the tank.

Advantages and challenges

Immersion can dramatically reduce cooling energy use and power usage effectiveness (PUE), eliminate fans, reduce noise and dust, and allow very high rack densities — attractive for supercomputing, cryptocurrency mining and AI training hardware. Challenges include fluid cost and environmental concerns (some two-phase fluids are potent greenhouse gases), maintenance practices, material compatibility, and the need for hardware designed or adapted for submersion.

History

Oil immersion was used for electrical transformers long before computing. Supercomputers such as the Cray-2 (1985) famously used liquid immersion with Fluorinert, and the technique has seen renewed commercial interest since the 2010s as power densities have risen.
