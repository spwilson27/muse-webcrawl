In mathematics, a square root of a number x is a number y such that y2 = x; in other words, a number y whose square (the result of multiplying the number by itself) is x. For example, 4 and −4 are square roots of 16, because 42 = 16 and (−4)2 = 16.

Every nonnegative real number x has a unique nonnegative square root, called the principal square root or simply the square root, denoted √x. The symbol √ is called the radical sign, and the number under it is the radicand. Square roots of negative numbers are not real; they are handled in the complex number system, where every nonzero number has two square roots.

History

The Old Babylonian clay tablet YBC 7289 (c. 1800–1600 BC) gives an approximation of √2 accurate to six decimal places — 1.41421296 — remarkable evidence of Mesopotamian computational skill. Egyptian and Indian mathematicians also computed roots; the Indian Śulba Sūtras contain another close value for √2. The Greeks proved that √2 is irrational, a discovery attributed to the Pythagoreans. The radical symbol √ was introduced by Christoff Rudolff in 1525, apparently derived from a stylised letter "r" for Latin radix ("root").

Properties

√(xy) = √x √y   (for x, y ≥ 0)

The principal square root function f(x) = √x maps the set of nonnegative real numbers onto itself. It is the inverse of squaring on that domain, satisfies √x = x1/2, and is used throughout geometry — most famously in the Pythagorean theorem, where the hypotenuse of a right triangle is the square root of the sum of the squares of the legs. The distance formula in Euclidean space, the quadratic formula, and the standard deviation in statistics all depend on square roots.

Computation

Methods for computing square roots include the ancient Babylonian (Heron's) method — an application of Newton's method that iterates x ← (x + a/x)/2 — the digit-by-digit "long division" algorithm once taught in schools, and Taylor-series and CORDIC methods in modern hardware. Fast inverse square root algorithms became famous in computer graphics, notably the 0x5f3759df routine used in Quake III Arena (1999).

Square roots of negative and complex numbers

In the complex numbers, every number has two square roots; for −1 they are the imaginary unit i and −i. The principal square root is defined by a branch cut, conventionally along the negative real axis, making √ a holomorphic function on the cut plane.
