Furman v. Georgia

Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972), was a landmark United States Supreme Court decision that held that the arbitrary and inconsistent imposition of the death penalty in the cases before the Court violated the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments, constituting cruel and unusual punishment. The decision effectively invalidated every capital punishment statute in the United States and commuted the sentences of more than 600 prisoners on death row.

Background

William Henry Furman was convicted of murder in Georgia after a householder was shot during a burglary. The case was consolidated with two others: Jackson v. Georgia, involving a death sentence for rape, and Branch v. Texas, a murder case. All three petitioners were Black men sentenced under statutes giving juries complete, unguided discretion over whether to impose death.

Decision

The Court ruled 5–4 in a brief per curiam opinion, with each justice in the majority writing separately. Justices William J. Brennan Jr. and Thurgood Marshall concluded that capital punishment was unconstitutional in all circumstances; Justices William O. Douglas, Potter Stewart, and Byron White rested on the arbitrary, capricious, and racially skewed way death sentences were actually imposed — Stewart famously likened the sentences to being "struck by lightning."

Aftermath

States responded by rewriting their capital statutes, either making death mandatory for certain crimes or introducing guided-discretion procedures with bifurcated trials. In Gregg v. Georgia (1976), the Court upheld Georgia's new guided-discretion statute, ending the four-year moratorium on executions that Furman had produced.
