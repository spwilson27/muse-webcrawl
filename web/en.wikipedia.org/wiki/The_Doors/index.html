The Doors

The Doors were an American rock band formed in Los Angeles in 1965, comprising vocalist Jim Morrison, keyboardist Ray Manzarek, guitarist Robby Krieger, and drummer John Densmore. They were among the most influential and controversial rock acts of the 1960s, primarily due to Morrison's lyrics and voice, theatrical stage persona, and the group's musical blending of blues, psychedelic rock and jazz influences. The band took its name from the title of Aldous Huxley's book The Doors of Perception, itself derived from a line in William Blake's The Marriage of Heaven and Hell.

History

Manzarek and Morrison met as film students at UCLA. After signing with Elektra Records, the band released its self-titled debut album in January 1967, featuring "Light My Fire", which spent three weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot 100. Follow-up albums included Strange Days (1967), Waiting for the Sun (1968, with the number-one single "Hello, I Love You"), The Soft Parade (1969), Morrison Hotel (1970) and L.A. Woman (1971). Morrison's alcohol dependence and erratic behaviour increasingly disrupted performances, most notoriously at a 1969 concert in Miami after which he was charged with lewd and lascivious behaviour.

Morrison's death and legacy

After recording L.A. Woman, Morrison moved to Paris, where he died on 3 July 1971 at the age of 27. The remaining members recorded two further albums before disbanding in 1973. The Doors have sold over 100 million records worldwide and were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1993. Their music remains a staple of classic rock radio, and Morrison—buried at Père Lachaise Cemetery in Paris—remains an enduring cultural icon of the 1960s counterculture.
