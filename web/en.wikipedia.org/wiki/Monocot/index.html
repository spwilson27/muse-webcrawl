Monocotyledons, commonly referred to as monocots, are a clade of flowering plants (angiosperms) whose seeds typically contain only one embryonic leaf, or cotyledon. They form one of the two great traditional divisions of flowering plants, contrasted with the dicotyledons (dicots), although modern classifications treat monocots as a clade while "dicots" are paraphyletic. There are roughly 60,000 monocot species, about a quarter of all angiosperms.

Characteristics

  • A single cotyledon in the seed.
  • Leaves usually with parallel venation.
  • Flower parts typically in multiples of three.
  • Vascular bundles scattered through the stem, which therefore usually lacks true secondary growth (no woody cambium ring).
  • Fibrous, adventitious root systems rather than a main taproot.
  • Pollen with a single pore or furrow (monosulcate), an ancestral feature.

Major groups and importance

Monocots include the grass family (Poaceae) — wheat, rice, maize, sugarcane and bamboo, the foundation of human food supply — the palm family (Arecaceae), the orchid family (Orchidaceae, one of the two largest plant families), lilies, irises, bananas, gingers, sedges and onions. In the APG IV system the monocots comprise 11 orders, including Asparagales, Liliales, Poales, Commelinales and Zingiberales.

Evolution

Monocots diverged from other angiosperms over 100 million years ago in the Cretaceous period. Their success rests partly on adaptations to herbaceous, fast-growing life histories and, in grasses, to open habitats and grazing.
