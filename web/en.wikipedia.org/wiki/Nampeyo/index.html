Nampeyo

Nampeyo (c. 1859 – 20 July 1942) was a Hopi-Tewa potter credited with reviving and transforming Native American ceramics in the American Southwest. Working from the village of Hano on First Mesa in Arizona, she became one of the most famous Indigenous artists of her era.

Life and work

Nampeyo learned pottery from her grandmother and initially made traditional Polacca ware for the tourist trade. In the 1890s, inspired by prehistoric Sikyatki pottery excavated near her home by archaeologist J. Walter Fewkes — and with the encouragement of traders such as Thomas Keam — she began studying the shard designs of the fifteenth- and sixteenth-century ware and recreating its forms and finely painted motifs in her own work.

Her "Sikyatki Revival" style, with its elegant low-shouldered vessels and sweeping abstract bird and feather designs, proved immensely popular with collectors. Nampeyo demonstrated at the Grand Canyon and at expositions, and her pottery entered major museum collections. Though her eyesight failed late in life, she continued forming vessels while her daughters painted them; several descendants, including Fannie Nampeyo, carried on the family tradition.
