Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE), commonly known as mad cow disease, is an incurable and inevitably fatal neurodegenerative disease of cattle. It belongs to the transmissible spongiform encephalopathies caused by prions — misfolded proteins that induce other proteins to misfold. BSE spread through feed containing meat-and-bone meal made from infected cattle, causing an epidemic in the United Kingdom in the 1980s and 1990s, and is believed to have caused variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans who ate contaminated beef products.

Signs and pathology

Infected cattle show progressive nervous signs: changes in behaviour, apprehension, tremors, incoordination and loss of condition. Microscopic examination of the brain reveals spongy vacuolation of neurons and deposits of the abnormal prion protein. The incubation period is long — typically four to six years — so most cases occur in adult dairy cows.

The British epidemic

BSE was first identified in 1986 in southern England. Changes in rendering practice had allowed the agent to survive in meat-and-bone meal fed back to cattle, amplifying the epidemic, which peaked in 1992–93 with over 37,000 cases in a year; more than 180,000 cases were confirmed in total and millions of cattle were slaughtered. A ruminant feed ban (1988, later extended) gradually brought the epidemic under control.

Human health

In 1996 the UK government announced a probable link between BSE and a new human disease, variant CJD, which had killed ten young people. The announcement triggered a worldwide ban on British beef exports and a crisis in the beef industry. In total around 178 people in the UK died of vCJD. Control measures — feed bans, removal of specified risk materials, and surveillance testing — have since been adopted internationally.
