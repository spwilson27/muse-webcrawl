Legal drafting

Legal drafting is the preparation of legal documents — contracts, statutes, wills, pleadings, deeds, regulations, and similar instruments — in written form intended to produce legal effects. Because such documents create, modify, or record rights and obligations, drafting emphasizes precision, certainty, and the careful anticipation of disputes about meaning.

Principles

Good drafting is generally said to rest on clarity, precision, and consistency. Defined terms are used in a single consistent sense throughout the document; operative language distinguishes duties ("shall", or "must" in modern usage) from permissions ("may") and declarations; and contingencies are addressed expressly rather than left to implication. Drafters conventionally organize a document in a logical order — parties, recitals, definitions, operative provisions, boilerplate, and execution — and number clauses so that cross-references can be made exact.

Ambiguity in drafting is hazardous because interpretive rules may work against the drafter: under the contra proferentem doctrine, ambiguities in a contract may be construed against the party that drafted it. Courts also distinguish patent ambiguity (apparent on the face of the document) from latent ambiguity (arising when language is applied to external facts).

Legislative drafting

Statutes and regulations are drafted by specialist legislative counsel in many jurisdictions — for example the Office of the Parliamentary Counsel in the United Kingdom and the Offices of the Legislative Counsel in the United States Senate and House of Representatives. Legislative drafting must fit the new measure within the existing body of law, provide commencement and transitional provisions, and comply with constitutional requirements.

Plain language movement

Since the late twentieth century there has been a sustained movement to replace archaic legalese — "hereinafter", "aforesaid", doubled synonyms such as "null and void" — with plain language that lay readers can understand. Consumer-protection and plain-writing legislation in several countries now requires that consumer contracts, government forms, and disclosure documents be written clearly. Supporters argue that precision and plainness are compatible; critics caution that settled legal expressions carry case law that fresh wording may unsettle.
