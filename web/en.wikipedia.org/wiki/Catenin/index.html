Catenin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Catenins are a family of proteins found in complexes with cadherin cell-adhesion molecules of animal cells. The name catenin derives from the Latin catena, meaning "chain," reflecting their role in linking cadherins to the actin cytoskeleton. The first two catenins identified were α-catenin and β-catenin; β-catenin was later found to be a central component of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway.

Types

There are four main types of catenin:

  • α-catenin (CTNNA1, CTNNA2, CTNNA3)
  • β-catenin (CTNNB1)
  • γ-catenin (plakoglobin, JUP)
  • δ-catenin (p120 catenin, CTNND1; and CTNND2)

Structure and function

All catenins except α-catenin contain armadillo repeats, a roughly 42-amino-acid motif that mediates protein–protein interactions. In adherens junctions, β-catenin binds the cytoplasmic tail of classical cadherins and recruits α-catenin, which in turn connects the complex to actin filaments, either directly or through proteins such as vinculin and formins.

Free cytosolic β-catenin is normally targeted for degradation by the destruction complex (containing APC, axin, and GSK3β). When Wnt ligands bind their receptors, the destruction complex is inhibited, β-catenin accumulates, enters the nucleus, and activates transcription together with TCF/LEF transcription factors. Mutations that stabilise β-catenin, or that inactivate APC, are frequent drivers of colorectal and other cancers.

Clinical significance

Loss of catenin-mediated adhesion contributes to tumour invasion and metastasis. Germline mutations in CTNNA1 cause a neurodevelopmental syndrome with visual defects, while plakoglobin mutations cause Naxos disease, an arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy with woolly hair and palmoplantar keratoderma.
