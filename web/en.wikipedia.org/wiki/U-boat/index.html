U-boat is an English rendering of the German word U-Boot (a shortening of Unterseeboot, "undersea boat"), and refers to military submarines operated by Germany, particularly in the First and Second World Wars. U-boats were most famously used in the Battle of the Atlantic, where they attempted to sever the maritime supply lines between North America and Britain.

First World War

At the outbreak of the First World War in 1914, Germany possessed fewer than 40 operational U-boats. They achieved early successes against British warships, including the sinking of three armoured cruisers by U-9 in September 1914. In February 1915 Germany declared a war zone around the British Isles and began unrestricted submarine warfare against merchant shipping.

The sinking of the passenger liner RMS Lusitania in May 1915, with the loss of 1,198 lives including 128 Americans, caused international outrage and led Germany to temporarily restrict its campaign. The resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare in February 1917 was a major factor in bringing the United States into the war. By the armistice, U-boats had sunk over 5,000 ships totalling more than 12 million tons.

Second World War

Under Admiral Karl Dönitz, the U-boat arm (U-Bootwaffe) grew rapidly after 1939. Operating from bases in occupied France and Norway, U-boats hunted Allied convoys in "wolfpacks" (Rudel), coordinated by radio from shore headquarters. The Type VII was the workhorse of the fleet, with over 700 built.

The turning point came in "Black May" 1943, when improved Allied escort carriers, very-long-range aircraft, radar, high-frequency direction finding and the breaking of the naval Enigma cipher made U-boat operations prohibitively costly. Germany lost 783 U-boats and some 28,000 of the roughly 40,000 men who served in them — the highest casualty rate of any German service branch. Late-war innovations such as the snorkel and the high-speed Type XXI "Elektroboot" arrived too late to alter the outcome.

Legacy

The U-boat campaigns remain defining episodes of naval history, and several boats survive as museum ships, including U-505 in Chicago and U-995 at Laboe, Germany. The term "U-boat" is also applied to submarines of the modern German Navy and, historically, to Austro-Hungarian submarines of the First World War.
