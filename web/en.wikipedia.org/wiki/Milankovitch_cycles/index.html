Milankovitch cycles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A diagram of Earth's changing orbital eccentricity, axial tilt and precession over tens of thousands of years]
A diagram of Earth's changing orbital eccentricity, axial tilt and precession over tens of thousands of years

Milankovitch cycles are the collective effects of changes in Earth's movements on its climate over thousands of years. Named for the Serbian geophysicist Milutin Milanković, who calculated them in the 1920s–30s, they comprise orbital eccentricity (~100,000 and 405,000-year cycles), axial obliquity (~41,000 years) and axial precession (~26,000 years). By redistributing sunlight by latitude and season, they pace the glacial cycles of the Quaternary.

Mechanism

The cycles barely change Earth's total annual sunlight; instead they change where and when it falls. High-latitude northern summer insolation — governing whether winter snow survives to build ice sheets — is the critical lever: when northern summers are cool, glaciation advances. Feedbacks (ice albedo, CO₂ released from oceans) amplify the orbital nudge into planet-wide climate swings of 5–6 °C.

Confirmation

Milanković's theory languished until deep-sea sediment cores in the 1970s (Hays, Imbrie and Shackleton, 1976) matched climate records to the orbital periods, confirming astronomical pacing of the ice ages. The dominant glacial rhythm switched from 41,000 to ~100,000 years about a million years ago (the Mid-Pleistocene Transition), for reasons still debated.

Present implication

On orbital grounds alone Earth would be heading, exceptionally slowly, toward glaciation over the next tens of thousands of years — the current interglacial may be anomalously long — but anthropogenic warming is expected to postpone the next ice age by at least 50,000 years on mainstream modelling.
