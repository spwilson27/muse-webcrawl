The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (French: Notre-Dame de Paris, "Our Lady of Paris") is a French Gothic novel by Victor Hugo, published in 1831. Its title refers to Notre-Dame Cathedral in Paris, the true protagonist of the book, Hugo wrote, intending the novel in part as a plea for the preservation of Gothic architecture then being demolished or neglected.

Plot

Set in Paris in 1482, the novel centres on the cathedral's deaf, hunchbacked bell-ringer Quasimodo; the beautiful Romani street dancer Esmeralda; the archdeacon Claude Frollo, whose obsessive desire for Esmeralda drives the tragedy; the gallant but faithless Captain Phoebus; and the poet Pierre Gringoire. Esmeralda, falsely convicted of murder, is hanged; Quasimodo, who had saved her once by sanctuary in the cathedral, kills Frollo and dies embracing her corpse in the charnel house of Montfaucon. Hugo weaves in long meditations on architecture, printing, fate — the Greek word ananke (necessity), which he claimed inspired the work — and the Parisian underworld of the Court of Miracles.

Legacy

The novel revived popular interest in medieval Paris and helped spur the Gothic Revival and the great nineteenth-century restoration of Notre-Dame. It has been adapted countless times, most famously the 1923 Lon Chaney and 1939 Charles Laughton films, Disney's 1996 animated feature, and the stage musical Notre-Dame de Paris (1998). Interest surged again after the 2019 fire at the cathedral, when the novel topped French bestseller lists.
