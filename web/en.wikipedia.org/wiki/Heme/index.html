Heme (American English), or haem (Commonwealth English), is an iron-containing compound that is a precursor to hemoglobin, which is necessary to bind oxygen in the bloodstream. It is a complex organic ring structure — a porphyrin — coordinating a single iron atom, which can bind or release molecular oxygen. Heme is biosynthesized in both the bone marrow and the liver.

Among the metalloproteins, heme proteins serve diverse biological functions, including oxygen transport (as in hemoglobin and myoglobin), electron transfer (as in the cytochromes of the respiratory chain and photosynthesis), catalysis (as in catalase and the cytochrome P450 enzymes), and sensing of diatomic gases.

Structure and types

Heme consists of an iron atom held in the center of a large heterocyclic organic ring called a porphyrin, made up of four pyrrolic subunits linked by methine bridges. Not all porphyrins contain iron, but a substantial fraction of porphyrin-containing metalloproteins have heme as their prosthetic group; these are known as hemoproteins.

The most common types are heme b, found in hemoglobin, myoglobin, and cytochrome b; heme a, found in cytochrome a and cytochrome c oxidase; and heme c, covalently attached to the protein in cytochrome c. The types differ in the side chains attached to the porphyrin ring.

Function

In hemoglobin, each of the four subunits carries a heme group whose iron atom binds one oxygen molecule, allowing a single hemoglobin molecule to carry four O2 molecules from the lungs to the tissues. The iron must remain in the ferrous (Fe2+) state to bind oxygen; oxidation to the ferric (Fe3+) state produces methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen. In the cytochromes, by contrast, the controlled shuttling of the iron between Fe2+ and Fe3+ states is exactly what permits electron transfer in respiration.

Synthesis and degradation

Heme synthesis begins with the condensation of succinyl-CoA and glycine to form δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), a step catalyzed in animals by ALA synthase — the rate-limiting step, subject to feedback repression by heme itself. Through a series of steps involving porphobilinogen and the porphyrinogens, protoporphyrin IX is assembled; the enzyme ferrochelatase then inserts Fe2+ to complete the molecule. Defects in the enzymes of this pathway cause the porphyrias, a group of disorders marked by accumulation of porphyrin precursors.

Senescent red blood cells are broken down in the spleen and liver; the enzyme heme oxygenase opens the heme ring to produce biliverdin (a green pigment), carbon monoxide, and free iron, which is recycled. Biliverdin is then reduced to bilirubin (yellow), which is transported to the liver, conjugated, and excreted in bile. Excess bilirubin causes jaundice.

Dietary heme

Heme iron is the form of iron found in animal tissues, especially red meat and fish, and is absorbed from the diet much more efficiently than non-heme (plant-derived) iron. High dietary heme intake has been studied in connection with the health effects of red and processed meat consumption.
