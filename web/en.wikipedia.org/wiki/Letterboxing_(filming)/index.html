Letterboxing (filming)

Letterboxing is the practice of transferring film shot in a widescreen aspect ratio to a standard-width video format while preserving the film's original aspect ratio. The resulting image has mattes — black bars — above and below it, resembling the slot of a letter box, from which the practice takes its name.

Background

Early television used a 4:3 (1.33:1) aspect ratio, while theatrical films increasingly used wider formats such as 1.85:1 and 2.39:1. To show the whole frame on television without cropping, distributors either used pan and scan, which cuts off the sides, or letterboxing, which preserves the full composition at the cost of a smaller image.

Use on home video and television

Letterboxing became common on laserdisc and DVD releases aimed at film enthusiasts, and was used for music videos and some broadcasts. With the adoption of 16:9 widescreen televisions, letterboxing is now mainly seen when very wide films (2.39:1) are shown, producing thinner bars. The vertical equivalent for narrow content on wide screens is called pillarboxing.
