Donaldson–Thomas theory

Donaldson–Thomas theory
A holomorphic curve in a Calabi–Yau threefold
FieldAlgebraic geometry, mathematical physics
Introduced bySimon Donaldson, Richard Thomas (c. 1998)

In mathematics, specifically algebraic geometry, Donaldson–Thomas theory is the theory of Donaldson–Thomas invariants of a Calabi–Yau threefold. For a given compact Calabi–Yau threefold, its Donaldson–Thomas invariant is a virtual count of points in the threefold. In particular, Donaldson–Thomas invariants count holomorphic curves in the threefold weighted by their geometry; they are named after mathematicians Simon Donaldson and Richard Thomas, who introduced the theory around 1998 in analogy with Donaldson's invariants of four-manifolds.

Definition and properties

Technically, the invariants are defined using moduli spaces of coherent sheaves (or stable pairs in the related Pandharipande–Thomas theory) on the threefold. Because these moduli spaces are not generally smooth of the expected dimension, the count is made rigorous through a virtual fundamental class, and the invariant is defined as a weighted Euler characteristic using the Behrend function. The resulting counts are deformation-invariant integers assembled into generating functions.

Relation to other theories

The MNOP conjecture, proposed by Maulik, Nekrasov, Okounkov, and Pandharipande, relates the Donaldson–Thomas partition function of a threefold to its Gromov–Witten partition function, which counts curves in the sense of quantum cohomology; the correspondence has been proved in many cases, notably for toric threefolds. Wall-crossing formulas of Joyce–Song and Kontsevich–Soibelman describe how the invariants change as the notion of stability varies, making the theory central to modern enumerative geometry and to BPS state counting in string theory.

Motivic and categorified extensions

Refined versions of the theory include motivic Donaldson–Thomas invariants and cohomological Hall algebras, which lift the numerical counts to richer algebraic structures.
