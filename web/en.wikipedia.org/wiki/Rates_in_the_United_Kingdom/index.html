Rates in the United Kingdom

Rates were a tax on property in the United Kingdom used to fund local government services. Originating in the Poor Relief Act of 1601, which empowered parish overseers to levy a rate on occupiers of land and property, the system of local rating survived in various forms for nearly four centuries. Domestic rates were abolished in England and Wales in 1993, when they were replaced by the Council Tax; business rates, formally National Non-Domestic Rates, continue to be levied on commercial property.

How rates were assessed

Each property was assigned a rateable value, notionally the annual rent the property might command. Local authorities set a rate poundage — the amount charged per pound of rateable value — and the product of the two determined the bill. Valuation was carried out by district valuers of the Inland Revenue, and periodic revaluations were politically contentious because they redistributed the burden between households.

Abolition and replacement

Domestic rates were widely criticised as unfair because a single person in a large house could pay many times more than several earners sharing a small property. The Thatcher government replaced rates in Scotland with the Community Charge (the "poll tax") in 1989 and in England and Wales in 1990, but the charge proved deeply unpopular and was itself replaced by Council Tax in 1993. In Northern Ireland, domestic rates survived until reforms in 2007.

Business rates

Non-domestic rates remain a major source of local government finance. Since 1990 the poundage (multiplier) has been set centrally, with proceeds redistributed between authorities, although reforms since 2013 have allowed councils to retain a share of growth in their local rates base.
