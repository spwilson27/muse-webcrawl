Robert Koch Institute

Founded: July 1, 1891
Headquarters: Berlin, Germany
Parent agency: Federal Ministry of Health
Named after: Robert Koch
Role: Public health institute

The Robert Koch Institute (RKI; German: Robert Koch-Institut) is the German federal government's central scientific institution for public health, responsible for disease control and prevention. It is the counterpart of agencies such as the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and reports to the Federal Ministry of Health.

History

The institute was founded in 1891 as the Royal Prussian Institute for Infectious Diseases, with Robert Koch—discoverer of the tuberculosis and cholera bacilli—as its first director. Its early researchers included Paul Ehrlich and Emil von Behring, pioneers of immunology. After Koch's death in 1910 it was renamed in his honor. In the twentieth century the institute passed through the Weimar and Nazi periods—during which its staff included scientists implicated in criminal wartime experiments—and postwar division, when East Germany maintained separate structures. After reunification the RKI absorbed parts of the former East German health institutes and in 1994 took over the functions of the dissolved Federal Health Office (Bundesgesundheitsamt).

Functions

The RKI monitors infectious diseases under the Protection against Infection Act, maintains notification and surveillance systems, investigates outbreaks, and issues recommendations through bodies such as the Standing Committee on Vaccination (STIKO), whose advice shapes the national immunization schedule. It publishes the weekly Epidemiologisches Bulletin, conducts population health monitoring through surveys and studies such as DEGS and KiGGS, and operates high-containment laboratories for dangerous pathogens. The institute came to wide public prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic, when its daily situation reports, case dashboards, and press briefings by president Lothar Wieler became fixtures of German public life.
