Body farm

A body farm is a research facility where decomposition of human bodies can be studied in a variety of settings. The first body farm was established in 1981 by anthropologist William M. Bass at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, after he realised that little was known about what happens to a human body after death in different environments. The research enables scientists and law enforcement to estimate time of death and to improve methods of identifying human remains. Facilities now operate in the United States, Australia, the Netherlands and elsewhere.

Research

Donated bodies are placed in scenarios that mimic real forensic cases — on the surface, buried in shallow graves, submerged in water, or enclosed in car boots — and are monitored as they decompose. Researchers document the sequence of decay, insect succession (particularly blowfly and beetle activity), scavenger behaviour, and the effects of temperature, moisture and soil chemistry. The data are used to refine estimates of the post-mortem interval, a crucial question in homicide investigations.

The Tennessee facility

The University of Tennessee Anthropological Research Facility, commonly known as "the Body Farm", occupies about 2.5 acres of wooded land in Knoxville and receives roughly 100 donated bodies per year, with several thousand people pre-registered as donors. It popularised the field through Patricia Cornwell's 1994 novel The Body Farm. Western Carolina University opened the second such facility in 2006, and Texas State University's facility at Freeman Ranch is among the largest. The Australian Facility for Taphonomic Experimental Research opened near Sydney in 2016, and the Amsterdam Medical Center established the first European facility in 2018.

Donation and ethics

Bodies come from voluntary whole-body donation programmes and unclaimed remains. After decomposition is complete, skeletons are cleaned and added to documented skeletal collections used for teaching and research.
