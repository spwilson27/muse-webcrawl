Inductive reasoning

Inductive reasoning is a method of reasoning in which a general principle is derived from a body of observations. It consists of making broad generalizations based on specific observations. Inductive reasoning is distinct from deductive reasoning, whose conclusions are certain if its premises are true: the truth of an inductive conclusion is at best probable, based upon the evidence given.

Types

Common forms include statistical generalization (inferring properties of a population from a sample), causal inference (inferring that one thing causes another from patterns of association), prediction (inferring future instances from past ones), and argument by analogy. The scientific method relies on induction when hypotheses are tested and confirmed by repeated observation.

The problem of induction

David Hume famously argued that inductive inference cannot be rationally justified without circularity: the belief that the future will resemble the past is itself based only on past experience of that resemblance. This "problem of induction" remains a central topic in the philosophy of science. Karl Popper responded by arguing that science does not use induction at all, but instead proceeds by conjecture and falsification; others have defended induction through Bayesian probability theory, in which evidence updates degrees of belief.

Enumerative induction and counterexamples

The classic illustration is the inference "all swans are white" drawn from European observations — overturned by the discovery of black swans in Australia. Good inductive arguments therefore attend to sample size, representativeness, and the search for counterexamples.
