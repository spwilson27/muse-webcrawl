Oogenesis

Oogenesis: the development of an ovum from an oogonium.

Oogenesis (or ovogenesis) is the differentiation of the ovum (egg cell) into a cell competent to further develop when fertilised. It is developed from the primary oocyte by maturation. In mammals, oogenesis begins in the germinal epithelium of the foetal ovary and is notable for its long arrests in meiosis: a primary oocyte may remain suspended in prophase I from before birth until ovulation decades later.

Stages in mammals

Primordial germ cells give rise to oogonia, which proliferate by mitosis in the foetal ovary and then enter meiosis, becoming primary oocytes. These arrest in the dictyate stage of prophase I, enclosed in primordial follicles. A female is born with essentially her lifetime supply — on the order of one to two million primary oocytes, of which only a few hundred will ever be ovulated.

At puberty, under the influence of follicle-stimulating hormone, a cohort of follicles develops each cycle; the dominant follicle's oocyte completes meiosis I, dividing asymmetrically into a large secondary oocyte and a small first polar body. The secondary oocyte begins meiosis II but arrests at metaphase II, and is ovulated in this state. Meiosis II is completed only if fertilisation occurs, producing the mature ovum and a second polar body.

Comparison with spermatogenesis

Unlike spermatogenesis, which produces four functional sperm from each primary spermatocyte and proceeds continuously from puberty, oogenesis yields a single ovum (plus polar bodies) per primary oocyte, conserving cytoplasm and nutrients in the egg. The asymmetric divisions and the decades-long dictyate arrest are thought to contribute to the rising incidence of aneuploidy with maternal age.

Folliculogenesis

Oocyte development is inseparable from the growth of its surrounding follicle: granulosa and theca cells proliferate, an antrum forms, and the mature Graafian follicle ruptures at ovulation, releasing the secondary oocyte into the fallopian tube.
