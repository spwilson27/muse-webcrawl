Harvester Case

Ex parte H.V. McKay
Citation(1907) 2 CAR 1
CourtCommonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration
JudgeH. B. Higgins J
Decided8 November 1907

The Harvester Case, formally Ex parte H.V. McKay (1907) 2 CAR 1, is a landmark Australian labour law judgment of the Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration, delivered by Justice H. B. Higgins in 1907. The case established the concept of the "basic wage" in Australia.

The case arose from an application by Hugh Victor McKay, a manufacturer of agricultural machinery whose Sunshine Harvester Works was one of Australia's largest factories, for a declaration that his business was fair and reasonable so as to be exempt from excise duty under the Excise Tariff Act 1906. The Act imposed an excise on agricultural implements unless the manufacturer paid wages considered "fair and reasonable".

The decision

Higgins inquired into the living costs of workers and determined that a fair and reasonable wage for an unskilled labourer must support a man, his wife, and three children in "frugal comfort". He fixed that amount at 7 shillings per day, or 42 shillings (£2 2s) per week. This became known as the basic wage, and it underpinned Australian wage-fixing for decades.

Significance

The Harvester judgment became the foundation of the Australian industrial relations system, linking wage determination to the cost of living and the needs of a family rather than solely to the market. The "Harvester standard" survived in various forms until wage indexation practices changed in the later twentieth century.
