Electro-osmosis

Electro-osmosis (also called electro-osmotic flow, EOF) is the motion of a liquid relative to a stationary charged surface under the influence of an applied electric field. It is one of the electrokinetic phenomena and is widely used in microfluidics, capillary electrophoresis and soil remediation.

Mechanism

When an electrolyte contacts a charged surface — for example, the deprotonated silanol groups of glass or silica — counter-ions accumulate in a thin electrical double layer next to the wall. An electric field applied along the surface exerts a force on the mobile charge in this layer, dragging the bulk liquid with it. Unlike pressure-driven flow, electro-osmotic flow has a nearly flat ("plug") velocity profile outside the double layer, which minimises dispersion in analytical separations. The flow velocity is proportional to the applied field and to the zeta potential of the surface.

History

The phenomenon was reported by Ferdinand Friedrich Reuss in 1809, who observed that water could be driven through a clay plug by an electric current — the first published description of electrokinetic effects.

Applications

Electro-osmosis pumps fluids in lab-on-a-chip devices without moving parts, drives the bulk flow that carries analytes in capillary zone electrophoresis, dewateres and consolidates wet soils and sludges, and can be used to remove contaminants from ground in electrokinetic remediation.
