Television series

A television series is a set of television programs sharing the same title, characters, and fictional world, broadcast in instalments called episodes, usually grouped into seasons (in Britain, "series"). Series may be scripted or unscripted and may run for a fixed number of episodes or indefinitely until cancelled.

Types

  • Episodic series — each episode tells a self-contained story with continuing characters (classic sitcoms and procedurals).
  • Serial — episodes continue an ongoing storyline; includes soap operas and heavily serialized dramas.
  • Miniseries / limited series — a pre-planned, closed story told over a few episodes.
  • Anthology series — each episode or season presents a new story and cast (e.g., Black Mirror).
  • Unscripted series — reality, documentary, and competition formats with recurring structures.

Production cycle

A series usually begins with a pilot episode commissioned to test the concept. If ordered to series, writers' rooms plan season arcs and individual episodes; production proceeds block by block under a showrunner. Networks traditionally ordered 22-episode seasons for broadcast schedules, while cable and streaming series more often run 6–13 episodes per season.

Viewing patterns

Historically, episodes premiered weekly, building habitual audiences and shared cultural moments. Streaming services popularized releasing entire seasons at once, enabling binge-watching and changing narrative pacing. Successful series can run for decades — The Simpsons, on air since 1989, is the longest-running American scripted prime-time series.

Economics

Series are expensive flagship products: they anchor network schedules, drive streaming subscriptions, and generate long-tail value through syndication and licensing. Reaching roughly 100 episodes was long considered the threshold for profitable daily syndication in the United States.
