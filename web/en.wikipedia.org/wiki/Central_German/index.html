Central German (German: Mitteldeutsch) is a group of High German dialects spoken in central Germany, forming the transitional band between Low German in the north and Upper German in the south. The Central German area is defined by the partial application of the High German consonant shift: its dialects shift only some of the consonants affected further south, lying between the maken/machen (Benrath) line to the north and the appel/apfel (Speyer) line to the south.

Subdivisions

West Central German includes Ripuarian (around Cologne and Aachen), Moselle Franconian (including Luxembourgish, now a standard language of Luxembourg), Rhine Franconian (Palatine and Hessian varieties) and the transitional Central Hessian and North Hessian groups. East Central German includes Thuringian, Upper Saxon (the dialect region around Dresden and Leipzig) and the Silesian and High Prussian dialects formerly spoken further east. Yiddish derives historically from medieval Central (and Upper) German varieties.

Significance

East Central German — particularly the language of the Saxon chancery and of Martin Luther's Bible translation — was highly influential in the formation of modern Standard German. Today the traditional dialects are receding in favour of regional colloquial varieties closer to the standard, a process most advanced in the east, though Ripuarian and Palatine dialect culture remains vigorous, notably in carnival traditions.
