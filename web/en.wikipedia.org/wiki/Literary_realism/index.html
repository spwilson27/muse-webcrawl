Literary realism

Literary realism
Periodc. 1830s–early 1900s (flourishing); continues today
Key figuresHonoré de Balzac, Gustave Flaubert, George Eliot, Fyodor Dostoevsky, Leo Tolstoy, Mark Twain, Henry James
Related movementsNaturalism, social realism, psychological realism, magical realism

Literary realism is a movement in literature that attempts to represent familiar things as they are. Realist authors chose to depict everyday and banal activities and experiences, instead of using a romanticised or similarly stylised presentation. The movement arose in 19th-century France and Russia in reaction against Romanticism, and it is generally considered to have begun with the works of Honoré de Balzac and to have reached maturity in Gustave Flaubert's Madame Bovary (1857).

Background

Realism grew out of the social changes of the 19th century: industrialisation, urbanisation, the rise of the middle class, and developments in science and journalism. Realist writers sought an objective, almost documentary fidelity to ordinary life, favouring plausible plots, detailed description of milieu and character psychology over the exotic settings and heightened emotion of Romantic fiction.

Characteristics

  • Detailed, verisimilar description of settings, objects and social conditions.
  • Characters drawn from ordinary and middle-class life, portrayed with psychological complexity.
  • Plots that avoid melodrama and rely on plausible causation.
  • An interest in social institutions, money, class and work.
  • Use of free indirect speech to blend narration with a character's consciousness.

Major authors

In France, Balzac's La Comédie humaine and the novels of Stendhal and Flaubert defined the mode. In England, George Eliot's Middlemarch (1871–72) is often cited as the greatest realist novel in English. Russian realism produced Tolstoy's War and Peace and Anna Karenina and Dostoevsky's psychological novels. In the United States, William Dean Howells championed realism, practised by Mark Twain and Henry James. Scandinavian dramatist Henrik Ibsen carried realism into the theatre.

Related movements

Naturalism, associated with Émile Zola, applied a more deterministic, quasi-scientific method to realist technique. Later variants include social realism, socialist realism, psychological realism, kitchen-sink realism in post-war British drama, and magical realism, which blends realistic narration with fantastic elements.
