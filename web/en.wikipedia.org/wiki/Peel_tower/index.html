A typical peel tower: a defensible stone tower providing refuge from cross-border raids.

Peel towers (also spelled pele towers) are small fortified towers or tower houses built across the Anglo-Scottish border region in northern England and southern Scotland, chiefly between the 15th and 17th centuries. They were constructed during centuries of conflict between England and Scotland, when the border reivers made the region lawless and raiding a way of life.

Design

A peel tower typically had massive stone walls up to several feet thick, three or four storeys, and a single entrance at ground or first-floor level, protected by an iron gate (yett). The ground floor, often barrel-vaulted, sheltered livestock during raids; the family lived above, and a beacon fire on the roof could warn neighbours of approaching raiders. Scottish legislation of 1535 required landowners of a certain value to build a "pele" of stone and lime for the defence of their household and goods.

Surviving examples

Many peel towers survive, some incorporated into later country houses and farms. Notable examples include Smailholm Tower in the Scottish Borders, memorably visited by the young Walter Scott, and towers such as Gilnockie and Hollows associated with the Armstrong reiver clan.
