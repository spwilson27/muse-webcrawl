Private international law

Private international law is the part of national law that deals with civil and commercial cases having a foreign element — where the parties, facts, or subject matter of a dispute are connected with more than one legal system. In common law jurisdictions it is traditionally called conflict of laws. It answers three main questions: which country's courts have jurisdiction to hear the case; which country's substantive law (lex causae) governs the dispute (choice of law); and whether a foreign judgment will be recognised and enforced.

Choice of law

Courts characterise the issue (e.g. as contractual, delictual, matrimonial, or proprietary) and then apply connecting factors — such as the parties' domicile, nationality, the place of contracting or of the harmful event, or the situs of property — to select the governing law. Many systems now give effect to the parties' express choice of law in contracts, subject to limits for consumer and employment protection. Doctrines such as renvoi (a forum's choice-of-law rules referring back to the forum's own law, or onward to a third system) add further complexity.

Harmonisation

Because rules differ between states, substantial harmonisation has occurred through treaties of the Hague Conference on Private International Law and, within the European Union, regulations such as Rome I (contractual obligations), Rome II (non-contractual obligations) and Brussels I (jurisdiction and judgments). Instruments like the Hague Service, Evidence, and Child Abduction Conventions coordinate procedure and cooperation between courts.

Distinction from public international law

Despite its name, private international law is not international in source: it is part of each state's domestic law (though shaped by treaties), and it governs relations between private persons, whereas public international law primarily governs relations between states.
