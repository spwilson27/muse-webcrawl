Sclerochronology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sclerochronology is the study of the physical and chemical variations in the accretionary hard tissues of organisms — shells, corals, otoliths (ear stones), teeth, bones and fish scales — and of the temporal context in which they formed. Analogous to dendrochronology (tree-ring dating), it reads the growth increments laid down in these tissues, which often record daily, tidal, seasonal and annual rhythms, to reconstruct an organism's age, growth history and the environmental conditions it experienced.

Principles

Many organisms deposit skeletal material incrementally, producing bands whose widths and composition vary with temperature, food supply, salinity and other conditions. By counting and measuring these bands — and by analysing their chemistry, including stable oxygen and carbon isotopes and trace elements such as strontium, magnesium and barium — researchers can build chronologies extending back years, decades or, in long-lived animals and fossil corals, centuries to millennia. Cross-dating between specimens, borrowed from dendrochronology, allows absolute dating and the detection of missing or false rings.

Materials and organisms

  • Bivalve molluscs: the ocean quahog Arctica islandica, whose individuals can live over 500 years, provides some of the longest marine chronologies.
  • Corals: annual density bands in massive corals record sea-surface temperature and salinity, extending instrumental records into past centuries.
  • Fish otoliths: daily and annual rings used throughout fisheries science to age fish and reconstruct nursery environments.
  • Teeth and bones: enamel and cementum increments used in archaeology, forensics and wildlife management.

Applications

Sclerochronology supports climate reconstruction (sclerochronologies are archives of ocean temperature and circulation before instrumental records), fisheries management, archaeology (season of capture and site occupation), palaeontology, environmental monitoring (pollution recorded in shells), and conservation biology. In archaeology, shell growth analysis — sometimes called molluscan sclerochronology — helps determine when coastal sites were occupied during the year.
