David Bloor (born 28 June 1942) is a British sociologist of science and a leading figure in science and technology studies. Based for most of his career at the University of Edinburgh, where he directed the Science Studies Unit, he is the founder, with Barry Barnes, of the influential Edinburgh school of the sociology of scientific knowledge.

The Strong Programme

In his book Knowledge and Social Imagery (1976), Bloor set out the four tenets of the "Strong Programme": sociological explanations of belief should be causal (concerned with the conditions that produce belief), impartial (addressing true and false beliefs alike), symmetrical (using the same types of cause to explain both truth and error), and reflexive (applicable to sociology itself). The symmetry principle was the programme's most controversial claim: rather than explaining false beliefs socially while treating true ones as self-evident, the sociologist should explain all accepted knowledge in the same causal terms.

Other work

Bloor later applied Wittgensteinian ideas about rule-following to the study of mathematics and logic, arguing in works such as Wittgenstein: A Social Theory of Knowledge (1983) that even mathematical necessity has a social dimension. His approach provoked sustained debate — the "science wars" of the 1990s and exchanges with philosophers such as Larry Laudan — and helped define the field of science studies.
