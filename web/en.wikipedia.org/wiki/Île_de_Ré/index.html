The Île de Ré (French pronunciation: [il də ʁe]; locally Île de Rhé) is an island off the Atlantic coast of France, opposite La Rochelle in the department of Charente-Maritime, to which it has been connected since 1988 by the Île de Ré bridge, at 2.9 km one of the longest bridges in France. Flat, narrow, and low-lying — its highest point is only about 20 metres — the island is a celebrated holiday destination, renowned for its salt marshes, oyster beds, vineyards, and mild, sunny microclimate.

History

The island's fortunes were built on salt — the "white gold" harvested from its marshes since the Middle Ages — and on wine and fishing. In 1627, during the Siege of Saint-Martin-de-Ré, an English expeditionary force under the Duke of Buckingham landed to relieve the Huguenots of La Rochelle but was defeated by the royal garrison; the island's capital, Saint-Martin-de-Ré, retains the star-shaped fortifications designed by Vauban, now part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing of Vauban's works. In the twentieth century the island hosted a famous penal colony at Saint-Martin, from which the convict Henri Charrière ("Papillon") was shipped to French Guiana.

Economy and tourism

Salt production continues in the northern marshes around Ars-en-Ré and Loix, where sauniers harvest fleur de sel by hand in summer; oysters and early potatoes (protected by an AOC) are other local products. Tourism dominates the economy: the permanent population of around 17,500 rises to well over 200,000 in peak season, and the island's 100 km of cycle paths make bicycles the characteristic means of transport. Building is strictly regulated, and the donkeys of Saint-Martin, traditionally dressed in culottes to protect them from flies in the marshes, are a beloved local emblem.
