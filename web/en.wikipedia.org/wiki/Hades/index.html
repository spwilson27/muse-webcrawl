Hades is a god of ancient Greek religion and mythology, eldest son of Cronus and Rhea, who received the underworld as his portion when he and his brothers Zeus and Poseidon divided the cosmos. His name — "the Unseen" — came to denote both the god and his realm, the abode of the dead (also called Hades), and because the earth yields both crops and precious metals he was euphemistically addressed as Plouton, "the Wealthy One", source of the Roman Pluto.

Mythology

Hades rarely leaves his kingdom and figures in few myths, the famous exception being his abduction of Persephone, daughter of Demeter, whom he carried to the underworld in his chariot and made his queen — a story told in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter to explain the seasons, since Persephone's annual return, after eating pomegranate seeds below, marks spring. He lent his helm of invisibility to heroes (Perseus used it against Medusa), and guarded the dead jealously: Heracles wrestled him when borrowing Cerberus, and Asclepius's raising of the dead drew his formal complaint to Zeus.

Character and cult

Unlike later devils, Hades is not evil: he is stern, just and implacable, administering irrevocable laws rather than punishing sinners (exceptional sinners like Tantalus and Sisyphus aside). Greeks avoided naming him directly, buried the dead with a coin for Charon the ferryman, and gave him few temples; his principal cult site was the oracle of the dead at Ephyra. In the afterlife his realm contained the Asphodel Meadows, Tartarus below for the damned, and — increasingly in later tradition — the blessed Elysium.
