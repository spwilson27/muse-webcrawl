Frequency-dependent selection

Frequency-dependent selection is an evolutionary process where the fitness of a phenotype or genotype depends on its relative frequency within a population. In negative frequency-dependent selection, the fitness of a phenotype decreases as it becomes more common, favouring rare types and thereby maintaining genetic polymorphism; in positive frequency-dependent selection, fitness increases with commonness.

Examples

Classic examples include predator search images (apostatic selection), which favour rare prey colour morphs; the maintenance of many alleles at self-incompatibility loci in plants, where rare alleles enjoy a mating advantage; and host–parasite coevolution, in which parasites adapt to common host genotypes, giving rare genotypes an advantage — a process linked to the Red Queen hypothesis and the evolution of sex.
