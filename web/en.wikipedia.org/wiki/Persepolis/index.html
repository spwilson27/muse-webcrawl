Persepolis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Apadana staircase at Persepolis with its tribute-bearer reliefs]

Persepolis was the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire, founded by Darius I in about 518 BC on a great stone terrace in the plain of Marvdasht, in modern Fars province, Iran. For two centuries Persian kings built its palaces, audience halls (the Apadana), treasury and royal tombs (at nearby Naqsh-e Rostam), receiving delegations from every nation of the empire — processions immortalised in the Apadana reliefs. Alexander the Great captured and burned Persepolis in 330 BC, in revenge, legend says, for Athens; its ruins remain among the most evocative of the ancient world.

The terrace city

Raised on a platform of 125,000 square metres, Persepolis was a ritual and administrative centre rather than a lived-in capital (the court rotated among Susa, Babylon and Ecbatana). Darius' and Xerxes' Apadana, reached by shallow staircases carved with hundreds of tribute-bearers, led to a hall of 72 columns. The Persepolis Fortification and Treasury tablets — thousands of Elamite records of rations and labour — reveal the empire's bureaucracy in unmatched detail, including women workers and their wages.

Destruction and rediscovery

Alexander occupied the city in 330 BC and, after some months, its palaces burned — whether by accident at a drunken revel (with the Athenian courtesan Thaïs cast as instigator) or as policy. The site, known locally as Takht-e Jamshid ('Throne of Jamshid'), was excavated by the Oriental Institute of Chicago in the 1930s; it hosted the Shah's extravagant 2,500-year celebration of the monarchy in 1971, and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
