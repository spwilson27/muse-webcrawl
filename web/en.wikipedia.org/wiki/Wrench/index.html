Wrench

Open-end, combination and adjustable wrenches.

A wrench or spanner is a hand tool used to provide grip and mechanical advantage in applying torque to turn objects — usually rotary fasteners, such as nuts and bolts — or to keep them from turning. The term "wrench" is standard in North American English, while "spanner" is used in British English for most fixed-size types; in British usage a "wrench" often denotes an adjustable or pipe tool. Common wrenches are made from chromium-vanadium alloy steel and are usually drop-forged and chrome-plated to resist corrosion.

Types

An open-end wrench has a U-shaped opening that grips two opposite faces of a fastener and can be applied from the side. A box-end wrench encloses the nut or bolt head in a ring with six or twelve points, giving a stronger grip. A combination wrench has an open end at one end of the handle and a box end at the other, both for the same size. An adjustable wrench has a movable jaw set by a worm screw, allowing one tool to fit many sizes, though with a less secure grip. Other types include socket wrenches (ratchets), torque wrenches, which measure and limit applied torque, Allen (hex) keys, pipe wrenches for gripping round pipe, and flare-nut wrenches used on soft fittings.

Sizes and standards

Wrenches are sized to the fastener they fit, in millimetres for metric fasteners or in fractions of an inch for imperial sizes such as Whitworth and SAE. Using a wrench of the wrong size can round the corners of a fastener. High-torque applications favour box-end and socket types because they distribute force over more of the fastener's surface.

History

Coins from the Roman era depict early gripping tools, but the modern spanner developed with industrial fastener standardisation in the 19th century, notably Joseph Whitworth's thread standard of 1841. The adjustable wrench was patented in the 1840s and refined by the Swedish inventor Johan Petter Johansson, whose 1892 patent produced the familiar worm-screw design.
