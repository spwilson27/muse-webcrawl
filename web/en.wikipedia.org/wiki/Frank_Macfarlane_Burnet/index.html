Sir Frank Macfarlane Burnet (3 September 1899 – 31 August 1985) was an Australian virologist and immunologist best known for his contributions to the understanding of the immune system. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1960 with Peter Medawar for the discovery of acquired immunological tolerance. Burnet was the dominant figure in Australian medical science for much of the twentieth century and directed the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research in Melbourne from 1944 to 1965.

Early life and career

Burnet was born in Traralgon, Victoria, the son of a bank manager. He studied medicine at the University of Melbourne, graduating in 1922, and completed a PhD at the Lister Institute in London in 1927, researching bacteriophages. Returning to Australia, he joined the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute, where he worked on viruses including influenza, herpes, and poliomyelitis. He developed widely used laboratory techniques, notably the cultivation of viruses in embryonated chicken eggs, which underpinned influenza research and vaccine production.

Immunological theory

In his 1949 book The Production of Antibodies, written with Frank Fenner, Burnet proposed that the immune system learns during embryonic development to distinguish "self" from "non-self," predicting that exposure to foreign tissue in early life would produce tolerance. Medawar's experiments in the early 1950s confirmed this, leading to their shared Nobel Prize. In 1957 Burnet advanced the clonal selection theory, which holds that each lymphocyte bears receptors for a single antigen and is selected to proliferate when it encounters that antigen — a framework that remains foundational to modern immunology.

Later life and legacy

Burnet was knighted in 1951 and was named Australian of the Year in 1960. After retiring from the Hall Institute in 1965, he wrote extensively on biology and society, sometimes controversially, on topics such as ageing, cancer, and human genetics. He died in 1985. The Burnet Institute in Melbourne, Australia's largest virology and infectious diseases research institute, is named in his honour.
