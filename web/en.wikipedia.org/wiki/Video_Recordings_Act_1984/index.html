Video Recordings Act 1984

The Video Recordings Act 1984 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that established a statutory classification and labelling system for video works (later extended to some video games) distributed in the UK. It made it a criminal offence to supply or possess for supply an unclassified video recording, and designated the British Board of Film Classification (BBFC) as the authority responsible for assigning age ratings and requiring cuts.

Background

The Act arose from the "video nasties" moral panic of the early 1980s, when cheap home video brought unregulated horror films into British homes. A campaign led by Mary Whitehouse and the National Viewers' and Listeners' Association, tabloid press pressure, and prosecutions under the Obscene Publications Act prompted the government of Margaret Thatcher to legislate. The measure passed as a private member's bill introduced by Graham Bright MP with government support.

Provisions

Under the Act, video works must be submitted to the designated authority, which issues a classification certificate (U, PG, 12, 15, 18, or R18 for material sold only in licensed sex shops), or refuses one, effectively banning the work. Supplying a recording without a certificate, supplying an age-restricted recording to someone below the age specified, or supplying an unclassified recording in licensed premises contrary to an R18 restriction are offences punishable by fines and imprisonment. Certain works — principally those concerned with sport, music, religion or education — are exempt unless they depict specified content such as human sexual activity or gross violence.

The 2009–2010 repeal and re-enactment

In August 2009 the government discovered that the Act had never been properly notified to the European Commission under a technical standards directive in 1984, rendering it unenforceable; prosecutions were halted. Parliament swiftly corrected the error by passing the Video Recordings Act 2010, which repealed and immediately re-enacted the 1984 Act in identical terms. A 2012 amendment transferred statutory classification of most video games to the Video Standards Council under the PEGI system, leaving the BBFC to classify other video content.
