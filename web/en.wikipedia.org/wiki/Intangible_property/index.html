Intangible property

Intangible property, also known as incorporeal property, is property that does not have a physical existence — its value lies in legal rights rather than in a physical object. It is the legal category that includes intellectual property (patents, copyrights, trademarks and trade secrets), as well as financial and contractual rights such as shares, bonds and other securities, bank deposits, debts and goodwill, franchises and licences. Intangible property contrasts with tangible property — land and physical goods (chattels) that can be touched and possessed.

Legal concepts

In English common law, personal property divides into choses in possession (tangible things that can be physically held) and choses in action (rights that can only be enforced by legal action — debts, shares, intellectual property and rights under contracts). Because intangible property cannot be physically seized, legal systems protect it through registration systems, specialised courts and remedies such as injunctions and accounts of profits. Transfer typically occurs by written assignment or by book-entry in electronic registries, as with modern dematerialised securities.

Intellectual property

The most prominent subset is intellectual property: patents protect inventions, copyright protects original expression, trademarks protect signs distinguishing goods and services, and trade secrets protect confidential information. Related rights cover industrial designs, plant varieties, geographical indications and databases. The term "intellectual property" itself became common only in the later 20th century, promoted by the creation of the World Intellectual Property Organization (1967) and the TRIPS Agreement (1994).

Economic significance

Intangible assets have grown to dominate the value of modern corporations: analyses of the S&P 500 estimate that intangibles rose from about 17% of market value in 1975 to around 90% by 2020. This shift poses challenges for accounting (most internally generated intangibles are not recognised on balance sheets under IFRS/US GAAP), for taxation (profit shifting through IP holding companies), and for competition policy. In insolvency and secured lending, intangible assets are increasingly important collateral, though their value can be highly context-dependent.
