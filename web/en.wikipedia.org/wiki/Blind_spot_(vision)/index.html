Blind spot (vision)

Blind spot
LocationOptic disc of the retina
CauseAbsence of photoreceptor cells where the optic nerve exits the eye
Discovered byEdme Mariotte, 1660s

In visual perception, the blind spot is the point in the visual field that corresponds to the optic disc of the retina, where the optic nerve passes through and where there are no photoreceptor cells to detect light. The brain interpolates the missing information from surrounding detail and from the other eye, so the blind spot is not normally perceived as a gap.

Discovery

The French physicist Edme Mariotte demonstrated the blind spot in the 1660s by showing that a small object could be made to disappear when its image fell on the optic disc. His demonstration reportedly entertained the court of King Charles II of England.

Demonstration

With one eye closed, a small mark placed at the appropriate distance and lateral position will vanish from view while the gaze is fixed on another mark, because its image lands on the optic disc. The blind spot is located roughly 12–15 degrees temporally (toward the ear) from the point of fixation and subtends about 5–7 degrees.

Comparison with other animals

All vertebrate eyes share this design, with the nerve passing through the retina. Cephalopods such as octopuses evolved eyes in which the nerve fibres pass behind the retina, so they have no blind spot — a classic example used in discussions of evolution and design.
