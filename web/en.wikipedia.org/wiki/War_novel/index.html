War novel

A war novel (or military fiction) is a novel about war. It is a novel in which the primary action takes place on a battlefield, in a military camp, or in a home front where lives are dominated by war. War novels are frequently written by veterans drawing on personal experience.

History

The genre has ancient roots in epic poetry such as the Iliad, but the modern war novel emerged in the 19th century with works such as Stephen Crane's The Red Badge of Courage (1895) and Leo Tolstoy's War and Peace (1869). The First World War produced a landmark generation of works, including Erich Maria Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front (1929), Ernest Hemingway's A Farewell to Arms (1929), and Robert Graves's Good-Bye to All That (1929).

Themes and conventions

Common themes include the loss of innocence of young soldiers, comradeship, the gulf between front-line troops and commanders or civilians, trauma and its aftermath, and the questioning of patriotism and glory. Many war novels are anti-war in outlook, depicting combat as brutal and futile; others celebrate heroism and adventure.

Notable examples

Notable later examples include Joseph Heller's Catch-22 (1961) about the Second World War, Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five (1969), James Jones's The Thin Red Line (1962), Norman Mailer's The Naked and the Dead (1948), and Tim O'Brien's The Things They Carried (1990) about the Vietnam War.
