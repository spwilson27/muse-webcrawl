Vasodilator

A vasodilator is a substance — usually a drug — that causes vasodilation, the widening of blood vessels, by relaxing the smooth muscle of the vessel walls. Vasodilators are widely used in the treatment of hypertension, angina pectoris, heart failure, and other cardiovascular conditions.

Classification

Vasodilators may act predominantly on arteries, on veins, or on both:

  • Arterial dilators reduce afterload and systemic vascular resistance. Examples: hydralazine, calcium channel blockers of the dihydropyridine type, minoxidil.
  • Venous dilators reduce preload by pooling blood in the venous system. Examples: organic nitrates such as nitroglycerin and isosorbide dinitrate.
  • Mixed dilators act on both arterial and venous beds. Examples: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, sodium nitroprusside, and prazosin.

Mechanisms of action

Vasodilators work through several molecular pathways. Nitrates and nitroprusside donate nitric oxide, activating guanylate cyclase and raising cyclic GMP. Calcium channel blockers reduce calcium entry into vascular smooth muscle cells. Other agents open potassium channels, block alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, or inhibit the renin–angiotensin system.

Clinical uses

Beyond chronic hypertension and heart failure, vasodilators are used in hypertensive emergencies (nitroprusside, clevidipine), acute coronary syndromes (nitroglycerin), pulmonary arterial hypertension (prostacyclin analogues, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors), and Raynaud's phenomenon.

Adverse effects

Common adverse effects include headache, flushing, reflex tachycardia, dizziness, and postural hypotension. Excessive vasodilation can cause a dangerous fall in blood pressure. Long-term hydralazine use has been associated with a lupus-like syndrome, and minoxidil with increased hair growth.
