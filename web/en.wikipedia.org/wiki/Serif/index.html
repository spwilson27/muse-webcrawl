Serif

In typography, a serif is a small line or stroke regularly attached to the end of a larger stroke in a letter or symbol within a particular font or family of fonts. A typeface or font family making use of serifs is called a serif typeface (or seriffed typeface), and a typeface that does not include them is sans-serif.

Origins and etymology

Serifs are generally thought to originate from the Roman monumental capitals carved in stone, where small flared strokes finished the main strokes of letters. The word "serif" is believed to derive from the Dutch schreef, meaning "line" or "stroke of the pen".

Classification

Serif typefaces are commonly grouped into four broad categories: old-style (such as Garamond), with low stroke contrast; transitional (such as Times New Roman), with more vertical stress; Didone or modern (such as Bodoni), with high contrast and hairline serifs; and slab serif (such as Rockwell), with thick, block-like serifs.

Readability

Serif typefaces have traditionally been favoured for long passages of printed text on the theory that serifs guide the eye along the line; empirical studies of this claim have produced mixed results. Sans-serif faces became dominant for screen text during the early web era, though high-resolution displays have narrowed the difference.
