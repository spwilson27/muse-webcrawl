Paper birch

Scientific nameBetula papyrifera Marshall
FamilyBetulaceae
GenusBetula
Common namesPaper birch, American white birch, canoe birch

Paper birch (Betula papyrifera), also called American white birch or canoe birch, is a species of birch native to northern North America. It is a medium-sized deciduous tree, typically growing 15–27 metres tall, notable for its thin, white bark that peels in papery horizontal strips. It is one of the most widely distributed birches on the continent, ranging across Alaska and Canada and into the northern United States, and is the provincial tree of Saskatchewan and the state tree of New Hampshire.

Ecology

Paper birch is a pioneer species that quickly colonises open ground after fire, logging or other disturbance; its tiny wind-borne seeds germinate on exposed mineral soil. It is relatively short-lived, often 60–90 years, and is browsed by moose and deer, while its seeds and buds feed birds such as chickadees and its catkins attract insects. Stands suffer from the bronze birch borer when stressed.

Uses

The waterproof, pliable bark was traditionally used by Indigenous peoples of the northern forests to build canoes, as well as baskets, containers, cradles and writing surfaces such as birch bark scrolls. The wood is used for pulp, firewood, veneer and turned objects, and the sap can be boiled into birch syrup.
