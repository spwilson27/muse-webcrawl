The Daytona 500 is a 500-mile (805 km) NASCAR Cup Series stock car race held annually at Daytona International Speedway in Daytona Beach, Florida. It is the first race of the Cup Series season and the most prestigious and lucrative event in NASCAR, often called "The Great American Race" and likened to stock car racing's Super Bowl.

History

Racing on Daytona's beach-and-road course dated to the 1930s, but the modern event began with the opening of Bill France Sr.'s 2.5-mile high-banked superspeedway in 1959. The inaugural Daytona 500 that year ended in a photo finish so close that officials took 61 hours to declare Lee Petty the winner over Johnny Beauchamp. Richard Petty won the race seven times between 1964 and 1981, a record that still stands; his son-era rivals included Cale Yarborough (four wins) and Jeff Gordon (three).

The 1979 race, the first Daytona 500 broadcast live flag-to-flag on national television (CBS), ended with leaders Donnie Allison and Cale Yarborough wrecking on the last lap and fighting in the infield while Richard Petty won — a spectacle, unfolding before a snowed-in eastern audience, credited with launching NASCAR's national boom. Dale Earnhardt, after two decades of heartbreak, finally won in 1998; three years later he was killed in a crash on the final lap of the 2001 race, an event that transformed NASCAR safety, leading to mandates for head-and-neck restraints, SAFER barriers and the redesigned "Car of Tomorrow".

Format

The race covers 200 laps of the 2.5-mile tri-oval. Qualifying is unique: single-car time trials set only the front row, while the rest of the 40-car field is determined by two 150-mile "Duel" qualifying races held the preceding Thursday. Because of the high banking and (historically) restrictor plates, and now tapered spacers, cars run in tight aerodynamic packs at around 200 mph, producing spectacular multi-car crashes ("the Big One") and frequent last-lap lead changes; the race is run in three stages under modern NASCAR rules, and often ends under an overtime "green-white-checkered" finish.

Significance

Winning the Daytona 500 — and receiving the Harley J. Earl Trophy — is a career-defining achievement that can outweigh a championship in public recognition; surprise winners such as Derrike Cope (1990), Michael Waltrip (2001, 2003), Trevor Bayne (2011, the youngest winner at 20) and Austin Cindric (2022) are remembered chiefly for this race. The event anchors "Speedweeks", drawing well over 100,000 spectators and one of American sport's largest television audiences each February.
