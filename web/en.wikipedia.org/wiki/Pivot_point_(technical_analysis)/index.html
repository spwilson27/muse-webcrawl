Pivot point (technical analysis)

A daily chart with the pivot point (P) and associated support (S1–S3) and resistance (R1–R3) levels.

In financial markets, a pivot point is a price level that is used by traders as a possible indicator of market movement. A pivot point is calculated as an average of significant prices (high, low, closing) from the performance of a market in the prior trading period. If the market in the following period trades above the pivot point it is usually evaluated as a bullish sentiment, whereas trading below the pivot point is seen as bearish.

It is customary to additionally calculate a number of support and resistance levels below and above the pivot point, by subtracting or adding price differentials calculated from previous trading ranges of the market.

Calculation

Several methods exist for calculating the pivot point (P) of a market. Most commonly, it is the arithmetic average of the high (H), low (L), and closing (C) prices of the market in the prior trading period:

P = (H + L + C) / 3.

Sometimes the average also includes the previous period's or the current period's opening price (O):

P = (O + H + L + C) / 4.

In other cases, traders like to emphasize the closing price, P = (H + L + C + C) / 4.

Support and resistance levels

Price support and resistance levels are key trading tools in any market. Their roles may be reversed once a level is breached. In a rising market, these levels identify where the price rise may pause or reverse; in a falling market, where a fall may pause or reverse. The first support and resistance levels are calculated from the pivot point and the previous period's range:

  • R1 = (2 × P) − L; S1 = (2 × P) − H
  • R2 = P + (H − L); S2 = P − (H − L)
  • R3 = H + 2 × (P − L); S3 = L − 2 × (H − P)

Trading ranges

The range of price movement expected for the following period can be anticipated by observing the position of the current price relative to the pivot point in the first hours of trading. If the market opens above the pivot point, long positions are favoured; below it, short positions. Other variants of pivot points, such as Woodie's, Camarilla, and DeMark's pivots, weight the inputs differently or use the opening price in different ways.
