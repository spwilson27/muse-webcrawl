Gin Craze

The Gin Craze was a period in the first half of the 18th century when the consumption of gin increased rapidly in Great Britain, especially in London. The craze is widely seen as the first modern "drug scare", and it provoked a series of legislative attempts to control the trade, culminating in the Gin Act 1751.

Causes

Several factors combined: the government encouraged domestic distilling to use surplus grain and to counter French brandy imports; duties were low and licences cheap; and economic distress among London's poor made cheap, strong gin — sold from shops, stalls, and even wheelbarrows — an attractive escape. At the peak, London had an estimated 7,000 gin shops, and annual consumption exceeded 11 million gallons by 1750. Contemporary moralists blamed gin, nicknamed "Mother's Ruin", for crime, disease, falling birth rates, and the rising death rate in the capital.

Legislation

Parliament passed a series of Gin Acts beginning in 1729. The Gin Act 1736 imposed punitive licence fees and duties but largely failed, driving the trade underground and into the hands of informers who became targets of mob violence. The Gin Act 1751, by contrast, restricted sales to licensed premises, raised duties moderately, and is generally considered successful — helped by rising grain prices and a growing temperance sentiment.

In art

William Hogarth's paired engravings Beer Street and Gin Lane (1751) were issued in support of the 1751 Act, contrasting wholesome beer-drinking prosperity with the degradation of the gin drinker. Henry Fielding's pamphlet An Enquiry into the Causes of the Late Increase of Robbers likewise blamed gin for the crime wave.
