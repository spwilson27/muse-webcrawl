In signal processing, a periodogram is an estimate of the spectral density of a signal. The term was coined by Arthur Schuster in 1898 in his work on detecting hidden periodicities in sunspot numbers. Today the periodogram is defined as the squared magnitude of the discrete Fourier transform of the observed data (possibly tapered by a window function), and it is the most basic nonparametric method for discovering periodic components in a time series.

Definition

For a sequence xn of N samples, the periodogram at frequency f is proportional to |Σ xn e−2πi·f·n|2. Peaks in the periodogram indicate frequencies at which the series has strong periodic content. The periodogram is an asymptotically unbiased estimate of the true power spectrum, but it is not consistent: its variance does not vanish as more data are collected, a problem addressed by averaging methods.

Variants and applications

Welch's method averages periodograms of overlapping windowed segments to reduce variance; Bartlett's method averages periodograms of non-overlapping segments. The Lomb–Scargle periodogram extends the idea to unevenly sampled data and is a standard tool in astronomy for detecting exoplanets and variable stars from irregular observations. Multitaper methods use several orthogonal windows to obtain low-bias, low-variance estimates. Periodograms are used throughout astronomy, geophysics, economics, neuroscience (EEG spectra) and engineering wherever periodic behaviour must be detected and quantified.

Limitations

Spectral leakage from strong components can mask weaker ones, and the raw periodogram's jagged appearance makes closely spaced frequencies hard to resolve; windowing and averaged estimators trade frequency resolution for statistical stability.
