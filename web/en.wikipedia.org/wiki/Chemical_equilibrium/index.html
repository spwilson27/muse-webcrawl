Chemical equilibrium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Graph of concentrations levelling off as a reversible reaction reaches equilibrium]

In a chemical reaction, chemical equilibrium is the state in which both the reactants and products are present in concentrations that have no further tendency to change with time, because the forward and reverse reactions are occurring at equal rates. Equilibrium is dynamic: reactions continue at the molecular level, but the macroscopic composition remains constant.

The equilibrium constant

For a general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the law of mass action gives the equilibrium constant

K = ([C]c[D]d) / ([A]a[B]b)

whose value at a given temperature determines the position of equilibrium: large K favours products, small K favours reactants. Comparing the reaction quotient Q with K predicts the direction in which a non-equilibrium mixture will shift.

Le Chatelier's principle

According to Le Chatelier's principle, if a system at equilibrium is disturbed — by changing concentration, pressure, or temperature — it responds in the direction that partially counteracts the disturbance. This principle guides industrial processes such as the Haber process, where high pressure and a compromise temperature are used to maximise ammonia yield.

Thermodynamic basis

Equilibrium corresponds to a minimum of the Gibbs free energy of the system; the standard free-energy change of reaction is related to the equilibrium constant by ΔG° = −RT ln K. Equilibrium constants figure throughout chemistry, in acid–base equilibria (Ka, Kb, Kw), solubility products, and complex formation.
