Tipping point (sociology)

In sociology, a tipping point is the moment when a previously stable phenomenon reaches a threshold and changes rapidly and irreversibly — the point at which a group rapidly and dramatically changes its behaviour by widely adopting a previously rare practice.

Origins and development

The concept was applied by Morton Grodzins to neighbourhood racial change in the 1950s and developed in Thomas Schelling's models of segregation, in which small individual preferences can tip a system into a very different equilibrium. Mark Granovetter's threshold models (1978) formalised how collective behaviour depends on the distribution of individual thresholds.

Popularisation

Malcolm Gladwell's book The Tipping Point (2000) popularised the term, likening social change to epidemics driven by "the law of the few", stickiness and the power of context. Related ideas include critical mass in collective action and tipping points in climate science.
