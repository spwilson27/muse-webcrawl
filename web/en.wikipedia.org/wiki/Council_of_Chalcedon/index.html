The Council of Chalcedon was the fourth ecumenical council of the Christian Church, held from 8 October to 1 November 451 at Chalcedon, across the Bosporus from Constantinople. Convoked by the Emperor Marcian, it is regarded as infallible in its dogmatic definitions by the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches. Its judgements and definitions regarding the divine and human natures of Jesus were later repudiated by the Oriental Orthodox churches, leading to a lasting schism.

Background

The council was called to settle Christological controversies that had continued after the Council of Ephesus (431), which had condemned Nestorianism. The monk Eutyches taught that Christ had only one nature after the Incarnation (Monophysitism), and was upheld at the Second Council of Ephesus in 449 — the so-called "Robber Council" (Latrocinium) — which Pope Leo I refused to recognise. Leo's Tome, addressed to Archbishop Flavian of Constantinople, set out the Western position that Christ has two natures, divine and human, united in one person.

The Chalcedonian Definition

The council deposed Dioscorus of Alexandria and issued the Chalcedonian Definition, which declares that Christ is to be acknowledged "in two natures, inconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably" — perfect in Godhead and perfect in manhood, consubstantial with the Father in divinity and with humanity in manhood. The definition became the standard of orthodoxy for the churches of Rome and Constantinople and underlies later Christology in Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant traditions.

Canons and aftermath

The council also issued 30 disciplinary canons. Canon 28 granted Constantinople privileges equal to those of Rome, a provision the papal legates rejected. The non-Chalcedonian churches of Egypt, Syria, Armenia and Ethiopia rejected the council's definition, and the resulting division between Chalcedonian and Oriental Orthodox churches persists to this day, although modern ecumenical dialogues have found substantial agreement on Christology.
