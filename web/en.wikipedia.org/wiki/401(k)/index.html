401(k)

Growth of a typical 401(k) balance over a career

In the United States, a 401(k) plan is a retirement savings plan sponsored by an employer. It lets employees save and invest part of their paycheck before taxes are taken out (or, in Roth 401(k) plans, after taxes). The name comes from subsection 401(k) of the Internal Revenue Code.

History

The Revenue Act of 1978 added section 401(k) to the tax code with little fanfare. In 1980 benefits consultant Ted Benna recognised that the provision could be used as a general salary-deferral retirement plan, and after the IRS issued clarifying regulations in 1981, employers adopted 401(k)s widely. They progressively replaced traditional defined-benefit pensions as the primary workplace retirement plan, shifting investment risk from employers to employees.

Features

Employees choose an elective deferral up to an annual IRS limit (with extra catch-up contributions allowed from age 50), and many employers match part of the contribution. Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce taxable income in the year made and are taxed on withdrawal; designated Roth contributions are taxed up front and withdrawn tax-free. Withdrawals before age 59½ generally incur a 10% early-withdrawal penalty with exceptions. Account holders must begin required minimum distributions in their early seventies. Loans and hardship withdrawals are permitted under plan rules, and balances can usually be rolled into an IRA when changing jobs.

Criticism

Critics cite high fees in some plans, inadequate participation and savings rates, leakage through loans and cash-outs, and unequal tax benefits favouring high earners. Auto-enrolment, introduced and expanded by the Pension Protection Act of 2006 and the SECURE Acts, has significantly raised participation.
