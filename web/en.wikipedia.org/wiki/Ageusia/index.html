Ageusia

Ageusia is the loss of the sense of taste. The term derives from the Greek a- (without) and geusis (taste). Complete ageusia is rare; partial loss of taste is more often described as hypogeusia, while a distorted sense of taste is known as dysgeusia. Because much of what is perceived as "taste" actually comes from the sense of smell (olfaction), many complaints of taste loss turn out on testing to be cases of anosmia or hyposmia instead.

Causes

Taste is mediated by taste buds on the tongue, soft palate, and pharynx, whose signals travel via the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII), the glossopharyngeal nerve (IX), and the vagus nerve (X). Damage to any of these pathways can produce ageusia. Causes include viral infections of the upper respiratory tract, head trauma, Bell's palsy, middle-ear surgery, certain medications (such as some antibiotics, antihypertensives, and chemotherapy agents), zinc deficiency, radiation therapy to the head and neck, and neurological conditions including Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease.

Taste disturbances were widely reported during the COVID-19 pandemic, where loss of taste and smell were hallmark symptoms, although olfactory dysfunction was generally the dominant deficit.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis involves taste testing with sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami solutions applied to different regions of the tongue, along with examination of the mouth and a review of medications. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause; many cases resolve spontaneously. Affected individuals are advised to take care with food safety, since the loss of taste can mask spoiled food, and to monitor salt and sugar intake.
