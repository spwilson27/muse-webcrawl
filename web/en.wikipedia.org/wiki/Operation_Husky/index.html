The Allied invasion of Sicily, codenamed Operation Husky, was a major campaign of the Second World War in which the Allies invaded and captured Sicily from the Axis powers. Beginning with large-scale amphibious and airborne landings on 9–10 July 1943, the operation opened the way for the invasion of mainland Italy and contributed to the fall of Benito Mussolini's Fascist government.

Background

After the Allied victory in North Africa, planners agreed at the Casablanca Conference to invade Sicily to secure Mediterranean shipping lanes and knock Italy out of the war. The invasion force, under General Dwight D. Eisenhower's Allied Forces Headquarters, comprised the British Eighth Army under Bernard Montgomery, landing in the southeast, and the U.S. Seventh Army under George S. Patton, landing in the south. A celebrated deception, Operation Mincemeat, helped convince the Axis that the main Allied blow would fall on Greece and Sardinia.

The landings and campaign

Airborne drops on the night of 9–10 July were scattered by high winds, but the seaborne landings achieved surprise and established beachheads. Montgomery's Eighth Army advanced up the east coast toward Messina but stalled against strong defences around Mount Etna. Patton's Seventh Army, after repelling a German armoured counterattack at Gela, swung west and north, capturing Palermo on 22 July and then racing along the north coast. The Axis conducted a skilful evacuation across the Strait of Messina, withdrawing some 100,000 troops and much equipment to the mainland before the Allies entered Messina on 17 August.

Aftermath

During the campaign Mussolini was deposed on 25 July 1943, and Italy soon sought an armistice, announced on 8 September. Germany responded by occupying much of Italy. Husky was one of the largest amphibious operations of the war in terms of troops landed on the first day, exceeded in scale only by the Normandy landings the following year.
