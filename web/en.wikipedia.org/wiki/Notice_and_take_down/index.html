Notice and take down

FieldInternet law, copyright law
Key legislationDMCA §512 (United States); EU E-Commerce Directive; EU Copyright Directive Art. 17
ActorsRights holders, online service providers, users
Counter-processCounter-notice / put-back

Notice and take down (NTD) is a procedure under which an online service provider removes or disables access to material hosted on its system after receiving a notice claiming that the material is unlawful — most often that it infringes copyright. In exchange for promptly complying, the provider typically gains a "safe harbour" from liability for its users' content. The best-known example is the system created by the U.S. Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) of 1998.

The DMCA model

Under 17 U.S.C. §512, a rights holder sends the provider a takedown notice identifying the copyrighted work and the infringing material. The provider expeditiously removes the material and informs the uploader, who may send a counter-notice asserting a good-faith belief that the removal was mistaken; the provider then restores the material after 10–14 business days unless the rights holder files a court action. Knowingly false notices can create liability under §512(f), and providers must terminate repeat infringers to keep the safe harbour.

Other jurisdictions

The European Union's E-Commerce Directive (2000) provides hosting providers with protection once they act expeditiously on obtaining "actual knowledge" of illegality, without spelling out a formal notice procedure; member states developed varying schemes. The 2019 EU Copyright Directive's Article 17 moved large content-sharing platforms toward licensing and filtering obligations, often described as a shift from "notice and take down" toward "notice and stay down". Similar regimes exist in countries such as Australia, Canada (which uses "notice and notice" — forwarding the complaint without mandatory removal) and Japan.

Criticism

The system is praised for enabling platforms to host user content at scale and criticised for over-removal: automated and bulk notices can silence lawful fair use, parody, journalism and political speech, while counter-notice procedures are little used. Transparency reports and databases such as the Lumen project archive millions of notices for study.
