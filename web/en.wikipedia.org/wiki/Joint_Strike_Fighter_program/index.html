The Joint Strike Fighter program (JSF) was a development and acquisition programme of the United States Department of Defense, begun in the 1990s, to replace a wide range of existing fighter, strike and ground-attack aircraft for the United States and its allies with a single family of stealth multirole fighters. It produced the Lockheed Martin F-35 Lightning II.

Origins

The programme merged several earlier studies, including the Common Affordable Lightweight Fighter and Joint Advanced Strike Technology projects, into a single competition intended to serve the US Air Force, Navy and Marine Corps as well as export customers. A central goal was a family of three variants sharing most of their structure and systems: a conventional take-off and landing variant (F-35A), a short take-off and vertical landing variant (F-35B) and a carrier-based variant (F-35C).

Competition

In 1996 Boeing and Lockheed Martin were selected to build competing demonstrators, the X-32 and X-35. After flight testing, on 26 October 2001 the X-35 was declared the winner and Lockheed Martin received the System Development and Demonstration contract. Partner nations, led by the United Kingdom as the only Level 1 partner, joined the programme with financial contributions and industrial workshare.

Development and criticism

The F-35's development was marked by cost growth, delays and technical problems, making JSF the most expensive weapons programme in history, with lifetime costs estimated in the trillions of dollars. Supporters argue the aircraft's stealth, sensors and networking give it unmatched capability; critics have questioned its cost, complexity and early reliability. The F-35 entered service from 2015 and has been ordered by numerous allied air forces.
