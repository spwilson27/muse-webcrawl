Upper limb

Upper limb

Regions: Shoulder, arm, forearm, hand

Bones: Clavicle, scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges

The upper limb or upper extremity is the region in humans extending from the deltoid region to the hand, including the arm, forearm and hand. It is freely movable at the shoulder joint and specialised for manipulation rather than weight-bearing, in contrast to the lower limb.

Structure

The skeleton of the upper limb consists of the pectoral girdle (clavicle and scapula), the humerus of the arm, the radius and ulna of the forearm, and the bones of the hand: eight carpals, five metacarpals and fourteen phalanges. Major joints include the shoulder, elbow, wrist and the joints of the fingers.

Muscles and nerves

The muscles of the upper limb are supplied chiefly by the brachial plexus, formed from the ventral rami of spinal nerves C5–T1. Its terminal branches — the median, ulnar, radial, axillary and musculocutaneous nerves — control movement and sensation. The pectoral and shoulder muscles position the limb, while the intricate intrinsic muscles of the hand permit fine dexterity.

Function

The upper limb's mobility, the opposable thumb and sensitive fingertips make it the principal organ of manipulation. Its development is closely connected with bipedalism, which freed the hands from locomotion.
