Poet laureate

Poet laureate
OriginLaurel crown of classical antiquity; formalised in England under Charles II
First English holderJohn Dryden (1668)
Appointing bodiesVarious: monarchs, governments, libraries, arts councils

A poet laureate is a poet officially appointed by a government, monarch, or institution, often expected to compose poems for special occasions and national events. The term derives from the laurel wreath awarded to poets in classical Greece and Rome, and from the university degree laureatus conferred on accomplished versifiers in the Middle Ages.

History

In ancient Rome the laurel crowned victors and poets alike; the Italian poet Petrarch was famously crowned laureate on the Capitoline Hill in 1341. In England, poets such as Ben Jonson received royal pensions, but the office was formalised in 1668 when Charles II appointed John Dryden by letters patent. For centuries the British laureate was expected to produce odes for royal birthdays and the New Year.

United Kingdom

The British Poet Laureate is appointed by the monarch on the advice of the Prime Minister. Since 1999 the appointment has been for a fixed term of ten years rather than for life. Holders have included William Wordsworth, Alfred, Lord Tennyson (the longest-serving, 1850–1892), John Betjeman, Ted Hughes, Andrew Motion, Carol Ann Duffy — the first woman and first Scot to hold the post — and Simon Armitage, appointed in 2019. Scotland, Wales, and Ireland maintain their own national poet titles.

United States

The United States appointed a "Consultant in Poetry to the Library of Congress" from 1937; in 1985 the title became Poet Laureate Consultant in Poetry. Laureates serve a one-year term, renewable once, receive a stipend, and undertake projects to promote poetry. Holders include Robert Frost, Robert Penn Warren, Billy Collins, Rita Dove, Tracy K. Smith, Joy Harjo, and Ada Limón. Many US states and cities also appoint their own laureates.

Other countries

Canada has a Parliamentary Poet Laureate, chosen alternately from English- and French-language poets. New Zealand, Australia, South Africa, and numerous other nations maintain laureate posts, varying from ceremonial honours to active literary ambassadorships.

Duties and significance

Modern laureates are less often required to write official verse and more often act as public advocates for poetry — giving readings, visiting schools, and commissioning new work — though many still mark national moments, from royal weddings to public mourning.
