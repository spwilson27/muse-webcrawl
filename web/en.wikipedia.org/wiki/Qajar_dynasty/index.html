The Qajar dynasty (Persian: سلسله قاجار‎) was an Iranian royal dynasty of Turkic origin, specifically from the Qajar tribe, ruling Iran from 1789 to 1925. The dynasty reunified the country after the chaos following the fall of the Safavids and Afsharids, established Tehran as the capital, and ruled through the long 19th century in which Iran was squeezed between the expansionist ambitions of the Russian and British empires — the so-called Great Game.

History

Agha Mohammad Khan, a eunuch chieftain of the Qajars, brutally reunified Iran by 1796, sacking Tbilisi in the process, but was assassinated the following year. His nephew Fath-Ali Shah presided over disastrous wars with Russia: the treaties of Gulistan (1813) and Turkmenchay (1828) ceded the Caucasian provinces and imposed heavy indemnities. Under Naser al-Din Shah, the reforming premier Amir Kabir modernised the military and founded the Dar ul-Funun college before being murdered in 1852; the Shah's assassination in 1896 followed decades of concessions to foreign powers, notably the 1890 tobacco monopoly whose nationwide protest foreshadowed constitutionalism.

The Persian Constitutional Revolution (1905–1911) forced Mozaffar ad-Din Shah and his successor to accept a parliament (Majlis) and constitution, though Mohammad Ali Shah bombarded the Majlis with the help of the Russian-officered Cossack Brigade in 1908. Under the child shah Ahmad, Iran was occupied by Russian, British, and Ottoman forces during the First World War despite its declared neutrality. In 1921 the Cossack officer Reza Khan staged a coup; in 1925 the Majlis deposed the Qajars and proclaimed him Reza Shah Pahlavi, ending the dynasty.

Culture

Qajar art is famous for its large-scale oil portraiture, lacquer work, and tilework, and Tehran's Golestan Palace preserves the court's splendour. The dynasty consolidated Twelver Shi'a clerical influence while pioneering modern education, the telegraph, photography, and the press in Iran.
