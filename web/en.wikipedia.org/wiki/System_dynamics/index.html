System dynamics

System dynamics (SD) is an approach to understanding the nonlinear behaviour of complex systems over time using stocks, flows, internal feedback loops, table functions, and time delays. It was created during the mid-1950s by Professor Jay Forrester of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, growing out of his work on servomechanisms, management science, and computer simulation.

Elements

  • Stocks accumulate over time (inventories, populations, bank balances) and are changed only by flows.
  • Flows are the rates that fill or drain stocks (production rates, birth rates).
  • Feedback loops — reinforcing (positive) loops that generate growth or collapse, and balancing (negative) loops that seek goals or stability — are the central structural cause of dynamic behaviour.
  • Delays between cause and effect create oscillation and instability.

Models are often drawn first as causal loop diagrams and then formalized as stock-and-flow diagrams, which translate directly into systems of differential (difference) equations simulated on a computer. Software tools include Vensim, Stella, and Powersim.

Applications

Forrester's early work applied the method to industrial supply chains (revealing amplification effects later called the bullwhip effect), then to urban problems in Urban Dynamics (1969) and global modelling in World Dynamics (1971), which fed into the Club of Rome's The Limits to Growth (1972). System dynamics is now used in business strategy, public policy, epidemiology, energy and climate modelling, and project management. Its central lesson — that the structure of a system, its feedback loops and delays, drives its behaviour, often counterintuitively — is summarized in Forrester's dictum that people mostly know the elements of a system but misjudge how the interactions produce outcomes.
