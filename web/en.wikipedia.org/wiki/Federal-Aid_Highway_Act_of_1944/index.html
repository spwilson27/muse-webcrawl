Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1944

EnactedDecember 20, 1944
Signed byPresident Franklin D. Roosevelt
Key provisionDesignation of a 40,000-mile National System of Interstate Highways

The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1944 was United States legislation that reshaped the federal highway programme and, most significantly, authorized the designation of a "National System of Interstate Highways" of up to 40,000 miles connecting major cities and industrial centres.

Provisions

The act divided federal-aid funds among the primary highway system, secondary and feeder roads — the first substantial federal recognition of farm-to-market routes — and urban extensions. It authorized $500 million per year for the first three postwar years, apportioned by a formula balancing area, population, and road mileage. State highway departments, working with the Public Roads Administration under Thomas H. MacDonald, selected the interstate routes, whose general locations were published in 1947.

Significance

Although the 1944 act provided no dedicated construction money for the Interstate System itself, it created the system's legal framework and route network. Full funding arrived with the Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956, which established the Highway Trust Fund and the 90-percent federal share that built the Interstate Highway System as it exists today.
