William Dudley Haywood (February 4, 1869 – May 18, 1928), known as "Big Bill" Haywood, was an American labor organizer and a founding leader of the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW). A former hard-rock miner who rose to lead the Western Federation of Miners (WFM), he was one of the most prominent — and to employers and officials, most feared — labor radicals of his era, advocating industrial unionism and the organization of all workers into "One Big Union."

Early life and the WFM

Born in Salt Lake City, Haywood went to work in the mines as a teenager, losing his right eye in a mining accident. He joined the Western Federation of Miners and rose quickly, becoming secretary-treasurer of the union in 1901 under president Charles Moyer. The WFM's strikes in Colorado — notably the Colorado Labor Wars of 1903–04 — were met with state militia, deportations, and martial law, radicalizing Haywood toward revolutionary industrial unionism.

Murder trial

In 1906 Haywood, Moyer, and George Pettibone were arrested in Denver and taken to Idaho to stand trial for the assassination of former Idaho governor Frank Steunenberg, based on the confession of Harry Orchard. The prosecution, assisted by Pinkerton detective James McParland, faced Clarence Darrow for the defense. Haywood was acquitted in 1907 in one of the most famous trials of the century.

The IWW

Haywood had helped found the IWW in Chicago in 1905, famously opening its convention by calling it "the Continental Congress of the working class." After leaving the WFM he became the IWW's most prominent figure, leading or supporting strikes including the Lawrence textile strike of 1912 (the "Bread and Roses" strike) and the Paterson silk strike of 1913. Opposed to American entry into the First World War, he was convicted in 1918 in a mass federal trial of IWW leaders under the Espionage Act and sentenced to twenty years in prison.

Exile and death

While his appeal was pending in 1921, Haywood fled to the Soviet Union, where he was welcomed by the Bolshevik leadership and worked on industrial projects, including the Kuzbass autonomous industrial colony in Siberia. He died in Moscow in 1928; his ashes were divided between the Kremlin Wall Necropolis and Chicago, near the Haymarket martyrs' monument. His autobiography, Bill Haywood's Book, was published posthumously in 1929.
