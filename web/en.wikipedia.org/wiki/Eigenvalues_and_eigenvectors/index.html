Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A vector being stretched by a transformation while keeping its direction, next to a vector that changes direction]

In linear algebra, an eigenvector of a linear transformation is a non-zero vector whose direction is unchanged by the transformation; it is only scaled, by a factor called its eigenvalue. In matrix terms, Av = λv.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors reveal a transformation's essential actions — its axes of stretching, rotation behaviour and stability — and are used in physics (vibration modes, quantum mechanics), engineering, statistics (principal component analysis) and computer science (Google's PageRank treats the web as a giant matrix).

Finding eigenvalues

The eigenvalues of a matrix are the roots of its characteristic polynomial, found by setting the determinant of (A − λI) to zero. Whether a matrix can be diagonalised — rewritten in a basis of eigenvectors — depends on having enough independent eigenvectors.

The word comes from the German eigen, 'own' or 'characteristic'; Hilbert popularised the terminology in the early 1900s.
