The Torpedinidae family of rays, commonly known as the electric rays, is one of the families in the order Torpediniformes. Members of this family are found worldwide in temperate and tropical oceans, typically in shallow coastal waters over sandy or muddy bottoms.

Like other electric rays, Torpedinidae possess a pair of kidney-shaped electric organs at the base of the pectoral fins, derived from modified muscle tissue. These organs can generate powerful electric discharges — in large species such as the Atlantic torpedo (Torpedo nobiliana) reaching around 220 volts — used to stun prey and deter predators.

Genera

The family includes the genera Torpedo and Tetronarce (the latter split from Torpedo in modern classifications), together comprising roughly twenty-two recognized species. Well-known species include the common torpedo (Torpedo torpedo), the marbled electric ray (Torpedo marmorata), the Atlantic torpedo (Tetronarce nobiliana) and the Pacific electric ray (Tetronarce californica).

Biology

Torpedinids have a rounded, flabby disc, a short muscular tail with two dorsal fins and a well-developed caudal fin. They are slow swimmers that often bury themselves in sediment during the day. They feed on bony fishes and invertebrates, which they envelop with the disc and stun with electric shocks. Reproduction is aplacental viviparous (ovoviviparous).

Electric rays were known to the ancients: the numbing effect of Torpedo was remarked by Aristotle and Pliny, and physicians such as Scribonius Largus recommended live torpedo shocks as a treatment for headache and gout.
