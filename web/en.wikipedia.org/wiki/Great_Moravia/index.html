Great Moravia

Great Moravia (Latin: Regnum Marahensium; Greek: Μεγάλη Μοραβία) was the first major West Slavic state, arising in Central Europe in the 9th century in the region of present-day Moravia, Slovakia and neighbouring lands. At its height under Svatopluk I it controlled territory stretching from Bohemia to parts of modern Hungary and Poland. Great Moravia is celebrated as the cradle of Slavonic literacy through the mission of the Byzantine brothers Cyril and Methodius.

History

The state was formed around 833 when Mojmir I of Moravia expelled Prince Pribina of Nitra, uniting the two principalities. Caught between the Frankish Empire to the west and Byzantium to the southeast, Mojmir's successor Rastislav sought to counter Frankish influence: in 862 he appealed to the Byzantine emperor Michael III, who sent Constantine (Cyril) and Methodius in 863. The brothers devised the Glagolitic alphabet, translated scripture and liturgy into Old Church Slavonic, and trained Slavic clergy — an achievement commemorated throughout the Slavic world.

Rastislav was overthrown and blinded after a Frankish-backed coup by his nephew Svatopluk I (r. 870–894), under whom Great Moravia reached its greatest extent and power, at times dominating Bohemia, Silesia, Lesser Poland and the northern Danube plain. Methodius, made archbishop of Sirmium, faced continued hostility from the Bavarian clergy until his death in 885, when his disciples were expelled and found refuge in Bulgaria.

Decline and fall

After Svatopluk's death his sons Mojmir II and Svatopluk II quarrelled, and the state fragmented as Bohemia broke away. Weakened Moravia could not withstand the Magyar (Hungarian) incursions into the Danube basin; Great Moravia collapsed sometime around 902–907, traditionally with the Battle of Pressburg (907). Its territory was divided among Hungary, Bohemia and Poland, while the Cyrillo-Methodian legacy survived in the Slavonic liturgy and literature of the Balkans and Rus'.
