Stimulated emission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of stimulated emission: an incoming photon causing an excited atom to emit an identical photon]
One photon in, two identical photons out.

Stimulated emission is the process by which an incoming photon of a specific frequency can interact with an excited atomic electron (or other excited molecular state), causing it to drop to a lower energy level and emit a second photon identical to the first in frequency, phase, polarisation and direction of travel. It is the physical principle of the Laser and the Maser.

Albert Einstein derived the process theoretically in 1917 from thermodynamic consistency, introducing his A and B coefficients: alongside absorption and Spontaneous emission, stimulated emission must exist for atoms to reach thermal equilibrium with radiation — one of the earliest triumphs of quantum reasoning about light, anticipating quantum mechanics itself. Because the emitted photon clones the incoming one, stimulated emission amplifies light coherently — but only if more atoms are excited than not (population inversion), a non-equilibrium condition that lasers and masers exist to engineer (Townes's maser, 1953–54; Maiman's ruby laser, 1960).

Relation to absorption

Einstein's coefficients relate the rates: stimulated emission and absorption are symmetric processes (same B coefficient), so net amplification requires inversion — achieved by optical or electrical pumping into metastable levels. The balance of the three processes in thermal equilibrium reproduces Planck's Black-body radiation law, which was Einstein's original goal.
