Monkey

Monkey is a common name that may refer to most mammals of the infraorder Simiiformes, also known as simians. Traditionally, all animals in the group now known as simians are counted as monkeys except the apes. Thus monkeys, in that sense, constitute an incomplete paraphyletic grouping; in the broader sense based on cladistics, apes (Hominoidea) are also included, making the terms monkeys and simians synonyms in regard to their scope.

Classification

Monkeys are divided into two main groups. New World monkeys (parvorder Platyrrhini) live in Central and South America and include marmosets, tamarins, capuchins, and howler monkeys; they typically have broad, flat noses and many have prehensile tails. Old World monkeys (family Cercopithecidae) are native to Africa and Asia and include baboons, macaques, and colobus monkeys; they have narrower noses and non-prehensile tails.

Characteristics

Monkeys range in size from the pygmy marmoset, about 14–16 cm long, to the male mandrill, which can approach a metre in length. Most species are arboreal, though some, such as baboons, live mainly on the ground. The majority have tails, unlike the apes. Monkeys generally have forward-facing eyes giving binocular vision, grasping hands with opposable thumbs, and relatively large brains. Most are diurnal and social, living in troops with complex hierarchies.

Diet and behaviour

Most monkeys are omnivorous, eating fruit, leaves, seeds, flowers, insects, and occasionally small animals. Many species use vocalisations, facial expressions, and grooming to maintain social bonds. Some, such as capuchins, are known for tool use.

Relationship with humans

Monkeys figure prominently in the cultures and religions of many societies, including the Hindu god Hanuman. They have been used in scientific research and were among the first animals sent into space. Many species are threatened by habitat loss and hunting.
