Pogrom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pogrom is a violent riot or mob attack directed against a particular ethnic or religious group, in particular the organised massacre of helpless people. The term entered English from Russian (pogrom, from gromit', "to destroy, wreak havoc") in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when it was used to describe waves of attacks on Jews in the Russian Empire. It has since been applied to similar outbreaks against other minorities in various countries.

Characteristics

Historians use the term for violence that is typically carried out by a mob drawn from a majority population against a vulnerable minority, often with looting of homes and businesses, and characteristically with the acquiescence, encouragement, or deliberate inaction of the authorities. Pogroms differ from both spontaneous riots and state-run extermination programmes, though they may overlap with either.

Notable examples

  • The Russian Empire pogroms of 1881–1882, following the assassination of Tsar Alexander II, and the Kishinev pogrom of 1903, which drew international condemnation.
  • The pogroms of the Russian Civil War (1918–1921), in which tens of thousands of Jews were killed by various armed forces in Ukraine.
  • Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938), the Nazi-organised nationwide pogrom in Germany and Austria in which synagogues were burned and Jewish businesses destroyed.
  • The Farhud in Baghdad in June 1941 and the Jedwabne pogrom in German-occupied Poland in July 1941.
  • Anti-Tamil riots in Sri Lanka (1983) and anti-Sikh violence in Delhi (1984) have also been described as pogroms.

Usage and scholarship

Because of its origins the word carries strong associations with antisemitic violence, but scholars and journalists have applied it to attacks on Armenians, Greeks, Chinese Indonesians, and other groups. Definitions remain debated, particularly over the degree of state complicity implied and the term's relationship to ethnic cleansing and genocide.
