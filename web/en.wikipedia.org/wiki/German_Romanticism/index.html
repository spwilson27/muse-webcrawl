German Romanticism

German Romanticism (German: Deutsche Romantik) was the dominant intellectual movement in German-speaking lands in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, influencing philosophy, literature, music, and the visual arts. Reacting against the rationalism of the Enlightenment and the upheavals of the Industrial Revolution, it prized emotion, nature, individualism, and the medieval past.

Phases

Historians usually divide the movement into Early Romanticism (Frühromantik, c. 1798–1804), centred on Jena, and High and Late Romanticism centred on Heidelberg, Berlin, and Munich. The Jena circle included the brothers August Wilhelm and Friedrich Schlegel, Novalis, Ludwig Tieck, and the philosopher F. W. J. Schelling. Key concepts included Romantic irony, the fragment, and the longing (Sehnsucht) for an unattainable absolute, symbolised by Novalis's "blue flower."

Literature and thought

Writers such as E. T. A. Hoffmann, Joseph von Eichendorff, and Clemens Brentano explored the uncanny, dreams, and folk tradition; the Brothers Grimm collected German folktales in the same spirit. In philosophy, Fichte and Schelling developed forms of idealism emphasising the creative self and nature as a living whole.

Art and music

In painting, Caspar David Friedrich produced sublime, contemplative landscapes charged with religious feeling, while the Nazarene movement sought to revive medieval and early Renaissance art. In music, German Romanticism came to dominate the 19th century through composers such as Carl Maria von Weber, Franz Schubert, Robert Schumann, and ultimately Richard Wagner.
