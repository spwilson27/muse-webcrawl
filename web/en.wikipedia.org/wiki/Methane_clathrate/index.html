Methane clathrate

Methane clathrate (CH4·5.75H2O or 4CH4·23H2O), also called methane hydrate, methane ice, or fire ice, is a solid clathrate compound in which a large amount of methane is trapped within a crystal structure of water, forming a solid similar to ice. It forms under conditions of low temperature and high pressure in marine sediments and beneath permafrost.

Occurrence

Methane clathrates occur along continental margins at water depths of several hundred metres, where cold temperatures and pressure stabilise the hydrate a few hundred metres below the seafloor, and in association with Arctic permafrost. The amount of organic carbon stored in hydrates is estimated to be on the order of thousands of gigatonnes — possibly rivalling or exceeding all other fossil fuels combined.

Climate significance

Because methane is a potent greenhouse gas, large-scale dissociation of hydrates has been proposed as a driver of past rapid warming events, the "clathrate gun hypothesis," notably for the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum. Destabilisation can also trigger submarine landslides; the Storegga slide off Norway has been linked by some researchers to hydrate breakdown. The near-term risk of catastrophic release from marine hydrates is debated, with many studies suggesting most hydrate-derived methane is oxidised in the sediment or water column before reaching the atmosphere.

Energy resource

Hydrates are a potential unconventional natural gas resource. Production tests have been conducted in permafrost (Mallik, Canada; Alaska North Slope) and offshore (Nankai Trough, Japan, in 2013 and 2017), typically by depressurisation, but commercial production has not been achieved.
