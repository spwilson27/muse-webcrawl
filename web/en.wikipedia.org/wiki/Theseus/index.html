Theseus is a hero and legendary king of Athens in Greek mythology, son of Aethra and either King Aegeus or Poseidon. The Athenians celebrated him as their national hero: a civilising slayer of monsters and brigands, the unifier (synoecist) of Attica, and a founder-figure of Athenian democracy in later tradition.

Labours and the Minotaur

Raised in Troezen, Theseus retrieved the sword and sandals his father had hidden under a rock and took the land road to Athens, clearing it of murderous brigands — Periphetes, Sinis, the Crommyonian Sow, Sciron, Cercyon and Procrustes, each killed by his own method. At Athens he was recognised by Aegeus just in time to escape Medea's poison. His greatest exploit was volunteering among the Athenian youths sent as tribute to Crete, entering the Labyrinth and killing the Minotaur with the help of Ariadne, whose ball of thread guided his escape — and whom he then abandoned on Naxos, where Dionysus found her. Forgetting to change his ship's black sails to white, he caused the despairing Aegeus to throw himself into the sea that bears his name.

Later life and cult

As king, Theseus was credited with uniting Attica's villages under Athens. His later story darkens: his marriage to Phaedra ended in her false accusation and suicide and the death of his son Hippolytus; with his friend Pirithous he attempted abductions of Helen and of Persephone from the underworld, from which Heracles rescued him. Exiled, he died on Skyros. In 475 BCE the Athenian general Cimon claimed to recover his bones, which were honoured in the Theseion sanctuary — an early example of hero-relic politics.
