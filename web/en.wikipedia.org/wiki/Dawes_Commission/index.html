The Dawes Commission, formally the Commission to the Five Civilized Tribes, was a United States government commission created in 1893 to negotiate with the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek (Muscogee) and Seminole nations — the "Five Civilized Tribes" — for the extinguishment of their communal land titles in Indian Territory and the allotment of their lands to individual tribal citizens.

Background

The Dawes Act of 1887 had authorised the President to allot reservation lands held in common to individual Native Americans, but it expressly exempted the Five Tribes, whose lands were guaranteed by treaty and held under fee-simple patents. Reformers, railroad interests and Oklahoma land seekers pressed to extend allotment to Indian Territory regardless. Congress therefore created the commission in 1893, naming Senator Henry L. Dawes of Massachusetts, author of the 1887 act, as its first chairman.

Negotiations and enrolment

For several years the tribes refused to negotiate, regarding the commission's mission as a violation of their treaties. The Curtis Act of 1898 removed the need for tribal consent and directed the commission to enrol tribal citizens and allot the land anyway. Working from tribal rolls, censuses and its own hearings, the commission determined citizenship — by blood, by marriage, as freedmen (descendants of the tribes' formerly enslaved people), and in other categories — recording each person on census cards that remain the master genealogical record of the Five Tribes.

Between 1898 and 1907 (with additions to 1914) the commission enrolled more than 101,000 people. Enrolled citizens received individual allotments, typically 160 acres for Choctaw and Chickasaw citizens and smaller parcels of "surplus" land being opened to non-Native settlement; tribal governments were scheduled for dissolution in 1906, paving the way for Oklahoma statehood in 1907.

The Dawes Rolls

The commission's final product, the Dawes Rolls, is the definitive list of citizens of the Five Tribes at allotment. Today the Rolls serve as the base roll for citizenship in the modern Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee and Seminole nations, and proof of descent from a person listed on them is the standard route to tribal enrolment and to a federal Certificate of Degree of Indian Blood.

Criticism

Enrolment was marred by fraud, arbitrary blood-quantum assignments, the refusal of many traditionalists — notably the Cherokee Keetoowah under Redbird Smith and the Creek "Snakes" under Chitto Harjo — and the relegation of freedmen to separate rolls with diminished rights, a source of citizenship disputes that persists in tribal courts and politics to the present day.
