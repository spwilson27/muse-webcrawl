Childhood cancer is cancer in a child, conventionally defined as malignancy diagnosed before the age of 15 (and often extended to adolescents and young adults up to 19). Cancers in children differ biologically from adult cancers: they are rarer, are usually not linked to lifestyle factors, and the most common types are leukemias (about a quarter to a third of cases), tumours of the brain and central nervous system, and lymphomas, followed by neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour, bone cancers, and soft-tissue sarcomas. Globally, an estimated 400,000 children and adolescents develop cancer each year.

Treatment and outcomes

Treatment typically combines chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation therapy, coordinated through cooperative clinical trial groups such as the Children's Oncology Group. Survival has improved dramatically since the mid-20th century: overall five-year survival now exceeds 80% in high-income countries, and acute lymphoblastic leukaemia — once almost uniformly fatal — has survival rates above 90%. In low- and middle-income countries, however, survival is often below 30% owing to delayed diagnosis, treatment abandonment, and limited resources; the WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer aims to raise global survival to at least 60% by 2030.

Causes

The causes of most childhood cancers are unknown. A minority arise in the context of inherited cancer predisposition syndromes (such as Li–Fraumeni syndrome or hereditary retinoblastoma) or conditions like Down syndrome, which increases leukaemia risk. Ionising radiation and certain prior chemotherapy exposures are established risk factors for second cancers.

Late effects

Survivors may face long-term "late effects" of treatment, including cardiac and pulmonary damage, infertility, cognitive effects, and increased risk of second malignancies, requiring lifelong follow-up care.
