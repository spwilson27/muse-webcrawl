Rights of Man (1791) is a book by Thomas Paine, including 31 articles, positing that popular political revolution is permissible when a government does not safeguard the natural rights of its people. Using these points as a base, it defends the French Revolution against Edmund Burke's denunciation in Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790). It was published in two parts in March 1791 and February 1792 by Joseph Johnson and J. S. Jordan, and became one of the best-selling political works of the 18th century.

Background

Paine, the author of Common Sense (1776), was in France during the early Revolution and knew its leaders. Burke's Reflections, with its defence of hereditary monarchy and the British constitution, provoked a flood of responses in the "Revolution controversy"; Paine's was the most famous. Part I was dedicated to George Washington.

Argument

Part I argues against Burke's contention that the British constitution of 1688 bound posterity forever, insisting that every generation must be free to act for itself. Paine distinguishes the natural rights retained by individuals (of the mind, of conscience, of acting for one's own happiness) from the civil rights exchanged for membership in society (such as security and protection), and locates the origin of government in a compact of individuals rather than in conquest or heredity; he attacks monarchy and hereditary succession as illegitimate and reads the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen as the model of legitimate government.

Part II turns constructive, proposing practical policies: representative democracy with broad suffrage, progressive income taxation, old-age pensions, public support for the education of poor children, maternity benefits, public works for the unemployed, and the abolition of the House of Lords and of laws of primogeniture. Paine argued that Britain's heavy expenditure on monarchy, aristocracy, and war could be redirected to such welfare measures, making Rights of Man an early blueprint of the modern welfare state.

Reception and prosecution

The book sold in extraordinary numbers — Part II was deliberately issued in a cheap edition — alarming the British government. In 1792 Paine was charged with seditious libel, tried in absentia (he had left for France, where he was elected to the National Convention), convicted, and outlawed. Booksellers who sold the work were prosecuted, yet the book's influence on British radicalism, Chartism, and later labour politics endured. Rights of Man remains a classic statement of democratic and egalitarian political thought.
