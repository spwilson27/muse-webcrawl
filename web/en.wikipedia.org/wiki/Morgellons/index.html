Morgellons

Morgellons is the informal name of a self-diagnosed, scientifically unsubstantiated skin condition in which individuals have sores that they believe contain fibrous material. Morgellons is not well understood, and the scientific consensus is that it is a form of delusional parasitosis (delusional infestation), in which individuals have a fixed, false belief that their skin is infested by parasites or living material.

History

The name was coined in 2002 by Mary Leitao, who applied the term — taken from a 17th-century description of children with coarse hairs on their backs in Sir Thomas Browne's writings — to her son's condition. The Morgellons Research Foundation promoted the idea of a distinct infectious disease, and media coverage in the mid-2000s greatly increased self-diagnosis.

Investigations

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention conducted a multi-year study (results published in 2012) of 115 identified cases in northern California. It found no common underlying medical condition or infectious source; the fibres were mostly cellulose consistent with cotton, and the sores were consistent with ordinary causes such as insect bites and scratching. The CDC concluded the phenomenon was an "unexplained dermopathy" similar to delusional infestation.

Views

A small number of researchers and physicians have argued that Morgellons may be associated with Lyme disease and tick-borne spirochaetes, but these claims have not been accepted by mainstream dermatology. Patient advocates dispute the delusional parasitosis classification and the stigma attached to it. Treatment in mainstream practice focuses on managing skin lesions and, where appropriate, treating the underlying psychiatric symptoms with empathy and antipsychotic medication.
