Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov (25 April 1903 – 20 October 1987) was a Soviet mathematician who contributed to the mathematics of probability theory, topology, intuitionistic logic, turbulence, classical mechanics, algorithmic information theory and computational complexity. He is widely regarded as one of the most important mathematicians of the 20th century.

Life

Kolmogorov was born in Tambov and raised by his aunt after his mother's early death. He studied at Moscow State University, where his early work on Fourier series attracted international attention while he was still a student. He became a professor at Moscow in 1931 and spent his career there, founding a school of probability and mentoring many students, including Vladimir Arnold and Yakov Sinai.

Contributions

In 1933 Kolmogorov published Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung ("Foundations of the Theory of Probability"), which axiomatized probability theory on the basis of measure theory and remains the standard framework. With Aleksandr Khinchin and others he developed the theory of stochastic processes, including the Kolmogorov extension theorem and the equations governing diffusion. His 1941 papers on turbulence introduced the statistical theory of locally isotropic turbulence and the famous −5/3 energy spectrum law. With Vladimir Arnold and Jürgen Moser he shares credit for KAM theory on the stability of integrable Hamiltonian systems. In the 1960s he independently developed, with Ray Solomonoff and Gregory Chaitin, the theory of algorithmic complexity — now called Kolmogorov complexity — measuring the information content of an object by the length of its shortest description. He also made major contributions to the theory of dynamical systems (Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy), approximation theory, and the reform of mathematics education in the Soviet Union.

Legacy

Numerous concepts bear his name, including the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test in statistics, the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations, and Kolmogorov's three-series theorem. He received many honours, including the Stalin Prize, the Lenin Prize, and the Wolf Prize in Mathematics.
