Transitional housing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transitional housing is temporary housing for certain segments of the homeless population, including working homeless people who are earning too little money to afford long-term housing. Transitional housing is set up to transition residents into permanent, affordable housing. It is not in an emergency homeless shelter, but usually a room or apartment in a residence with support services.

Transitional housing typically involves a supportive community environment for a limited period of stay, often up to 24 months. It provides housing, social services, and case management designed to help residents develop the skills and resources needed to secure and maintain permanent housing.

History

The concept of transitional housing developed in the 1980s as a response to the homelessness crisis in the United States. The Stewart B. McKinney Homeless Assistance Act of 1987 established federal funding for transitional housing programs. The model was intended to bridge the gap between emergency shelters, which offer only short-term relief, and permanent housing.

For many years transitional housing was a dominant element of the federal Continuum of Care system. Beginning around 2009, federal policy under the HEARTH Act shifted emphasis toward rapid rehousing and Housing First approaches, which move people directly into permanent housing with voluntary supportive services. Critics of the older transitional model argued that its program requirements — such as sobriety or participation in services before "graduating" — could screen out the most vulnerable people. Supporters counter that structured transitional programs remain valuable for particular populations, including veterans, young people aging out of foster care, people leaving domestic violence situations, and people in recovery.

Models

Transitional housing programs take several forms:

  • Congregate facilities, in which residents live in a single building with shared facilities and on-site staff.
  • Scattered-site housing, in which residents live in apartments leased by a provider throughout the community.
  • Transitional housing for families, which keeps households together and provides childcare and parenting support.
  • Bridge housing, a shorter-term variant used while a permanent housing placement is arranged.

Services

Programs commonly provide case management, employment assistance, life-skills training, mental health and substance-use treatment referrals, and help obtaining identification documents, benefits, and permanent housing. Residents may pay a portion of their income as rent or program fees, sometimes held in escrow to build savings for a security deposit.

Effectiveness

Studies generally find that participants who complete transitional housing programs have better housing outcomes than those who leave early, though researchers note selection effects: people who succeed in structured programs may differ from those who do not. The model remains a recognized component of homeless services systems in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia, alongside emergency shelter, rapid rehousing, and permanent supportive housing.
