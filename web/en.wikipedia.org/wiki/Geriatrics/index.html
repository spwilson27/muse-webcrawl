Geriatrics

Geriatrics, or geriatric medicine, is the medical specialty focused on providing care for the unique health needs of older adults. Its aim is to promote health and prevent and treat diseases and disabilities in older people, taking account of the complexity caused by multimorbidity, frailty, polypharmacy, and cognitive and functional decline. The term was coined by Ignatz Leo Nascher in 1909, from the Greek geron ("old man") and iatros ("healer"). It differs from gerontology, the broader study of ageing.

History

The modern discipline owes much to Marjory Warren, who in the 1930s–40s at the West Middlesex Hospital in London showed that systematic assessment and rehabilitation of elderly patients, previously left bedridden in chronic wards, greatly improved outcomes — work that led to geriatrics becoming a recognised specialty in the new National Health Service in 1948. Bernard Isaacs later described the classic "geriatric giants": immobility, instability, incontinence, and intellectual impairment.

Approach

Geriatric care is typically multidisciplinary, involving physicians, nurses, pharmacists, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and social workers, coordinated through comprehensive geriatric assessment. Core concerns include the "geriatric syndromes" — frailty, falls, delirium, dementia, sarcopenia, and malnutrition — which often matter more than any single disease. Geriatricians manage age-related changes in drug metabolism (deprescribing harmful or unnecessary medicines), assess decision-making capacity, plan perioperative and palliative care, and address ethical issues around autonomy and end-of-life care. Specialised models include acute care for elders (ACE) units, orthogeriatrics (co-management of hip fractures), and hospital-at-home services.
