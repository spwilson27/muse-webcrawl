Virchow's triad

Virchow's triad (or the triad of Virchow) describes the three broad categories of factors that contribute to thrombosis — the formation of blood clots within vessels. The three components are:

  1. Stasis — alterations in normal blood flow (haemostasis of flow), such as venous stasis from immobility, long-haul travel, bed rest, or heart failure;
  2. Endothelial injury — injury or dysfunction of the vessel wall (endothelium), from trauma, surgery, inflammation, hypertension, or atherosclerosis;
  3. Hypercoagulability — alterations in the constituents of the blood that increase its tendency to clot, whether inherited (e.g. factor V Leiden) or acquired (e.g. malignancy, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, antiphospholipid syndrome).

History

The triad is named after the German pathologist Rudolf Virchow (1821–1902), whose mid-nineteenth-century work on thrombosis and embolism laid the foundations of the subject; he coined terms such as "thrombosis" and "embolism". Virchow himself did not state a neat three-part "triad" in modern form — the tidy formulation was attributed to him by later writers in the twentieth century — but the three domains faithfully reflect factors his work identified. The framework remains a standard teaching model for venous thromboembolism, including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.

Clinical significance

The triad guides risk assessment and prevention: measures such as early mobilisation, compression stockings, and prophylactic anticoagulation after surgery target stasis and hypercoagulability, while minimizing vessel trauma reduces endothelial injury. Risk factors for venous thromboembolism are commonly taught by assigning them to one of the three arms of the triad.
