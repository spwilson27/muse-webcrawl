The Hellenic arc or Aegean arc is an arcuate mountain range and subduction zone in the eastern Mediterranean Sea, south of Greece. It is formed where the African Plate is being subducted beneath the Aegean Sea Plate at a rate of a few centimetres per year. The arc runs from the Ionian Islands in the west, south of Crete, to Rhodes and the coast of southwestern Turkey in the east.

Geology

The Hellenic arc is the surface expression of one of the most seismically active subduction zones in Europe. The subducting slab descends to depths of several hundred kilometres beneath the Aegean. The curvature of the arc reflects the geometry of the slab and the southwestward motion of the Aegean microplate relative to Africa, driven in part by the westward extrusion of Anatolia along the North Anatolian Fault.

North of the Hellenic arc lies the South Aegean volcanic arc — a chain of volcanic islands including Methana, Milos, Santorini, Nisyros, and Kos — formed by magma generated above the subducting slab. The eruption of Santorini (Thera) around 1600 BC was one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recorded history.

Seismicity and tsunamis

Great earthquakes along the arc have repeatedly devastated the region. The 365 Crete earthquake, whose magnitude is estimated above 8, uplifted western Crete by up to 9 metres and sent a tsunami across the Mediterranean. In 1303 another great earthquake near Crete generated a destructive tsunami that struck Heraklion and Alexandria. The 1956 Amorgos earthquake (magnitude 7.5) produced a tsunami with run-ups locally exceeding 20 metres. Modern hazard assessments treat the Hellenic arc as capable of magnitude 8+ megathrust events.
