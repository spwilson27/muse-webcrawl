Extensometer

An extensometer is a device used to measure changes in the length of an object. The name derives from "extension" — the change in the linear dimension of a body — plus "meter". Extensometers are central to materials testing, where they measure the strain of a specimen under load, and they are also used in structural and geotechnical monitoring to record crack opening, ground movement, and displacement over time.

Types

Contact extensometers

The commonest laboratory type is the clip-on contact extensometer, which attaches directly to a test specimen with knife edges or clips and measures the extension between two points a known gauge length apart. Transducers inside the device — historically mechanical dial mechanisms, and in modern instruments strain-gauge or capacitive sensors — convert the displacement into an electrical signal. Axial models measure elongation along the loading direction; transverse (diametral) models measure the change in width or diameter, enabling determination of Poisson's ratio; and averaging and biaxial variants measure strain in two directions.

Non-contact extensometers

Video extensometers track marks on the specimen with a camera, and laser extensometers scan reflective markers or the specimen surface, computing strain without touching the specimen. Non-contact measurement is essential where contact would damage the specimen (soft materials, thin films, wires), where the specimen is inside an environmental chamber at high or low temperature, or where strain must be followed to failure — a clip-on device is normally removed before fracture to avoid damage. Digital image correlation extends this principle to full-field two- or three-dimensional strain maps.

Field extensometers

In geotechnical engineering, borehole extensometers use anchors fixed at various depths connected by rods or wires to a reference head, measuring ground movement, settlement, or slope displacement. Crack gauges and laser distance meters serve similar roles on structures.

Use in materials testing

In a standard tensile test, an extensometer measures the specimen's elongation while a universal testing machine records the applied force; dividing elongation by the original gauge length gives the strain, from which the stress–strain curve, Young's modulus, yield strength, and Poisson's ratio are derived. Accuracy classes of extensometers are specified in standards such as ASTM E83 and ISO 9513, which define permissible errors and the calibration of gauge length and displacement.
