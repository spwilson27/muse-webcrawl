Globe

A globe is a spherical model of Earth, of some other celestial body, or of the celestial sphere. Globes serve purposes similar to maps, but unlike maps they do not distort the surface they portray except to scale it down; the relative shapes, areas, distances, and directions of features are preserved. A globe of Earth is called a terrestrial globe, and a globe of the celestial sphere a celestial globe.

History

Spherical models of the Earth date to classical antiquity: Crates of Mallus is credited with constructing a globe in the second century BC, and the Greek geographer Ptolemy's writings informed globe-making for centuries. The oldest surviving terrestrial globe is the Erdapfel ("earth apple") made by Martin Behaim in Nuremberg in 1492, just before news of Columbus's voyage reached Europe, so it shows no Americas. Gerardus Mercator and other early modern cartographers developed methods of printing map sheets (gores) that could be pasted onto spheres, making globes reproducible in quantity.

Types and manufacture

Traditional globes are mounted on an axis tilted at 23.5°, matching Earth's axial tilt, in a fixed meridian ring or a cradle that allows free rotation. Modern globes may be physical-relief, political, illuminated, magnetic floating, or fully digital (such as Google Earth). Celestial globes show the stars on the inside-outward sphere as seen from Earth; "armillary spheres" and planetaria are related instruments. Classroom and reference globes remain common educational tools, often at scales around 1:40 million.
