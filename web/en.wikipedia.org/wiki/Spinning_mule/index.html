The spinning mule is a machine used to spin cotton and other fibres. It was invented by Samuel Crompton of Bolton, England, in 1779, combining features of James Hargreaves's spinning jenny and Richard Arkwright's water frame — hence the name "mule", a hybrid. The mule produced yarn that was simultaneously fine, soft, and strong, allowing British manufacturers to spin the high-count yarns needed for fine muslins and to dominate world cotton markets through the 19th century.

Operation

In the mule, rovings pass through rollers and are mounted behind a moving carriage carrying the spindles. As the carriage draws away (a "stretch" of about 1.4 metres), the rollers pay out roving and the rotating spindles draw and twist the fibres; as the carriage returns, the spun yarn is wound onto the spindles in a cop. The intermittent action — draw, twist, then wind — gives finer control than continuous frames. Early mules were hand-operated; later ones were driven by steam engines through line shafts, and Roberts's self-acting mule (patented 1825–1830) automated the winding motion. A single large mule could carry over 1,300 spindles and required a skilled minder, usually a man, assisted by young piecers and scavengers who repaired broken threads and cleaned the machinery.

Historical significance

The mule was a central technology of the Industrial Revolution in textiles. Mule spinning spread through Lancashire towns such as Bolton and Oldham and was adapted for wool (the woollen mule and condenser system). Although ring spinning eventually displaced it for most counts because it was faster and needed less skill, mule spinning survived into the late 20th century for the finest yarns; the last commercial mules in Britain ran at mills in Lancashire until the 1980s–90s. Working examples are preserved at museums such as Quarry Bank Mill and the Museum of Science and Industry, Manchester.
