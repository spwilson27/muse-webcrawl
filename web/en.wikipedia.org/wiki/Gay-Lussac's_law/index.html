Gay-Lussac's law

Gay-Lussac's law usually refers to Joseph-Louis Gay-Lussac's 1802 law of thermal expansion of gases, also known as the pressure law: for a given mass of gas at constant volume, the pressure is directly proportional to its absolute temperature. The name is also applied to Gay-Lussac's 1809 law of combining volumes, which states that the volumes of gases reacting together are in simple whole-number ratios.

At constant volume, pressure is proportional to absolute temperature.

The pressure law

Mathematically, the pressure law is expressed as:

P ∝ T  or  P1/T1 = P2/T2

where P is pressure and T is absolute temperature in kelvin. Gay-Lussac published the result in 1802 in a paper on the expansion of gases, crediting unpublished work by Jacques Charles from the 1780s; the underlying volume–temperature relation is separately known as Charles's law. Together with Boyle's law and Avogadro's law, the pressure law is a special case of the ideal gas law.

Law of combining volumes

In 1808–1809 Gay-Lussac announced that gases combine in simple volumetric proportions and that the volume of the products, if gaseous, also bears a simple ratio to the reactants. For example, two volumes of hydrogen combine with one volume of oxygen to form two volumes of water vapour. This regularity helped support Avogadro's hypothesis and the emerging atomic theory, although Gay-Lussac himself did not draw the molecular conclusions.
