The Scottish Enlightenment (Scots: Scots Enlichtenment; Scottish Gaelic: Soillseachadh na h-Alba) was the period in 18th- and early-19th-century Scotland characterised by an outpouring of intellectual and scientific accomplishments. By the 18th century, Scotland had a network of parish schools in the Lowlands and five universities (St Andrews, Glasgow, Edinburgh, King's College and Marischal College, Aberdeen), and the Enlightenment culture was centred in these institutions and in clubs and societies in Scotland's burghs.

Sharing the humanist and rationalist outlook of the European Enlightenment of the same period, the thinkers of the Scottish Enlightenment asserted the fundamental importance of human reason combined with a rejection of any authority that could not be justified by reason. They held to an optimistic belief in the ability of humanity to effect changes for the better in society and nature, guided only by reason. It was this latter feature that gave the Scottish Enlightenment its special flavour, distinguishing it from its continental counterparts.

Major figures and ideas

Francis Hutcheson, professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow, is often regarded as a founding figure; his pupil Adam Smith published The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) and An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776), founding works of modern economics. David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40) and his essays transformed philosophy with their empiricism and scepticism. Thomas Reid founded the Scottish school of Common Sense philosophy in reply to Hume. Adam Ferguson is seen as a founder of sociology; William Robertson and Hume pioneered modern historiography; James Hutton founded modern geology with his theory of uniformitarianism; Joseph Black discovered latent heat and carbon dioxide; and James Watt's improvements to the steam engine helped power the Industrial Revolution.

Institutions and society

The movement was sustained by Scotland's universities, by the Church of Scotland's "Moderate" party, and by a dense culture of clubs and societies — the Select Society, the Poker Club, the Royal Society of Edinburgh (founded 1783) and the Speculative Society among them. Publishing flourished, with Edinburgh becoming a major centre of the British book trade. Voltaire remarked that "we look to Scotland for all our ideas of civilisation".

Legacy

Scottish Enlightenment ideas influenced the American Founding Fathers, political economy, geology, sociology and literature across Europe and North America. Its achievements emerged remarkably soon after the Union of 1707, and the era is sometimes called a golden age of Scottish culture; Edinburgh earned the nickname "Athens of the North". By the early 19th century the movement waned, as Romanticism and industrial upheaval reshaped Scottish intellectual life.
