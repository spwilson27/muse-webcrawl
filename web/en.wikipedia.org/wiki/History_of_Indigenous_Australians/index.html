History of Indigenous Australians

The history of Indigenous Australians began with the arrival of the ancestors of modern Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples on the Australian continent at least 65,000 years ago, making theirs among the oldest continuous cultures on Earth. From 1788, British colonisation brought dispossession, disease and frontier violence, alongside Indigenous resistance and survival; from the late 20th century, legal and political movements have pursued recognition, land rights and reconciliation.

Deep history

Archaeological evidence, including the Madjedbebe rock shelter in Arnhem Land, indicates human occupation by around 65,000 years ago. As sea levels rose after the last Ice Age, Australia became isolated; mainland groups developed diverse languages (more than 250 at European contact), complex kinship systems, fire-stick farming, and trade networks. Tasmanian Aboriginal people were cut off when Bass Strait flooded around 12,000 years ago. In the Torres Strait, Melanesian-influenced island cultures developed horticulture.

Contact before 1788

From at least the early 18th century, Macassan trepangers from Sulawesi visited northern Australia seasonally, trading with Yolngu and other groups. Dutch, French and British navigators charted the coasts from 1606 onward.

Colonisation and the frontier

The First Fleet established a penal colony at Sydney Cove in 1788. Smallpox and other diseases devastated Indigenous populations, which fell dramatically over the following century. Expansion of pastoral settlement produced sustained frontier conflict — massacres such as Myall Creek (1838) and Pinjarra (1834), and resistance led by figures including Pemulwuy, Windradyne and Jandamarra. In Tasmania, the Black War of the 1820s–30s nearly destroyed the Aboriginal population.

Protection, assimilation and the Stolen Generations

From the later 19th century, colonial and state "protection" regimes confined many Aboriginal people to missions and reserves and controlled their labour, movement and families. Under assimilation policies in the mid-20th century, large numbers of Indigenous children — the Stolen Generations — were forcibly removed from their families, a practice documented in the 1997 Bringing Them Home report.

Rights movements and recognition

The 1962 grant of federal voting rights and the 1967 referendum allowing the Commonwealth to legislate for Aboriginal people and count them in the census were landmarks. The Wave Hill walk-off (1966) and the Aboriginal Tent Embassy (1972) pressed land-rights claims. The High Court's Mabo decision (1992) rejected the doctrine of terra nullius and recognised native title, codified in the Native Title Act 1993. Prime Minister Kevin Rudd delivered a national apology to the Stolen Generations in 2008. The Uluru Statement from the Heart (2017) called for a constitutionally enshrined Indigenous Voice to Parliament; the 2023 referendum on that proposal was unsuccessful.
