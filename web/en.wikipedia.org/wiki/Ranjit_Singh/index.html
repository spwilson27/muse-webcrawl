Ranjit Singh (13 November 1780 – 27 June 1839) was the founder of the Sikh Empire, which ruled the northwest of the Indian subcontinent in the early 19th century. Born in Gujranwala to Maha Singh, chief of the Sukerchakia misl (confederacy), he survived smallpox in infancy which left him blind in his left eye. He first fought in battle at age 10 alongside his father, and at 12, on his father's death, took command of the misl.

Rise to power

After several campaigns, Ranjit Singh was proclaimed Maharaja of Punjab on 12 April 1801, having captured Lahore in 1799 and made it his capital. He progressively unified the fractious Sikh misls under his rule and extended his empire to include Amritsar, Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819) and Peshawar (1834), while the Treaty of Amritsar of 1809 with the British East India Company fixed the Sutlej river as his south-eastern boundary and established relations with the British that largely kept the peace for the rest of his reign.

Administration and army

Ranjit Singh modernised the Khalsa army, employing European officers — among them the French generals Jean-François Allard and Jean-Baptiste Ventura, and the Italian Paolo Avitabile — to train infantry and artillery on modern lines, and founding foundries to cast cannon and manufacture muskets. Despite leading a state founded in the name of the Khalsa, he governed a religiously plural empire: Muslims and Hindus held high office, including his foreign minister Fakir Azizuddin, factories and taxes were administered impartially, and he is remembered in Sikh tradition for acts of devotion to Sikh shrines — including covering the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar with gold, giving it its popular name, the Golden Temple — alongside patronage of Hindu temples and mosques.

He forbade the death penalty for many offences, abolished taxes he considered oppressive, developed infrastructure including canals and roads, and maintained a court at Lahore that became a centre of art, learning and diplomacy.

Death and aftermath

Ranjit Singh died in Lahore on 27 June 1839. His empire, held together largely by his personal authority and his relationship with the British, rapidly fell into succession struggles and court intrigue. Within a decade of his death, after two Anglo-Sikh wars, Punjab was annexed by the British East India Company in 1849. The fate of his most famous possession, the Koh-i-Noor diamond, which he had obtained in 1813, passed to Queen Victoria.
