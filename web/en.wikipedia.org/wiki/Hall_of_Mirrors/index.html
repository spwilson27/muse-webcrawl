The Hall of Mirrors (French: Grande Galerie, Galerie des Glaces, Galerie de Louis XIV) is a grand Baroque gallery and one of the most emblematic rooms in the Palace of Versailles in Versailles, France. The grandeur of its creation and its architectural design established the Hall of Mirrors as a widely renowned work of art and a symbol of royal French power, commissioned by King Louis XIV.

The gallery measures 73 metres (240 ft) long, 10.5 metres (34 ft) wide and 12.3 metres (40 ft) high. Seventeen huge mirrors face seventeen arched windows overlooking the gardens of the palace. At the time of its construction, mirrors were among the most expensive items to possess, and the Venetian Republic held a monopoly on their manufacture; to circumvent this, Louis XIV's finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert enticed Venetian mirror-makers to work in France.

History

Construction began in 1678 under the architect Jules Hardouin-Mansart, who enclosed a terrace designed by Louis Le Vau. The painter Charles Le Brun directed the decoration; the ceiling's thirty compositions celebrate the political successes of Louis XIV during the first eighteen years of his reign, including the signing of the Peace of Nijmegen.

For centuries the hall served as a passageway and a venue for court ceremonies. It was the setting for the proclamation of the German Empire on 18 January 1871, following France's defeat in the Franco-Prussian War, when Wilhelm I was proclaimed Kaiser in the presence of Bismarck and the German princes. On 28 June 1919 the Treaty of Versailles, formally ending the First World War, was signed in the hall.

Description

The hall combines Italian-style painted ceilings with French craftsmanship. The 357 mirrors that compose the gallery's seventeen arches were, in the 17th century, a miraculous luxury; mirrors of such quality could previously only be produced in Venice. The hall is flanked by the Salon of War (Salon de la Guerre) at its northern end and the Salon of Peace (Salon de la Paix) at its southern end.
