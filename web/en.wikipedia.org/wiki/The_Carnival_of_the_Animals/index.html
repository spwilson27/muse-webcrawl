The Carnival of the Animals

The Carnival of the Animals (French: Le Carnaval des animaux) is a humorous musical suite of fourteen movements by the French composer Camille Saint-Saëns. Written in February 1886 while the composer was holidaying in Austria, it was conceived as a private joke for friends, and Saint-Saëns forbade its publication during his lifetime, fearing it would damage his reputation as a serious composer. It was published in 1922, the year after his death, and has become one of his best-known works.

Movements

The suite is scored for piano duo and chamber ensemble and depicts a menagerie: the Royal March of the Lion; Hens and Roosters; Wild Asses; Tortoises (which dance Offenbach's can-can at a ponderous tempo); The Elephant (a double bass solo parodying Berlioz and Mendelssohn); Kangaroos; Aquarium; Characters with Long Ears; The Cuckoo in the Depths of the Woods; Aviary; Pianists (practising tedious exercises); Fossils (quoting the composer's own Danse macabre); and The Swan, the only movement Saint-Saëns allowed to be published in his lifetime, a celebrated cello solo. A reprise of the opening material closes the finale.

Reception and legacy

Though written as light entertainment, the suite is admired for its wit, economy and orchestral colour, and is frequently performed in concerts for children, sometimes with comic verses by Ogden Nash. "The Swan" became famous as a solo for the ballerina Anna Pavlova, who danced it as The Dying Swan.
