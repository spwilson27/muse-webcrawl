Immunoglobulin A

Dimeric IgA with its J chain and secretory component

Immunoglobulin A (Ig A, also referred to as sIgA in its secretory form) is an antibody that plays a role in the immune function of mucous membranes. The amount of IgA produced in association with mucosal membranes is greater than all other types of antibody combined. In absolute terms, between three and five grams of IgA are secreted into the intestinal lumen each day in an adult. This represents up to 15% of total immunoglobulins produced throughout the body.

Forms

IgA exists in two isotypic forms in humans, IgA1 and IgA2, and can occur as a monomer or as a dimer joined by a J chain. In serum, IgA is predominantly monomeric; at mucosal surfaces it is secreted as a dimer complexed with the secretory component, which protects it from proteolytic degradation in the gut and respiratory tract.

Function

Secretory IgA performs immune exclusion: it binds pathogens and antigens in the mucus layer, preventing their attachment to and penetration of epithelial surfaces. Unlike IgG, IgA does not strongly activate complement or provoke inflammation, making it well suited to defending mucosal surfaces without damaging the tissues it protects. IgA is also present in colostrum and breast milk, contributing to the passive immunity of newborns.

Clinical significance

Selective IgA deficiency is the most common primary immunodeficiency, and affected individuals may suffer recurrent sinopulmonary and gastrointestinal infections. IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease), in which IgA deposits accumulate in the glomeruli of the kidney, is the most common cause of glomerulonephritis worldwide. Serum IgA antibodies against tissue transglutaminase are used in the diagnosis of coeliac disease.
