Michaelmas (/ˈmɪkəlməs/; also known as the Feast of Saints Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael, the Feast of the Archangels, or the Feast of Saint Michael and All Angels) is a Christian festival observed in some Western liturgical calendars on 29 September. In the Eastern Orthodox Church, the feast of the Archangels, principally Michael, is kept on 8 November.

History

The feast of St Michael originated in the fifth century with the dedication of a basilica to the archangel on the Via Salaria near Rome. Michaelmas became one of the most important feasts of the medieval calendar, falling close to the autumn equinox and marking the end of the harvest season. In England, Wales and Ireland it was one of the four traditional quarter days, when rents were due, servants were hired, and magistrates were elected.

Customs

Traditional customs associated with Michaelmas include eating a goose — said to bring financial prosperity for the coming year — and the baking of special breads such as the Scottish Struan Micheil. The Michaelmas daisy (aster) is among the last flowers of autumn and is traditionally linked with the feast. A piece of English folklore held that blackberries should not be eaten after Michaelmas (Old Michaelmas Day, 11 October in some traditions), because the devil was said to have spoiled them.

Modern observance

Michaelmas gives its name to the autumn term in many British and Irish universities and schools (the Michaelmas term), and to the legal term of the courts of England and Wales beginning in October. The feast remains a significant day in Anglican, Roman Catholic and Lutheran calendars.
