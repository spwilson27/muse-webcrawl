Horopter

Horopter
FieldVision science, binocular vision
DefinitionLocus of points in space seen as single (fused) at a given fixation
Theoretical formVieth–Müller circle (through both eyes' nodal points and the fixation point)
Related conceptsPanum's fusional area, binocular disparity, corresponding retinal points

In the study of binocular vision, the horopter is the locus of points in three-dimensional space that, for a given position of fixation, project onto corresponding points in the two retinas and are therefore seen as single rather than double. Points lying on the horopter have zero binocular disparity relative to the fixation point; points significantly in front of or behind it are seen double (physiological diplopia) or, within a small tolerance zone, still fused.

Theoretical and empirical horopters

If the eyes were perfect optical systems with corresponding points arranged symmetrically, the horopter for distance fixation would be the Vieth–Müller circle: the circle passing through the fixation point and the nodal points of the two eyes. The empirical horopter, measured by having observers align targets until they appear single (or by nonius methods), deviates systematically from that circle — it is flatter, and at far distances can even become concave toward the observer. This discrepancy is called the Hering–Hillebrand deviation, after Ewald Hering and Franz Hillebrand, and is attributed to asymmetries in the distribution of corresponding retinal points (aniseikonic magnification differences between the nasal and temporal retina).

Panum's area

Around the horopter lies a small region, Panum's fusional area, within which slightly disparate images are still fused into a single percept; the residual disparity within this zone is used by the visual system to compute stereoscopic depth. The horopter therefore marks the zero-disparity reference surface for stereopsis.

Clinical and practical importance

The horopter's shape matters in optometry (it changes with fixation distance and convergence), in the design of stereoscopic displays and VR headsets, and in understanding disorders of binocular vision such as strabismus. Historically the concept goes back to Franciscus Aguilonius, who introduced the term in 1613.
