Corrosion

Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of materials, usually metals, by chemical or electrochemical reaction with their environment. The most familiar example is the rusting of iron and steel. Corrosion imposes enormous costs — studies have estimated several percent of GDP in industrialized countries — and can cause catastrophic failures of bridges, pipelines, and aircraft.

Electrochemical mechanism

Aqueous corrosion of a metal is an electrochemical process: at anodic sites the metal oxidizes and dissolves as ions, releasing electrons that are consumed at cathodic sites, commonly by the reduction of oxygen. The electrolyte (water containing dissolved salts) completes the circuit. In the rusting of iron, hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust) forms a porous, non-protective layer that flakes away and exposes fresh metal. Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar metals are in electrical contact in an electrolyte, accelerating attack on the less noble metal; the galvanic series ranks metals by their tendency to corrode in seawater.

Forms of corrosion

Engineers distinguish uniform attack from localized forms that are often more dangerous: pitting corrosion, crevice corrosion, intergranular corrosion, stress corrosion cracking, erosion corrosion, dezincification of brass, and microbiologically influenced corrosion. High-temperature corrosion (oxidation, sulfidation) occurs in furnaces and engines without an aqueous electrolyte.

Passivation and resistance

Some metals resist corrosion through passivation: aluminium, chromium-bearing stainless steels, and titanium form thin, adherent oxide films that seal the surface. Weathering steel develops a stable patina, and copper roofs acquire green verdigris.

Prevention

Protective methods include coatings (paint, galvanizing with zinc, electroplating), corrosion inhibitors, environmental control (dehumidification, water treatment), cathodic protection using sacrificial anodes or impressed currents on ships, pipelines, and storage tanks, and material selection using inherently resistant alloys. Design measures — avoiding crevices, allowing drainage, and separating dissimilar metals — are also important.
