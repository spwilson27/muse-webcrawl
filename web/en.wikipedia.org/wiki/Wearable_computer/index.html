Wearable computer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A head-mounted wearable computer prototype.

A wearable computer (or wearable device) is a small technological device that is worn on the body, incorporating a computer capable of sensing, processing, and displaying information. Unlike a handheld device, a wearable is designed to be used while moving and often operates hands-free, with sensors continuously collecting data about the wearer or the environment. Common examples include smartwatches, fitness trackers, smart glasses, and medical monitors.

History

Early examples of wearable computing predate electronics: the 16th-century "counting ring" of abacus beads was one of the first wearable calculators. The modern field began in the 1960s, when mathematicians Edward Thorp and Claude Shannon built a cigarette-pack-sized wearable computer to predict roulette outcomes. Steve Mann at MIT and the University of Toronto pioneered head-mounted and body-worn systems in the 1980s and 1990s, coining the concept of a wearable computer as an extension of the user's mind and body. The commercial wearable market arrived with products such as the Fitbit tracker (2009), Google Glass (2013), the Apple Watch (2015), and a wave of smart rings and smart clothing in the 2020s.

Applications

Wearables are used in health and fitness (step counting, heart-rate monitoring, sleep analysis, ECG, blood oxygen), in industry (heads-up displays for warehouse picking and maintenance), in military and emergency services (heads-up navigation, vital-sign telemetry), and in accessibility (hearing aids and haptic feedback systems). Implantables and "hearables" — smart earbuds with sensors — are adjacent categories.

Challenges

Design challenges include battery life, miniaturisation, heat dissipation, privacy of continuous sensing, and social acceptability of always-on cameras and microphones. Health wearables also raise questions about the accuracy of consumer sensors and about how personal biometric data is stored and shared.
