Jesuit reductions were a type of settlement for Indigenous peoples in colonial South America established by the Jesuits in the 17th and 18th centuries. The best known were the missions among the Guaraní in the region of modern Paraguay, northeastern Argentina, and southern Brazil.

History

Founded from 1609 onward, the reductions gathered Guaraní families into ordered towns centred on a church and plaza, with communal agriculture, workshops, and celebrated musical training. At their peak in the 1730s the thirty Guaraní missions held over 140,000 people. The missions protected their residents from slave-raiders and the encomienda, but imposed strict Jesuit discipline.

The Treaty of Madrid (1750) triggered the Guaraní War when seven missions east of the Uruguay River were ordered evacuated. The expulsion of the Jesuits from Spanish territory in 1767 ended the system; the missions decayed, though several ruins — including La Santísima Trinidad de Paraná and Jesús de Tavarangue — are now UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The story inspired the 1986 film The Mission.
