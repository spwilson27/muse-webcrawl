Lagrange's four-square theorem

Lagrange's four-square theorem, also known as Bachet's conjecture, states that every natural number can be represented as the sum of four integer squares: p = a² + b² + c² + d², where a, b, c, d are integers (some of which may be zero).

History

The statement first appeared in the writings of Diophantus and was stated as a conjecture by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac in 1621, in his translation of Diophantus's Arithmetica. Fermat claimed to have a proof, but the first complete proof was published by Joseph Louis Lagrange in 1770. Euler had earlier proved the four-square identity, a key step showing that the theorem need only be checked for prime numbers.

Proof outline

By Euler's four-square identity, the set of sums of four squares is closed under multiplication, so it suffices to prove the theorem for odd primes. For a prime p, one shows that some multiple mp is a sum of four squares smaller than p², and then applies infinite descent to reduce to p itself.

Generalizations

The Jacobi four-square theorem counts the number of representations. Related results include Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares, Legendre's three-square theorem, and Waring's problem. The theorem can also be viewed as a consequence of the fact that the Hurwitz quaternions form a Euclidean domain.
