Taylor's theorem

[Image: Successive Taylor polynomials converging on the sine curve]

In calculus, Taylor's theorem gives an approximation of a k-times differentiable function around a given point by a polynomial of degree k, the Taylor polynomial, together with a quantitative estimate of the error of the approximation. For a real-valued function f differentiable up to order k+1 near a:

f(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x−a) + f″(a)(x−a)²/2! + … + f(k)(a)(x−a)k/k! + Rk(x)

where Rk is the remainder term. If the remainder tends to zero as k grows without bound, the function equals its infinite Taylor series and is called analytic.

Remainder forms

Several forms of the remainder are used. The Lagrange form states that Rk(x) = f(k+1)(ξ)(x−a)k+1/(k+1)! for some ξ between a and x; the Cauchy form and the integral form, Rk(x) = ∫ax f(k+1)(t)(x−t)k/k! dt, are alternatives. Special cases include the mean value theorem (k = 0) and linear approximation (k = 1).

History

Brook Taylor published the result in 1715 in his Methodus Incrementorum, building on work by Gregory, Newton and others; Joseph-Louis Lagrange supplied the remainder estimate in 1797. The theorem is foundational in analysis and numerical methods, underpinning power-series computations of functions such as ex, sin x and ln(1+x), and multivariate versions generalise it to functions of several variables.
