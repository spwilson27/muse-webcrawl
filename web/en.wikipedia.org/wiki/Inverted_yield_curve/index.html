Inverted yield curve

Inverted yield curve
Common measures10-year minus 2-year Treasury spread; 10-year minus 3-month spread
InterpretationExpectations of falling rates / recession signal

An inverted yield curve is a yield curve in which short-term debt instruments have a greater yield than long-term instruments of the same credit quality, the reverse of the usual relationship. It is often taken as a predictor of economic recession: in the United States, the curve has inverted before each recession since the 1970s, with the spread between 10-year and 2-year Treasury yields (and the Federal Reserve's preferred 10-year/3-month measure) among the most closely watched indicators.

Causes

Under the expectations hypothesis, long-term rates reflect the average of expected future short-term rates plus a term premium. The curve inverts when investors expect the central bank to cut rates in future — typically because they anticipate a downturn — or when aggressive current tightening pushes short rates above long rates while long rates stay anchored by low inflation expectations and strong demand for safe long-dated assets. Heavy buying of long-term bonds, including by foreign central banks and pension funds, can also flatten or invert the curve.

Recession prediction

Research associated with economist Campbell Harvey, whose 1986 dissertation modelled the curve's relation to growth, and Federal Reserve studies showing the probit model based on the 10-year/3-month spread, established the inversion as a leading indicator. Inversions preceded the US recessions of 1990–91, 2001, 2007–09 and 2020, usually by 6 to 24 months, and the prolonged inversion beginning in 2022 was widely debated as a possible exception before the curve re-steepened. Critics note the indicator can give false or early signals and that quantitative easing may have distorted the term premium.

Effects

An inverted curve compresses banks' net interest margins, since banks borrow short and lend long, potentially tightening credit supply. It also signals that monetary policy may be restrictive relative to the economy's neutral rate.
