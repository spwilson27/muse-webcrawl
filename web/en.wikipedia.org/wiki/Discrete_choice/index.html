Mode choice — car, bus, train or bicycle — is a classic discrete choice problem.

Discrete choice models describe, explain and predict choices between two or more discrete alternatives, such as which transport mode to take, which product to buy, or which candidate to vote for — in contrast to standard consumer theory, where quantities demanded are continuous. The methods were developed largely by Daniel McFadden, whose work on discrete choice analysis contributed to his 2000 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics.

Random utility framework

The standard approach assumes a decision-maker obtains utility U = V + ε from each alternative, where V is a systematic component depending on the attributes of the alternative and the chooser (price, travel time, income, taste variables) and ε is a random component capturing unobserved factors. The decision-maker chooses the alternative with the highest utility; the distribution assumed for ε determines the model — extreme-value errors give the logit model, normal errors give probit.

Models

The binary logit and probit handle two alternatives. Multinomial logit extends to many alternatives but imposes the controversial independence of irrelevant alternatives property — the source of the famous "red bus / blue bus" problem — which nested logit, cross-nested logit and mixed logit (random parameters) relax by allowing correlated errors and taste variation across individuals. Ordered models handle ranked outcomes such as survey ratings.

Applications

Discrete choice models underpin travel demand forecasting for new rail lines and road pricing, marketing research and conjoint analysis for product design, health economics (choice of provider or treatment), environmental valuation, and political science. Data come from revealed preferences (observed choices) or stated preferences (hypothetical choice experiments).
