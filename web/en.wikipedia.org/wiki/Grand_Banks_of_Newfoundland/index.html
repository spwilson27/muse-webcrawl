Grand Banks of Newfoundland

The Grand Banks comprise several named banks on the continental shelf off Newfoundland.

The Grand Banks of Newfoundland are a group of submerged plateaus on the continental shelf off the east coast of Canada, southeast of the island of Newfoundland. They are among the world's most productive marine ecosystems and historically one of its richest fishing grounds.

Component banks

The Grand Banks consist of a number of individual banks, including Grand Bank itself — the largest — as well as Green Bank, St. Pierre Bank, Burgeo Bank, and others. To the east lie the Flemish Cap and Flemish Pass, separated from the Grand Banks by deeper water. The banks typically lie at depths of roughly 25 to 100 metres.

Formation

The banks are remnants shaped by the advance and retreat of Pleistocene glaciers, which deposited till and outwash over the continental shelf and carved channels between the banks. As sea levels rose after the last glacial period, the plateaus were submerged, forming broad, shallow, nutrient-rich fishing grounds.

Ecology

The confluence of the cold, nutrient-bearing Labrador Current with the warm Gulf Stream generates upwelling and plankton blooms that support a food web of capelin, sand lance, Atlantic cod, haddock, redfish, flatfish, shrimp and snow crab, as well as seabirds and marine mammals. The area falls partly within Canada's 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone and partly (the "nose" and "tail") under the management of the Northwest Atlantic Fisheries Organization (NAFO).

Human use

Exploited by European fishermen since about 1500, the banks sustained one of the great cod fisheries of the world until the northern cod stock collapsed and Canada imposed a moratorium in 1992. Since 1997 the banks have also produced offshore petroleum from fields operated with gravity-based structures and floating production vessels designed to withstand icebergs — the region lies along "Iceberg Alley", and in April 1912 RMS Titanic sank after striking an iceberg south of the banks.
