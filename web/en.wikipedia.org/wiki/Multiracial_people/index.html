Multiracial people

Multiracial people, also known as mixed-race people, are people whose ancestry comprises two or more racial groups. The terms used vary by country and culture, and many societies have long-standing mixed-ancestry populations with their own names and histories, such as Métis in Canada, Coloureds in South Africa, Eurasians in Southeast Asia, Anglo-Indians in India, mestizos in Latin America, and Hāfu in Japan.

Terminology and classification

How mixed-ancestry people are classified has varied widely. In the United States, the historical "one-drop rule" assigned people with any African ancestry to the Black category, and census forms allowed only one race until the 2000 census first permitted respondents to mark "two or more races". The 2020 United States census recorded a multiracial population of about 33.8 million, a large increase partly reflecting changes in question design and coding as well as demographic change.

History

Mixed-ancestry populations arose wherever distinct groups met — through migration, trade, colonization, enslavement, and intermarriage. Legal regimes in many places restricted interracial marriage, most notoriously under apartheid in South Africa and anti-miscegenation laws in the United States, which were not fully struck down until Loving v. Virginia (1967). Since the late 20th century, legal barriers have fallen in most countries and the number of people identifying as multiracial has grown, along with public discussion of multiracial identity.

Identity and research

Research on multiracial identity examines how individuals negotiate multiple heritages, how identity can be situational, and how experiences differ by appearance, family, and community. Health and social research increasingly disaggregates multiracial respondents rather than forcing a single category. Critics of the concept of "race" itself note that racial categories are social constructs, and that multiracial identification both reflects and challenges those categories.
