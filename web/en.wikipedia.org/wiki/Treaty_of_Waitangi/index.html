The Treaty of Waitangi (Māori: Te Tiriti o Waitangi) is a treaty first signed on 6 February 1840 by representatives of the British Crown and Māori chiefs (rangatira) from the North Island of New Zealand. It is widely considered the founding document of New Zealand, establishing British sovereignty over the islands while recognising Māori ownership of their lands, forests, and fisheries—although the English and Māori texts differ significantly in meaning.

Background

British interest in New Zealand grew with trade, whaling, and missionary activity in the early nineteenth century. Concerned about lawlessness among settlers and French ambitions, and pressed by the New Zealand Company's colonising plans, the British government appointed Captain William Hobson as lieutenant-governor. Hobson drafted the treaty, with translation into Māori by the missionary Henry Williams and his son Edward, in a matter of days in early February 1840.

The articles and the translation problem

The English text has three articles: (1) Māori chiefs cede "sovereignty" to the Crown absolutely; (2) the Crown guarantees Māori "full exclusive and undisturbed possession" of their lands, estates, forests, and fisheries, while Māori yield a right of pre-emption (first purchase) to the Crown; (3) Māori receive the rights of British subjects. In the Māori text, however, the word rendered for "sovereignty" was kāwanatanga (governorship), while rangatiratanga—chieftainship or self-determination—was retained by the chiefs over their lands and taonga (treasures). Most chiefs signed the Māori text, and the two versions have underpinned debate ever since.

Signings and aftermath

After the first signing at Waitangi, copies were carried around the country; in total about 540 Māori, including some women, signed, while some important chiefs refused. Hobson proclaimed British sovereignty over all New Zealand in May 1840. In the following decades land wars and confiscations stripped many iwi of their land, and the treaty was largely sidelined—famously called a "simple nullity" by a judge in 1877.

Modern status

From the 1970s, protest and scholarship revived the treaty as a living document. The Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975 created the Waitangi Tribunal to investigate breaches; since 1985 it can consider historical claims back to 1840, leading to major settlements with iwi such as Waikato-Tainui and Ngāi Tahu. The "principles of the Treaty"—commonly summarised as partnership, participation, and protection—inform legislation and public policy. Waitangi Day, 6 February, is New Zealand's national day.
