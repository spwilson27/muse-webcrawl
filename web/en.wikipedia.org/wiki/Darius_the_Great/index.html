Darius the Great

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Relief of Darius I enthroned at Persepolis, receiving an official]

Darius I (c. 550–486 BC), called Darius the Great, was the third king of the Achaemenid Empire, reigning from 522 to 486 BC. Coming to the throne amid crisis — by his own account, he overthrew the usurper Gaumata and suppressed revolts across the empire — Darius reorganised Persia's government so thoroughly that he is often considered its second founder: he fixed the satrapies, taxation, law and royal coinage (the daric), built at Susa and Persepolis, and extended the empire to its greatest extent in Europe and India. His invasion of Greece ended at Marathon in 490 BC.

Accession and reforms

Darius' Behistun inscription, carved in three languages on a cliff face, tells how the Magian Gaumata seized power impersonating Cambyses' dead brother Bardiya, and how Darius with six helpers killed him and crushed nine rebel kings in a year. Historians debate the story — possibly Darius was himself the usurper — but his organising achievement is clear: twenty satrapies with assessed tribute, royal judges, Aramaic as administrative language, the Royal Road with relay posts, a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea, and standard gold and silver coinage.

Wars and death

Darius subdued Thrace and Macedon, annexed the Indus valley — the empire's richest province — and campaigned unsuccessfully against the Scythians. When Athens and Eretria backed the Ionian Revolt of the Greek cities of Asia (499–494 BC), Darius vowed revenge; his expedition of 490 BC burned Eretria but was defeated by the Athenians at Marathon. He died in 486 BC while preparing a larger response, leaving the task and the throne to Xerxes I.
