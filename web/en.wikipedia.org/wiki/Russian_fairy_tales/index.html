Russian fairy tales (Russian: сказки, skazki) are the traditional folk narratives of Russia, transmitted orally for centuries and collected in large numbers in the nineteenth century, most famously by Alexander Afanasyev, whose Russian Fairy Tales (1855–1863), some 600 tales, stands comparison with the collection of the Brothers Grimm. They form a central part of Russian culture and have profoundly influenced literature, ballet, opera and film.

Characters and motifs

Recurring figures include the witch Baba Yaga, who lives in a hut on chicken legs and may help or hinder the hero; the deathless sorcerer Koschei, whose soul is hidden in a needle, in an egg, in a duck, in a hare, in a chest on a distant island; the questing Ivan Tsarevich or the underestimated youngest son Ivan the Fool; the radiant Firebird; and heroines such as Vasilisa the Beautiful. Magical helpers — the Grey Wolf, the horse Sivka-Burka, grateful animals — guide heroes through tasks in the thrice-ninth kingdom, a far-off land beyond forests and seas.

Types of tale

Russian tradition distinguishes wonder tales (volshebnye skazki), full of magic and quests; animal tales, often trickster fables; and everyday or satirical tales about soldiers, priests, masters and clever peasants. Wonder tales typically open with a formula such as "In a certain kingdom, in a certain land" and close with the narrator's claim to have been at the wedding feast: "I was there, I drank mead and beer; it ran down my moustache but never got into my mouth."

Scholarship

The tales became foundational for folklore studies. Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) analysed one hundred of Afanasyev's wonder tales and reduced them to a sequence of 31 functions — absence, interdiction, violation, departure, trial, victory, return, wedding — performed by a small set of character roles. Propp's structural analysis influenced narratology worldwide.

Adaptations

The tales inspired classic works of Russian art: Stravinsky's ballet The Firebird (1910), Rimsky-Korsakov's operas, the paintings and book illustrations of Ivan Bilibin, and beloved Soviet fairy-tale films such as Jack Frost (Morozko, 1964). Pushkin's verse tales, themselves modelled on folk material told by his nurse Arina Rodionovna, occupy a place between folklore and literature.
