Power of a point

In elementary plane geometry, the power of a point with respect to a circle is a real number that reflects the relative distance of the point from the circle. If a point P lies at distance d from the centre of a circle of radius r, its power is d² − r². The power is positive outside the circle, zero on the circle, and negative inside it.

Intersecting chords theorem

For any line through P intersecting the circle at points A and B, the product PA · PB of the (signed) segment lengths equals the power of P, and is thus the same for every choice of line. This yields the classical theorems: the intersecting chords theorem (two chords crossing inside the circle), the secant theorem (two secants from an external point), and the tangent–secant theorem, in which the tangent segment satisfies PT² = power of P.

Radical axis

The set of points having equal powers with respect to two circles is a straight line called the radical axis of the two circles. The concept of power was used by Euclid implicitly and developed in the 19th century by geometers such as Jakob Steiner. It also generalises to spheres in three dimensions and underlies inversion geometry and the theory of coaxal circles.
