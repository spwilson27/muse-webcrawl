Fallschirmjäger

Fallschirmjäger (German: [ˈfalʃɪʁmˌjɛːɡɐ]) is the German word for paratroopers. They played a key role in several airborne operations during the Second World War, and in the modern German armed forces (Bundeswehr) they are part of the army's airborne infantry. The Nazi-era Fallschirmjäger belonged to the Luftwaffe and were commanded for most of the war by General Kurt Student, the pioneer of German airborne forces.

Second World War

Formed from 1936, the Fallschirmjäger spearheaded the invasions of Denmark and Norway (1940), seized the Belgian fortress of Eben-Emael and bridges in the Netherlands with glider assaults, and jumped in the Corinth Canal operation of 1941. Their most famous action, the invasion of Crete (Operation Mercury, May 1941), succeeded but at such heavy cost that Hitler forbade further large-scale airborne operations; they thereafter fought mainly as elite ground infantry — at Monte Cassino, in Normandy, at Arnhem, and in the Battle of the Bulge. They wore distinctive jump helmets, smocks and the RZ-series parachutes, and by the war's end some 12 Fallschirmjäger divisions had been raised, of uneven quality.

Post-war

In the Bundeswehr, Fallschirmjäger form the core of the Rapid Forces Division (Division Schnelle Kräfte), based at Stadtallendorf and Seedorf; East Germany's NVA also maintained a small paratroop battalion (Fallschirmjägerbataillon 40). The name is retained by airborne units of Germany and Austria today.
