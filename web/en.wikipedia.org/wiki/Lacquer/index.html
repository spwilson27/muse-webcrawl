Lacquer

Lacquer is a type of hard, usually glossy finish or coating applied to materials such as wood and metal. The term originates in the resinous sap of the lacquer tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum), used in East Asia for millennia to produce highly durable and decorative finishes. Modern lacquers are fast-drying, solvent-based coatings that dry by evaporation of the solvent.

East Asian lacquer

True lacquer, based on urushiol from the lacquer tree, has been used in China since the Neolithic period and refined in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam into major art forms. Cured lacquer is waterproof, resistant to heat, acids, and decay, and can be carved (as in Chinese carved cinnabar lacquer), inlaid with mother-of-pearl, or sprinkled with gold in the Japanese maki-e technique. Layers must cure slowly in a warm, humid cabinet, and artisans may apply dozens of coats.

Modern lacquers

Industrial lacquers developed in the 1920s, notably nitrocellulose lacquer, revolutionized automobile and furniture finishing because they dried rapidly to a hard, polishable film. Today acrylic and catalyzed lacquers are common; "lacquer" finishes on furniture are often polyurethane-based. In hair care, lacquer also refers to hair spray.

Health and safety

Urushiol is a potent skin sensitizer related to poison ivy toxin, so raw lacquer must be handled with care until fully cured. Solvent-based lacquers are flammable and require ventilation.
