Machine ethics

Machine ethics (or machine morality, computational ethics or computational morality) is a part of the ethics of artificial intelligence concerned with adding or ensuring moral behaviours and ethics to man-made machines that use artificial intelligence, otherwise known as artificial intelligent agents. Machine ethics differs from other ethical fields related to engineering and technology: rather than concentrating on the human use of manually operated machines, it concentrates on the behaviour of machines operated autonomously.

It is also distinct from roboethics, which concerns the moral behaviour of humans as they design, construct, use and treat robots and other artificially intelligent beings, and from computer ethics, which concerns the use of computers by humans.

History

Popular discussion began with Isaac Asimov's "Three Laws of Robotics", introduced in his 1942 story "Runaround", though Asimov's stories mainly explored how such rules could fail. Academic machine ethics developed from the early 2000s, with symposia organised by the AAAI and works such as Wendell Wallach and Colin Allen's Moral Machines (2009), which surveyed approaches ranging from top-down rule systems to bottom-up learning of moral behaviour, and hybrids of the two.

Topics

  • Moral agency and patiency: whether machines can be moral agents, moral patients, or neither.
  • Explicit ethical agents: machines that reason about ethics, e.g. using deontological rules or consequentialist calculation; contrasted with implicit ethical agents whose design constrains them to avoid unethical outcomes.
  • Value alignment: ensuring that autonomous systems pursue goals compatible with human values, a central concern in AI safety.
  • Autonomous weapons and vehicles: trolley-problem-style dilemmas applied to self-driving cars and "killer robots", and the question of responsibility when machines cause harm.
  • Bias and fairness: ethical problems arising from training data in machine-learned systems.
