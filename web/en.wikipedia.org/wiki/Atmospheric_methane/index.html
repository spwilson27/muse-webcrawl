Atmospheric methane is the concentration of methane (CH₄) in Earth's atmosphere. Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas — over a century, roughly 28–30 times more potent per unit mass than carbon dioxide, and far more potent over 20 years. Its concentration has risen from about 722 parts per billion in pre-industrial times to around 1,900 ppb, the highest in at least 800,000 years based on ice-core records.

Sources and sinks

Natural sources include wetlands (the largest), termites, oceans and geological seeps. Human-caused sources — roughly 60% of the total — include livestock enteric fermentation, rice cultivation, landfills and waste, coal mining, and leaks from oil and gas systems. The main sink is oxidation by hydroxyl radicals in the troposphere, which converts methane ultimately to CO₂ and water vapour; soils also consume a small fraction.

Recent trends

After a plateau in the early 2000s, methane concentrations resumed rising in 2007, with record growth rates in 2020 and 2021. Scientists debate the relative contributions of fossil-fuel emissions, agriculture and a possible climate feedback from tropical wetlands. Methane is responsible for roughly a third of observed warming since the industrial era, and cutting emissions is a focus of the Global Methane Pledge launched at COP26.

Monitoring

Global concentrations are tracked by surface networks such as NOAA's and by satellites including TROPOMI, which can also localise large point sources ("super-emitters") such as leaking pipelines and coal mines.
