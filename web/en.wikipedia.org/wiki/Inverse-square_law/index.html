Inverse-square law

The inverse-square law states that a specified physical quantity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from its source. It arises whenever influence spreads uniformly in all directions from a point source into three-dimensional space, so that the intensity is diluted over the surface of a sphere whose area grows as 4πr².

Formulation

Intensity ∝ 1 / r²

Doubling the distance from a point source reduces the intensity to one quarter; tripling it reduces it to one ninth. The law is a consequence of geometry: the same total flux passes through every concentric sphere, so it is spread ever more thinly as the area grows.

Examples

Gravitation. Newton's law of universal gravitation gives a force proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, F = G·m₁m₂/r². Electrostatics. Coulomb's law has the same form for the force between point charges. Light, sound, and radiation. The illuminance from a point light source, the intensity of sound in a free field, and the dose rate from a point gamma source all fall off with the inverse square of distance — a fact used practically in photography (flash exposure), acoustics, and radiation protection.

History

Johannes Kepler inferred an inverse-square-like dilution for light in 1604, though for him light spread only within a plane. Ismaël Bullialdus argued in 1645 that gravity weakens as the inverse square of distance. Isaac Newton derived the law for gravitation in the Principia (1687), showing it explained Kepler's laws of planetary motion. Robert Hooke also claimed priority for the idea, prompting a famous dispute. Joseph Priestley later conjectured the same form for electric force, confirmed experimentally by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in 1785.

Limitations

The law applies exactly to point sources in a non-absorbing medium. For extended sources, near fields, or media that absorb or scatter, the falloff is modified; sound in enclosed spaces and antenna near-fields are common exceptions.
