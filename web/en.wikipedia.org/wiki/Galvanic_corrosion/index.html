Galvanic corrosion

Galvanic corrosion
Type: Electrochemical corrosion
Also called: Bimetallic corrosion, dissimilar metal corrosion
Key factor: Electrical contact between dissimilar metals in an electrolyte

Galvanic corrosion (also called bimetallic corrosion or dissimilar metal corrosion) is an electrochemical process in which one metal corrodes preferentially when it is in electrical contact with another, in the presence of an electrolyte. This same galvanic reaction is exploited in primary batteries to generate an electrical voltage.


Galvanic corrosion between dissimilar metals

Overview

Galvanic corrosion occurs when two different metals have physical or electrical contact with each other and are immersed in a common electrolyte, or when the same metal is exposed to electrolyte with different concentrations. In a galvanic couple, the more active metal (the anode) corrodes at an accelerated rate and the more noble metal (the cathode) corrodes at a slower rate. When immersed separately, each metal corrodes at its own rate. Which metal will be the anode and which the cathode can be determined from the galvanic series.

Galvanic series

The relative nobility of a material can be predicted by measuring its corrosion potential with a reference electrode. The resulting series, the galvanic series, ranks metals from most noble (least reactive, e.g. platinum and gold) to most active (e.g. magnesium and zinc). A commonly encountered example is the use of steel fasteners in aluminium structures.

Examples

A famous example is the Statue of Liberty, where the copper skin and the iron armature were separated by shellac-impregnated canvas; as the separator failed, the iron corroded galvanically and the structure had to be renovated in the 1980s. Another example is the degradation of aluminium aircraft structures joined by steel rivets, and the use of sacrificial anodes of zinc or magnesium to protect steel ship hulls and pipelines.

Prevention

Galvanic corrosion can be prevented by electrically insulating the dissimilar metals, applying coatings, selecting metals with similar corrosion potentials, or using cathodic protection with sacrificial anodes or impressed current systems.
