Advaita Vedanta (Sanskrit: अद्वैत वेदान्त, "non-dual end of the Vedas") is a school of Hindu philosophy that teaches the essential identity of the individual self (Atman) with the ultimate reality (Brahman). Its best-known proponent is the 8th-century philosopher Adi Shankara, who consolidated earlier teachings — including those of his teacher's teacher Gaudapada — into a systematic doctrine.

Core doctrine

Advaita holds that Brahman — pure consciousness, existence, and bliss (sat-chit-ananda) — is the sole reality, and that the world of plurality is an apparent manifestation dependent on Brahman. The individual self is not ultimately distinct from Brahman; the sense of separateness arises from avidya (ignorance) and the superimposition (adhyasa) of the not-self on the self. The great sayings (mahavakya) of the Upanishads, such as tat tvam asi ("that thou art") and aham brahmasmi ("I am Brahman"), express this identity.

The empirical world is often described through the concept of maya and levels of reality: the absolute (paramarthika), the empirical (vyavaharika), and the illusory (pratibhasika) — illustrated by analogies such as mistaking a rope for a snake, or silver in a shell. Liberation (moksha) is not produced but recognized: knowledge (jnana) removes ignorance and reveals the ever-free self.

Texts and method

Advaita exegesis rests on the prasthanatrayi, the "three sources": the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, and the Brahma Sutras. Shankara's commentaries on these, together with independent works such as the Upadesasahasri and the attributed Vivekachudamani, define the tradition. The method of inquiry combines scriptural hearing (shravana), reflection (manana), and deep contemplation (nididhyasana), as well as the negative dialectic of neti neti ("not this, not this").

History and later developments

After Shankara, the school developed through sub-commentarial traditions, including the Bhamati and Vivarana schools, named after works by Vachaspati Mishra and Prakashatman. Later teachers such as Vidyaranya (associated with the Sringeri monastic seat) and Madhusudana Sarasvati refined its logic and dialectics, often in debate with rival schools such as Madhva's Dvaita (dualism) and Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism). Shankara is traditionally credited with founding four monastic centers (mathas) at Sringeri, Dwarka, Puri, and Joshimath.

In the modern period, Swami Vivekananda popularized a form of Advaita ("Neo-Vedanta") worldwide, notably at the 1893 Parliament of the World's Religions, and figures such as Ramana Maharshi taught self-inquiry as a direct path. Advaita has influenced modern discussions in comparative philosophy, consciousness studies, and perennial philosophy.
