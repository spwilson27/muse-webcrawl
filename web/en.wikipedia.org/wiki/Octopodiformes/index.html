Octopodiformes

Octopodiformes is a superorder of the subclass Coleoidea (the soft-bodied cephalopods), comprising the octopuses and the vampire squid. Its members are characterised by having eight arms — in contrast to the ten appendages of the Decapodiformes (squids and cuttlefish), where the fourth arm pair has been modified into retractable tentacles.

Classification

Within the Octopodiformes are two living orders:

  • Octopoda — the true octopuses, with about 300 described species, divided into the cirrate octopuses (finned, deep-sea forms) and the incirrate octopuses, which include most familiar shallow-water species.
  • Vampyromorphida — represented today by a single living species, the vampire squid (Vampyroteuthis infernalis), an inhabitant of the deep ocean's oxygen-minimum zones.

Evolution and anatomy

Molecular and fossil evidence suggests the octopodiforms diverged from the decapodiforms during the Jurassic period or earlier. Octopodiformes have largely lost the internal shell (gladius) inherited from their ancestors; in octopuses it is reduced to vestigial stylets or absent entirely, while the vampire squid retains a gladius. Their arms bear suckers (and, in cirrates, paired hair-like cirri), and the body is typically soft and highly deformable, allowing octopuses to squeeze through narrow openings.
