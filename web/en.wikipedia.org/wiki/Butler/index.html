Butler

A butler is a person working in a large household, traditionally the senior male servant in charge of the dining room, wine cellar, and pantry, and often the head of the entire domestic staff. The word derives from the Old French bouteillier, the cup-bearer responsible for wine.

History

The office evolved from medieval cup-bearers and cellarers. By the Victorian era in Britain, the butler stood at the top of the indoor servant hierarchy in great houses, answering the door, announcing guests, serving wine at table, and guarding the family silver, while the housekeeper managed female staff. Butlers were typically unmarried and prized for discretion and dignity.

Decline and revival

The great-house system collapsed after the First and Second World Wars, and the profession nearly vanished. From the late 20th century, demand revived among wealthy households worldwide, in luxury hotels, and on cruise ships. Modern butlers — trained at schools such as the International Butler Academy — combine traditional service with estate management, technology, travel coordination, and staff administration, and the profession is now open to women as well.

In fiction

Fictional butlers are a cultural staple, from P. G. Wodehouse's supremely competent Jeeves to Batman's Alfred Pennyworth and Stevens, the repressed narrator of Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. The "butler did it" trope of detective fiction plays on the butler's intimate access to the household.
