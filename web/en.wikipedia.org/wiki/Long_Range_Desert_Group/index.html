The Long Range Desert Group (LRDG) was a reconnaissance and raiding unit of the British Army during the Second World War. Founded in Egypt in June 1940 by Major Ralph Alger Bagnold, the unit specialised in motorised reconnaissance deep behind enemy lines, intelligence gathering, and navigation across the Libyan Desert during the North African campaign.

Operations

Operating in small, self-sufficient patrols of specially modified Chevrolet and Ford trucks, the LRDG travelled thousands of miles across terrain considered impassable, using sun compasses and theodolites for navigation. The unit ran the "Road Watch" — a continuous surveillance of the Via Balbia, the main Axis supply route along the Libyan coast — reporting enemy vehicle movements to British headquarters. It also ferried Special Air Service raiders to and from targets, attacked airfields and supply dumps (notably the raid on Barce in September 1942), and mapped vast areas of previously uncharted desert.

Personnel and legacy

Although British-led, the LRDG drew heavily on volunteers from New Zealand and Rhodesian units, whose farming backgrounds suited vehicle maintenance and desert hardship. Its members included the explorer and navigator W. B. Kennedy Shaw and cartographer John Olivey. After the North African campaign ended in 1943, the LRDG operated in Greece, Italy and the Balkans before disbanding in August 1945. The unit is regarded as a forerunner of modern special forces reconnaissance units.
