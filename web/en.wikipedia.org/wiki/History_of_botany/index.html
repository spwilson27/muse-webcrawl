History of botany

For the journal, see Botanical history. This article traces the development of the scientific study of plants.


A botanical plate in the style of the great printed herbals.

The history of botany examines the human effort to understand life on Earth by tracing the historical development of the discipline of botany — that part of natural science dealing with organisms traditionally treated as plants. It stretches from ancient herbal medicine to modern molecular phylogenetics.

Ancient botany

Early plant knowledge was practical: agriculture, food and medicine. Theophrastus (c. 371–287 BC), a student of Aristotle, wrote Historia Plantarum and De Causis Plantarum, describing some 500 plants and founding botany as a discipline. In Rome, Pliny the Elder's Natural History and Dioscorides' De Materia Medica — a pharmacopoeia used for 1,500 years — catalogued medicinal plants. Parallel traditions developed in China, where the Shennong Bencao Jing compiled herbal lore, and in India through Ayurvedic texts.

Medieval herbals and the Renaissance

Medieval herbals largely copied classical authorities. The Islamic Golden Age advanced plant science through works such as Ibn al-Baitar's compendium of simples. In sixteenth-century Europe the "German fathers of botany" — Otto Brunfels, Hieronymus Bock and Leonhart Fuchs — published illustrated herbals based on direct observation. Botanical gardens were founded at Pisa (1543) and Padua (1545), and exotic plants arrived from the Americas and Asia.

Classification and the Linnaean system

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century botanists sought natural systems of classification. John Ray grouped plants by overall similarity; Joseph Pitton de Tournefort emphasised the corolla. Carl Linnaeus revolutionised taxonomy with binomial nomenclature in Species Plantarum (1753), using a sexual system based on stamens and pistils. Later, the natural systems of Jussieu and de Candolle replaced Linnaeus's artificial scheme.

Plant physiology and evolution

Stephen Hales's Vegetable Staticks (1727) pioneered experimental plant physiology; Jan Ingenhousz and others unravelled photosynthesis in the late eighteenth century. In the nineteenth century, cell theory (Schleiden), plant geography (Humboldt) and Darwin's theory of evolution transformed botany. Mendel's genetics, rediscovered in 1900, merged with selection in the modern synthesis.

Modern botany

Twentieth-century botany embraced ecology, biochemistry and electron microscopy. The discovery of plant hormones, the elucidation of the Calvin cycle, and finally DNA sequencing produced molecular phylogenetics; the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group classifications (APG, from 1998) reorganised flowering-plant taxonomy on genetic evidence.
