Current liability

In accounting, a current liability is a debt or obligation that is due to be settled within one fiscal year or within the company's normal operating cycle, whichever is longer. Current liabilities are shown on the balance sheet and are paid out of current assets or by the creation of other current liabilities.

Examples

  • Accounts payable — amounts owed to suppliers for goods and services received.
  • Short-term loans and overdrafts, and the portion of long-term debt falling due within the year.
  • Accrued expenses — wages, interest, and taxes incurred but not yet paid.
  • Unearned (deferred) revenue — payments received from customers for goods or services not yet delivered.
  • Dividends payable and income taxes payable.

Analysis

Current liabilities are compared with current assets to assess a company's short-term liquidity. The current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities) and the more conservative quick ratio are standard measures: a business whose current liabilities exceed its current assets may struggle to meet obligations as they fall due, although acceptable levels vary by industry.

Classification

Obligations due beyond one year are classified as non-current (long-term) liabilities. The classification matters because analysts, lenders, and covenants depend on it; refinancing agreements reached before the balance-sheet date may allow a short-term obligation to be presented as non-current under some accounting standards.
