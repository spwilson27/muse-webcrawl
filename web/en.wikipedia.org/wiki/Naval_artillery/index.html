Naval artillery
Era14th century – present
TypesCannon, rifled breech-loaders, naval guns, railguns (experimental)

Naval artillery is artillery mounted on a warship, originally used only for naval warfare and later also for naval gunfire support against coastal targets and for anti-aircraft roles. It developed from the early gunpowder weapons of the fourteenth century into the massive turret-mounted guns of the dreadnought era, before yielding primacy to aircraft and guided missiles in the mid-twentieth century.

Age of sail

Early naval guns were smoothbore, muzzle-loading cannon firing solid iron shot, arranged in broadside batteries along a ship's decks. Tactics centred on the line of battle, and gunnery was short-ranged and relatively inaccurate. Shell-firing guns, pioneered by Henri-Joseph Paixhans in the 1820s, made wooden warships highly vulnerable and spurred the adoption of armour and iron hulls.

Industrial era

Rifling, breech-loading, and steel construction greatly increased range and power. By the First World War, battleships carried guns of up to 15 inches in calibre, directed by optical rangefinders and mechanical fire-control computers. The largest naval guns ever mounted were the 18.1-inch guns of the Japanese Yamato-class battleships. The Second World War confirmed that aircraft carriers and submarines had displaced the gun-armed battleship, though naval bombardment remained important in amphibious operations.

Modern naval guns

Modern warships typically mount a single dual-purpose gun of 76–127 mm (3–5 inch) calibre, used against surface, shore, and air targets with guided ammunition. Experimental electromagnetic railguns have been tested but have not entered widespread service.
