The Right to Be Lazy

Author: Paul Lafargue
Original title: Le Droit à la paresse
Published: 1880 (serialised); 1883 (pamphlet)
Subject: Critique of the work ethic

The Right to Be Lazy (French: Le Droit à la paresse) is a pamphlet by the Franco-Cuban socialist Paul Lafargue, first published in the newspaper L'Égalité in 1880 and issued in revised pamphlet form in 1883. Written partly from prison, it is a celebrated polemic against the glorification of work under capitalism and one of the most widely translated texts of early French Marxism.

Argument

Lafargue attacks what he calls the "dogma of work": the morality, promoted alike by capitalists, priests and respectable labour leaders, that sanctifies labour and treats unemployment and leisure as vices. Against the demand for a "right to work", he argues that machinery and productivity have made a drastic reduction of the working day possible — he proposes three hours a day of labour — and that overproduction crises are caused by workers' enforced overwork and under-consumption. He points to classical antiquity, where he claims leisure was honoured and labour left to slaves, and to the misery of factory workers of his own day as evidence.

Influence

The pamphlet's wit and its advocacy of leisure made it a classic of anti-work thought. It influenced later critiques of the work ethic, including Bertrand Russell's essay "In Praise of Idleness" (1932), and has been repeatedly invoked by movements for shorter working hours and by critics of consumer society. Lafargue, Karl Marx's son-in-law, campaigned with Jules Guesde in the French Workers' Party; the pamphlet was among the most popular Marxist texts in France before 1914, second only to the Communist Manifesto in some accounts.
