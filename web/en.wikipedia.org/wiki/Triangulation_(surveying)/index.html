Triangulation (surveying)

Triangulation is the process of determining the location of a point by forming triangles to the point from known points. In surveying, it was the standard method for building accurate maps from the seventeenth century until the arrival of satellite positioning. A surveyor measures one baseline very precisely and a chain of angles with a theodolite; all other distances in the network then follow from trigonometry.

Method

Starting from a measured baseline, the surveyor observes the angles at both ends toward a third visible point, fixing that point by the resulting triangle. Each new side becomes the base for further triangles, and the network grows across the region. Because angles can be measured more accurately than long distances, errors accumulate slowly. Networks were anchored to the Earth's figure by astronomical observations for latitude, longitude and azimuth, and later refined by least-squares adjustment.

History

The principle was described by Gemma Frisius in 1533 and put into practice by Willebrord Snellius, who surveyed a chain of triangles between Alkmaar and Breda around 1615. Great national surveys followed: the Principal Triangulation of Great Britain (1791–1853) under the Ordnance Survey, the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India (begun 1802), which measured the Himalayas and led to the identification of the world's highest peak, and similar arcs in France, Germany and the United States. India's survey adopted the figure of Everest's ellipsoid and produced the famous Great Arc.

Decline

Electronic distance measurement and then GPS/GNSS made classical triangulation obsolete for most purposes in the late twentieth century; national triangulation pillars and stations remain as heritage landmarks in many countries.
