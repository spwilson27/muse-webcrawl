Landrace

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A landrace is a domesticated, locally adapted, often traditional variety of a species of animal or plant that has developed over time through adaptation to its natural and cultural environment of agriculture or pastoralism, and through isolation from other populations of the species. Landraces are distinguished from cultivars (bred varieties) and from standardised breeds by their dynamic, heterogeneous character and their close association with a particular place and farming culture.

Characteristics

Landraces are genetically diverse within the population, which gives them resilience: individual plants or animals vary, so a disease or climate stress rarely destroys the whole population. They are typically well adapted to local soils, climate, pests and low-input farming, though yields may be lower or less uniform than those of modern commercial varieties. Selection is informal, by farmers saving seed or breeding stock from the best-performing individuals year after year, rather than by systematic scientific breeding.

Plant landraces

Most of the world's staple crops began as landraces: wheats of the Fertile Crescent, maize landraces of Mexico (still numbering in the dozens), Andean potato varieties (thousands survive in Peru and Bolivia), and rices of Asia. Plant landraces are a crucial reservoir of genetic diversity for breeding disease resistance and climate adaptation, which is why they are conserved both in situ on farms and ex situ in seed banks such as the Svalbard Global Seed Vault.

Animal landraces

Livestock landraces include the Icelandic horse and Icelandic sheep, Finnish Landrace sheep and pigs, British primitive cattle such as the White Park, and numerous regional chicken, goat, and pig populations. Like plant landraces, they tend to be hardy, long-lived and suited to local conditions, but many are endangered as commercial breeds spread.

Conservation

The twentieth-century spread of a small number of high-yielding cultivars caused substantial genetic erosion of landraces. Conservation efforts — by seed-saver networks, gene banks, and organisations such as the FAO — aim to document and preserve landraces before they disappear.
