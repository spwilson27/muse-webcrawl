Aspartate transaminase

Abbreviation: AST

Former name: Serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT)

EC number: 2.6.1.1

Cofactor: Pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6)

Aspartate transaminase (AST) or aspartate aminotransferase, also known as serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT), is a pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent transaminase enzyme that catalyses the reversible transfer of an α-amino group between aspartate and glutamate, and as such is important in amino acid metabolism.

Function

AST catalyses the interconversion of aspartate and α-ketoglutarate to oxaloacetate and glutamate. The reaction links amino acid catabolism with the citric acid cycle and plays a role in the malate–aspartate shuttle that moves reducing equivalents into mitochondria. Two isoenzymes exist: a cytosolic form (GOT1) and a mitochondrial form (GOT2).

Clinical significance

AST is found in the liver, heart, skeletal muscle, kidneys, brain and red blood cells. It is released into the blood when these tissues are damaged, and is measured, together with alanine transaminase (ALT), as one of the "liver function tests". The AST/ALT ratio (De Ritis ratio) can aid diagnosis: a ratio above 2 suggests alcoholic liver disease, while viral hepatitis typically shows a ratio below 1. Marked elevations occur in acute hepatitis, ischaemic or toxic liver injury, myocardial infarction and muscle damage.
