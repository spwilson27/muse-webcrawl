Fortified wine

Fortified wine is a wine to which a distilled spirit, usually brandy, has been added. In the course of time, winemakers have developed many different styles of fortified wine, including port, sherry, Madeira, Marsala, Commandaria wine, and the aromatised wine vermouth. Fortification originally served to preserve wines on long sea voyages: the added spirit raised the alcohol level, typically to between 15.5% and 22% ABV, inhibiting spoilage, and in the case of wines fortified during fermentation it also halted the process, leaving residual sugar and a sweet wine; wines fortified after fermentation finishes are dry unless sweetened later.

Production

When spirit is added determines style. In port, neutral grape spirit (aguardente) is added midway through fermentation, killing the yeast and leaving a sweet, strong wine (about 19–22% ABV) that then ages in cask or bottle. Sherry is fermented dry and fortified afterwards; lighter fortification (about 15%) permits a film of flor yeast to form, producing fino styles, while stronger fortification (about 17%) yields oxidative oloroso styles. Madeira is fortified and then subjected to heat and oxidation (the estufagem process), giving exceptional longevity. Vermouth is a fortified wine flavoured with botanicals including wormwood.

Principal types

  • Port — from the Douro Valley, Portugal; styles include ruby, tawny, vintage and white port.
  • Sherry — from the Jerez region of Andalusia, Spain; aged in the fractional solera system.
  • Madeira — from the Portuguese Madeira Islands; styles graded by sweetness from sercial (dry) to malmsey (sweet).
  • Marsala — from Sicily, Italy, used in cooking as well as drinking.
  • Vermouth — aromatised fortified wine from Italy and France, a cocktail staple.
  • Commandaria — sweet wine from Cyprus, among the oldest named wines still produced.
  • Vins doux naturels — French fortified wines such as Muscat de Beaumes-de-Venise and Banyuls.

Regulation

Under European Union law, fortified wines form the category of "liqueur wines", defined as having an actual alcoholic strength between 15% and 22% by volume, produced by adding wine spirit or brandy to wine or must. Names such as port, sherry and Madeira are protected designations of origin.
