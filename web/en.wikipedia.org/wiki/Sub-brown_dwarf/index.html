Sub-brown dwarf

Sub-brown dwarf
Mass rangeRoughly 1–13 Jupiter masses
FormationStar-like collapse of a gas cloud
Also calledPlanetary-mass brown dwarf; rogue planet (if ejected)

A sub-brown dwarf or planetary-mass brown dwarf is a planetary-mass object that formed in the manner of stars and brown dwarfs — by the direct gravitational collapse of a gas cloud — but whose mass is below the threshold for the thermonuclear fusion of deuterium, about 13 Jupiter masses. Such objects are not bound to a star and drift through interstellar space as free-floating bodies.

Definition and debate

The distinction between a sub-brown dwarf and a rogue planet is one of formation rather than mass: both can have masses of a few times that of Jupiter. If the object condensed directly from a collapsing cloud, it is classified as a sub-brown dwarf; if it formed within a protoplanetary disk around a star and was later ejected, it is a rogue planet. Because the formation history of a solitary object is usually impossible to observe directly, the terminology remains contested, and some astronomers prefer the neutral term "planetary-mass object" (planemo).

Properties

Young sub-brown dwarfs can be comparatively warm, with effective temperatures of several hundred to around a thousand kelvin, shining by the residual heat of their formation. Over billions of years they cool and fade, becoming extremely difficult to detect except in the infrared. The smallest opacity limit for fragmentation of a collapsing cloud is thought to be around a few Jupiter masses, setting a rough lower bound for the class.

Candidate objects

Candidates include members of young star clusters such as the Sigma Orionis cluster and free-floating objects identified by infrared surveys, such as those found by the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE). Some objects once proposed as sub-brown dwarfs have later been reinterpreted as young brown dwarfs or ejected planets, reflecting the difficulty of the classification.
