Erich Seligmann Fromm (23 March 1900 – 18 March 1980) was a German-American social psychologist, psychoanalyst, sociologist, humanistic philosopher and democratic socialist. He was associated with the Frankfurt School of critical theory in his early career before breaking with it, and became one of the most widely read psychoanalytic thinkers of the 20th century.

Life

Born in Frankfurt am Main to Orthodox Jewish parents, Fromm studied sociology and psychology at Heidelberg, earning his doctorate in 1922. He trained as a psychoanalyst in Berlin and co-founded the Frankfurt Psychoanalytic Institute. After the Nazi seizure of power he emigrated in 1934 to the United States, teaching at Columbia and other institutions, and later lived in Mexico, where he founded the Mexican Psychoanalytic Institute, before retiring to Switzerland.

Ideas

In Escape from Freedom (1941) Fromm argued that modern freedom from traditional authority, while liberating, also produces isolation and anxiety, which people may escape through authoritarianism, destructiveness or automaton conformity — an analysis aimed at explaining the appeal of Nazism. The Art of Loving (1956), his most popular book, treats love as an active art requiring knowledge and effort rather than a passive feeling. Works such as Man for Himself (1947) and To Have or to Be? (1976) developed his humanistic ethics and contrasted the "having" and "being" modes of existence.
