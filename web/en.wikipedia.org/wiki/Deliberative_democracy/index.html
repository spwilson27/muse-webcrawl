Deliberative democracy

Deliberative democracy

Deliberative democracy or discursive democracy is a form of democracy in which deliberation is central to decision-making. It holds that political decisions should be the product of fair and reasonable discussion and debate amongst citizens, rather than merely the aggregation of preferences through voting. In deliberative democracy, authentic deliberation — free from distortions of unequal political power — is a necessary condition for the legitimacy of democratic decisions.

Origins and theory

The term was coined by Joseph Bessette in 1980, and the theory was developed extensively in the 1990s by thinkers including Jürgen Habermas, whose ideal speech situation and theory of communicative action provided philosophical foundations, and John Rawls, who linked deliberation to public reason. Joshua Cohen argued that deliberative procedures embody ideals of free and equal citizenship. Deliberative theorists distinguish their model from aggregative democracy, in which fixed preferences are simply counted, and from purely procedural accounts, emphasising that preferences themselves can and should be formed and revised through reason-giving dialogue.

Institutions and practice

Practical deliberative institutions include deliberative polls, developed by James Fishkin, in which a representative sample of citizens is briefed, deliberates in moderated small groups, and is polled before and after; citizens' assemblies, such as the British Columbia Citizens' Assembly on electoral reform (2004) and the Irish Citizens' Assembly (2016–2018), whose recommendations preceded referendums on marriage equality and abortion; consensus conferences; citizens' juries; and participatory budgeting, pioneered in Porto Alegre, Brazil. Deliberative mini-publics are usually chosen by sortition to be descriptively representative of the population.

Criticism

Critics argue that deliberation can amplify inequalities of education and rhetorical skill, that consensus-oriented talk may suppress legitimate conflict (a point pressed by agonistic theorists such as Chantal Mouffe), and that deliberation among like-minded people can polarise opinions, as Cass Sunstein's work on group polarisation suggests. Others question whether large-scale polities can institutionalise genuine deliberation, prompting a "systemic turn" toward evaluating deliberation across whole political systems rather than single forums.
