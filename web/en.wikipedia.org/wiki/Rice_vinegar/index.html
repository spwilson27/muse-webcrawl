Rice vinegar

Rice vinegar
RegionEast and Southeast Asia (China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam)
BaseFermented rice (rice wine oxidised to vinegar)
AcidityTypically c. 4–5% acetic acid (Japanese rice vinegar c. 4.2%)
Main typesWhite, black, red

Rice vinegar is a vinegar made from fermented rice in East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) and Southeast Asia (Vietnam). Like other vinegars it is produced in two stages: yeasts convert rice sugars to alcohol (rice wine), and acetic acid bacteria then oxidise the alcohol to acetic acid. Rice vinegar is generally milder and sweeter than Western distilled or wine vinegars and is a fundamental seasoning in many Asian cuisines.

Types

Chinese rice vinegars

  • White rice vinegar — colourless to pale yellow, the most common; used in sweet-and-sour dishes and pickling.
  • Black vinegar — dark, malty and slightly smoky, made from glutinous rice (often with other grains), aged and complex; famous brands include Shanxi aged vinegar and Chinkiang (Zhenjiang) vinegar, a protected regional specialty used for dumplings and dipping sauces.
  • Red rice vinegar — made with red yeast rice, with a distinctive colour and flavour, popular in Fujian cuisine and shark-fin-style soups.

Japanese rice vinegar

Japanese komezu is pale and delicately flavoured; seasoned with salt and sugar (awasezu) it dresses sushi rice. Sake lees (kasu) can be aged to make red akazu, prized by some high-end sushi chefs.

Korean rice vinegar

Korean ssal-sikcho is used in salads, cold noodle dishes and dipping sauces, often alongside fruit vinegars.

Uses

Rice vinegar seasons sushi rice, pickles (such as Chinese pao cai and Japanese sunomono), dipping sauces, marinades, hot-and-sour soups, and stir-fries. In the West it has become a pantry staple for vinaigrettes and quick pickles because of its gentle acidity.

Substitution

Apple cider vinegar or diluted white wine vinegar is commonly substituted, though the flavour is sharper; Western distilled vinegar is much harsher and used sparingly in comparison.
