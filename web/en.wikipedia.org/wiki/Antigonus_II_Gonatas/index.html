Antigonus II Gonatas (Greek: Ἀντίγονος Γονατᾶς; c. 320–239 BC) was a powerful ruler of the Antigonid dynasty of Macedon who succeeded his father Demetrius Poliorcetes and restored and consolidated Antigonid rule in Macedonia and Greece. His long reign, from 276 BC until his death in 239 BC, gave the kingdom a stability it had not known since the death of Alexander the Great, and he is generally regarded as the true founder of the Antigonid monarchy as a lasting state. His nickname "Gonatas" is of uncertain origin, possibly referring to his birthplace Gonnoi in Thessaly or to a physical feature.

Early life and accession

Antigonus was the son of Demetrius I Poliorcetes and Phila, daughter of Antipater. He served his father loyally during Demetrius's turbulent career and governed his Greek possessions during Demetrius's final eastern campaign, which ended with Demetrius's capture by Seleucus in 286 BC. After a confused period in which Macedonia was overrun by the Gauls (Galatians) in 279 BC and then contested by rival claimants, Antigonus defeated the Gauls near Lysimachia in 277 BC and was acclaimed king; by 276 BC his position in Macedonia was secure.

Reign

Antigonus defended Macedonian supremacy in Greece against a series of challenges. In the Chremonidean War (c. 267–261 BC) Athens and Sparta, backed by Ptolemaic Egypt, tried to throw off Macedonian control; Antigonus besieged Athens into surrender and retained garrisons at strategic points such as Corinth and the Piraeus. He also fought Pyrrhus of Epirus, who invaded Macedonia in 274 BC, and eventually recovered. His naval victories over the Ptolemies, notably at Cos and Andros, ended Egyptian ambitions in the Aegean. In his later years he faced the rising power of the Achaean League under Aratus, who seized Corinth in 243 BC.

Court and philosophy

Antigonus was himself interested in philosophy and gathered thinkers at his court in Pella, including the Stoic-influenced philosopher Persaeus and the historian Hieronymus of Cardia. He was known for his remark that kingship was an "honourable servitude" and for a comparatively modest, disciplined style of rule. He was succeeded by his son Demetrius II Aetolicus.
