Sacred Tradition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sacred Tradition is the living transmission of the message of the Gospel in the Church, handed on from the apostles through their successors under the guidance of the Holy Spirit. In Catholic and Orthodox theology, Sacred Tradition together with Sacred Scripture forms the one deposit of faith (depositum fidei): revelation is conveyed not by Scripture alone but also through the Church's preaching, worship, doctrine and practice across the centuries.

Content

Tradition includes the creeds, liturgical rites, sacramental practice, the writings of the Church Fathers, and doctrines defined by ecumenical councils. Practices such as infant baptism, the canon of Scripture itself, and the observance of Sunday are commonly cited as resting on tradition. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) taught that the Gospel is preserved "in written books and in unwritten traditions", both to be received with equal reverence.

Development and debate

The nineteenth-century theologian John Henry Newman argued in his Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine that tradition unfolds over time while preserving its type, an idea taken up by the Second Vatican Council in Dei Verbum. The doctrine is a major point of division with most Protestant traditions, which uphold sola scriptura — that Scripture alone is the supreme authority in matters of faith.
