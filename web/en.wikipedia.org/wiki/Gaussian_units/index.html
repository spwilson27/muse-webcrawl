Gaussian units

Gaussian units constitute a metric system of physical units based on the centimetre–gram–second (cgs) system. They are the most common cgs variant used for electromagnetism and remain widespread in theoretical physics, especially in electrodynamics, relativity and astrophysics, even though the International System of Units (SI) is standard elsewhere.

Key features

In Gaussian units, Coulomb's law takes the simple form F = q₁q₂/r², with no vacuum permittivity constant, and the electric and magnetic fields E and B have the same dimensions and units. Maxwell's equations contain the speed of light explicitly: for example, ∇ × B − (1/c) ∂E/∂t = (4π/c) J. Electric charge is measured in statcoulombs (esu) and magnetic field in gauss.

Comparison with SI

The Gaussian system is one of several cgs electromagnetic systems; it combines electrostatic (esu) definitions of charge with electromagnetic (emu) definitions of current. Conversion to SI involves factors of c and 4π that are a frequent source of error. The Heaviside–Lorentz system is a "rationalised" relative that removes the 4π factors from Maxwell's equations and is preferred in high-energy physics.
