Tachycardia, also called tachyarrhythmia, is a heart rate that exceeds the normal resting rate. In general, a resting heart rate over 100 beats per minute is accepted as tachycardia in adults. Heart rates above the resting rate may be normal (such as with exercise) or abnormal (such as with electrical problems within the heart).

Classification

Tachycardias are commonly classified by the width of the QRS complex on the electrocardiogram and by their site of origin. Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) arises above the ventricles and includes sinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, and AV nodal reentrant tachycardia. Ventricular tachycardia arises within the ventricles and is more often life-threatening, particularly when sustained or associated with structural heart disease.

Causes

Common causes include physical exercise, fever, pain, anxiety, dehydration, blood loss, hyperthyroidism, anaemia, infection, and stimulants such as caffeine, nicotine, and cocaine. Primary cardiac causes include myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, valvular disease, and congenital conduction abnormalities such as Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome.

Symptoms and complications

Some people with tachycardia have no symptoms. When present, symptoms may include palpitations, dizziness, shortness of breath, chest discomfort, and syncope. Sustained rapid rates can reduce cardiac output and, in the case of ventricular tachycardia, may degenerate into ventricular fibrillation and sudden cardiac death.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is by electrocardiography, sometimes supplemented by ambulatory monitoring and blood tests. Management depends on the type and the patient's stability: unstable patients may require synchronised electrical cardioversion, while stable patients may be treated with vagal manoeuvres, adenosine, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, catheter ablation, or an implantable defibrillator. The opposite condition, an abnormally slow heart rate, is bradycardia.
