Sugar substitute

A sugar substitute is a food additive that provides a sweet taste like that of sugar while containing significantly less food energy than sugar-based sweeteners, making it a zero-calorie or low-calorie sweetener. Artificial sweeteners may be derived through manufacturing of plant extracts or processed by chemical synthesis. Sugar substitutes are also commonly used in beverages and food products marketed as "sugar-free" or "diet".

Types

Sugar substitutes are generally divided into artificial sweeteners, natural high-intensity sweeteners, and sugar alcohols.

History

The first widely used sugar substitute was saccharin, discovered in 1879. Cyclamate followed in 1937, aspartame in 1965, and sucralose in 1976. Consumer demand grew with rising awareness of tooth decay, diabetes, and obesity, and with the popularity of diet soft drinks in the second half of the 20th century.

Health considerations

Regulatory agencies including the U.S. FDA and the European Food Safety Authority have reviewed approved sweeteners and set acceptable daily intake levels. Aspartame must carry a warning for people with phenylketonuria because it is metabolized to phenylalanine. Sugar alcohols can cause laxative effects in large amounts. In 2023, the WHO issued a conditional guideline advising against the use of non-sugar sweeteners for weight control, based on low-certainty evidence of long-term associations.

Industry

Sugar substitutes are used in soft drinks, candies, baked goods, dairy products, and tabletop sweetener packets. Leading tabletop brands include Sweet'N Low (saccharin), Equal (aspartame), Splenda (sucralose), and Truvia (stevia-based).
