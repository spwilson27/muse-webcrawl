The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR; Regulation (EU) 2016/679) is a European Union regulation on information privacy in the EU and the European Economic Area. It governs the processing of personal data and its transfer outside the EU/EEA, and is regarded as the strongest privacy and security law in the world, serving as a model for legislation in many other jurisdictions.

Key provisions

The GDPR requires a lawful basis for processing personal data (such as consent, contract or legitimate interests) and codifies principles of lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation and security. It grants data subjects rights including access, rectification, erasure (the "right to be forgotten"), data portability and objection to automated decision-making.

Controllers must report personal-data breaches to supervisory authorities within 72 hours where feasible, appoint data protection officers in specified cases, and conduct impact assessments for high-risk processing. Because it is a regulation rather than a directive, it applies directly and uniformly across member states, supervised by national data protection authorities coordinated through the European Data Protection Board.

Scope and enforcement

The GDPR applies extraterritorially to organisations anywhere in the world that offer goods or services to, or monitor, people in the EU. Fines can reach €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. Major penalties have included a €1.2 billion fine against Meta in 2023 over transatlantic data transfers, and earlier large fines against Amazon and Google.

History and influence

Adopted in April 2016 after four years of negotiation, the GDPR became enforceable on 25 May 2018, triggering worldwide changes in privacy notices, cookie-consent practice and corporate compliance. It has inspired laws such as California's CCPA/CPRA, Brazil's LGPD and reforms in Japan and elsewhere; the United Kingdom retained a near-identical "UK GDPR" after Brexit.
