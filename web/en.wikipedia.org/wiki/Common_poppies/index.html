Common poppy

Scientific name: Papaver rhoeas
Family: Papaveraceae
Also called: Corn poppy, field poppy, Flanders poppy, red poppy

The common poppy, Papaver rhoeas, is an annual flowering plant in the poppy family Papaveraceae, native to Europe, North Africa and western Asia and widely naturalised elsewhere. It is an agricultural weed and an iconic wildflower of disturbed ground, with vivid red, single flowers with dark centres, flowering in late spring and summer.

Description

Plants grow up to about 70 cm tall, with hairy stems exuding milky latex, pinnately lobed leaves, and papery four-petalled flowers followed by distinctive ribbed seed capsules that release many tiny seeds through pores. Seeds can remain dormant in soil for decades and germinate when ground is disturbed — famously on battlefields.

Symbolism

After the First World War, the common poppy — inspired by John McCrae's 1915 poem "In Flanders Fields" — became the remembrance poppy, a symbol of remembrance for fallen soldiers in Britain, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and other Commonwealth countries, worn around Armistice Day / Remembrance Sunday.

Uses

Its seeds are edible and similar to opium poppy seeds, though the plant does not produce opium in significant amounts. It is grown ornamentally, including the Shirley poppy cultivars.
