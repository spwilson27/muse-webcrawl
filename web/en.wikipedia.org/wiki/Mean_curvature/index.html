Mean curvature

The mean curvature at a point is the average of the two principal curvatures.

In differential geometry, the mean curvature H of a surface is a measure of how the surface bends, defined at each point as the average of the two principal curvatures κ1 and κ2:

H = (κ1 + κ2) / 2.

The concept was introduced by Sophie Germain in her work on elasticity theory in the early nineteenth century. Unlike the Gaussian curvature, which is intrinsic, mean curvature is an extrinsic quantity: it depends on how the surface is embedded in space, and its sign depends on the choice of unit normal.

Minimal surfaces

A surface whose mean curvature vanishes at every point is called a minimal surface. Such surfaces locally minimise area and model soap films; examples include the plane, the catenoid and the helicoid. For a sphere of radius r, the mean curvature is constant with magnitude 1/r.

Applications

Mean curvature appears in the Young–Laplace equation relating pressure differences across a curved interface to surface tension, in the study of capillary surfaces, and in geometric analysis through mean curvature flow, in which a surface evolves in the direction of its normal with speed equal to its mean curvature. In general relativity, the notion extends to the mean curvature of hypersurfaces used in initial-value formulations and in the definition of apparent horizons.
