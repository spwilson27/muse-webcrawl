In the philosophy of mind, dualism is the view that the mental and the physical—or mind and body, or mind and brain—are fundamentally different kinds of things. Its best-known form, substance dualism, holds that reality consists of two kinds of substance: material substance, which is extended in space, and mental substance (the soul or mind), which thinks but has no spatial extension. Dualism contrasts with monist views such as physicalism (everything is physical) and idealism (everything is mental).

Cartesian dualism

René Descartes articulated the classic version in the Meditations on First Philosophy (1641). Through methodological doubt he found he could conceive of his mind existing without his body, and argued that the mind is therefore a distinct substance—res cogitans—from the body, res extensa. Descartes proposed that the two interact in the pineal gland, a guess that invited immediate criticism, most famously from Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, who asked how an immaterial substance could move a material one—the interaction problem. Contemporary responses included Malebranche's occasionalism (God causes each correlated event) and Leibniz's pre-established harmony.

Forms of dualism

Property dualism accepts one kind of substance (the physical) but holds that mental properties are fundamental and irreducible to physical properties; David Chalmers's naturalistic dualism, built on the "hard problem of consciousness" and philosophical-zombie arguments, is a leading modern version. Epiphenomenalism grants mental properties causal impotence—they are byproducts of brain activity. Karl Popper and John Eccles defended an interactionist "dualism of three worlds." Earlier and non-Western precedents include Plato's separation of soul and body and the Sāṃkhya school of Indian philosophy, which distinguishes pure consciousness (puruṣa) from matter (prakṛti).

Arguments and objections

Arguments for dualism appeal to the conceivability of disembodied minds and zombies, the privacy and intentionality of thought, Frank Jackson's knowledge argument (Mary the color scientist), and the irreducibility of subjective experience (Thomas Nagel's "What is it like to be a bat?"). Critics respond with the causal closure of physics, parsimony, the dependence of mental functions on brain states shown by neuroscience and brain damage, and Gilbert Ryle's charge in The Concept of Mind (1949) that Cartesian dualism is a "category mistake"—the myth of "the ghost in the machine."
