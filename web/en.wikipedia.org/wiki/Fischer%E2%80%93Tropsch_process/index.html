Fischer–Tropsch process

InventorsFranz Fischer, Hans Tropsch
Developed1920s, Germany
FeedstockSynthesis gas (CO + H₂)
ProductsLiquid hydrocarbons, fuels, waxes

The Fischer–Tropsch process (FT) is a collection of chemical reactions that converts a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, known as synthesis gas or syngas, into liquid hydrocarbons. Developed by Franz Fischer and Hans Tropsch at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Coal Research in Germany in the 1920s, the process produces synthetic fuels and lubricants from coal, natural gas or biomass.

(2n + 1) H₂ + n CO → CnH(2n+2) + n H₂O

History

FT technology was used extensively by Germany during World War II to produce fuels from coal, and later by South Africa's Sasol during the apartheid-era oil embargoes. Modern gas-to-liquids plants, such as Shell's Pearl GTL in Qatar, operate on a very large scale.

Process

Syngas is passed over iron- or cobalt-based catalysts at elevated temperature and pressure; chain growth on the catalyst surface yields a distribution of alkanes, alkenes and oxygenates that can be refined into diesel, jet fuel, naphtha and waxes.
