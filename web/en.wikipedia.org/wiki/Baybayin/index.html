Baybayin

Baybayin (Tagalog pronunciation: [bai̯ˈbaːjɪn], precolonial ᜊᜌ᜔ᜊᜌᜒᜈ᜔, virama-krus-kudlit: ᜊᜌ᜔ᜊᜌᜒᜈ᜔, virama-pamudpod: ᜊᜌ᜔ᜊᜌᜒᜈ᜔; also incorrectly known as alibata) is an abugida script of the Philippines. It is a member of the Brahmic family of scripts and was used widely in Luzon and other islands before and during early Spanish colonisation. The script is a descendant of the Old Kawi script through regional variants such as the related scripts of the Tagbanwa, Hanunó'o and Buhid peoples.

Structure

Baybayin has three independent vowel signs (a, e/i, o/u) and fourteen consonant signs, each with an inherent /a/ vowel. A diacritic mark called a kudlit, placed above or below a consonant sign, changes its vowel to e/i or o/u respectively. In the traditional system, final consonants were not written, an ambiguity accepted by readers. Spanish friars introduced a cross-shaped kudlit (+) to cancel the inherent vowel, an innovation later adapted into modern revival proposals.

History

Spanish chroniclers such as Antonio de Morga reported widespread literacy in the script among coastal peoples in the 16th century. The first book printed in the Philippines, the Doctrina Christiana (1593), included Baybayin text. The script declined under Spanish rule as the Latin alphabet spread, but survived among some groups and saw continuous use into the 18th century in certain regions.

Modern revival

Baybayin has enjoyed a strong cultural revival since the late 20th century, appearing in art, tattoos, signage, logos and education. Proposed legislation (the National Writing System Act) has sought official recognition of Baybayin as the Philippines' national writing system, and the script is encoded in Unicode in the Tagalog block.
