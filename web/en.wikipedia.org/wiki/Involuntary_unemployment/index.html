Involuntary unemployment

Involuntary unemployment occurs when a person is unemployed despite being willing to work at the prevailing wage. It stands in contrast to voluntary unemployment, where a person chooses not to work because the wage is too low, or because they value leisure or other activities more.

Keynesian economics

The concept is central to Keynesian economics. In The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), John Maynard Keynes argued that economies could settle at an equilibrium with persistent involuntary unemployment, because deficient aggregate demand means firms will not hire all those willing to work at the going wage, and wage cuts alone cannot be relied on to restore full employment.

Classical and neoclassical responses

Classical economists generally held that unemployment beyond frictional levels must be voluntary or caused by rigidities — minimum wages, trade unions, or unemployment benefits — preventing wages from adjusting to clear the labour market. Later theories proposed mechanisms that generate involuntary unemployment even with rational agents, such as efficiency wages (paying above market-clearing wages to raise productivity), insider–outsider dynamics, and search and matching frictions, in which finding a job takes time even when vacancies exist.

Measurement

Official unemployment statistics, such as the ILO definition, count people who are without work, available for work, and actively seeking work; such unemployment is typically treated as largely involuntary, though the boundary with voluntary non-participation is debated.
