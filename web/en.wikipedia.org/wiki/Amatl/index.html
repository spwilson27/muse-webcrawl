Amatl

Amatl (Nahuatl: āmatl [ˈaːmatɬ]; Spanish: amate) is a form of paper that was manufactured in Mesoamerica from the bark of wild fig trees (Ficus species) since the pre-classic period. It was the principal writing surface of the Aztec and Maya worlds, used for folded-screen codices, tribute records, maps and ritual offerings, and the Spanish word amate also gives its name to the folk art of amate painting still produced in Guerrero, Mexico.

To make amatl, the inner bark of the fig tree is stripped, soaked in water or an alkaline solution (historically with ash or lime, or boiled), and the softened strips are laid crosswise and beaten together with a stone beater until they fuse into a single sheet, which is then dried in the sun, smoothed and, for writing, coated with a white gesso of lime. The result ranges from coarse brown ritual paper to fine, pale sheets.

Manufacture

Stone bark-beaters are found at Mesoamerican sites from very early periods, indicating great antiquity for the craft. The Otomí town of San Pablito in Puebla is the best-known modern centre of production, where the technique has been maintained continuously. Sheets of amatl are also ritually cut into paper figures (recortes) representing spirits, a practice of Otomí and Nahua religion.

Uses

The Aztec state consumed vast quantities of amatl: tribute lists record hundreds of thousands of sheets delivered annually to Tenochtitlan for the records of scribes (tlacuiloque), who painted codices recording history, genealogy, land, taxation and ritual calendars. Amatl was also burned as an offering and used to make banners, costumes and offerings decorated with liquid rubber and paint.

Colonial suppression and survival

Because of its central role in indigenous religion, amatl and the codices written on it were targeted by Spanish colonial authorities and missionaries, who burned manuscripts and banned ritual paper; production survived mainly in remote villages. Nearly all surviving pre-conquest-style codices on amatl are post-conquest works.

Modern amate art

From the 1960s, Nahua painters of the Balsas River region of Guerrero began painting village scenes and stories on amate sheets, creating a celebrated Mexican folk-art form sold worldwide; the bark paper itself is still supplied largely by Otomí makers in San Pablito.
