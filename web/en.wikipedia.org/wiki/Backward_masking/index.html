Backward masking

In perceptual psychology, backward masking is a phenomenon in which the perception of a briefly presented target stimulus (such as a word, shape, or sound) is impaired or suppressed by a second stimulus — the mask — presented shortly after it. Masking is "backward" because the mask follows, rather than precedes, the target; when a mask precedes the target the effect is called forward masking.

Visual masking

In vision, backward masking is strongest when the interval between target and mask (the stimulus onset asynchrony) is roughly 20–100 milliseconds. Two broad classes are distinguished: masking by noise or pattern, which interrupts the target's processing, and metacontrast, in which a mask with contours adjacent to (but not overlapping) the target suppresses its visibility. Backward masking is a central tool in the study of visual awareness, because a target that is masked can be rendered subjectively invisible while still influencing behaviour — demonstrating unconscious perception.

Auditory masking

Analogous effects occur in hearing, where a loud sound can mask a quieter sound presented shortly before it; auditory backward masking is generally effective over shorter intervals than the simultaneous-masking case.

Applications

Masking paradigms are used to study the timing of conscious perception, feedforward and recurrent processing in the visual cortex, and deficits in conditions such as schizophrenia and dyslexia, where abnormal masking performance has been reported. The term should not be confused with "backmasking", the recording technique of embedding reversed messages in audio.
