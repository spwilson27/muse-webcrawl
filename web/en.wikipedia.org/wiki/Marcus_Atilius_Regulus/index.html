Marcus Atilius Regulus

Regulus returning to Carthage, a later artistic tradition

Marcus Atilius Regulus (died c. 250 BC) was a Roman statesman and general of the gens Atilia who served as consul twice, most famously during the First Punic War. His invasion of Africa, capture by Carthage, and — in the celebrated Roman tradition — his voluntary return to captivity and death made him an enduring exemplum of Roman fides (good faith) and duty.

African campaign

As consul in 256 BC with Lucius Manlius Vulso Longus, Regulus shared command of the Roman fleet that defeated Carthage at the Battle of Cape Ecnomus and landed an army in Africa. After early successes, including victory at Adys, his harsh peace terms were rejected. In 255 BC the reorganized Carthaginian army, led by the Spartan mercenary Xanthippus, crushed the Romans at the Battle of Tunis, and Regulus was taken prisoner.

The legend

According to the famous story told by Horace and other later writers, the Carthaginians released Regulus on parole to carry their peace proposals (or a prisoner exchange) to Rome, bound by an oath to return if the mission failed. Regulus advised the Senate to reject the terms, then honoured his oath and went back to Carthage, where he was tortured to death. Modern historians generally regard the embassy story as a later patriotic invention, although his capture and death in captivity are accepted.

Legacy

Regulus became a stock figure of Roman moralising literature, praised by Cicero and invoked for centuries as the model of keeping faith with an enemy. The tale was a popular subject in Enlightenment-era painting and drama.
