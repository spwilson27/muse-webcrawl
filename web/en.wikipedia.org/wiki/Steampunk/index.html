Steampunk is a subgenre of science fiction that incorporates retrofuturistic technology and aesthetics inspired by 19th-century industrial steam-powered machinery. Works of steampunk are often set in an alternative history of the Victorian era or the American frontier, where steam power has maintained mainstream usage, or in fantasy worlds that similarly rely on steam, clockwork, and spring-driven contraptions. Beyond literature it has become a design style and a maker subculture of costumes, gadgets, music, and conventions.

Origins

The genre's roots lie in the "scientific romances" of Jules Verne and H. G. Wells and in Edisonades — tales of boy inventors. Its modern form crystallized in California in the late 1970s and 1980s in the novels of K. W. Jeter (Morlock Night, 1979), Tim Powers (The Anubis Gates, 1983), and James Blaylock (Homunculus, 1986). Jeter coined the name "steampunk" in a 1987 letter to Locus magazine, tongue-in-cheek, by analogy with cyberpunk — the movement launched by William Gibson, who then co-wrote the genre's defining text with Bruce Sterling: The Difference Engine (1990), in which Charles Babbage's mechanical computers transform Victorian Britain into an information age of brass and coal-smoke.

Characteristics

Steampunk worlds run on anachronistic technology — difference engines, airships, steam-driven automatons, mechanical prosthetics — imagined with the materials and manners of the 1800s: brass, rivets, goggles, waistcoats. The better works interrogate the era's realities — empire, class, industrial labor, the dark satanic mills behind the nostalgia — while the aesthetic side of the movement celebrates craftsmanship against mass-produced modernity, often through do-it-yourself modification of objects and clothing.

Influence

Steampunk spans novels (China Miéville's Perdido Street Station), comics (The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen), film (Wild Wild West, Studio Ghibli's Castle in the Sky and Howl's Moving Castle), games (BioShock Infinite, Dishonored), and fashion. Variants include dieselpunk, clockpunk, and regional or postcolonial steampunks that relocate the machinery of empire to other settings. As a named subculture it peaked in visibility in the late 2000s and early 2010s, even attracting mainstream fashion and tech-industry attention, and it remains a fixture of fan conventions worldwide.
