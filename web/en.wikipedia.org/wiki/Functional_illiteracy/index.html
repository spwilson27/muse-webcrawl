Functional illiteracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adult literacy classes help functionally illiterate adults handle everyday texts such as forms and labels.

Functional illiteracy consists of reading and writing skills that are inadequate "to manage daily living and employment tasks that require reading skills beyond a basic level". It is contrasted with illiteracy in the strict sense, meaning the inability to read or write simple sentences in any language. A person may be able to sign their name and read short, familiar texts yet still be unable to fill in a job application, understand a medicine label, or follow a bus timetable.

Definition and measurement

There is no single definition. UNESCO defines functional literacy in terms of engaging in activities in which literacy is required for effective functioning in one's group and community. Large-scale assessments, such as the OECD's Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC), grade adults on proficiency levels (Levels 1–5 and below Level 1); adults at or below Level 1 are commonly described as functionally illiterate or at risk, since they can only locate single pieces of information in short texts.

Prevalence

PIAAC results across OECD countries typically find that between roughly 10% and 25% of adults score at or below Level 1 in literacy, with wide variation by country, age and educational attainment. In the United States, studies such as the 2003 National Assessment of Adult Literacy found about 14% of adults at "below basic" prose literacy. In parts of the developing world, functional illiteracy overlaps with high absolute illiteracy rates.

Causes and consequences

Causes include interrupted or poor-quality schooling, learning disabilities such as dyslexia, poverty, and — for immigrants — limited proficiency in the language of instruction. Consequences include restricted employment, lower earnings, poorer health outcomes (partly through low health literacy), and reduced civic participation. Functional illiteracy is associated with higher rates of unemployment and incarceration, although the direction and strength of these links are debated.

Remediation

Remedies include adult basic education programmes, workplace literacy schemes, family literacy interventions, and plain-language design of public documents. Critics note that functional standards rise with the demands of society — the growth of digital bureaucracy has added "digital literacy" to the skills a person needs to function — so the problem cannot be solved by schooling alone.
