Golden Madonna of Essen

The Golden Madonna of Essen is a sculpture of the Virgin Mary and the infant Jesus. It is a wooden core covered over with sheets of thin gold leaf. The piece is part of the treasury of Essen Cathedral, formerly the church of Essen Abbey, in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany, and is kept on display in the north chapel of the cathedral.

Dating to around 980, it is the oldest known fully three-dimensional sculpture of the Madonna in Western art, and the oldest surviving major work of Ottonian sculpture north of the Alps. Only the Lorsch Abbey's golden Virgin from the 8th century might be older, but it is lost.

Description

The statue is 74 cm (29 in) high, with a wooden core of poplar. The Virgin is depicted seated, in the Sedes Sapientiae ("Seat of Wisdom") pose, with the Christ Child seated sideways on her left knee. Mary wears a close-fitting tunic, a mantle, and a veil; in her right hand she holds a sphere or apple-like orb. The Child holds a book, a reference to Christ as the embodiment of divine wisdom and the Word. The eyes of both figures are inlaid with cloisonné enamel.

History

The sculpture was probably made during the abbacy of Mathilde, abbess of Essen (971–1011), a granddaughter of Otto the Great, and may have been produced in Cologne or Hildesheim. It became the focus of devotion at the abbey and a symbol of the identity of the Essen convent of canonesses. During processions it was carried before the community, and its position in liturgical ceremonies — such as the Easter and Ascension rites recorded in medieval sources — was carefully prescribed.

The statue has been repeatedly restored; in the 14th century the Child's head was partly renewed, and a major restoration in 1905 removed later overpainting. During the Second World War it was evacuated for safekeeping. It remains an object of veneration, and a procession in its honour is still held.

Significance

The Golden Madonna marks the beginning of monumental sculpture in the medieval West after the early Christian period. Together with the Gero Cross in Cologne, it is one of the foundational works of Ottonian art, notable for combining Byzantine-influenced iconography with a new, fully plastic treatment of the human figure.
