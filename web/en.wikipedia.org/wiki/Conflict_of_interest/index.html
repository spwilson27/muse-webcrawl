Conflict of interest
Type: Ethical and legal concept
Applies to: Public officials, professionals, researchers, fiduciaries
Management: Disclosure, recusal, divestment, blind trusts
A balance-scale illustration symbolising the weighing of private interests against public duty that defines a conflict of interest.

A conflict of interest (COI) is a situation in which a person or organisation is involved in multiple interests, financial or otherwise, and serving one interest could involve working against another. Typically this occurs where an individual's personal interests might compromise their judgment, decisions, or actions in a professional capacity — for example, a public official who owns shares in a company affected by their decisions, or a researcher funded by the manufacturer of a drug they are evaluating.

Types

Conflicts are commonly classified as actual (a real, existing conflict), potential (circumstances that could develop into one), and perceived (a situation that a reasonable person might think creates a conflict, even if none exists). They may be financial — shareholdings, fees, gifts, outside employment — or non-financial, such as family relationships, personal loyalties, and academic rivalry. A conflict of interest is a situation, not in itself wrongdoing; the wrong lies in failing to disclose or manage it, or in allowing it to distort decisions.

Regulation

In government, ethics laws typically require officials to disclose financial interests, recuse themselves from affected decisions, divest conflicting assets, or place them in a blind trust. Criminal provisions such as 18 U.S.C. § 208 prohibit U.S. federal employees from participating in matters in which they have a financial interest. In law and medicine, professional rules impose disclosure duties and disqualification from matters where a professional's interests diverge from a client's or patient's. Academic journals require authors to declare funding and competing interests alongside publications.

Related concepts

Closely related ideas include self-dealing by fiduciaries, insider trading, revolving-door employment between regulators and industry, and bias in research arising from industry sponsorship — studies have repeatedly found that industry-funded research is more likely to reach conclusions favourable to the sponsor.
