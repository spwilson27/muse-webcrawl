Tumor lysis syndrome

Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) is a potentially life-threatening complication of cancer treatment in which the rapid breakdown of large numbers of malignant cells releases their intracellular contents into the bloodstream.

Findings

The characteristic abnormalities are hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia and secondary hypocalcemia. Excess uric acid and calcium phosphate can precipitate in the kidneys, causing acute kidney injury; high potassium can cause fatal cardiac arrhythmias. TLS occurs most often after chemotherapy for high-grade lymphomas (such as Burkitt lymphoma) and acute leukemias with high tumour burden, but can also occur spontaneously.

Prevention and treatment

Management centres on aggressive intravenous hydration, and control of uric acid with allopurinol or rasburicase, which enzymatically degrades uric acid. Electrolytes are monitored closely, and haemodialysis may be required for refractory hyperkalemia, fluid overload or kidney failure. The Cairo–Bishop criteria are commonly used to define laboratory and clinical TLS.
