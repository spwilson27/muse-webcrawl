Lydian language

Lydian is an extinct Indo-European Anatolian language spoken in the region of Lydia in western Anatolia (modern Turkey). It was the language of the Lydian people, whose capital was Sardis, and is attested in inscriptions dating from the end of the 8th century BCE to the 3rd century BCE. Lydian is one of the least understood of the Anatolian languages, although it is generally considered to be most closely related to Luwian within the Luwic subgroup.

Corpus

The Lydian corpus consists of a little over one hundred inscriptions, most of them short funerary and dedicatory texts found at Sardis and in its surroundings. The longest and most informative texts include a Lydian–Aramaic bilingual inscription from Sardis and a Lydian–Greek bilingual, which have been of great help in the decipherment of the language. Coin legends from the Lydian kingdom also preserve early examples of the script.

Alphabet

Lydian was written in its own alphabet, related to the Greek alphabet and to other alphabets of Asia Minor such as Carian and Lycian. The script ran predominantly from right to left and comprised about 26 letters. It includes several signs not found in Greek, among them letters for nasalised vowels, conventionally transliterated with a tilde (e.g. ã, &etilde;), and signs such as ƒ, q and ττ. Word dividers were used only sporadically.

Phonology and grammar

Lydian phonology is notable for its series of palatalised consonants and its nasal vowels, which arose from vowel plus nasal sequences. The morphology is broadly Anatolian in character: nouns distinguish animate and neuter genders and nominative, accusative and dative-locative cases; verbs inflect for person and number in present and preterite tenses, and a mediopassive voice existed. However, Lydian also shows many developments not shared with Hittite, making a straightforward comparison difficult. Word order appears to have been subject–object–verb.

History of decipherment

Serious study of Lydian began in the early 20th century, following excavations at Sardis by American archaeologists. The bilingual texts allowed scholars to establish the values of the letters and the broad outlines of the grammar, but many words remain obscure, and Lydian is still considered imperfectly understood compared with Hittite or Luwian. The language died out as its speakers shifted to Greek during the Hellenistic period, probably by the 1st century BCE.
