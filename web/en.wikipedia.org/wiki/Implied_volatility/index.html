Implied volatility
FieldMathematical finance
Related modelBlack–Scholes model

In financial mathematics, the implied volatility (IV) of an option contract is the value of the volatility parameter of the underlying asset that, when input into an option pricing model such as Black–Scholes, returns a theoretical price equal to the option's actual market price. Because it is derived from market prices, implied volatility is regarded as the market's forward-looking estimate of how much the underlying asset will fluctuate over the life of the option.

Calculation

Implied volatility cannot be read directly from the Black–Scholes formula; it must be found numerically (for example by the Newton–Raphson method) by inverting the pricing formula. It is usually quoted as an annualised percentage. Option prices — and hence IV — rise with market demand for protection and uncertainty, so IV tends to spike during market crises.

Volatility smile and surface

In practice, options on the same underlying with the same expiry but different strike prices trade at different implied volatilities, producing a "volatility smile" or "smirk" when IV is plotted against strike — a deviation from Black–Scholes's constant-volatility assumption first appearing prominently in equity options after the 1987 crash. Plotting IV across strikes and maturities gives the volatility surface used by traders for pricing and risk management.

Indices

The best-known measure is the VIX index, computed from the implied volatilities of S&P 500 index options and widely called the market's "fear gauge".
