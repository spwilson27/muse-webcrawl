John Singer Sargent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Madame X (1883–84): Madame Gautreau in a black gown against a dark ground, her profile turned away — the portrait that scandalised Paris]

John Singer Sargent (12 January 1856 – 15 April 1925) was an American expatriate artist, the leading portrait painter of his generation and of the Edwardian era's international high society. Born in Florence to American parents and trained in Paris, he dazzled with bravura brushwork learned from Velázquez and Hals, producing some 900 oil paintings and over 2,000 watercolours.

His famous works include Madame X (1883–84), Carnation, Lily, Lily, Rose (1885–86), El Jaleo (1882), The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit (1882), the Boston Public Library murals, and his official war painting Gassed (1919).

Paris sensation and scandal

Sargent entered the atelier of Carolus-Duran and the École des Beaux-Arts, exhibiting at the Paris Salon from 1877. The Spanish-flavoured El Jaleo made his name, but the 1884 Salon showing of Madame X — his portrait of the society beauty Virginie Gautreau, one jewelled strap originally painted slipped from her shoulder — caused such scandal that he left Paris for London.

Portraitist and muralist

In England and America Sargent became the portraitist of presidents, plutocrats and fellow artists, charging high fees and chafing at the work — 'a portrait is a picture in which there is just a tiny little something not quite right about the mouth'. He largely gave up commissioned portraits after 1907 for landscape, travel watercolours and the mural cycles in Boston and Widener Library, and served as an official war artist in 1918.
