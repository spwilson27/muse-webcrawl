Locus of control

Locus of control is the degree to which people believe that they, as opposed to external forces (beyond their influence), have control over the outcome of events in their lives. The concept was developed by Julian B. Rotter in 1954, and has since become an important aspect of personality psychology. A person's "locus" is conceptualised as internal (a belief that life events are within one's own control) or external (a belief that life is controlled by outside factors which the person cannot influence, or that chance or fate controls their lives).

Individuals with an internal locus of control believe that the events in their life derive primarily from their own actions: for example, a student with an internal locus of control who scores well on a test is likely to attribute the result to their hard work and preparation. Those with an external locus of control tend to attribute outcomes to luck, fate, powerful others, or circumstances beyond their control.

Measurement

Locus of control is typically measured with questionnaire scales, most famously Rotter's Internal–External (I–E) scale, a 29-item forced-choice instrument. Later psychologists developed multidimensional scales distinguishing, for example, internality, powerful others, and chance, and domain-specific scales measuring control beliefs in health, work, and academic settings.

Correlates and consequences

Research links an internal locus of control with higher academic achievement, better health behaviours, greater job satisfaction and performance, and greater resilience under stress, though the associations are usually moderate. An internal locus is not always beneficial: people may blame themselves for uncontrollable outcomes. Cultural research has found differences between individualistic and collectivistic societies in typical control beliefs, and locus of control also tends to shift across the lifespan, becoming more internal through middle age.
