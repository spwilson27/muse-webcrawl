Dravidian languages

The Dravidian languages are a family of about 70 languages spoken by some 250 million people, mainly in southern India and parts of Sri Lanka, Pakistan and Nepal.

Dravidian languages
Region: South Asia
Speakers: ~250 million
Largest: Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, Malayalam
Scripts: Brahmic-derived

Major languages

The four literary languages — Telugu (most speakers), Tamil (with a classical literature over two millennia old), Kannada and Malayalam — are official languages of Indian states. Northern outliers include Kurukh, Malto and Brahui in Balochistan, whose presence far west fuels debate about the family's origin, possibly linked to the Indus Valley civilisation.

Features

Dravidian languages are agglutinative, with SOV word order, retroflex consonants, inclusive/exclusive "we", and extensive case suffixes; verbs mark tense, aspect, mood and agreement. Heavy Sanskrit influence supplies much vocabulary, while the family is unrelated to Indo-Aryan. Robert Caldwell's Comparative Grammar (1856) established the family and coined its name from Sanskrit drāviḍa.
