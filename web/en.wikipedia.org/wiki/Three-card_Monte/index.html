Three-card Monte

TypeConfidence trick / card game
PlayersDealer ("tosser"), victim ("mark"), shills
Cards3 — typically a queen and two indifferent cards
Related gamesShell game, Find the Lady

Three-card Monte—also known as Find the Lady and three-card trick—is a confidence game in which the victim, or "mark", is tricked into betting money on the assumption that they can find the "money card" among three face-down playing cards. It is the same game as the shell game, adapted to cards.

How the game works

The dealer shows three cards—commonly the queen of hearts and two other cards, often bent or marked subtly—places them face down, and rearranges them rapidly. The mark bets on which card is the queen. In an honest demonstration the game would be a test of observation, but the operator uses sleight of hand (such as the "hype" throw, in which the card apparently thrown first is actually the second) so the mark cannot win when real money is at stake. Shills planted in the crowd pretend to win, encouraging the mark to bet larger amounts; when the mark finally bets big, the dealer ensures they lose—often by a confederate secretly bending or switching cards ("the Mexican turnover") or by a shill distracting the mark.

History

The game is documented from the early nineteenth century and was a staple of traveling fairs and city streets; famous operators included the American gambler and con man "Canada Bill" Jones and, in New York, the gangs of operators on street corners. It persists in tourist areas of major cities despite being illegal in most jurisdictions as gambling or fraud.

Variations and countermeasures

Because the operator controls the outcome with sleight of hand, the only reliable countermeasure is refusal to play. Magicians perform three-card Monte as entertainment, openly demonstrating the methods—classic routines include Dai Vernon's and Ricky Jay's presentations—while emphasizing that spectators would never win against a real street operator.
