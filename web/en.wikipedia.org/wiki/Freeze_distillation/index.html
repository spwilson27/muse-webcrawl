Freeze distillation

Freeze distillation, also called fractional freezing or freeze concentration, is a process of concentrating a solution by partially freezing it and removing the ice crystals that form. Because water freezes out as nearly pure ice while dissolved substances — such as ethanol, sugars, and flavour compounds — remain in the liquid fraction, repeated freezing progressively concentrates the liquid.

Principle

The process exploits the difference between the freezing behaviour of pure water and of a solution: as the mixture cools, relatively pure ice crystallizes first, and the remaining liquid becomes richer in solutes. Removing the ice and refreezing the liquid can be repeated many times. Unlike heat distillation, freeze distillation does not separate compounds by volatility, so substances that heat distillation would remove — including congeners and, notably, methanol — are concentrated along with the ethanol.

Alcoholic beverages

The best-known application is applejack, an American apple brandy traditionally made by "jacking" hard cider in winter: barrels of cider were left outdoors, and the ice was skimmed off to concentrate the alcohol, sometimes reaching 30–40% ABV. German Eisbock beer is produced by partially freezing a strong doppelbock and removing the ice, giving a smoother, stronger beer. Similar techniques have been applied to ice wine production (cryoextraction of grapes) and to concentrating other fermented drinks.

Other uses and legality

Freeze concentration is used industrially to concentrate fruit juices, coffee extracts, and milk while preserving heat-sensitive flavours, and in desalination research. In several jurisdictions, concentrating alcohol by freezing at home occupies a legal grey area or is regulated like distillation, even though no still is used.
