Homelessness in England

Homelessness in England is a significant social policy issue encompassing rough sleeping, statutory homelessness assessed by local authorities, and "hidden" homelessness such as sofa surfing. England has one of the more developed legal frameworks for homelessness in the world, based on duties owed by local housing authorities under the Housing (Homeless Persons) Act 1977 and the Housing Act 1996, extended by the Homelessness Reduction Act 2017.

Legal framework

Under the statutory system, a household that is homeless or threatened with homelessness may apply to a local authority, which must assess it and, where the applicant is eligible, in priority need (for example families with children, or people who are vulnerable) and not intentionally homeless, secure accommodation. The Homelessness Reduction Act 2017, in force from April 2018, added prevention and relief duties owed to all eligible applicants regardless of priority need. Households owed the main duty but not yet rehoused are frequently placed in temporary accommodation, including bed-and-breakfast hotels and hostels, sometimes for long periods.

Scale and trends

Numbers fell substantially during the 2000s under government rough-sleeping programmes, then rose through the 2010s: official autumn street counts recorded a peak of roughly 4,750 people sleeping rough in 2017, and statutory homelessness acceptances and temporary accommodation placements climbed to record levels in the 2020s, with well over 100,000 households — including more than 150,000 children — in temporary accommodation by the mid-2020s. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the "Everyone In" initiative of March 2020 rapidly accommodated rough sleepers in hotels. Charities such as Shelter, Crisis and St Mungo's provide services and campaign on causes including housing supply, welfare policy and eviction from private tenancies, the leading immediate trigger of statutory homelessness.

Responses

Government strategies have combined targets for halving and ending rough sleeping, funding for Housing First pilots (which provide unconditional permanent housing with support), and duties on public bodies to refer people at risk. Critics argue that structural shortages of affordable and social housing are the underlying driver of rising homelessness in England.
