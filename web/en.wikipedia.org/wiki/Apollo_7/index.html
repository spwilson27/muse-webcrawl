Apollo 7

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Apollo 7
CrewWally Schirra, Donn Eisele, Walter Cunningham
Launch11 October 1968
Duration10 days, 20 hours
SpacecraftCSM-101, Saturn IB
Apollo 7 lifts off on a Saturn IB.

Apollo 7 was the first crewed flight of the Apollo program after the Apollo 1 fire, launched on 11 October 1968. Commander Wally Schirra, command module pilot Donn Eisele and lunar module pilot Walter Cunningham spent nearly 11 days in Earth orbit testing the redesigned Block II command and service module — the spacecraft that would have to carry astronauts to the Moon.

The mission met all its objectives: the service propulsion system fired repeatedly, the crew rendezvoused with the spent S-IVB stage, and Apollo's first live television broadcasts from space won an Emmy. Success cleared the way for the audacious decision to send Apollo 8 to the Moon two months later.

A tense mission

The flight is also remembered for strained relations between crew and ground: all three astronauts developed bad head colds, and Schirra — protective of his sick crew and near retirement — repeatedly disputed flight controllers' requests, in exchanges later dubbed the "Apollo 7 mutiny". None of the three flew in space again, though the mission itself was judged a complete technical success.

Schirra became the only astronaut to fly in Mercury, Gemini and Apollo, and Apollo 7 was the last crewed launch from Cape Canaveral's Launch Complex 34.
