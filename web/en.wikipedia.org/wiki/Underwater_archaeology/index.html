Underwater archaeology is archaeology practised underwater — the study of human material remains that lie submerged in seas, lakes and rivers. It encompasses shipwrecks, sunken settlements and harbours, submerged prehistoric landscapes and aircraft lost at sea. Because waterlogged, oxygen-poor environments can preserve organic materials such as wood, textiles and food, underwater sites often preserve evidence that is lost on land.

History

Salvage of antiquities from the sea began in the Renaissance, but scientific underwater archaeology emerged in the 20th century. The development of the aqualung by Jacques Cousteau in the 1940s made sustained work possible. In 1960 George Bass directed the first excavation of an ancient shipwreck (at Cape Gelidonya, Turkey) carried out to full archaeological standards, and he founded the Institute of Nautical Archaeology. Famous projects include the raising of the Swedish warship Vasa (1961) and the English Mary Rose (1982), and the excavation of the Late Bronze Age Uluburun shipwreck.

Methods

Survey methods include side-scan sonar, magnetometers, sub-bottom profilers and remotely operated vehicles. Excavation underwater adapts terrestrial techniques — grids, measured drawing and photography — to limited visibility and bottom time; photogrammetry now produces precise 3D site plans. Waterlogged finds require lengthy conservation, since wood and iron can disintegrate rapidly on exposure to air.

Challenges and law

Treasure hunting and looting threaten many sites. The UNESCO Convention of 2001 established principles for protecting underwater cultural heritage, favouring preservation in situ and prohibiting commercial exploitation.
