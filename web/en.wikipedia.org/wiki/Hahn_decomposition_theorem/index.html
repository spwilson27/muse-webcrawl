Hahn decomposition theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the Hahn decomposition theorem, named for the Austrian mathematician Hans Hahn, states that for a measurable space (X, Σ) and a signed measure μ defined on the σ-algebra Σ, there exists a Σ-measurable set P and a Σ-measurable set N such that:

  • P ∪ N = X and P ∩ N = ∅;
  • every measurable subset of P has non-negative measure under μ (P is a positive set);
  • every measurable subset of N has non-positive measure under μ (N is a negative set).

Such a pair (P, N) is called a Hahn decomposition of X with respect to μ.

Uniqueness and consequences

The decomposition is essentially unique: if (P′, N′) is another Hahn decomposition, then the symmetric differences between P and P′, and between N and N′, have μ-measure zero. A direct consequence is the Jordan decomposition theorem: every signed measure μ can be written uniquely as the difference of two mutually singular non-negative measures, μ = μ+ − μ−, where μ+(E) = μ(E ∩ P) and μ−(E) = −μ(E ∩ N) are the positive and negative variations of μ. The sum μ+ + μ− is the total variation measure |μ|, fundamental to the theory of integration with respect to signed and complex measures and to the Radon–Nikodym theorem.

History and proof

Hahn proved the theorem in the 1920s as part of the foundations of modern measure theory. The standard proof proceeds by contradiction: if no suitable decomposition existed, one could construct an infinite sequence of sets with ever more negative measure, extracting a subset of negative measure and iterating; careful bookkeeping of suprema of the measure shows that the process assembles the required positive and negative sets. The result underlies the treatment of signed measures in functional analysis, including the polar decomposition of measures and dual-space representations.
