Colonial Brazil (Portuguese: Brasil Colonial) comprises the period from 1500, when the Portuguese first arrived, to 1815, when Brazil was elevated to a kingdom within the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves. Portuguese explorer Pedro Álvares Cabral landed at Porto Seguro in April 1500 and claimed the land for Portugal under the Treaty of Tordesillas.

Early settlement

For the first three decades the Portuguese mainly extracted brazilwood, a source of red dye, trading with Indigenous peoples. From the 1530s, the crown divided the coast into hereditary captaincies and promoted settlement. Sugarcane plantations — engenhos — in the northeast, especially Pernambuco and Bahia, became the colony's economic engine, worked first by Indigenous labour and then, on a vast scale, by enslaved Africans. Brazil received more enslaved Africans than any other destination in the Americas.

Expansion and gold

Bandeirantes from São Paulo explored the interior in search of slaves and minerals, pushing Brazil's effective borders far west of the Tordesillas line. In the 1690s gold was discovered in Minas Gerais, sparking a gold rush, shifting the colony's centre of gravity southward and prompting the transfer of the capital from Salvador to Rio de Janeiro in 1763. The colonial period ended politically in 1808–1815 when the Portuguese court, fleeing Napoleon, moved to Rio and Brazil was raised to a kingdom; independence followed in 1822.
