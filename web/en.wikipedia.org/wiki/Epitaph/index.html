Epitaph

An epitaph (from Ancient Greek ἐπιτάφιος epitáphios "a funeral oration", from ἐπί epí "at, over" and τάφος táphos "tomb") is a short text honoring a deceased person. Strictly speaking, it refers to a text inscribed on a tombstone or plaque, but it may also be used in a figurative sense. Some epitaphs are specified by the person themselves before their death, while others are chosen by those responsible for the burial. An epitaph may be written in prose or in poem form; poets have been known to compose their own epitaphs prior to their death.

History

Epitaphs were widespread in the ancient world: Egyptian tomb inscriptions, Greek epigrams (including the famous epigram attributed to Simonides for the Spartan dead at Thermopylae — "Go tell the Spartans, passerby, that here by Spartan law we lie") and Roman funerary inscriptions record names, deeds and moral reflections. In early modern England the epitaph became a flourishing literary genre, and collections of churchyard epitaphs were popular in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Famous epitaphs

Noted examples include W. B. Yeats's "Cast a cold eye / On life, on death. / Horseman, pass by!"; the epitaph of Mel Blanc, "That's all Folks"; Benjamin Franklin's printer metaphor; and the communal epitaph at the Kohima war cemetery: "When you go home, tell them of us and say, for your tomorrow, we gave our today." Writers such as Alexander Pope composed celebrated epitaphs for others, including the couplet for Isaac Newton.
