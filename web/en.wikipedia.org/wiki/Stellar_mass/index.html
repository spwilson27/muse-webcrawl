Stellar mass

Stellar mass is a phrase used by astronomers to describe the mass of a star. It is usually enumerated in terms of the Sun's mass as a proportion or fraction of one solar mass (M☉). Stellar mass is the single most important determinant of a star's evolution, lifetime, luminosity and ultimate fate.

Range

Stars range from about 0.075 M☉ — the minimum for sustained hydrogen fusion — to well over 100 M☉ for the most massive known stars, such as R136a1 in the Large Magellanic Cloud. Objects below the fusion limit are brown dwarfs.

Measurement and effects

The most direct measurements come from binary star systems, where Kepler's laws applied to the observed orbits yield the component masses. Mass correlates strongly with luminosity (the mass–luminosity relation): more massive stars are far brighter and live much shorter lives, exhausting their fuel quickly, while low-mass red dwarfs may shine for trillions of years. A star's mass also decides whether it ends as a white dwarf, neutron star or black hole.
