Panopticon

Bentham's panopticon: plan, elevation and section

The panopticon is a design of institutional building conceived by the English philosopher and social theorist Jeremy Bentham in the late 18th century. It consists of a ring of cells arranged around a central inspection tower: a single watchman in the tower can observe every inmate, while the inmates — backlit and separated — cannot tell whether they are being watched at any moment, and so must assume constant surveillance.

Design and history

Bentham developed the idea from his brother Samuel's designs for a factory in Russia, publishing it in letters of 1787 and the 1791 work Panopticon; or, the Inspection-House. He proposed it for prisons, schools, hospitals and workhouses. Bentham lobbied for years to build a panopticon penitentiary in London; the project was eventually abandoned and Millbank Prison built instead. A handful of near-panoptic prisons were built elsewhere, including the Dutch koepelgevangenis round prisons, Cuba's Presidio Modelo, and the roundhouse cellblocks of Stateville Correctional Center in Illinois.

Philosophical influence

In Discipline and Punish (1975), Michel Foucault made the panopticon the emblem of modern "disciplinary society": surveillance internalised so that power operates automatically, whether or not a guard is present. The concept has since been extended widely — to CCTV, workplace monitoring and digital surveillance — inspiring terms such as "panopticism" and counter-concepts like the synopticon and sousveillance.
