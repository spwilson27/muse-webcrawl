A desert pavement, also called a reg (in the western Sahara), serir (eastern Sahara), gibber (in Australia), or sai (in central Asia), is a desert surface covered with closely packed, interlocking angular or rounded rock fragments of pebble and cobble size. They typically top alluvial fans. Desert varnish collects on the exposed surface rocks over time.

Formation

Several theories explain pavement formation. The traditional view held that wind and occasional rain remove fine material (deflation and sheetwash), leaving the coarse fraction behind as a lag deposit. A later theory, developed from studies in the Mojave Desert, proposes that the stones start on the surface while wind-blown dust accumulates beneath them; the growing dust layer, affected by expansion and contraction as clays wet and dry, gradually lifts the stones so the pavement effectively rides up on accumulating silt, remaining a single stone thick for tens of thousands of years.

Characteristics

Mature pavements are remarkably flat and stable, protect the fine soil beneath from erosion, and develop dark coatings of desert varnish (manganese and iron oxides). Because they can remain undisturbed for millennia, pavements preserve archaeological material and are used to date surfaces. They are fragile: vehicle tracks and footprints can persist for decades, a concern for desert ecology and land management.
