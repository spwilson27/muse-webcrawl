Intelligence quotient


Distribution of IQ scores, normalised to a mean of 100

An intelligence quotient (IQ) is a total score derived from a set of standardised tests or subtests designed to assess human intelligence. The abbreviation "IQ" was coined by the psychologist William Stern for the German term Intelligenzquotient, his proposed method of scoring early intelligence tests. Originally, IQ was computed as the ratio of mental age to chronological age, multiplied by 100.

Modern IQ tests report a "deviation IQ": raw scores are converted to a scale on which the population mean is 100 and the standard deviation is set at 15 points (on the Wechsler scales) or 16 (Stanford–Binet). Scores follow an approximately normal distribution, so that about 68 per cent of the population score between 85 and 115, and about 95 per cent between 70 and 130.

History

The first practical intelligence test was developed by Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon in France in 1905, to identify schoolchildren needing additional help. Lewis Terman at Stanford University revised the test as the Stanford–Binet scale in 1916, popularising the IQ concept in the United States. During the First World War, the US Army used the Army Alpha and Beta tests to assess recruits. David Wechsler introduced his scales in 1939, separating verbal and performance subscales and using the deviation IQ.

Major test batteries

  • Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) and the corresponding children's scale (WISC)
  • Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales
  • Raven's Progressive Matrices, a non-verbal test of abstract reasoning
  • Cattell Culture Fair tests

Validity and correlates

IQ scores are among the most reliable measures in psychology, with high test–retest reliability. Scores correlate moderately with school achievement, job performance, income and various health outcomes, and they reflect the general intelligence factor, g, extracted from the positive correlations among cognitive tests. Population average scores rose substantially over the 20th century — the Flynn effect — requiring periodic re-norming of tests.

Controversies

IQ testing has been controversial since its beginnings. Disputes concern what the tests measure, cultural and linguistic bias in test items, the heritability of intelligence and group differences in average scores, and the historical misuse of testing in eugenics programmes and immigration restriction. Most mainstream researchers hold that the tests measure cognitive abilities relevant to scholastic and occupational performance, while cautioning that intelligence is broader than what any single score captures.

High-IQ societies

Societies such as Mensa International (founded 1946) admit members scoring in the top 2 per cent on an approved test (roughly IQ 130 or above on Wechsler-type scales).
