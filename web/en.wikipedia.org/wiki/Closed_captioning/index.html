Closed captioning

Closed captioning (CC) and subtitling are both processes of displaying text on a television, video screen, or other visual display to provide additional or interpretive information. Both are typically used as a transcription of the audio portion of a program as it occurs (either verbatim or in edited form), sometimes including descriptions of non-speech elements. Other aspects of CC include transmission of the text (in encoded form) and a decoder in the receiver.

Terminology

The term closed indicates that the captions are not visible until activated by the viewer, usually via a remote control or menu option; this contrasts with open captions, which are burned into the video and always visible. In North America, closed captions primarily transcribe dialogue and sound effects for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, whereas the term "subtitles" often refers to translations of dialogue for hearing audiences.

History

Captioned television was first demonstrated in the United States in 1971, and regular closed captioning of broadcasts began in 1980 with decoders developed through the National Captioning Institute. The Television Decoder Circuitry Act of 1990 required televisions sold in the U.S. to include built-in caption decoders. The 21st Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act of 2010 extended captioning requirements to internet-delivered video. For North American analog television, captions were carried in line 21 of the vertical blanking interval under the EIA-608 standard; digital television uses the CTA-708 standard.
