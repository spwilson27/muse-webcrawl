Brigandage

Brigandage is the practice of robbery and plunder by armed bands — brigands — typically operating in remote or mountainous regions beyond effective state control. It has occurred throughout history, from the Roman world to nineteenth-century southern Italy, the Balkans, China, and Latin America.

History

Brigands often preyed on travellers, merchants, and villages. Historians, notably Eric Hobsbawm, have debated the figure of the "social bandit" — an outlaw regarded by peasants as a hero resisting unjust authority, as in legends of Robin Hood or the Italian brigand Carmine Crocco.

Suppression

States have repeatedly launched military campaigns against brigandage, most famously in southern Italy after unification in the 1860s, where the phenomenon had political as well as criminal dimensions.
