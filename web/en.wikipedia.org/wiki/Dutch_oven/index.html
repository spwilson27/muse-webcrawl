Dutch oven

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A cast-iron Dutch oven with a tight-fitting lid]

A Dutch oven is a thick-walled cooking pot with a tight-fitting lid, traditionally made of cast iron (often enamel-coated in modern versions), used for braising, stewing, roasting, frying, soup-making and even baking bread. Its heavy construction distributes heat evenly and its lid — sometimes designed to hold coals on top — turns the pot into a miniature oven. The name is generally traced to the Dutch method of casting iron in sand moulds, commercialised by English ironfounders in the early 18th century.

History

Englishman Abraham Darby patented a sand-casting method in 1707 after observing Dutch founders, and "Dutch oven" became the English name for the cast-iron pot. Dutch ovens travelled with American pioneers — Lewis and Clark carried them, and the camp version with three legs and a flanged lid (for stacking coals above and below) became an icon of frontier cooking; Utah, whose Mormon pioneers relied on them, made the Dutch oven its official state cooking pot. Brands such as Le Creuset (enamelled, from 1925) and Staub made the French version a prestige kitchen staple.

Types and use

Kitchen Dutch ovens are flat-bottomed and enamelled or bare iron; camp ovens are legged, bare cast iron. The pot's thermal mass makes it ideal for long, slow braises and for no-knead and sourdough breads, where the lidded pot traps steam for a crisp crust. Capacities of 4–7 litres suit most households.
