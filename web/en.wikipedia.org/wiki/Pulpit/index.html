Pulpit

A pulpit is a raised platform or lectern in a church from which the clergy preach and read the Scriptures. The word derives from the Latin pulpitum, meaning a scaffold or platform. Positioned typically to the side of the chancel or in the nave, the pulpit elevates the preacher above the congregation so that the sermon can be heard throughout the building; in many designs a flat canopy called a tester or sounding board is suspended above it to reflect the speaker's voice.

History

The pulpit developed from the ambo of the early church, an elevated reading desk used for the proclamation of the Gospel. In the medieval period, pulpits became increasingly elaborate: stone pulpits projecting from pillars, richly carved with figures of the evangelists and doctors of the Church, are features of many Gothic cathedrals. Famous examples include the pulpits of Pisa Baptistery (by Nicola Pisano, 1260) and Siena Cathedral (also by the Pisano workshop). Outdoor pulpits were built against church walls and at preaching crosses for addressing crowds.

After the Reformation, the sermon became the centre of Protestant worship, and the pulpit often became the most prominent furnishing of the church, placed centrally and rising in tiers — in some English churches, three-decker pulpits combined the reading desk, clerk's desk and pulpit in one structure. Catholic churches of the Baroque era likewise produced monumental carved pulpits, such as those in St. Peter's Church in Leuven and Brussels Cathedral. In many modern churches the pulpit has been simplified or replaced by a plain lectern or ambo.

Design

A traditional pulpit consists of a drum or tub-like body, reached by a flight of stairs, resting on a pillar or brackets against a wall or pier. It is often decorated with carving, inlay or painted panels depicting biblical scenes. The sounding board (tester), sometimes topped by a dove representing the Holy Spirit, served an acoustic purpose before electronic amplification. In nautical slang, a raised guard rail structure at the bow of a yacht is also called a pulpit.

Figurative use

By extension, "the pulpit" is used metonymically for the preaching ministry and the clergy generally, and expressions such as "bully pulpit" — coined by U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt — describe a prominent public position from which a speaker can advocate an agenda.
