Econometrics is the application of statistical and mathematical methods to economic data in order to test economic theories, estimate relationships, evaluate policies and forecast economic trends. Ragnar Frisch, who coined the term and co-founded the Econometric Society in 1930, defined it as the unification of economic theory, mathematics and statistics. Unlike much of experimental science, econometrics must usually work with observational data not generated by controlled experiments, so a central concern of the discipline is credible causal inference.

Core methods

The basic tool is the linear regression model, typically estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS): an outcome variable is related to explanatory variables plus an error term capturing unobserved influences. Reliable causal interpretation requires that the error term be uncorrelated with the regressors — an assumption threatened by omitted variables, simultaneity (reverse causation) and measurement error. Econometricians respond with a toolkit including:

  • Instrumental variables and two-stage least squares, using external variation that affects the outcome only through the variable of interest.
  • Panel data methods (fixed and random effects) that follow the same individuals, firms or regions over time.
  • Time-series econometrics — stationarity, cointegration, autoregression (ARIMA) and vector autoregressions (VARs) — used for macroeconomic forecasting and analysis.
  • Quasi-experimental designs: difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, and randomised controlled trials, emphasised in the modern "credibility revolution" in applied microeconomics.

History

Early work grew from demand estimation and business-cycle research in the 1920s–30s (Tinbergen's League of Nations models; the Cowles Commission's simultaneous-equation systems). Trygve Haavelmo's probabilistic foundations (1944) placed econometric models within modern statistics. Lawrence Klein's macroeconometric models dominated post-war forecasting until the Lucas critique (1976) argued that estimated relationships would shift when policy changed, pushing macroeconomics toward microfounded models. From the 1980s, applied work shifted toward design-based identification of treatment effects, recognised by the 2021 Nobel Memorial Prize to David Card, Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens, while earlier prizes honoured Frisch and Tinbergen (1969), Klein (1980), Haavelmo (1989), James Heckman and Daniel McFadden (2000), and Robert Engle and Clive Granger (2003).

Applications and criticism

Econometrics underpins central-bank forecasting, programme evaluation, labour and health economics, industrial organisation and finance. Criticisms include sensitivity of results to specification choices ("data mining"), over-reliance on statistical significance, and the replication problems common across the social sciences — issues the field increasingly addresses through pre-registration and open data.
