The price of anarchy is a concept in game theory and theoretical computer science that measures how much the efficiency of a system degrades when agents act selfishly rather than cooperating or following a central plan. Formally, it is the ratio between the welfare of an optimal coordinated outcome and the welfare of the worst Nash equilibrium.

History and definition

The term was introduced by Christos Papadimitriou and popularised in work by Elias Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou (1999) on selfish task scheduling, and independently developed in research on selfish routing by Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos. If the price of anarchy is 1, selfish behaviour costs nothing; larger values quantify the "anarchy" penalty.

Examples

In the classic Pigou example of traffic flow over two parallel routes, selfish routing produces a price of anarchy of 4/3. Braess's paradox — in which adding a road makes everyone's journey slower — illustrates how equilibrium flow can be worse than optimal flow. For the prisoner's dilemma, the price of anarchy can be arbitrarily large, since mutual defection is the unique equilibrium. Smoothness arguments, developed by Roughgarden, provide a general framework for bounding the price of anarchy across many classes of games, including congestion games and auctions; for first-price auctions and simultaneous item auctions the price of anarchy is a small constant.

Related concepts

The price of stability compares the optimum with the best equilibrium, measuring the cost of stability rather than anarchy. Both concepts are central to algorithmic game theory and the design of systems — networks, markets, auctions — that must function with self-interested participants.
