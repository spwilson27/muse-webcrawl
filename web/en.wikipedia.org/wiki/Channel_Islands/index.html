Channel Islands

The Channel Islands (Norman: Îles d'la Manche; French: îles Anglo-Normandes) are an archipelago in the English Channel, off the French coast of Normandy. They are divided into two Crown Dependencies: the Bailiwick of Jersey and the Bailiwick of Guernsey. The islands are not part of the United Kingdom, but the UK is responsible for their defence and international representation.

Geography

The inhabited islands are Jersey, Guernsey, Alderney, Sark and Herm, together with smaller islets such as Jethou, Brecqhou and Lihou. Jersey is the largest, at about 119 square kilometres. The islands lie close to the Cotentin Peninsula; Jersey is only about 22 kilometres from France but roughly 137 kilometres from the English coast.

History

The islands were historically part of the Duchy of Normandy, whose duke William became King of England in 1066. When mainland Normandy was lost to France in 1204, the Channel Islands remained loyal to the English Crown, and they have been self-governing dependencies of the Crown ever since. During the Second World War they were the only British soil occupied by German forces, from 1940 until liberation on 9 May 1945.

Politics and economy

Each bailiwick has its own parliament (the States), legal system rooted in Norman customary law, and tax regime. The islands are major offshore financial centres, and Jersey and Guernsey issue their own pound-sterling notes and coins. Neither bailiwick was ever a member of the European Union, though they were in its customs territory for goods.

Culture

Norman languages such as Jèrriais and Guernésiais survive as heritage languages. Victor Hugo lived in exile in Jersey and then Guernsey, where he wrote Les Misérables. The islands are also known for their dairy cattle breeds, the Jersey and the Guernsey.
