Cell-free fetal DNA

Cell-free fetal DNA (cffDNA) is fetal DNA that circulates freely in the maternal bloodstream during pregnancy. It originates mainly from placental trophoblast cells and typically makes up about 10–20% of the total cell-free DNA in maternal plasma by the end of the first trimester. Its discovery in maternal blood was reported by Dennis Lo and colleagues in 1997.

Testing

Analysis of cffDNA from a maternal blood sample forms the basis of non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT). Sequencing and counting chromosome-derived fragments can screen with high accuracy for trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), trisomy 18 and trisomy 13, and can determine fetal sex and some single-gene conditions. Because it is a screening test, positive results are usually confirmed by diagnostic procedures such as amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling.

Characteristics

Fetal fragments are shorter than maternal ones (around 143 versus 166 base pairs) and are cleared from the mother's circulation within hours after delivery, so tests reflect the current pregnancy only.
