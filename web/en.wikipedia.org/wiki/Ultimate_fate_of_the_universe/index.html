Ultimate fate of the universe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ultimate fate of the universe is a topic in physical cosmology, whose theoretical restrictions are limited by being able to describe the long-term future of an expanding universe. Once it was understood that the universe began with a rapid expansion known as the Big Bang, most cosmologists turned their attention to determining the fate of the universe.

The curvature of space, the amount of matter and radiation, and — decisively since the 1998 discovery that the expansion is accelerating — the nature of dark energy determine which scenario applies. In the standard ΛCDM model, with a cosmological constant driving acceleration, the universe expands forever and cools toward a state of maximum entropy.

Scenarios

Big Freeze (heat death)

In the most widely accepted scenario, expansion continues indefinitely. Star formation ceases within roughly 100 trillion years; stellar remnants cool, galaxies disperse their stars, supermassive black holes evaporate by Hawking radiation over timescales up to 10100 years, and the universe approaches thermodynamic equilibrium — a cold, dark, nearly empty state called heat death.

Big Rip

If dark energy is phantom energy, whose density increases with time, its repulsion could eventually tear apart galaxies, stars, atoms and finally spacetime itself in a Big Rip, at a finite time in the future (one estimate placed it ~22 billion years from now, though such estimates are strongly model-dependent).

Big Crunch and Big Bounce

If the density of the universe were great enough, or dark energy decayed into an attractive form, expansion could reverse into a Big Crunch, possibly followed by a new expansion in a cyclic or Big Bounce model.

Vacuum decay

If the Higgs field occupies a metastable false vacuum, a quantum tunnelling event could nucleate a bubble of true vacuum expanding at nearly the speed of light, rewriting the laws of physics within it without warning.

Observational basis

Measurements of the cosmic microwave background, type Ia supernovae and baryon acoustic oscillations indicate a spatially flat universe in which dark energy behaves consistently with a cosmological constant, favouring the Big Freeze. In 2024–2025, results from the DESI survey hinted — but did not establish — that dark energy might evolve with time, which would reopen other possibilities.
