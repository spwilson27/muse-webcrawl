PDP-1

The PDP-1 (Programmed Data Processor-1) is the first computer in Digital Equipment Corporation's PDP series and was first produced in 1959. It is famous for being the computer most important in the creation of hacker culture at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Bolt, Beranek and Newman, and elsewhere. The PDP-1 is the original hardware for the first video game on a minicomputer, Steve Russell's Spacewar! of 1962.

History

The design of the PDP-1 was based on the pioneering TX-0 and TX-2 computers at MIT's Lincoln Laboratory. Benjamin Gurley, who had worked on those machines, was the PDP-1's chief designer. DEC sold the first PDP-1 to Bolt, Beranek and Newman in November 1960 for US$120,000. In total, about 53 PDP-1s were sold before production ended in 1969.

Description

The PDP-1 used an 18-bit word length and had 4,096 words of magnetic core memory as standard, expandable to 65,536 words. Its instruction cycle time was 5 microseconds. The machine used punched paper tape as its primary storage medium. Optional peripherals included a precision CRT display (the Type 30), light pens, and magnetic tape and drum storage.

Legacy

The PDP-1 hosted several landmarks of early computing: Spacewar!, one of the first video games; the first word processor for a general-purpose computer (Expensive Typewriter); early computer music programs by Peter Samson; and the first computer chess program. A restored PDP-1 is demonstrated regularly at the Computer History Museum in Mountain View, California.

The PDP-1's successor in the PDP line was followed by machines such as the PDP-10, which became a mainstay of timesharing systems.
