Ethnic federalism

Ethnic federalism, or multi-ethnic federalism, is a form of federal system in which the federated regional or state units are defined by ethnicity. The central government and the constituent units are both federations of ethnic groups, in the sense that ethnic pluralism is built into the constitutional structure rather than treated as a private matter. Ethnic federal arrangements are intended to accommodate the demands of distinct peoples within a single state, particularly in post-colonial and post-imperial settings marked by ethnic conflict.

Characteristics

In an ethnic federation, internal boundaries are drawn so that each major ethnic group forms a majority in at least one constituent unit, giving it a territorial homeland in which it can exercise a measure of self-government, conduct education in its own language, and control local affairs. Such systems are often contrasted with territorial federations (such as the United States or Germany), whose units are not designed to match ethnic settlement patterns, and with consociational arrangements that share power without territorial division.

Examples

The clearest modern example is Ethiopia, whose 1995 constitution organises the federation into regions named for their titular nationalities and grants an explicit right of secession. The former Soviet Union, Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia were ethnofederal states whose constituent republics were homelands of titular nations; all three broke apart along those internal lines when the centre weakened. Elements of ethnic federalism also appear in Bosnia and Herzegovina (with its two entities and cantons), in India's linguistic states reorganisation, and in proposals for Iraq, Nepal and Myanmar.

Criticism and debate

Political scientists debate whether ethnic federalism contains or manufactures conflict. Critics argue that it hardens ethnic categories, creates minorities within each homeland, equips regional elites with the institutional resources of statehood, and thereby raises the risk of secession and inter-communal violence. Defenders reply that, where groups are territorially concentrated and distrust the centre, recognising ethnic homelands may be the only bargain capable of holding a diverse state together, and that failures often reflect authoritarian centre–region relations rather than the federal design itself.
