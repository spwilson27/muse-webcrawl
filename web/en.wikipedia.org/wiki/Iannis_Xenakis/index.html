Iannis Xenakis (29 May 1922 – 4 February 2001) was a Romanian-born Greek-French avant-garde composer, music theorist, architect, performance director, and engineer. After 1947 he fled Greece for Paris, where he became a French citizen. He is considered one of the most important post-war avant-garde composers.

Xenakis pioneered the use of mathematical models in music — probability theory, game theory, group theory, and set theory — in a practice he named stochastic music. He also pioneered computer composition and designed the UPIC system, which converts drawn shapes into sound. Influential works include Metastaseis (1953–54) for orchestra, the percussion works Psappha (1975) and Pléïades (1979), and the large-scale multimedia "Polytopes."

Architecture

In Paris Xenakis worked for twelve years in the studio of Le Corbusier, contributing to projects including the Philips Pavilion for Expo 58 in Brussels, whose hyperbolic paraboloid forms grew from the same ruled-line geometry as the glissandi of Metastaseis; Edgard Varèse's Poème électronique was composed for the pavilion.

Life

Born in Brăila, Romania, to Greek parents, Xenakis studied engineering in Athens and fought in the Greek resistance during the Second World War, suffering a serious facial wound. Sentenced to death in absentia by the Greek government, he lived in exile in France for decades before being able to return.
