Stigler's law of eponymy

Stigler's law of eponymy, proposed by the University of Chicago statistics professor Stephen Stigler in his 1980 paper "Stigler's Law of Eponymy", states that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer. Stigler himself attributed the observation to the sociologist Robert K. Merton — an act deliberately making the law an example of itself.

Background

The law belongs to a family of remarks about the misallocation of scientific credit. Merton had described the "Matthew effect", in which credit accrues to already-famous scientists, and earlier writers such as Alfred North Whitehead had remarked that "everything of importance has been said before by somebody who did not discover it". Related formulations include Boyer's law in the history of mathematics and the folk observation that discoveries are usually named after the person who popularizes or publishes them most effectively rather than the first to find them.

Examples

  • Halley's Comet was recorded as early as 240 BC; Edmond Halley merely predicted its return.
  • The Gaussian distribution was first described by Abraham de Moivre.
  • Stokes' theorem was communicated to George Stokes by Lord Kelvin.
  • Hubble's law was derived and published earlier by Georges Lemaître.
  • The Pythagorean theorem was known to Babylonian, Indian, and Chinese mathematicians centuries before Pythagoras.
  • Benford's law was published earlier by Simon Newcomb.

Stigler's law is not a falsifiable scientific law but a humorous sociological generalization; it focuses attention on how eponyms arise through communication, institutional authority, and the dynamics of citation rather than priority alone.
