Depictions of nudity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Depictions of nudity include all representations of the unclothed human body in visual art, photography, film and other media. Nudity has been a central subject of art since prehistory: the earliest known representations of the human figure, such as the Venus figurines of the Upper Palaeolithic, are nude. Attitudes toward the depiction of nudity vary widely across cultures and periods, from celebration to censorship.

History

In ancient Greek art, heroic nudity glorified the idealised male body in sculpture and vase painting; Roman art largely adopted Greek conventions. With the spread of Christianity, depictions of nudity in early medieval Europe became largely restricted to biblical scenes such as Adam and Eve, the Crucifixion and the Last Judgment. The Renaissance saw a revival of the classical nude in the work of artists such as Botticelli and Michelangelo, legitimised by mythological subjects. The female nude became a dominant genre in European painting — the Venus tradition studied by art historians such as Kenneth Clark in The Nude (1956).

Photography, film and modern media

The invention of photography made realistic depictions of nudity widely reproducible, leading to both artistic movements and legal battles over obscenity. In cinema, nudity was restricted under codes such as the Hays Code in Hollywood before liberalisation in the 1960s and 1970s; ratings systems continue to treat nudity as a key criterion. Depictions in advertising, fashion, and on the internet remain frequent subjects of public debate, community standards and content moderation policies.

Contexts and interpretation

Art historians and critics distinguish depictions of nudity by purpose and context — fine art, erotic art, pornography, medical and scientific illustration, protest and performance art — while scholars have debated the "male gaze" and the objectification of the body, as in John Berger's Ways of Seeing (1972). Non-erotic, social nudity (naturism) also generates its own visual culture.
