Belém Tower

Belém Tower (Portuguese: Torre de Belém), officially the Tower of Saint Vincent, is a 16th-century fortification located in the parish of Belém in Lisbon, Portugal. Commissioned by King John II and completed under Manuel I around 1519, it served as a fortress defending the entrance to Lisbon's harbour on the Tagus River and as a ceremonial gateway from which Portuguese explorers departed and arrived during the Age of Discoveries.

Architecture

Built in limestone in the Manueline style, the tower combines a medieval keep with a hexagonal bastion. Its ornamentation includes armillary spheres, crosses of the Order of Christ, twisted rope mouldings, and a famous carved rhinoceros — one of the earliest depictions of the animal in European art. The architect Francisco de Arruda incorporated Moorish and Venetian influences from his work in North Africa.

History

The tower originally stood on an island in the Tagus; silting of the river has since joined it to the bank. Over the centuries it served as a fortress, a customs post, a telegraph station and a lighthouse. In 1983 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List together with the nearby Jerónimos Monastery, and it has become one of the most recognized symbols of Lisbon and of Portugal's maritime age.
