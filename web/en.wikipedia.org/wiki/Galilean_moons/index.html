The Galilean moons, or Galilean satellites, are the four largest moons of Jupiter: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. They are among the largest objects in the Solar System outside the Sun and the eight planets, with radii larger than any dwarf planet. They were first seen by Galileo Galilei in January 1610 and recognized by him as orbiting Jupiter — the first clear evidence of celestial bodies orbiting something other than Earth, and a decisive blow to the geocentric model of the universe.

The moons

Io is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System, its silicate surface dotted with hundreds of sulfurous volcanoes driven by tidal flexing. Europa has a smooth icy crust almost certainly hiding a global salty ocean, making it a prime target in the search for extraterrestrial life. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System — bigger than Mercury — and the only moon known to generate its own magnetic field; it too likely holds a subsurface ocean. Callisto, the outermost, is the most heavily cratered and geologically ancient of the four.

Orbital resonance

Io, Europa and Ganymede are locked in a 4:2:1 mean-motion resonance known as the Laplace resonance: for every orbit of Ganymede, Europa completes two and Io four. The resonance maintains the moons' orbital eccentricities and drives the intense tidal heating that powers Io's volcanoes and Europa's ocean.

Exploration

The moons have been visited by Pioneer 10 and 11, Voyagers 1 and 2, Galileo, Cassini, New Horizons and Juno. The European Space Agency's JUICE spacecraft, launched in 2023, will orbit Ganymede in the 2030s, while NASA's Europa Clipper, launched in 2024, will study Europa in detail.
