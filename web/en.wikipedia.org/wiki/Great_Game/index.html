The Great Game

Central Asia in the nineteenth century, between British India and the Russian Empire.

The Great Game was the political and diplomatic confrontation between the British Empire and the Russian Empire over Afghanistan and neighbouring territories in Central and South Asia during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The term was popularised by Rudyard Kipling in his novel Kim (1901) and is usually attributed to the British intelligence officer Arthur Conolly, executed at Bukhara in 1842.

Origins

As Russia expanded southward through the Kazakh steppe and Britain consolidated its rule in India, the two empires moved towards one another across Central Asia. British fears centred on the security of India: that a European power might advance through Persia or Afghanistan and threaten the subcontinent, as Napoleon and Tsar Paul I had once planned in 1801. Russian motives included frontier security, trade and the prestige of expansion; annexations followed at Tashkent (1865), Samarkand (1868) and Khiva (1873), and the khanates of Bukhara and Kokand became vassals or were absorbed.

Afghanistan and the Anglo-Afghan wars

Afghanistan, ruled from Kabul, became the critical buffer state. British attempts to control its foreign policy led to the disastrous First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–42), in which an occupying army was annihilated in retreat from Kabul, and to the Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–80), after which Britain recognised Abdur Rahman Khan as amir while retaining control of Afghan foreign relations under the Treaty of Gandamak system. Players of the Game — officers and explorers such as Alexander Burnes, William Moorcroft and the pundits trained by the Great Trigonometrical Survey — mapped the region, often in disguise and at mortal risk.

The Pamirs and the settlement of frontiers

In the 1880s and 1890s the rivalry shifted to the Pamir mountains and the northern borders of Kashmir and Afghanistan, following the Panjdeh incident of 1885, when Russian and Afghan forces clashed and war seemed near. Joint commissions delimited the Afghan–Russian boundary along the Amu Darya (Oxus), and the Durand Line (1893) fixed the border between Afghanistan and British India. The narrow Wakhan Corridor was assigned to Afghanistan as a buffer so that British and Russian territory never touched.

End of the Game

The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 settled the rivalry: Persia was divided into spheres of influence, Afghanistan was recognised as a British sphere, and both powers disclaimed designs on Tibet. The entente was directed increasingly at Germany, and the strategic contest moved into a new phase culminating in the First World War. The term "Great Game" has since been applied retrospectively — and sometimes controversially — to later great-power competition in Central Asia, including the Cold War and the post-2001 involvement in Afghanistan.

In popular culture

Kipling's Kim remains the classic literary portrait of the Game. Peter Hopkirk's books, beginning with The Great Game: On Secret Service in High Asia (1990), revived popular interest in the explorers and intelligence officers involved.
