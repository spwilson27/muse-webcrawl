Agraphia

Agraphia
SpecialtyNeurology, neuropsychology
SymptomsLoss of ability to write
CausesStroke, brain injury, dementia

Agraphia is an acquired neurological disorder causing the loss of the ability to communicate through writing, typically resulting from brain damage. It ranges from partial impairment of spelling or letter formation to a complete inability to write, and frequently co-occurs with aphasia, alexia (loss of reading), or apraxia, although it can also occur in isolation.

Types

Central agraphias involve impaired linguistic processing of writing: surface agraphia (difficulty spelling irregular words), phonological agraphia (difficulty spelling unfamiliar words and nonwords), deep agraphia, and lexical agraphia. Peripheral agraphias involve the motor and spatial execution of writing, including apraxic agraphia, micrographia (associated with Parkinson's disease), and spatial agraphia, which may accompany right-hemisphere damage and hemispatial neglect.

Causes

Agraphia is most often caused by stroke affecting the language-dominant (usually left) hemisphere, including regions such as the angular gyrus and the posterior middle frontal gyrus ("Exner's area"). Other causes include traumatic brain injury, tumors, infections, and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and primary progressive aphasia.

Diagnosis and treatment

Assessment involves tasks such as copying, writing to dictation, spontaneous writing, and spelling aloud. Treatment focuses on rehabilitation of the impaired process — for example, retraining letter formation in apraxic agraphia or using compensatory technology such as word processors for severe cases.
