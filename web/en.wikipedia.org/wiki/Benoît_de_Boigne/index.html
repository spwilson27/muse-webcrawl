Benoît Leborgne, comte de Boigne (8 March 1751 – 21 June 1830), usually known in English as Benoît de Boigne, was a Savoyard military adventurer who made his fortune in India in the service of the Maratha ruler Mahadji Scindia of Gwalior. He is credited with creating the first modern, European-drilled regular brigades of a Maratha army, which for a time made Scindia the dominant power in northern India.

Early life and service

Born in Chambéry, then part of the Kingdom of Sardinia, de Boigne received a military education and served successively in the French army, in a Greek regiment in Russian service during the war against the Ottoman Empire, and then in India under the East India Company. Unsuccessful in his attempt to find backing in Calcutta and Lucknow for an overland expedition to explore a new trade route, he entered Maratha service in 1784.

Service with Scindia

Mahadji Scindia charged de Boigne with raising two battalions of infantry trained on European lines. His force grew into brigades of around 10,000 men each, mixing sepoys, Afghan and Rajput cavalry, and a powerful artillery train cast and drilled to European standards. With this army de Boigne won decisive victories for Scindia, including at Lalsot (1787), Patan (1790) and Merta (1790), defeating the Rajput states and the Mughal commander Ismail Beg and securing Maratha supremacy between the Yamuna and Rajasthan.

De Boigne administered the rich Doab territories assigned for the upkeep of his brigades with notable efficiency. Privately he also traded profitably in indigo and other goods. His position at the Gwalior court, where he lived in considerable state, made him one of the most powerful Europeans in India.

Return to Europe and benefactions

Ill health led de Boigne to leave India in 1796, carrying a vast fortune. After years in England he settled back in Chambéry, which he transformed with charitable and civic benefactions: he funded streets, a theatre, poor relief and public works, and the elephant fountain (the "Quatre sans cul") erected in the town in 1838 commemorates his Indian career. Napoleon granted him the Legion of Honour, and the restored Sardinian monarchy made him a count. He died in Chambéry in 1830.

Legacy

De Boigne is often ranked with the most successful of the European military entrepreneurs in eighteenth-century India. The brigades he created passed on his retirement to his son-in-law Pierre Cuillier-Perron, whose defeat by the British in the Second Anglo-Maratha War ended the system de Boigne had built.
