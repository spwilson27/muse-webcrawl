Lucretia (died c. 510 BC) was a noblewoman of ancient Rome whose rape by Sextus Tarquinius, son of the last Roman king, and her subsequent suicide were, in the traditional account, the immediate cause of the overthrow of the Roman monarchy and the founding of the Roman Republic.

The traditional account

As told by Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Lucretia was the wife of Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus, famed for her beauty and exemplary virtue. During a drinking bout at the siege of Ardea, the king's sons and Collatinus wagered on their wives' virtue and rode to Rome, where Lucretia alone was found dutifully spinning late into the night. Inflamed by her, Sextus returned days later and raped her at knifepoint, threatening to frame her with the corpse of a slave. The next day Lucretia summoned her father and husband, demanded vengeance, and — though they protested her innocence — stabbed herself, declaring that no unchaste woman should live by her example.

Consequences

Her kinsman Lucius Junius Brutus drew the dagger from her body, swore vengeance, and led the uprising that expelled the Tarquins in 509 BC. Lucretia's body was carried into the forum and displayed to rouse the people.

Legacy

The story became a central Roman exemplum of chastity and tyranny, retold by Ovid, Augustine (who questioned her moral logic in The City of God), Chaucer, and Shakespeare, whose narrative poem The Rape of Lucrece (1594) is devoted to it. Painters from Titian to Rembrandt depicted her suicide, and Benjamin Britten's opera The Rape of Lucretia premiered in 1946.
