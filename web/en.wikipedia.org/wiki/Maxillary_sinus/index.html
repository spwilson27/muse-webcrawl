The maxillary sinus is the largest of the paranasal sinuses, a pyramidal air-filled cavity within the body of the maxilla (upper jaw) on each side of the face, beneath the orbit and lateral to the nasal cavity. First described in detail by the English anatomist Nathaniel Highmore in the 17th century, it is also known as the antrum of Highmore.

Anatomy and development

The maxillary sinus is present at birth as a small cavity and expands throughout childhood as the face grows and the permanent teeth erupt, reaching an average volume of about 15 millilitres in adults. Its floor lies close to the roots of the upper premolar and molar teeth, so dental infections can spread into the sinus. It drains through the maxillary ostium, located high on its medial wall, into the hiatus semilunaris of the middle meatus of the nose — a position that makes drainage against gravity inefficient and contributes to the frequency of maxillary sinusitis.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the maxillary sinus is the most common form of sinusitis, presenting with facial pressure, congestion, and toothache-like pain in the upper jaw. The close dental relationship is also important in oral surgery: extraction of upper molars can create an oroantral communication, and dental implant placement in the posterior maxilla may require a sinus lift procedure to augment bone. Tumours and cysts of the sinus may remain silent until large because of the cavity's size.
