Induced radioactivity

Also called: Artificial radioactivity

Discovered: 1934, by Irène and Frédéric Joliot-Curie

Induced radioactivity, also called artificial radioactivity, is radioactivity produced in a previously stable material when it is exposed to radiation, typically neutrons or other particles, which transmute its nuclei into unstable isotopes.

Discovery

In 1934, Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot-Curie showed that aluminium bombarded with alpha particles became radioactive phosphorus-30, the first clear demonstration of artificially produced radioactivity, for which they received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1935.

Applications

Neutron activation is used to produce medical and industrial radioisotopes, in neutron activation analysis, and is a safety consideration in nuclear reactors and particle accelerators, where structural materials become activated over time.
