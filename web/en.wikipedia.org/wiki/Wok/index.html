Wok

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A round-bottomed carbon-steel wok with a long handle, over a gas flame]

A wok is a round-bottomed (or, in modern versions, slightly flattened) cooking vessel originating in China, used for stir frying, steaming, deep frying, pan frying, searing, smoking and making soups. Its sloped sides create a gradient of heat — fiercely hot at the base, cooler up the walls — so food can be tossed through the hottest zone and rested on the sides, giving the cook unusual control with a single pan.

History

Woks have been used in China for roughly two thousand years, spreading throughout East and Southeast Asia (as the Malay/Indonesian kuali and similar pans). Their design suited fuel-scarce cooking: thin metal heats fast and food is cut small to cook in minutes. Cantonese cooking prizes wok hei, the smoky flavour imparted by a seasoned wok over intense flame.

Materials and seasoning

Traditional woks are carbon steel or cast iron, with one long handle (Cantonese style) or two loop handles. Carbon-steel woks are seasoned like cast iron: heated oil polymerises into a dark, increasingly non-stick patina that is protected by washing without harsh detergent. Flat-bottomed versions suit electric and induction hobs; professional Chinese stoves use a jet-like burner with a wok ring.

Use

Beyond stir frying, a wok with a bamboo steamer basket becomes a steamer, its depth makes it a safe deep fryer using less oil than a pot, and its lidless cone shape is used for tea-smoking and dry-toasting spices. In much of Asia it is the single most versatile pan in the kitchen.
