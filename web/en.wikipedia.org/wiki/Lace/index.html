Lace

Lace is a delicate fabric made of yarn or thread in an open weblike pattern, made by machine or by hand. Originally linen, silk, gold, or silver threads were used; lace is now often made with cotton thread, although linen and silk threads are still available. Manufactured lace may be made of synthetic fibre. A defining characteristic of lace is the absence of a pre-existing fabric: its openwork patterns are built up by looping, twisting, or braiding threads.

Types

There are many kinds of lace classified by how they are made. Needle lace, such as Venetian gros point and Alençon, is built with a needle and a single thread on a paper pattern. Bobbin lace, such as Honiton, Bruges, and Chantilly (traditionally), is woven by braiding and twisting threads wound on bobbins around pins stuck into a pillow. Other types include tape lace, knotted lace (tatting), crocheted lace such as Irish crochet, knitted lace, and machine-made laces such as Leavers lace and chemical lace.

History

Lace-making developed in Europe in the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, with Venice and Flanders as early centres. It became a costly luxury for collars, cuffs, and church furnishings, subject to sumptuary laws and heavy smuggling. Regional styles flourished across Belgium, France, England, Malta, and elsewhere. Handmade lace industries declined after the nineteenth century, when machines—beginning with the bobbinet and Leavers machines—could imitate handmade laces cheaply. Fashion revivals and heritage crafts keep traditional hand-lacemaking alive, and lacemaking traditions such as those of Croatia are inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage lists.

Uses

Lace is used in bridal and formal dress, lingerie, table linens, curtains, and ecclesiastical vestments, and as edging or insertion on garments and household textiles.
