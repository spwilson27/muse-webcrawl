Abhidharma

Abhidharma (Sanskrit: अभिधर्म; Pali: Abhidhamma) is the third division, or "basket" (piṭaka), of the Buddhist canon, alongside the Sutras and the Vinaya. The Abhidharma literature consists of systematic, analytical treatises that organise the teachings of the sutras into rigorous scholastic frameworks, analysing reality into its ultimate constituents, called dharmas.

Origins

Tradition attributes the Abhidhamma to the Buddha himself, said to have taught it in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven; scholars generally date the core texts to the centuries after his death, roughly the 3rd century BCE onwards, as elaborations of early doctrinal lists (mātṛkā). The two great surviving collections are the Theravāda Abhidhamma Piṭaka of seven books, in Pali, and the Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma, preserved largely in Chinese translation, whose great compendium is Vasubandhu's Abhidharmakośa.

Doctrine

Abhidharma analysis reduces persons and objects to momentary, conditioned events — dharmas — classified by type, function and causal relation. Key topics include the five aggregates (skandhas), sense bases (āyatanas), elements (dhātus), dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda), karma, and the path of liberation. The Sarvāstivāda school held that dharmas exist in past, present and future ("all exists"), a thesis the Sautrāntikas and later Mahāyāna philosophers attacked; Vasubandhu's Kośa presents the Sarvāstivāda system while refuting it from a Sautrāntika standpoint. In Theravāda, Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga systematised Abhidhamma for meditative practice.

Influence

Abhidharma scholasticism shaped Buddhist philosophy across Asia, influencing theories of mind, time and causation, and remains a living curriculum in Burmese, Thai and Tibetan monastic education.
