Axonometric projection

TypeParallel (orthographic) projection
SubtypesIsometric; dimetric; trimetric
UsesTechnical drawing, engineering illustration, video games, infographics

Axonometric projection is a type of parallel projection used to create a pictorial drawing of an object in which the object is rotated about one or more of its axes relative to the plane of projection. Because the projection rays are parallel and (in the strict sense) perpendicular to the picture plane, measurements along the three principal axes remain to scale — subject to a fixed foreshortening factor on each axis — making axonometric drawings popular for technical illustration where a three-dimensional impression is desired without losing measurability.

Types

Axonometric projections are classified by the foreshortening of the three axes:

  • Isometric projection — all three axes are equally foreshortened and the angles between them are all 120°. In the usual drawing convention the axes are oriented at 30° to the horizontal, and lengths along each axis are scaled by √(2/3) ≈ 0.816 (often simplified to true length in drafting practice).
  • Dimetric projection — two axes share the same scale and angle while the third differs, giving a less symmetric but often more natural view.
  • Trimetric projection — all three axes have different scales and unequal angles between them.

Axonometric projection is distinguished from oblique projection, in which the projection rays are not perpendicular to the picture plane, and from perspective projection, in which rays converge at a viewpoint and parallel lines meet at vanishing points.

History and applications

Precursors appear in Chinese and Islamic art and in military "parallel perspective" drawing; the method was formalized for engineering in the 19th century, notably by William Farish, who described isometric drawing in 1822, and was placed on a rigorous mathematical footing by Karl Pohlke's theorem. Axonometric views are standard in engineering manuals, patents, and assembly instructions, and the isometric variant became a hallmark of classic video games (e.g. Zaxxon, Q*bert, SimCity 2000) and of modern infographic and voxel art styles.
