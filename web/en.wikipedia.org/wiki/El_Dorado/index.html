El Dorado

El Dorado (Spanish for "the Golden One") is the term used by Europeans to describe a mythical king of the Muisca (Chibcha) people of Colombia who was said to cover himself in gold dust and plunge into Lake Guatavita. Over time, El Dorado came to refer to a legendary city or empire of gold in the Americas, which drove numerous expeditions by Spanish conquistadors and other adventurers.

The Muisca ritual

The original legend centred on the zipa, the ruler of the southern Muisca Confederation. According to accounts collected by chroniclers such as Juan Rodríguez Freyle, at his initiation the new zipa would be covered in gold dust aboard a ceremonial raft laden with gold and emeralds, sail to the centre of Lake Guatavita, and dive into the water as an offering to the gods, while spectators threw gold objects into the lake. A golden raft depicting this ceremony, discovered in 1969, is now held in the Museo del Oro in Bogotá.

Searches for El Dorado

From the 1530s onward, expeditions by Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada, Sebastián de Belalcázar, Nikolaus Federmann, Gonzalo Pizarro and Francisco de Orellana searched for the golden land. Later, Sir Walter Raleigh made two expeditions to Guiana (1595 and 1617) in search of the city of Manoa on Lake Parime. None found any such city, but the legend persisted in European imagination for centuries.

Modern usage

"El Dorado" has become a metaphor for any place of fabulous wealth or opportunity, and the name has been applied to numerous places, including El Dorado County, California, and the city of Eldorado, Kansas.
