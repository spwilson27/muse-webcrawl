Catacombs are man-made underground passageways and chambers used for burial and religious practice. The term originally referred to a specific burial ground near the Appian Way in Rome — the Catacombs of St Sebastian — and was extended to similar cemeteries elsewhere, most famously the early Christian catacombs of Rome and the ossuary known as the Catacombs of Paris.

Catacombs of Rome

From the 2nd century AD, Christians (and also Jews and pagans) excavated networks of tunnels in the soft volcanic tufa around Rome, because Roman law forbade burial within the city and land above ground was expensive. Bodies were placed in niches (loculi) stacked in the gallery walls, sealed with slabs; wealthier families used arched recesses (arcosolia) decorated with frescoes showing biblical scenes — some of the earliest Christian art. The network includes the catacombs of Callixtus, Domitilla, Priscilla and San Sebastiano, totalling hundreds of kilometres of galleries on multiple levels. Contrary to popular legend, they were primarily cemeteries rather than hiding places during persecutions.

Other catacombs

Catacombs exist in Naples, Sicily, Malta, Alexandria and elsewhere. The Paris "catacombs" are former quarries converted in the late 18th century into a municipal ossuary for millions of skeletons from overflowing cemeteries.
