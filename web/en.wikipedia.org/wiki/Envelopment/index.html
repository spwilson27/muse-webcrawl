Envelopment

Envelopment is a military tactic in which forces attack the flanks or rear of an enemy formation, aiming to encircle it so that the enemy is surrounded, cut off from supply and retreat, and can be destroyed or forced to surrender. It is one of the oldest and most decisive manoeuvres in warfare.

Forms

A single envelopment turns one flank while the rest of the army pins the enemy frontally; a double envelopment — the pincer movement — attacks both flanks and closes behind the enemy, as Hannibal achieved against Rome at Cannae in 216 BC. A complete encirclement cut off from relief is sometimes called a cauldron battle (German: Kesselschlacht). Vertical envelopment uses airborne or airmobile troops dropped behind enemy lines to achieve the same effect from a new dimension.

Historical examples

Famous envelopments include Alexander the Great's battles in Persia, the German Schlieffen-inspired operations of 1914 and the vast encirclements on the Eastern Front in 1941 (Kiev, Białystok–Minsk) and, in reverse, the Soviet destruction of the German Sixth Army at Stalingrad in 1942–43. The 1991 Gulf War's "left hook" through the Iraqi desert was a modern single envelopment executed at operational scale.

Counters

Defences against envelopment include refusing a flank (bending it back), holding reserves to counterattack the enveloping force, echeloning forces in depth, and breaking out of an encirclement before it hardens — at the risk of heavy losses.
