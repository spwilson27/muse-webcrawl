Second Fitna

Date680–692 CE
LocationArabian Peninsula, Syria, Iraq, Persia
Main partiesUmayyad Caliphate vs. Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr; Alid loyalists; Kharijites
OutcomeUmayyad victory under Abd al-Malik; reunification of the caliphate

The Second Fitna was a civil war and period of political and military disorder in the early Islamic world, lasting from about 680 to 692 CE during the Umayyad Caliphate. The term fitna (Arabic for trial or sedition) describes the struggle over legitimate leadership of the Muslim community that followed the death of Mu'awiya I, the first Umayyad caliph.

Course of the war

Mu'awiya's designation of his son Yazid I as successor — an innovation widely resented as turning the caliphate into a hereditary monarchy — triggered opposition. Husayn ibn Ali, grandson of the Prophet Muhammad, refused allegiance and was killed with his small party by Umayyad forces at the Battle of Karbala in October 680, an event commemorated ever since in Shia Islam. In Mecca, Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr established a rival caliphate that at its height controlled much of the Islamic world, while revolts by the pro-Alid movement of Mukhtar al-Thaqafi in Kufa and by radical Kharijite groups in Arabia, Iraq, and Persia further fragmented the empire. Umayyad forces suppressed Medina at the Battle of al-Harra (683) and twice besieged Mecca.

Resolution

After Yazid's death in 683 and a succession crisis in Damascus, Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan consolidated Umayyad power from 685, defeated the Kharijite-influenced and tribal rivals in Iraq under his general al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf, and in 692 captured Mecca, where Ibn al-Zubayr was killed. The victory reunified the caliphate and inaugurated Abd al-Malik's program of centralization, administrative Arabization, and Islamic coinage reform. The war entrenched the Sunni–Shia divide, permanently shaping Islamic political theology.
