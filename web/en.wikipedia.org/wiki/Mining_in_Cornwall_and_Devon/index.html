Mining in Cornwall and Devon

Mining in Cornwall and Devon, in the south-west of England, is an ancient industry centred principally on tin and copper, with smaller production of lead, silver, zinc, arsenic, tungsten, and china clay (kaolin). Cornwall and Devon provided most of the United Kingdom's tin, copper, and arsenic output, and the region's mining landscape was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2006.

History

Bronze Age traders sought Cornish tin from at least 2000 BC; the metal, essential for bronze, drew Phoenician and later Roman interest. Medieval mining was governed by the stannary system, with its own parliaments and courts. The industry boomed in the 18th and 19th centuries: Cornish beam engines, developed by engineers such as Richard Trevithick, pumped water from deep mines, and by the mid-19th century Cornwall dominated world copper production. Competition from overseas deposits caused a long decline, and waves of Cornish miners emigrated to mining districts worldwide — it is said that anywhere in the world with a hole in the ground, a Cornishman will be found at the bottom of it.

Legacy

The last tin mine, South Crofty, closed in 1998. Distinctive granite engine houses dot the landscape, and the Cornwall and West Devon Mining Landscape World Heritage Site protects ten areas of mining remains. Cornish mining technology, terminology, and even pasties spread across the globe with the diaspora.
