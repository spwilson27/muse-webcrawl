Tisha B'Av (Hebrew: תִּשְׁעָה בְּאָב, "the ninth of Av") is an annual fast day in Judaism, on which a number of disasters in Jewish history are commemorated, primarily the destruction of both Solomon's Temple by the Neo-Babylonian Empire and the Second Temple by the Roman Empire in Jerusalem.

Tisha B'Av is regarded as the saddest day in the Jewish calendar, and it is thus believed to be a day which is destined for tragedy. It falls in July or August in the Gregorian calendar.

Historical events commemorated

According to the Mishnah (Ta'anit 4:6), five calamities befell the Jewish people on the ninth of Av: the spies brought an evil report about the Land of Israel, causing the Israelites to wander in the desert for forty years; the First Temple was destroyed by the Babylonians (586 BCE); the Second Temple was destroyed by the Romans (70 CE); the city of Betar, the last stronghold of the Bar Kokhba revolt, fell (135 CE); and the site of the Temple and Jerusalem were ploughed by the Romans. Later tradition added other tragedies associated with the date, including the expulsion of Jews from Spain in 1492.

Observances

The fast lasts about 25 hours, from sunset on the eve of the ninth until nightfall the following day. Like Yom Kippur, it involves abstention from eating, drinking, washing, wearing leather shoes, and marital relations. The Book of Lamentations is read in synagogue in the evening and morning, often by candlelight, with worshippers seated on the floor or on low stools as a sign of mourning. Kinnot (dirges) are recited, and the Torah reading and some prayers are modified. The restrictions are preceded by the Three Weeks, a period of mourning beginning with the fast of the 17th of Tammuz.

In modern Israel

In Israel, Tisha B'Av is a public day of commemoration; restaurants and places of entertainment are closed by law on the eve of the fast. Large numbers gather at the Western Wall in Jerusalem. There is ongoing discussion in Israeli society about the day's meaning, including its national as well as religious dimensions.
