Barrow (tomb)

A barrow is a burial mound, an artificial heap of earth or stones raised over a grave or graves. Barrows are found across much of Europe and Asia and date from the Neolithic period through the early Middle Ages. They are among the most widespread and enduring forms of prehistoric monument, serving both as tombs and as markers in the landscape.

Types

Barrows are commonly classified by shape. A long barrow is an elongated mound, often covering a timber or stone burial chamber, characteristic of the Early Neolithic in Britain. A round barrow is a circular mound, usually covering one or more burials, most common from the Early Bronze Age onward. Round barrows include bowl, bell, disc, saucer and pond barrows, distinguished by the profile of the mound and the presence of surrounding ditches and banks.

Distribution and chronology

In Britain, long barrows were built roughly between 4000 and 3400 BC, while round barrows proliferated from about 2200 BC. Comparable monuments include the kurgans of the Eurasian steppe, the tumuli of Etruria, and the burial mounds of Japan (kofun) and North America.

Excavation

Barrows were among the first monuments investigated by antiquarians such as William Stukeley and Richard Colt Hoare. Excavation has recovered grave goods including pottery, bronze weapons, and jewellery, providing key evidence for prehistoric burial practice and social organisation.
