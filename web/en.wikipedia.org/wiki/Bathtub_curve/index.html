Bathtub curve

The bathtub curve is a widely used model in reliability engineering for the failure rate of a product or population of components over its lifetime. Plotted against time, the hazard rate traces a shape resembling a bathtub, with three distinct regions.

The three phases

The first phase, infant mortality, shows a decreasing failure rate as defective units fail early and are weeded out; manufacturers counter this with burn-in testing. The second phase, useful life, has an approximately constant, low failure rate dominated by random chance failures. The third phase, wear-out, shows an increasing failure rate as components age, fatigue and degrade.

Modelling

The curve can be modelled by combining distributions — for example, a Weibull distribution with shape parameter below 1 for early failures, an exponential distribution for the constant-rate region, and a Weibull with shape parameter above 1 for wear-out.

Criticism and use

Although influential in maintenance planning, warranty analysis and product design, the bathtub curve is an idealisation: many real systems never show all three phases, and empirical studies have found that only a minority of failure patterns in complex equipment conform to it. It nevertheless remains a standard teaching model in reliability engineering.
