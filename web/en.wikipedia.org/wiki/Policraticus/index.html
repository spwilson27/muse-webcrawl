The Policraticus (1159) by John of Salisbury is a landmark work of medieval political philosophy, subtitled Of the Frivolities of Courtiers and the Footprints of Philosophers. Dedicated to Thomas Becket, it combines satire of court life with a systematic theory of kingship and the commonwealth.

A medieval manuscript of John of Salisbury's Policraticus.

Contents

The first books attack the vanities of courtiers — hunting, gambling, flattery, and superstition — while the later books turn to philosophy and government. Its most famous passage presents the commonwealth as a body: the prince is the head, the senate the heart, judges and governors the eyes, ears, and tongue, soldiers the hands, peasants the feet, all ordered under the soul, which is religion and its priests.

Tyranny and tyrannicide

John argued that a prince rules by law and for the common good, whereas a tyrant oppresses by will. The Policraticus contains the medieval era's most celebrated discussion of tyrannicide, suggesting that a tyrant — an "image of depravity" — may in certain circumstances be resisted, deposed, or even killed, a doctrine that influenced political thought for centuries.
