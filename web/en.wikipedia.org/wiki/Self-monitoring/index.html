Self-monitoring

Self-monitoring is a personality trait introduced by psychologist Mark Snyder in 1974. It describes the extent to which people monitor and regulate their self-presentation, expressive behaviour, and nonverbal affective displays in social situations, adjusting what they say and do to fit the audience and context.

Introduced byMark Snyder (1974)
Measured bySelf-Monitoring Scale (25 items; revised 18-item version)

High and low self-monitors

High self-monitors are attentive to social cues and modify their behaviour to create desired impressions; they are often described as social chameleons, tend to emerge as group leaders, and have more differentiated social relationships. Low self-monitors express their inner attitudes and feelings consistently across situations, place greater value on congruence between attitudes and behaviour, and tend to form relationships based on shared interests rather than social versatility.

Measurement

Snyder's Self-Monitoring Scale originally contained 25 true–false items. Later revisions reduced it to 18 items, and factor analyses have suggested it comprises subcomponents such as acting ability, extraversion, and other-directedness.

Research

Self-monitoring predicts differences in consumer behaviour, advertising responsiveness, dating patterns, job performance in boundary-spanning roles, and leadership emergence. It has also been studied in relation to attitude–behaviour consistency: the attitudes of low self-monitors predict their behaviour more strongly than those of high self-monitors.
