Ludovisi Gaul

Artist: Roman copy of a Hellenistic original, attributed to the circle of Epigonus
Year: Original c. 230–220 BC; marble copy 2nd century AD
Medium: Marble
Location: Palazzo Altemps, Museo Nazionale Romano, Rome

The Ludovisi Gaul (sometimes called the "Galatian Suicide") is a Roman marble copy of a lost Hellenistic bronze sculpture. It depicts a Galatian warrior who, facing defeat, has just killed his wife and is driving his sword into his own chest rather than be captured. The group takes its name from the Ludovisi collection in Rome, where it was first recorded in the seventeenth century; it is now in the Palazzo Altemps.

History and attribution

The original bronze is generally associated with the victory monuments erected by Attalus I of Pergamon around 230–220 BC to celebrate his victories over the Galatians (Gauls) of Asia Minor, and is attributed to the sculptor Epigonus or his workshop. The Dying Gaul in the Capitoline Museums is thought to come from the same monument complex. The marble version was found in Rome, probably on the site of the Gardens of Sallust, and entered the collection of Cardinal Ludovico Ludovisi by 1623; Gian Lorenzo Bernini is said to have restored it.

Description

The warrior, identifiable as a Gaul by his moustache, torc and wavy hair, supports the limp body of his dead wife with one arm while plunging his sword beneath his collarbone with the other, his head turned away from the viewer. The composition is pyramidal and dramatic, characteristic of the "Pergamene baroque" style, and presents the defeated enemy with a dignity that has made the work famous as an image of noble defiance.
