Allostasis

Allostasis is the process of achieving stability, or homeostasis, through physiological or behavioural change. Whereas homeostasis describes keeping internal variables near fixed set points, allostasis describes how the brain and body actively adjust those set points in anticipation of, and in response to, changing demands. The term was introduced by Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer in 1988 and was developed extensively by Bruce McEwen.

Allostatic load

The cumulative physiological cost of chronic adaptation is called allostatic load. Mediators such as cortisol, adrenaline, and inflammatory cytokines are protective in the short term but damaging when overused or poorly regulated. Sustained allostatic load is associated with cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, depression, and cognitive decline. McEwen described several patterns of dysregulation: repeated stress "hits", failure to habituate, failure to shut off the response, and inadequate responses that trigger compensatory overactivity in other mediators.

Applications

The allostatic load model is used in research on health disparities, occupational stress, ageing, and psychiatry, where composite biomarker indices are used to quantify wear and tear on the body. Critics note that the concept overlaps with older notions of stress and that measurement approaches vary widely between studies.
