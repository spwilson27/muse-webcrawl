Opioid overdose
Symptoms: Pinpoint pupils, unconsciousness, slow or stopped breathing
Complications: Brain damage, death
Treatment: Airway support, naloxone

An opioid overdose is toxicity due to excessive consumption of opioids, such as morphine, codeine, heroin, fentanyl, tramadol or methadone. The classic triad of symptoms is pinpoint pupils, unconsciousness and respiratory depression; the immediate cause of death is usually respiratory failure. Opioid overdoses account for the majority of drug-overdose deaths in many Western countries and are a central feature of the opioid epidemic.

Signs and symptoms

Overdose produces central nervous system and respiratory depression: breathing becomes slow, shallow or stops; the person cannot be roused; pupils constrict to pinpoints; lips and fingernails may turn blue from lack of oxygen. Risk is greatly increased by combining opioids with alcohol, benzodiazepines or other sedatives, by reduced tolerance after a period of abstinence, and by the presence of potent synthetic opioids such as fentanyl in the drug supply.

Treatment

Treatment focuses on supporting the airway and breathing, and administering the antagonist naloxone, which displaces opioids from their receptors and can restore breathing within minutes. Because naloxone wears off faster than most opioids, monitoring and repeat dosing may be needed. Good Samaritan laws in many jurisdictions protect bystanders who call emergency services or administer naloxone.

Epidemiology

Deaths involving opioids rose sharply in the United States from the late 1990s in successive waves — prescription opioids, then heroin, then illicitly manufactured fentanyl — exceeding 80,000 deaths per year in the early 2020s. Harm-reduction measures such as take-home naloxone, drug checking and supervised consumption services aim to reduce fatal outcomes.
