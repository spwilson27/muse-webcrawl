Prehistoric Wales

Prehistoric Wales covers the period from the earliest known human habitation of the area that is now Wales until the Roman conquest in the first century AD, when written records begin. The earliest evidence of human presence comes from Neanderthal remains at Bontnewydd in Denbighshire, dated to around 230,000 years ago.

Palaeolithic

Human occupation was intermittent, following the advance and retreat of ice sheets. Paviland Cave on the Gower Peninsula yielded the "Red Lady of Paviland" — actually a young man buried around 33,000 years ago with red ochre and ornaments, one of the earliest formal burials in Western Europe.

Neolithic

Farming reached Wales around 4000 BC. Neolithic communities built impressive chambered tombs such as Barclodiad y Gawres and Bryn Celli Ddu on Anglesey, and Pentre Ifan in the Preseli Hills, whose bluestones are also associated with Stonehenge.

Bronze and Iron Ages

The Bronze Age (c. 2500–800 BC) saw metalworking, including the gold cape found at Mold, and elaborate burial mounds. In the Iron Age, the landscape became dominated by hillforts — more than 600 are recorded in Wales, including Tre'r Ceiri on the Llŷn Peninsula, whose stone huts survive within its walls. By the Roman invasion, Wales was inhabited by Celtic-speaking tribes including the Silures, Ordovices and Deceangli.
