Legal tender

Legal tender is a form of money that courts of law are required to recognise as satisfactory payment for any monetary debt. A creditor must accept legal tender in discharge of a debt (though parties may contract beforehand to use another payment method, and shops generally may refuse a transaction before any debt exists). Typically, a country's legal tender consists of its national currency in the form of banknotes and coins issued by the state or central bank.

Scope and limits

Legal tender laws are often narrower than popular belief suggests. In many jurisdictions, coins are legal tender only up to a specified amount (for example, small-denomination coins in the United Kingdom are legal tender only for small sums), while banknotes are legal tender for any amount. Some central-bank notes have special status: in Scotland and Northern Ireland, locally issued commercial banknotes are not strictly legal tender anywhere in the UK, yet circulate by custom. In the United States, federal law (31 U.S.C. § 5103) makes US coins and currency legal tender for all debts, public charges, taxes, and dues — but this does not oblige a private business to accept cash for a purchase, since no debt exists until a transaction is agreed.

History

The concept developed with state-issued coinage and paper money. Landmark United States Supreme Court decisions — the Legal Tender Cases, including Knox v. Lee (1871) and Juilliard v. Greenman (1884) — upheld Congress's power to issue paper money ("greenbacks") as legal tender even in peacetime, after the earlier Hepburn v. Griswold (1870) had partly held otherwise. Governments can also withdraw legal-tender status through demonetisation — famously, India's 2016 withdrawal of ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes.

Modern developments

Several US states and cities (including Massachusetts, New York City, San Francisco, and Philadelphia) have passed laws requiring brick-and-mortar stores to accept cash, motivated by financial inclusion for the "unbanked". In 2021 El Salvador made Bitcoin legal tender alongside the US dollar (a requirement scaled back in 2025 under an IMF agreement), the first such experiment with a cryptocurrency. Euro banknotes and coins are legal tender throughout the eurozone, though some member states cap large cash payments.
