A colorectal polyp is a polyp (fleshy growth) occurring on the lining of the colon or rectum. Untreated colorectal polyps can develop into colorectal cancer. Colorectal polyps are often classified by their behaviour (i.e. benign or malignant) or cause (e.g. as a result of inflammatory bowel disease). They may be benign (e.g. hyperplastic polyps), pre-malignant (e.g. tubular adenoma) or malignant (e.g. colorectal adenocarcinoma).

Classification

Adenomatous polyps

Adenomas are benign glandular tumors that are precursors of most colorectal cancers through the adenoma–carcinoma sequence. They are subdivided into tubular, tubulovillous and villous adenomas, with malignant potential increasing with size, villous architecture and degree of dysplasia. Roughly 5–10% of people over the age of 40 have adenomas, most of which never progress.

Hyperplastic and serrated polyps

Hyperplastic polyps are common, small, and were historically considered harmless, but the serrated pathway — involving sessile serrated lesions, often in the proximal colon — is now recognized as an alternative route to colorectal cancer, particularly cancers with BRAF mutation and CpG island methylation.

Other types

Inflammatory polyps arise in inflammatory bowel disease; hamartomatous polyps occur in syndromes such as Peutz–Jeghers syndrome and juvenile polyposis. Inherited conditions such as familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) produce hundreds to thousands of adenomas and near-certain cancer without colectomy.

Signs and symptoms

Most polyps are asymptomatic and are found during screening. Larger polyps may cause rectal bleeding, changes in bowel habit, mucus discharge or, rarely, intussusception.

Diagnosis and treatment

Polyps are detected by colonoscopy, flexible sigmoidoscopy, CT colonography or stool-based tests that prompt follow-up endoscopy. Removal (polypectomy) during colonoscopy, by snare or forceps, both confirms the histology and interrupts progression to cancer; population screening programs are built largely on this principle.
