Khmelnytsky Uprising

Khmelnytsky Uprising Date: 1648–1657
Location: Ukraine, Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth
Belligerents: Zaporozhian Cossacks and Crimean Khanate vs. Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth
Commanders: Bohdan Khmelnytsky; Mikołaj Potocki; Jeremi Wiśniowiecki
Result: Treaty of Pereyaslav (1654); creation of the Cossack Hetmanate

The Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648–1657) was a Cossack rebellion in the eastern territories of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, led by the hetman Bohdan Khmelnytsky. The uprising created a Cossack state, the Hetmanate, and led to a major realignment of power in Eastern Europe, including the 1654 Pereyaslav Agreement with the Tsardom of Russia. In Jewish history the events are remembered as the Gzeirot Taḥ veTat ("Decrees of 1648–49"), because of the massacres of Jewish communities that accompanied the revolt.

Bohdan Khmelnytsky, leader of the uprising, with his Cossack host.

Background

By the mid-seventeenth century, the Ukrainian lands of the Commonwealth were dominated by Polish and Polonized magnates whose vast estates were worked by an increasingly enserfed peasantry. Orthodox Christians faced discrimination in a state increasingly aligned with Roman Catholicism following the Union of Brest (1596). The registered Cossacks of the Zaporozhian Host resented repeated reductions of their register and privileges, while unregistered Cossacks and peasants chafed under magnate officials and leaseholders, among them many Jewish arendators who administered estates and collected taxes. A series of Cossack revolts in the 1620s and 1630s had been suppressed, but the grievances remained unresolved.

Outbreak and early victories

The immediate spark was a personal feud: Khmelnytsky, a Cossack officer and landowner, had his estate seized and his son beaten by a Polish official, Daniel Czapliński, and was denied redress by the courts. Fleeing to the Zaporozhian Sich in early 1648, he was elected hetman and concluded an alliance with the Crimean Tatars under Khan İslâm III Giray. In the spring of 1648 the Cossack–Tatar army destroyed Crown forces at the battles of Zhovti Vody and Korsun, capturing the Grand Crown Hetman Mikołaj Potocki. News of the victories triggered a general rising of peasants and townspeople across Ukraine.

Massacres and the war of 1648–1649

As the uprising spread, Cossack and peasant bands massacred Polish nobles, Roman Catholic clergy, and Jews, who were targeted as agents of the magnate order. Contemporary chronicles, most famously Nathan Hanover's Yeven Mezulah ("Abyss of Despair"), describe the destruction of hundreds of Jewish communities; modern historians estimate the number of Jewish dead in the tens of thousands, a demographic catastrophe for Polish Jewry. In September 1648 Khmelnytsky routed the Crown army at Pyliavtsi and advanced into Galicia, though he withdrew after negotiating with the newly elected King John II Casimir.

Treaties and the Pereyaslav Agreement

The 1649 Treaty of Zboriv recognized a Cossack Hetmanate over the Kyiv, Chernihiv, and Bratslav palatinates and raised the Cossack register to 40,000, but its terms satisfied neither side. War resumed; the Cossack defeat at Berestechko in 1651, after the Tatars withdrew mid-battle, forced the harsher Treaty of Bila Tserkva. Seeking a stronger protector, Khmelnytsky turned to Moscow. The Pereyaslav Agreement of January 1654 placed the Hetmanate under the tsar's authority — an arrangement whose precise legal character (alliance, protectorate, or submission) has been debated ever since — and drew Russia into war with the Commonwealth.

Legacy

The uprising marks a foundational event in Ukrainian national history: the Hetmanate is regarded as a proto-Ukrainian state, and Khmelnytsky as a national hero. For Poland, the revolt began the catastrophic period known as the Deluge. For Jewish communities, 1648–49 remained for centuries a byword for catastrophe, commemorated in fasts and chronicles. The ensuing Polish–Russian war ended with the 1667 Truce of Andrusovo, which partitioned Ukraine along the Dnieper River.
