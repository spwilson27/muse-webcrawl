Project MKUltra was a human experimentation program conducted by the U.S. Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) from 1953 to 1973 in which the agency sought to develop procedures and identify drugs that could be used in interrogations to weaken individuals and force confessions through brainwashing and psychological torture. It was preceded by Project Artichoke and used front organizations to fund research at universities, hospitals, prisons and pharmaceutical companies, often without the knowledge of the institutions' full purposes or of the human subjects involved.

Scope and experiments

MKUltra comprised some 149 subprojects. Experiments administered LSD and other drugs to mental patients, prisoners, drug addicts, sex workers and other unwitting subjects, sometimes with fatal consequences. Other lines of work involved hypnosis, sensory deprivation, isolation, electroconvulsive therapy, and the development of poisons and delivery devices. Under Operation Midnight Climax, the CIA set up safe houses in San Francisco and New York where prostitutes lured clients who were dosed with LSD while agents observed from behind one-way mirrors. Army scientist Frank Olson, who had been covertly dosed with LSD in November 1953, died days later in a fall from a New York hotel window — a case that became central to the later scandal.

Exposure and investigations

CIA Director Richard Helms ordered the destruction of most MKUltra records in 1973 as the Watergate scandal unfolded. The program nevertheless came to public attention through the Church Committee hearings of 1975 and a Freedom of Information Act request that in 1977 uncovered a cache of some 20,000 financial documents that had survived, prompting further Senate hearings. President Gerald Ford formally apologised to the Olson family, and the government paid a settlement. In 1977 President Jimmy Carter's administration acknowledged the program's extent.

In the Supreme Court case United States v. Stanley (1987), the Court held that a serviceman who had been given LSD without his knowledge could not sue the government. Canada also faced claims: patients of psychiatrist Donald Ewen Cameron at Montreal's Allan Memorial Institute had been subjected to "depatterning" and psychic driving experiments under Subproject 68, and the Canadian government eventually compensated victims.

Legacy

MKUltra is widely regarded as a profound violation of research ethics and American law, and it figures prominently in subsequent reforms of human-subject protections. It has also become a persistent reference point in popular culture and conspiracy discourse, often in exaggerated forms far beyond the documented record.
