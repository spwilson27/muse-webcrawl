Arthur Henry Hallam (1 February 1811 – 15 September 1833) was an English poet, best known as the subject of Alfred Tennyson's elegy In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850), one of the most celebrated poems of the Victorian age. Hallam's sudden death at 22 cut short a literary career that his contemporaries, including Tennyson and William Ewart Gladstone, expected to be brilliant.

Life

Hallam was the son of the historian Henry Hallam. Educated at Eton, where he was a precocious linguist and writer, he went up to Trinity College, Cambridge in 1828. There he joined the secret debating society known as the Cambridge Apostles and formed an intense friendship with the young Alfred Tennyson, becoming engaged to Tennyson's sister Emily. Hallam championed Tennyson's early poetry, writing a perceptive review of his 1830 volume in the Englishman's Magazine (1831), "On Some of the Characteristics of Modern Poetry", which distinguished the poetry of "sensation" from that of "reflection".

Death and In Memoriam

In the summer of 1833, travelling in Europe with his father, Hallam died suddenly in Vienna of a cerebral haemorrhage (an apoplectic stroke, possibly related to a congenital condition). He was buried at St Andrew's Church, Clevedon, Somerset. Tennyson, devastated, spent the next seventeen years composing the sequence of lyrics published anonymously in 1850 as In Memoriam A.H.H., which meditates on grief, faith and the challenge of contemporary science, and whose success established Tennyson as Poet Laureate.

Writings

Hallam's surviving works — poems, including the sonnet sequence written to Emily Tennyson, and essays — were published posthumously by his father as Remains in Verse and Prose of Arthur Henry Hallam (1834). His letters and criticism are valued as evidence of the intellectual ferment of early Victorian Cambridge.
