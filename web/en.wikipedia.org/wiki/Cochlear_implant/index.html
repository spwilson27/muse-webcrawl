A cochlear implant (CI) is a surgically implanted neuroprosthesis that provides a person with moderate-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss a modified sense of sound. Unlike a hearing aid, which amplifies sound for a damaged ear, a cochlear implant bypasses damaged hair cells in the cochlea and directly stimulates the auditory nerve with electrical signals.

How it works

An external sound processor captures sound, converts it into digital signals, and transmits it across the skin to an internal receiver. An electrode array threaded into the cochlea's scala tympani then stimulates different regions of the auditory nerve, roughly corresponding to different frequencies. With training, users can understand speech, though the signal is coarser than natural hearing and music perception is often limited.

History and outcomes

Multichannel devices were developed in the 1970s–80s by teams led by Graeme Clark in Australia and others in the United States and Austria. Outcomes are best for children implanted early and for post-lingually deafened adults. The devices have also been debated within parts of the Deaf community, some of whom view them as a threat to Deaf culture and sign languages.
