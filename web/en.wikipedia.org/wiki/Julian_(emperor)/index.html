Julian (Latin: Flavius Claudius Julianus; 331/332 – 26 June 363) was Roman emperor from 361 to 363. A nephew of Constantine the Great and the last non-Christian ruler of the empire, he attempted to reverse the Christianisation of the Roman state and revive traditional Greco-Roman religion, earning the epithet "Julian the Apostate" in Christian tradition. He was also a noted philosopher and author in Greek.

Early life

Julian was born in Constantinople, the son of Julius Constantius, half-brother of Constantine I. In the purge that followed Constantine's death in 337, Julian's father and much of his family were murdered; Julian and his half-brother Gallus survived, likely because of their youth. Raised a Christian under supervision in Cappadocia, Julian was educated at Constantinople, Nicomedia — where he secretly encountered the pagan orator Libanius's works — and Athens, where he studied Neoplatonist philosophy and was initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries. By his early twenties he had privately renounced Christianity for a philosophically refined paganism influenced by the theurgist Maximus of Ephesus.

Caesar in Gaul

In 355 his cousin, the emperor Constantius II, appointed Julian Caesar and sent him to restore Gaul, overrun by Frankish and Alamannic invaders. Julian proved an outstanding commander, crushing the Alamanni at the Battle of Strasbourg in 357 and campaigning across the Rhine, while his tax reforms made him popular with Gallic provincials. When Constantius ordered Julian's best troops east for the Persian war, the legions at Lutetia (Paris) in 360 proclaimed Julian Augustus in mutiny. Civil war was averted when Constantius died in November 361, naming Julian his heir.

Reign and religious policy

As sole emperor Julian announced religious toleration for all cults, recalled exiled Christian bishops (hoping to set the sects against one another), restored temples and sacrifices, and removed Christian privileges. His edicts were mostly non-violent but pointed: an education law effectively barred Christians from teaching the classical texts, and he favoured pagans in appointments. His writings, including the treatise Against the Galileans and the satire The Caesars, aimed to give paganism the intellectual and charitable organisation Christianity possessed. He also reformed the court, cut palace expenditure, and challenged the bureaucracy of Constantinople before moving to Antioch.

Persian campaign and death

In March 363 Julian invaded the Sasanian Empire with the largest Roman army of the era (see Julian's Persian expedition). After initial victories and an advance to Ctesiphon, the army was forced into a disastrous retreat. Julian died of a spear wound received at the Battle of Samarra on 26 June 363. A later Christian legend attributed his dying words as "You have won, Galilean" (Vicisti, Galilaee), for which there is no contemporary evidence. He was succeeded by Jovian, and buried eventually at Tarsus.

Legacy

Julian's reign — only twenty months as sole emperor — has generated an outsized literature, from Ammianus Marcellinus's admiring history to Gore Vidal's novel Julian (1964). Because his pagan restoration died with him, he stands as the last champion of the old Roman religion, variously judged a reactionary dreamer, a tolerant reformer, or a precursor of the secular state.
