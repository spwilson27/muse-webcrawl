Hypertensive emergency

A hypertensive emergency is very high blood pressure with potentially life-threatening symptoms and signs of acute damage to one or more organ systems — especially the brain, eyes, heart and kidneys. It is distinguished from hypertensive urgency, in which blood pressure is similarly elevated but without acute end-organ damage.

Signs and symptoms

Manifestations depend on the organs affected and include hypertensive encephalopathy (headache, confusion, seizures, coma), intracerebral haemorrhage or stroke, papilledema and retinal haemorrhages, acute heart failure with pulmonary oedema, myocardial infarction, aortic dissection, and acute kidney injury. Malignant hypertension is the historical term for this syndrome accompanied by characteristic retinal changes.

Causes

Most cases occur in people with known hypertension who stop or poorly adhere to medication. Other causes include kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, endocrine disorders such as pheochromocytoma, eclampsia, and sympathomimetic drugs such as cocaine.

Treatment

Hypertensive emergencies require prompt but controlled reduction of blood pressure in a monitored setting, typically with intravenous agents such as labetalol, nicardipine, clevidipine or sodium nitroprusside, chosen according to the organs involved. Because autoregulation is shifted in chronic hypertension, overly rapid reduction can cause cerebral or coronary ischaemia; guidelines generally advise lowering mean arterial pressure by no more than about 20–25% in the first hour, with exceptions such as aortic dissection, which requires faster control.
