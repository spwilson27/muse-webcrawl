Service à la française ("service in the French style") is the historic French practice of serving a meal in courses, each course consisting of a great variety of dishes placed on the table simultaneously for diners to serve themselves or be served from. It was the dominant style of formal dining in France and much of Europe from the Middle Ages until the mid-nineteenth century.

Structure of the meal

A grand dinner in this style typically comprised three courses (in French usage, services), plus dessert. Each course presented a symmetrical arrangement of dishes: soups, roasts, entrées, entremets, large joints, fish, and elaborate centrepieces all appeared together, and diners generally ate mainly from the dishes within their reach. Carving was done at the table, often by the host or a senior servant. The meal was as much a visual spectacle—a display of abundance and artistry, codified by chefs such as Antonin Carême—as a sequence of flavours.

Decline

From the 1850s and 1860s, service à la russe, in which individual dishes are served one after another in portions, was popularised in France (notably through the chef Urbain Dubois and the example of Russian ambassador Alexander Kurakin) and gradually replaced the French style in restaurants and private dining. Service à la française survives in modified form in buffet service and family-style dining, and in the concept of dishes shared at the table.
