Speenhamland system

The Speenhamland system was a form of outdoor relief — a welfare payment supplementing the wages of the rural poor — adopted in 1795 at the Pelican Inn in Speenhamland, a parish in Berkshire, England. Under the system, magistrates set a scale of relief tied to the price of bread and the size of a labourer's family, topping up earnings so that even the employed would receive a minimum income.

The Pelican Inn, Speenhamland, where the bread scale was adopted on 6 May 1795.

The bread scale

When a gallon loaf of bread cost 1 shilling, a "poor and industrious man" was guaranteed 3 shillings a week for himself and 1s 6d for each family member; the scale rose proportionally with the price of bread. Because wartime inflation and failed harvests had driven wheat prices sharply upward, the system spread through the southern and midland counties of England, though it was never national law and its adoption was patchy.

Contemporary and later criticism

Critics from Malthus and Ricardo to the 1834 Royal Commission argued that Speenhamland subsidised employers paying sub-subsistence wages, depressed wage rates generally, discouraged thrift and labour mobility, and burdened ratepayers with exploding poor rates. The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834, influenced by the Commission's condemnation of allowance systems, sought to abolish outdoor relief to the able-bodied in favour of the workhouse test.

Historiographical reassessment

Karl Polanyi made Speenhamland famous in The Great Transformation (1944) as a disastrous pre-market paternalism whose failure created the modern labour market. Later historians — Mark Blaug's "The Myth of the Old Poor Law" (1963) and subsequent work by George Boyer and others — have questioned how widespread and how harmful the allowance system really was, noting that relief costs were driven more by unemployment, demobilisation and harvest failure, and that Speenhamland's direct effects were modest and regional. Modern commentators frequently cite it in debates over wage subsidies and guaranteed minimum income, both as precedent and as cautionary tale.
