Political satire

Political satire is a form of satire directed at politics, politicians and public policy, using humour, irony, exaggeration and ridicule to expose and criticise folly, corruption and abuse of power. It appears in literature, theatre, cartoons, film, television and online media.

History

Political satire is as old as political theatre: Aristophanes mocked Athenian leaders in comedies such as The Knights. Roman satirists Horace and Juvenal ridiculed the powerful of their day. In the eighteenth century, Jonathan Swift's A Modest Proposal (1729) became a landmark of the genre, and the caricatures of James Gillray defined British political cartooning.

Modern forms

The twentieth century brought satirical magazines such as Punch, Private Eye and Mad, radio and television programmes including the BBC's That Was the Week That Was, and puppet shows such as Spitting Image. In the United States, programmes such as The Daily Show, The Colbert Report and Saturday Night Live made satirical news a major source of political commentary, while websites like The Onion publish parody news stories that are sometimes mistaken for real reporting.

Function and limits

Scholars debate satire's effects: it can puncture pretension, inform disengaged audiences and provide a safety valve for dissent, but it may also deepen cynicism. In authoritarian states political satire has often been suppressed, though traditions of coded humour — such as Soviet-era jokes — flourished underground. See also satire and caricature.
