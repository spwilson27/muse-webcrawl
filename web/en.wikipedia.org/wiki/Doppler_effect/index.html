Doppler effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of sound waves bunching ahead of a moving source and stretching behind it]
Waves from a moving source: compressed ahead, stretched behind.

The Doppler effect (or Doppler shift) is the change in frequency of a wave in relation to an observer who is moving relative to the source of the wave. Proposed by Christian Doppler in 1842 for light (and confirmed for sound by Buys Ballot's 1845 experiment with trumpeters on a train), it compresses waves ahead of an approaching source (higher frequency, blueshift for light) and stretches them behind a receding one (lower frequency, Redshift).

Applications fill physics and daily life: police radar and weather radar, medical ultrasound imaging of blood flow, sonar, the radial-velocity method that found the first exoplanets (Michel Mayor), stellar rotation and galaxy rotation curves, and the acoustic levitation of everyday experience when an ambulance passes. In relativity the effect gains a transverse component from Time dilation (the relativistic Doppler effect), measured with canal rays by Ives and Stilwell (1938) and now with storage-ring ions to parts in 10⁹ — among the most precise confirmations of special relativity.

Formula

For sound (with a medium), frequency shifts depend on source and observer speeds separately relative to the medium; for light in vacuum only the relative speed matters, and the relativistic formula f′ = f√[(1 − β)/(1 + β)] for recession reduces to the classical z ≈ v/c at low speeds while never permitting superluminal recession paradoxes. At cosmic scales, most redshift is not Doppler at all but the expansion of space itself.
