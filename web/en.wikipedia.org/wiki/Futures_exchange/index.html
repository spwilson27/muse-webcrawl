Futures exchange

A futures exchange or futures market is a central financial exchange where people can trade standardised futures contracts; that is, a contract to buy specific quantities of a commodity or financial instrument at a specified price with delivery set at a specified time in the future.

The exchange itself provides the venue and infrastructure for trading, clearing, and settlement. Traders buy and sell contracts on agricultural commodities, metals, energy, interest rates, stock indices, and currencies. Unlike the spot market, futures trading does not necessarily involve the physical delivery of the underlying asset, as most contracts are closed out before expiry.

History

The world's oldest futures exchange is the Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT), founded in 1848, originally trading grain contracts. The Dojima Rice Exchange in Osaka, Japan, operated as an organised rice futures market from 1730 and is regarded as an early predecessor. Major exchanges today include the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME Group), the New York Mercantile Exchange (NYMEX), ICE Futures, and Eurex.

Functions

Futures exchanges serve two main purposes: hedging — allowing producers and consumers to lock in prices and manage risk — and price discovery, providing a transparent reference price for the underlying assets. Clearing houses interpose themselves between buyers and sellers, guaranteeing performance and reducing counterparty risk, supported by margin requirements.

Participants

Participants include hedgers (farmers, miners, airlines, manufacturers), speculators, and arbitrageurs. Regulation in the United States is overseen by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC); in the UK by the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA).
