Bonn–Oberkassel dog

The Bonn–Oberkassel dog is the skeleton of a domestic dog buried with two humans in a grave at Oberkassel, a district of Bonn in Germany, about 14,000 years ago, during the late Upper Palaeolithic (Magdalenian). Discovered in 1914 and re-examined in detail in the 2010s, it is among the earliest undisputed remains of a domestic dog and the oldest known dog buried together with people — evidence that the human–dog bond, including care for a sick animal, reaches back to the very beginnings of domestication.

The grave

Workmen quarrying basalt at Oberkassel in 1914 uncovered a grave containing the skeletons of an older man (about 50) and a younger woman (about 25), sprinkled with red hematite and accompanied by carved bone and antler objects. The animal remains were initially stored with the human bones and only recognised as a dog decades later; the assemblage is now held by the LVR-LandesMuseum Bonn. Radiocarbon dating places the burial at roughly 14,200 years ago.

The dog

Reanalysis published in 2018 by Luc Janssens and colleagues showed the dog died as a young animal of about 27–28 weeks. Its teeth and bones bore signs of serious illness — probably canine distemper — suffered weeks before death, with enamel defects recording episodes of disease. The puppy would have needed intensive care, including cleaning, feeding and warmth, to survive as long as it did, and it had no practical working value. The authors argue this implies an emotional bond: Palaeolithic people cared for a sick puppy without expectation of benefit, and buried it with family members.

Significance

Together with genetic evidence placing dog domestication in the Late Pleistocene, the burial supports the view that dogs were companions as well as hunting partners from very early in the relationship. It remains the earliest secure archaeological example of a dog treated as part of a human social group.
