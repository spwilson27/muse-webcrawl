Thulium is a chemical element with the symbol Tm and atomic number 69. It is the thirteenth and third-last element in the lanthanide series. Like the other lanthanides, the most common oxidation state is +3, seen in its oxide, halides and other compounds. Thulium is the second-rarest lanthanide in the Earth's crust, after promethium.

History

Thulium was discovered by Swedish chemist Per Teodor Cleve in 1879 when he separated two new oxides from impure erbia: holmia and thulia. The element is named after Thule, a mythical island in the far north in ancient Greek and Roman literature, often associated with Scandinavia.

Properties

Pure thulium is a bright, silvery metal that tarnishes slowly in air. It is soft and malleable enough to be cut with a knife. Despite its rarity and high price, thulium is used as a radiation source in portable X-ray devices: thulium-170, bombarded with neutrons, serves as a lightweight gamma source. Thulium-doped materials are used in high-efficiency lasers operating near 2 micrometres, and thulium-doped fibres serve in fibre lasers and amplifiers.

Occurrence

Thulium is never found in nature in pure form; it occurs in small quantities in minerals such as monazite and gadolinite, typically around 0.5 parts per million in the Earth's crust.
