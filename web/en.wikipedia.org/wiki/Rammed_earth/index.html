Rammed earth


Illustration placeholder: stratified layers of a rammed earth wall

Rammed earth is a technique for constructing foundations, floors, and walls using compacted natural raw materials such as earth, chalk, lime, or gravel. It is an ancient method that has been revived in recent years as a sustainable building method.

Under its French name of pisé it is also a material. Rammed-earth buildings are found on every continent except Antarctica, in a wide range of environments including temperate, wet, semiarid desert, mountain, and tropical regions. The availability of suitable soil and a building design appropriate for local climatic conditions are the factors that favour its use.

Process

Making rammed-earth involves compacting a damp mixture of subsoil that has suitable proportions of sand, gravel, clay, and stabilizer, if any, into a formwork. Historically, additives such as animal blood were used to stabilise it, but modern practice may add a small percentage of cement. The wall is compacted in layers (courses) of about 10 to 25 cm, each tamped to roughly half its original height. After a form is filled, it is immediately removed and the next course begun; the wall is left to cure and dry.

Properties

Rammed earth walls are thick (typically 30–60 cm), strong in compression, and provide significant thermal mass, moderating indoor temperatures. The material is proof against fire and resistant to termite damage, and it is both recyclable and low in embodied energy. Its weaknesses are susceptibility to water erosion — managed by overhanging eaves and protective coatings — and relatively low tensile strength compared with modern framed construction, which can matter in earthquake-prone regions unless the walls are reinforced.

History

The technique is ancient: rammed-earth (hangtu) foundations in China date to the Neolithic era, and much of the Great Wall of China is of rammed earth. In Europe, rammed-earth (pisé) building is widespread in France, Germany, and Spain, and was promoted in Britain in the 19th century for cottages. A revival began in the 1970s, and codes now exist in countries including Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and the United States (where a 2006 ASTM standard covers it).
