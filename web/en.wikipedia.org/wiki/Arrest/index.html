Arrest

An arrest is the act of apprehending a person and taking them into custody, usually because they have been suspected of, or observed, committing a crime. After being taken into custody, the person may be questioned further and may be charged and required to appear in court. An arrest is a procedure in a criminal justice system, and in many jurisdictions it may also be made for the purpose of bringing a person before a court to answer for civil contempt or other matters.

Legal requirements

In the United States, an arrest is a seizure of the person governed by the Fourth Amendment. Police may arrest when they have probable cause to believe the person has committed an offence, or pursuant to an arrest warrant issued by a neutral magistrate. Warrants are generally required for arrests inside a person's home (Payton v. New York), but most arrests in public places are made without one. An arrest is more intrusive than an investigatory stop based on reasonable suspicion, which permits only a brief detention.

Private citizens may in many jurisdictions effect a "citizen's arrest", typically for a felony committed in their presence. An arrest made without legal justification constitutes the tort (and sometimes the crime) of false arrest or false imprisonment.

Procedure after arrest

Following arrest, suspects are typically searched (a search incident to arrest is permitted under Chimel v. California), transported to a station, and "booked" — photographed, fingerprinted and recorded. Before custodial interrogation, officers must give Miranda warnings advising the suspect of the right to silence and to counsel. The arrested person must be brought before a magistrate without unnecessary delay; in County of Riverside v. McLaughlin (1991), the Supreme Court held that a judicial probable-cause determination within 48 hours will generally satisfy this requirement. At the first appearance or arraignment, charges are read, counsel is appointed if necessary, and bail or other release conditions are set.

Powers and limitations

Officers may use reasonable force to effect an arrest; the use of deadly force against a fleeing suspect is constitutionally limited (Tennessee v. Garner, 1985). Arrest records are generated even when no charges follow, and their collateral consequences — for employment, housing and immigration status — have prompted expungement and "ban the box" reforms in various jurisdictions. Arrestees typically have the right to be informed of the reason for the arrest, to communicate with counsel and family, and to be free from prolonged detention without charge.
