Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher

Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, Prince of Wahlstatt (16 December 1742 – 12 September 1819), was a Prussian Generalfeldmarschall (field marshal) who led the Prussian army in the wars against Napoleon in 1813–1815 and, with the Duke of Wellington, commanded the allied forces that defeated Napoleon at the Battle of Waterloo in 1815. Fiery, profane, half-educated and adored by his soldiers, who called him Marschall Vorwärts — "Marshal Forwards" — Blücher embodied the Prussian war of liberation: beaten again and again, at Lützen, Bautzen, Ligny and a dozen lesser fields, he always returned to the attack, and his refusal, at 72 years of age and half-crushed from a fall at Ligny, to abandon Wellington two days later decided the Waterloo campaign.

Early career

Blücher ran away to the Swedish army at 14, was captured by Prussian hussars in 1760, and entered Prussian service instead, serving under Frederick the Great in the Seven Years' War. Cashiered in 1773 after a insolent letter of resignation ("Captain von Blücher can take himself off to the devil"), he farmed in Silesia for fifteen years before being recalled after Frederick's death. He distinguished himself as a cavalry commander in the wars against revolutionary France, and in 1806, in Prussia's catastrophe, fought a stubborn retreat to Lübeck before surrendering at Ratekau. Exchanged for the French general Victor, he spent the years of Prussian humiliation agitating for reform and revenge, his eccentricities — he believed at intervals that he was pregnant by an elephant, a delusion politely managed by his staff — overlooked for his energy.

The Wars of Liberation, 1813–14

In 1813, at seventy, Blücher was given the Army of Silesia, with the brilliant Gneisenau as his chief of staff — the partnership, fire and brain together, that directed Prussian operations for the rest of the wars. Beaten at Lützen and Bautzen in the spring, he destroyed Macdonald's army at the Katzbach in August, crossed the Elbe, and joined the converging allied armies that crushed Napoleon at Leipzig in October — the "Battle of the Nations". In the winter campaign of 1814 he invaded France and, despite repeated defeats at Napoleon's hands in the Six Days' Campaign, kept advancing on Paris, which fell in March. He was made Prince of Wahlstatt and fêted in London with Wellington that summer.

Hundred Days and Waterloo

Recalled in 1815, Blücher concentrated the Prussian Army of the Lower Rhine in Belgium. On 16 June Napoleon attacked him at Ligny; leading a cavalry charge in person, Blücher was unhorsed, ridden over by cavalry of both sides, and rescued insensible by his aide-de-camp Nostitz. His chief of staff Gneisenau — distrustful of Wellington but faithful to the alliance — directed the retreat on Wavre, keeping the Prussians within reach. Blücher, bruised, dosed with schnapps and liniment, insisted on marching to Wellington's aid: on 18 June, after a gruelling march through mud, his corps arrived on the Waterloo battlefield from the late afternoon, fell on Napoleon's right flank at Plancenoit, and turned defeat into rout in the pursuit that followed — a pursuit Blücher drove through the night, arguing that the allies must not "let the enemy breathe". He met Wellington at the inn of La Belle Alliance late that evening. He entered Paris for the second time in July and wanted, among other things, to blow up the Pont d'Iéna — Wellington forbade it.

Death and legacy

Blücher retired to his Silesian estate of Krieblowitz and died there in 1819. Prussia and later Germany made him a national icon: statues in Berlin and Rostock, warships in both world wars named for him, and the unique Star of the Grand Cross of the Iron Cross created for him — known as the Blücherstern — whose only later recipient was Hermann Göring. His reputation rests less on tactical genius, where Gneisenau's staff work guided him, than on an indomitable offensive spirit that made him, among all Napoleon's opponents, the one who never accepted that a beaten army was a finished one.
