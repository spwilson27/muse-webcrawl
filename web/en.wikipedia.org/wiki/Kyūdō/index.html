Kyūdō (弓道, "the way of the bow") is the Japanese martial art of archery. Evolved from kyūjutsu, the samurai's battlefield skill with the bow, kyūdō in its modern form is practised as a discipline of physical, moral, and spiritual development. Its practitioners, kyūdōka, seek shin-zen-bi — truth, goodness, and beauty — holding that a correct and beautiful shot, performed with a pure state of mind, will naturally strike the target.

Equipment and practice

The yumi is an exceptionally long bow, over two metres, traditionally laminated from bamboo and wood with an asymmetric grip below the centre. Arrows (ya) are of bamboo with feather fletching. Shooting is done with the right hand protected by a stiff leather glove (yugake), using a thumb draw. Most practice takes place in a dōjō at the standard distance of 28 metres, with formal shooting governed by the sharei ceremonial form.

The shooting sequence is codified as the hassetsu, eight stages: ashibumi (footing), dōzukuri (body forming), yugamae (bow readiness), uchiokoshi (raising the bow), hikiwake (drawing apart), kai (the full draw, "the meeting"), hanare (the release), and zanshin (remaining form and spirit after the shot).

History

Archery held ritual and military importance in Japan from prehistory; mounted archery (yabusame) survives as Shinto ceremony. With the Tokugawa peace, bowmanship became a contemplative discipline, influenced by Zen — an association popularised in the West by Eugen Herrigel's Zen in the Art of Archery (1948). The modern federation standardised practice after the Second World War; today kyūdō is practised worldwide under the International Kyudo Federation.
