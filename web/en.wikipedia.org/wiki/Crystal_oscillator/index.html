Crystal oscillator

A crystal oscillator is an electronic oscillator circuit that uses a piezoelectric crystal, usually quartz, as a frequency-selective element. The crystal's mechanical resonance is extremely stable, so the oscillator produces a precise periodic signal used as a clock in digital integrated circuits, as a reference in radio transmitters and receivers, and in quartz clocks and watches.

Principle

Quartz exhibits piezoelectricity: it deforms when an electric field is applied and generates a voltage when deformed. Cut to a precise orientation and size, a crystal vibrates at a sharply defined resonant frequency with a very high quality factor (Q). In the common Pierce oscillator, the crystal is placed in the feedback path of an amplifier so the circuit oscillates at the crystal's resonant frequency. Standard frequencies include 32.768 kHz for watch crystals — chosen because 215 cycles equal one second.

History and applications

The first crystal oscillator was built by Alexander M. Nicholson in 1917, and Walter Guyton Cady developed practical crystal-controlled oscillators in the early 1920s. Quartz oscillators displaced tuning forks and pendulums in timekeeping, enabled stable radio channels, and remain ubiquitous in computers, microcontrollers and communications equipment, supplemented in precision roles by oven-controlled (OCXO) and temperature-compensated (TCXO) variants.
