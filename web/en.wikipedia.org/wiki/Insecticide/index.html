Insecticide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Insecticide
Pesticides formulated to kill, harm, repel, or mitigate insects.

Insecticides are pesticides used to kill insects, or to disrupt their growth, development, or reproduction. They include ovicides and larvicides against eggs and larvae, adulticides, insect growth regulators, and repellents. Insecticides are used in agriculture, public health (especially mosquito control against malaria and dengue), veterinary medicine, and households.

Classes

The major chemical classes are the organochlorines (e.g., DDT, now widely banned), organophosphates and carbamates (acetylcholinesterase inhibitors), pyrethroids (synthetic analogues of pyrethrins from chrysanthemum flowers, acting on sodium channels), neonicotinoids (nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonists such as imidacloprid, restricted in some jurisdictions because of effects on pollinators), and others including spinosyns, diamides, and avermectins. Biological insecticides exploit the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), insect viruses, and entomopathogenic fungi; Bt toxin genes have also been engineered into crops such as maize and cotton.

Modes of action

Most insecticides act on the insect nervous system, but growth regulators such as methoprene mimic juvenile hormone and prevent maturation, while chitin synthesis inhibitors prevent proper molting. Because resistance evolves quickly, insecticide resistance management rotates modes of action classified by the Insecticide Resistance Action Committee (IRAC).

Environmental and health effects

Non-target effects on pollinators, aquatic invertebrates, birds, and natural pest enemies, together with human exposure risks, drive regulation of registration, residues, and application. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) publicized the ecological harms of organochlorines and spurred modern environmental regulation. Integrated pest management (IPM) reduces reliance on insecticides by combining monitoring, biological control, cultural practices, and targeted applications.
