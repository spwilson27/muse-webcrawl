Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion is a series of reports prepared by the Scientific Assessment Panel of the Montreal Protocol, coordinated by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), with contributions from NASA, NOAA, and the European Commission. Published roughly every four years, the assessments summarise the state of scientific understanding of stratospheric ozone depletion and provide the evidence base for decisions by the parties to the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer.

History

The assessment process began in the 1980s, when international scientific reviews — including major NASA- and WMO-led reports — established that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and related chemicals were destroying stratospheric ozone. The discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole in 1985 by the British Antarctic Survey intensified concern. The first full Scientific Assessment under the Montreal Protocol process appeared in 1989, and subsequent assessments followed in the 1990s and 2000s, with major reports in 2006, 2010, 2014, 2018, and 2022.

Findings

Successive assessments have documented the peak and subsequent decline of ozone-depleting substances in the atmosphere following the phase-out mandated by the Montreal Protocol and its amendments. The 2014 and 2018 assessments reported the first fingerprints of healing in the Antarctic ozone layer. The 2022 assessment concluded that, if current policies remain in place, ozone values are expected to return to 1980 levels around 2066 over Antarctica, around 2045 over the Arctic, and around 2040 for the global average. The assessments also examine the climate benefits of the Protocol and of the 2016 Kigali Amendment, which phases down hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), powerful greenhouse gases introduced as substitutes for ozone-depleting substances.

Role

The assessments are written and reviewed by hundreds of scientists worldwide and are widely regarded as a model of science-policy interface: an authoritative, consensus, peer-reviewed synthesis delivered directly to policymakers. They inform adjustments and amendments to the Protocol's control schedules and track compliance by monitoring atmospheric concentrations of controlled substances — a process that in 2018 helped detect unexpected emissions of CFC-11 traced to eastern Asia, which subsequently declined after enforcement action.
