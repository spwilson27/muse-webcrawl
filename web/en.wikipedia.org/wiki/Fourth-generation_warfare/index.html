Fourth-generation warfare (4GW) is conflict characterised by a blurring of the lines between war and politics, soldier and civilian. The term was first used in 1989 by a team of United States analysts, including William S. Lind, in a Marine Corps Gazette article describing the return of warfare to a decentralised form, in which the major participants are not nation-states but rather violent non-state actors and transnational networks.

The generations model

In the framework, the first generation corresponds to the tactics of line and column of the smoothbore-musket era; the second to massed firepower and attrition epitomised by the First World War; the third to manoeuvre warfare — the Blitzkrieg emphasis on speed and infiltration. Fourth-generation war, by contrast, seeks to undermine the enemy's political will rather than destroy his forces, using guerrilla tactics, terrorism, propaganda, and the manipulation of media and law.

Characteristics

Analysts associate 4GW with dispersed, networked organisations; reliance on an ideological or cultural base; psychological operations aimed at an adversary's home population; and the use of a society's own legal and political freedoms against it. Examples commonly cited include the Vietnam War, the Soviet–Afghan War, and later insurgencies and terrorist campaigns.

Criticism

The theory has been criticised by military historians and theorists who argue the generations scheme oversimplifies history, and that 4GW relabels long-established insurgency and unconventional warfare rather than describing something new. Proponents respond that the novelty lies in the loss of the state's near-monopoly on organised violence.
