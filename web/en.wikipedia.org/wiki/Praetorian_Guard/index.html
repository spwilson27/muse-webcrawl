The Praetorian Guard (Latin: cohortes praetoriae) was the elite unit of the Imperial Roman army whose members served as personal bodyguards and intelligence agents for the Roman emperors. Established permanently by Augustus in 27 BC, the Guard was stationed in Rome — uniquely, the only substantial military force permitted in the capital — and over three centuries it grew from a bodyguard into a decisive political force that made and unmade emperors, until Constantine I disbanded it after the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in AD 312.

Praetorians depicted on a Roman relief.

Origins

During the Republic, commanders had kept a cohors praetoria, a guard named after the praetorium, the commander's tent. Augustus retained nine cohorts of about 500 men each, initially dispersing most of them in towns around Rome with only three billeted in the city. Under Tiberius, at the urging of the prefect Sejanus, the Guard was concentrated in a single fortified camp, the Castra Praetoria on the Viminal Hill (AD 23) — a concentration that greatly increased its political weight.

Role and privileges

Praetorians guarded the emperor and his family, policed Rome, and accompanied the emperor on campaign. Recruited at first mainly from Italians, they enjoyed shorter service (16 years against 20–25 for legionaries), substantially higher pay, and generous accession donatives from new emperors. Command was held by one or two equestrian praetorian prefects, who in time also accumulated judicial and administrative powers.

Kingmakers

The Guard proclaimed Claudius emperor in AD 41 after murdering Caligula, and thereafter repeatedly intervened in succession crises. Its most notorious episode came in AD 193, when the Guard murdered the emperor Pertinax and literally auctioned the throne to the wealthy senator Didius Julianus — a scandal that provoked civil war and led Septimius Severus to disband and reconstitute the Guard from his own legionaries, opening it to provincials. In the third century praetorian prefects such as Philip the Arab themselves seized the throne. Constantine, after defeating Maxentius in 312, finally abolished the Guard and demolished its camp.

Legacy

"Praetorian" has become a byword for an over-powerful palace guard prone to intervening in politics; political scientists speak of "praetorianism" in states where the military habitually displaces civilian rule.
