The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 was an agreement between the United States and bands of the Lakota, Dakota (Yanktonai and Santee) and Arapaho, concluded at Fort Laramie in present-day Wyoming. It ended Red Cloud's War, in which the Lakota and their allies had successfully resisted U.S. forts along the Bozeman Trail in the Powder River Country.

Terms

The treaty established the Great Sioux Reservation — all of present-day South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the sacred Black Hills — for the "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux, and designated the Powder River Country as unceded Indian territory in which whites were forbidden to settle. The United States abandoned the Bozeman Trail forts, promised annuities, provisions, education and farming assistance, and agreed that no further land cession would be valid without the signatures of three-quarters of adult Sioux males.

Violation and aftermath

The discovery of gold in the Black Hills in 1874, followed by a rush of miners, led the United States to demand the Hills; when negotiations failed it seized them by the Agreement of 1876, ratified in 1877, without obtaining the required consent. The Great Sioux War of 1876 — including the Battle of the Little Bighorn — followed. In United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980), the U.S. Supreme Court held the taking unconstitutional and awarded compensation, which the Sioux have refused to accept, maintaining their claim to the Black Hills themselves.
