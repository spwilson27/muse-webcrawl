Solar constant

The solar constant is a flux density measuring mean solar electromagnetic radiation (total solar irradiance) per unit area. It is measured on a surface perpendicular to the rays, one astronomical unit (au) from the Sun (roughly the distance from the Sun to the Earth). The solar constant is an average; the measured value is approximately 1,361 W/m².

Measurement

Early estimates by Claude Pouillet (1837) and John Herschel gave values near 1.2 kW/m². Ground measurements must correct for atmospheric absorption; modern values come from absolute cavity radiometers aboard satellites (ACRIM, VIRGO/SOHO, TIM on SORCE and TSIS-1), whose records since 1978 show the total solar irradiance varying by about 0.1% in step with the solar activity cycle.

Calculation and variation

The constant follows from the solar luminosity divided by the surface area of a sphere of radius 1 au. Actual irradiance at Earth varies ±3.3% annually because of the eccentricity of Earth's orbit, and minutely with sunspots and faculae. Long-term changes are studied for their role in climate; the eclipse expeditions of the 19th century were among the early occasions for precise solar physics.

Other planets

Values scale with the inverse square of distance: about 2,601 W/m² at Venus and 590 W/m² at Mars — the basis of the heat gains considered in spacecraft thermal design and planetary energy budgets.
