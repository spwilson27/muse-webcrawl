Hyacinthoides non-scripta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hyacinthoides non-scripta (formerly Endymion non-scriptus or Scilla non-scripta) is a bulbous perennial plant in the asparagus family Asparagaceae, known commonly as the common bluebell or simply bluebell. It is native to the Atlantic fringe of western Europe, from northern Spain to the British Isles, and is famed for forming dense carpets of violet-blue flowers in spring woodland.

Description

The plant grows from a bulb to about 30–50 cm tall, producing linear, strap-shaped leaves and a nodding flower stem (raceme) bearing 5–12 pendent, bell-shaped flowers. The flowers are a rich violet-blue (rarely pink or white), with strongly recurved tepals and cream-coloured pollen, and are sweetly scented. The one-sided, nodding raceme and cream pollen distinguish it from the introduced Spanish bluebell (Hyacinthoides hispanica), whose flowers are paler, less scented, borne around the stem, and have blue pollen.

Distribution and ecology

The common bluebell is strongly associated with ancient woodland, where it can dominate the ground flora in spring before the tree canopy closes, but it also grows in hedgerows, bracken, coastal cliffs and upland grassland. The British Isles hold a large proportion — often estimated at around half — of the world population. Flowering occurs mainly in April and May. The species is pollinated chiefly by bees, and its seeds are spread over short distances.

Conservation

In the United Kingdom the common bluebell is protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: it is an offence to uproot wild bulbs or to trade in wild bulbs and seed without a licence. Threats include habitat loss, bulb collection, and hybridisation with the Spanish bluebell, producing the fertile hybrid Hyacinthoides × massartiana, which is common in gardens and increasingly found in the wild.

In culture

The bluebell is one of Britain's best-loved wildflowers and is frequently voted among the nation's favourites; "bluebell woods" are a celebrated feature of the British spring. In folklore, bluebells were associated with fairies and were sometimes called "fairy flowers".
