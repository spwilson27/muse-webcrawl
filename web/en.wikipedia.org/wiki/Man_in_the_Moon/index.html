Man in the Moon

The Man in the Moon is an imaginary figure resembling a human face, head or body that observers in many cultures perceive in the disc of the full Moon. The perception is an example of pareidolia: the dark lunar maria (basaltic plains) and lighter highlands form patterns that the eye resolves into features.

Interpretations

In European tradition the figure is usually a man's face in profile or full-face, or a man carrying a bundle of sticks or thorns on his back, sometimes accompanied by a dog. Medieval folklore held that he was banished to the Moon for gathering sticks on the Sabbath, a story alluded to in Shakespeare and in nursery rhymes. In East Asian traditions the figure is more often a rabbit — the Jade Rabbit pounding the elixir of life for the moon goddess Chang'e — while Mesoamerican and other traditions variously see a rabbit, a woman, or other figures.

What the features are

The commonly perceived "face" is formed by Mare Imbrium and Mare Serenitatis as eyes, with other maria forming the nose and mouth region. Because the Moon is tidally locked, the same face is always presented to Earth, so the pattern — and the folklore built on it — is stable.

In culture

The Man in the Moon has appeared for centuries in literature, from Dante and Shakespeare to children's picture books, and remains a familiar motif in songs, logos and films, including the famous image of a rocket landing in the Moon's eye in Georges Méliès' A Trip to the Moon (1902).
