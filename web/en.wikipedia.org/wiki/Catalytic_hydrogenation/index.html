Catalytic hydrogenation

Hydrogenation is a chemical reaction between molecular hydrogen (H2) and another compound or element, usually in the presence of a catalyst such as nickel, palladium, or platinum. Catalytic hydrogenation — the addition of hydrogen to unsaturated organic compounds, particularly alkenes and alkynes, reducing them to alkanes — is one of the most important applications of catalysis in chemistry, discovered in its modern form by Paul Sabatier in the late 1890s, work for which he shared the 1912 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Mechanism and catalysts

In heterogeneous hydrogenation, hydrogen and the unsaturated substrate adsorb onto the surface of a finely divided metal catalyst, such as palladium on carbon (Pd/C), platinum oxide (Adams's catalyst), or Raney nickel. The hydrogen is split into atoms bound to the surface, which are transferred stepwise to the double or triple bond. Homogeneous catalysts, such as Wilkinson's catalyst (a rhodium complex), perform the same chemistry in solution and allow finer control of selectivity. Addition occurs predominantly from the same face of the molecule (syn addition).

Industrial applications

The largest-scale use of hydrogenation is the partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils to produce margarine and shortening, a process that converts liquid oils into semi-solid fats but can also create trans fatty acids, now restricted in many countries for health reasons. Hydrogenation is also essential in petroleum refining (hydrocracking and desulfurisation), in the production of ammonia feedstocks and methanol, and throughout pharmaceutical and fine-chemical synthesis, where it reduces alkenes, alkynes, aromatic rings, nitro groups, and carbonyl compounds. Selective semi-hydrogenation of alkynes to (Z)-alkenes is achieved with the poisoned Lindlar catalyst.
