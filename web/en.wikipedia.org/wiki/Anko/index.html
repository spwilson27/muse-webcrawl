Anko

Anko

Type: Sweet bean paste

Place of origin: Japan (with roots in China)

Main ingredients: Azuki beans, sugar

Anko (Japanese: 餡子), also known as an, is a sweet paste made from azuki beans (and sometimes other beans) that have been boiled, mashed and sweetened with sugar. It is a staple ingredient of Japanese confectionery (wagashi) and is also used in Chinese, Korean and other East and Southeast Asian desserts.

Types

Anko is commonly classified by texture. Tsubuan is a chunky paste in which the beans are boiled and sweetened whole, retaining their skins and much of their shape. Tsubushian is partially mashed, while koshian is a smooth paste made by passing the cooked beans through a sieve to remove the skins. A further refined variety, sarashian, is made from reconstituted dried anko powder.

Other bean pastes

Besides azuki, anko can be made from white kidney beans (shiroan, "white bean paste"), which has a milder, creamier flavour and is often coloured for decorative sweets. Chestnut paste (kurian) and sweet potato paste are related preparations, and regional variants use sesame or other flavourings.

Uses

Anko fills or accompanies a wide range of foods: dorayaki pancakes, daifuku and other mochi sweets, taiyaki fish-shaped cakes, anpan bread rolls, shaved-ice desserts such as kakigōri and uji kintoki, and the sweet red bean soup zenzai (or shiruko). In Korea the paste, called patso, appears in patbingsu and rice cakes; in China, red bean paste fills mooncakes, tangyuan and steamed buns.

History

Bean pastes arrived in Japan from China, and sweetened anko developed alongside the tea ceremony and the wagashi culture of the Edo period. Because Buddhist vegetarian cuisine avoided animal products, bean pastes became a versatile substitute filling, sometimes seasoned in savoury forms before sugar became widely available and cheap.
