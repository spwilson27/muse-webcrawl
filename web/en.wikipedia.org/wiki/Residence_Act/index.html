Residence Act

The Residence Act of 1790, officially titled An Act for establishing the temporary and permanent seat of the Government of the United States, was a United States federal statute adopted during the second session of the First United States Congress and signed into law by President George Washington on July 16, 1790. It provided for a national capital and permanent seat of government to be established at a site along the Potomac River, in what became Washington, D.C., and designated Philadelphia as the temporary capital for ten years.

Background

Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress had met in several cities, and the Pennsylvania Mutiny of 1783 — in which unpaid soldiers surrounded Independence Hall — convinced many leaders that the federal government needed a district under its own control. The Constitution of 1787 authorised Congress to create a federal district "not exceeding ten miles square". The question of where to place it became entangled with the dispute over Alexander Hamilton's plan for the federal government to assume state debts; the resulting bargain is often called the Compromise of 1790, reportedly arranged at a dinner hosted by Thomas Jefferson between Hamilton and James Madison.

Provisions

The Act authorised the President to appoint three commissioners to survey and acquire land along the Potomac between the Eastern Branch (Anacostia River) and the Conococheague Creek, and to provide suitable buildings for Congress, the President and public offices by the first Monday in December 1800. Maryland and Virginia ceded land and appropriated funds for the public buildings. The government moved from Philadelphia to the new district in late 1800.
