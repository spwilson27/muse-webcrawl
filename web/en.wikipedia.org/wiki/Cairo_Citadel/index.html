The Citadel of Cairo (Arabic: قلعة صلاح الدين الأيوبي, Qalaʿat Salāḥ ad-Dīn) is a medieval Islamic fortification in Cairo, Egypt, built by Saladin (Salah al-Din al-Ayyubi) between 1176 and 1183 to protect the city from Crusader attacks. Perched on a spur of the Mokattam Hills overlooking the city, it was the seat of Egypt's rulers for nearly 700 years, from the Ayyubid dynasty through the Mamluks and Ottomans until the mid-19th century.

History

Saladin's walls and towers were extended by his successors, particularly Sultan al-Kamil, and the Citadel was repeatedly embellished with palaces, mosques and halls. The most famous structure within the walls is the Mosque of Muhammad Ali, built between 1830 and 1848 in Ottoman style, whose alabaster-clad walls and twin minarets dominate Cairo's skyline. Other notable buildings include the Mosque of Sultan al-Nasir Muhammad (1318), the Mosque of Sulayman Pasha (1528), the Mamluk-era palace remains, and the al-Gawhara Palace.

In 1811 the Citadel was the scene of Muhammad Ali's massacre of the Mamluk beys, whom he had invited to a banquet — an event that ended Mamluk power in Egypt. Today the Citadel is a preserved historic site and major tourist attraction forming part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of Historic Cairo, containing several museums including the Egyptian National Military Museum.
