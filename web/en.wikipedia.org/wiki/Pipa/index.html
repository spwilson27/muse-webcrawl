The pipa (Chinese: 琵琶; pinyin: pípá) is a traditional Chinese musical instrument: a four-stringed, pear-shaped lute with a short neck and a fretted wooden body. It has been played in China for nearly two thousand years and is one of the most prominent instruments in Chinese music.

History

The pipa developed from Central Asian lutes carried along the Silk Road, combined with earlier Chinese instruments. It reached its classic form during the Tang dynasty (618–907), when it was a favourite court instrument; Tang-era depictions of pipa players appear in the murals of the Mogao Caves at Dunhuang. The instrument spread to Japan as the biwa, to Korea as the bipa, and to Vietnam as the đàn tỳ bà.

Playing technique

The pipa is held upright on the lap and played with the fingernails (historically a plectrum) using all five fingers. Its technique is extraordinarily rich, with dozens of named fingerings producing tremolos, glissandos, harmonics, percussive effects, and dramatic tone-painting. Famous solo pieces include "Ambush from Ten Sides" (Shimian Maifu), a martial depiction of a Han-dynasty battle, and "White Snow in Early Spring".

Modern era

The modern pipa was redesigned in the 20th century with more frets (19–26), steel strings, and equal temperament, making it suitable for large Chinese orchestras. Renowned contemporary players and composers, such as Wu Man, have brought the instrument into Western concert halls and cross-cultural collaborations.
