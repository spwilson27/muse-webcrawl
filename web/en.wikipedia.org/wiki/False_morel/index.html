False morel

Gyromitra esculenta, the best-known of the false morels.

The name false morel is given to several species of mushroom which bear a resemblance to the highly regarded true morels of the genus Morchella. Like morels, false morels are members of the Pezizales, but within that group represent several unrelated genera, most notably Gyromitra, Verpa, and to some extent Helvella.

Identification

True morels have caps that are hollow and attached to the stem at the base, with a honeycomb-like surface of pits and ridges. False morels, in contrast, typically have caps that are wrinkled, lobed, or brain-like rather than truly pitted, and are internally chambered or filled with cottony tissue rather than hollow. A common field rule states that if a morel-like mushroom is not hollow from top to bottom, it is potentially dangerous.

Toxicity

Gyromitra species, particularly Gyromitra esculenta, contain the toxin gyromitrin, which the body metabolises into monomethylhydrazine, a compound also used as rocket fuel. Poisoning can cause vomiting, diarrhoea, dizziness, and in severe cases liver damage, convulsions, and death. Despite this, G. esculenta is eaten in parts of Scandinavia and Eastern Europe after careful parboiling, though modern food safety authorities discourage the practice.

Genera

The principal genera called false morels include:

  • Gyromitra – brain mushrooms, beefsteak mushrooms
  • Verpa – thimble caps, early morels
  • Helvella – elfin saddles
  • Disciotis

Foragers are advised never to eat a morel-like fungus raw and to confirm identification carefully before consumption.
