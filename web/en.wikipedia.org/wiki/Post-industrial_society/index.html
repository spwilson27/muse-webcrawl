Post-industrial society

Coined by: Daniel Bell (1973)
Preceded by: Industrial society
Key sectors: Services, information, knowledge

A post-industrial society is a society in which the service sector generates more wealth than the manufacturing sector, following the decline of heavy industry. The term was popularised by the sociologist Daniel Bell in his 1973 book The Coming of Post-Industrial Society.

Bell's model

Bell argued that post-industrial societies are characterised by: a shift from goods production to services; the dominance of professional and technical occupations; the centrality of theoretical knowledge as a source of innovation and policy; orientation toward controlling and assessing technology; and the rise of new "intellectual technologies" such as computing and information systems. Related concepts include the "information society" and "knowledge economy".

Criticism and debate

Critics note that manufacturing has often been relocated rather than eliminated, moving to developing economies, and that many service jobs are low-paid and insecure rather than professional. Others argue that Bell underestimated the continuing power of finance and property, and that digital platform work complicates the picture of a knowledge-based professional society. The concept nonetheless remains a standard frame for discussing deindustrialisation and economic change in Western countries since the 1970s.
