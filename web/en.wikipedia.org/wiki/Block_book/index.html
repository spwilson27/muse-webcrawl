Block book

[Hero image: a page from the Biblia Pauperum, a fifteenth-century block book, showing text and image cut into a single woodblock]

Block books, also called xylographica, are short books of up to about 50 leaves, printed in Europe in the second half of the 15th century using woodcut blocks. Both text and images were carved into a single block of wood for each page, and the impression was made by rubbing or pressing paper against the inked block. Nearly all block books are illustrated devotional works, often in Latin or the vernacular.

History

Block books appeared in the Netherlands and Germany from around the 1450s, shortly after the invention of printing with movable type by Johannes Gutenberg. They flourished for a few decades alongside typographic books, and it was long debated whether they preceded or followed Gutenberg's invention; the consensus is that the surviving examples are later, though they may derive from earlier Chinese and European woodblock traditions.

Famous block books include the Biblia pauperum ("Bible of the Poor"), the Ars moriendi ("Art of Dying"), the Apocalypse, the Ars memorandi, and the Speculum Humanae Salvationis (whose earliest editions combine xylographic and typographic pages).

Production

Most block books were printed on one side of the sheet only (anopisthographic printing), because the rubbing technique used to take impressions showed through or damaged the reverse; the blank sides were often pasted together. Water-based brownish ink and hand-colouring of the images were typical. Later editions were printed on presses with both sides used.

Decline

As movable type became cheaper and more flexible, block books lost their economic advantage and production largely ceased by around 1500, though single-leaf woodcuts continued to thrive.
