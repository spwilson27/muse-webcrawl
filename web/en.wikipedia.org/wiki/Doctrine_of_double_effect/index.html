Doctrine of double effect

Doctrine of double effect
FieldEthics, moral theology
OriginThomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae

The doctrine of double effect is a principle of applied ethics, rooted in the writings of Thomas Aquinas, holding that an action having both a good and a harmful effect may be morally permissible even though the same harm brought about intentionally would be forbidden.

Conditions

Classical formulations require that: (1) the action itself be good or morally neutral; (2) the good effect, not the bad effect, be intended; (3) the bad effect not be a means to the good effect; and (4) there be a proportionately serious reason for allowing the bad effect.

Applications

The doctrine is classically illustrated by the trolley problem variant distinguishing diverting a trolley (foreseeing deaths) from pushing a person into its path (intending harm as a means). It is invoked in medical ethics — for example, administering pain relief that foreseeably shortens life, or removing a cancerous uterus in pregnancy — in just war theory, and in Roman Catholic moral theology, which distinguishes direct from indirect effects, notably in debates over abortion.

Criticism

Critics question the moral relevance of the intention/foresight distinction and argue that outcomes described differently can be recharacterised to evade or trigger the doctrine, the so-called "closeness" problem.
