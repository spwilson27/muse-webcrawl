Supercontinuum

In optics, a supercontinuum is formed when a collection of nonlinear processes act together upon a pump beam to cause severe spectral broadening of the original beam — for example, converting an intense narrow-band laser pulse into bright white light spanning hundreds of nanometres, from the ultraviolet to the infrared. Supercontinuum generation is most commonly achieved by propagating femtosecond or picosecond laser pulses through a photonic-crystal fibre with carefully engineered dispersion.

Field: Nonlinear optics
Typical medium: Photonic-crystal fibre
Applications: Spectroscopy, OCT, frequency metrology

Mechanisms

The physics responsible depends on the pump pulse duration and fibre dispersion. For femtosecond pulses pumped in the anomalous-dispersion regime, the broadening is driven by soliton fission and the emission of dispersive waves; for longer pulses and continuous-wave pumping, modulation instability, Raman scattering and four-wave mixing dominate. The output can retain the high spatial coherence of a laser while offering the broad spectrum of a thermal source — a combination first dramatically demonstrated in photonic-crystal fibre in 2000.

Applications

Supercontinuum sources are used in optical coherence tomography, confocal and fluorescence microscopy, absorption spectroscopy, optical communications testing, and — because every comb line can be phase-locked — in frequency-comb metrology and precision measurement. Commercial supercontinuum lasers ("white light lasers") are now standard laboratory instruments.
