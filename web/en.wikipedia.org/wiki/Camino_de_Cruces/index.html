The Camino de Cruces (Spanish for "Road of the Crosses") is a historic cobblestone road across the Isthmus of Panama, built by the Spanish Empire to carry goods and treasure between the Pacific and Atlantic coasts. Together with the Camino Real to Portobelo, it formed the trans-isthmian route over which Peruvian silver and other wealth of the Spanish Main passed on its way to Spain.

Route and construction

The road ran from Panama City on the Pacific side inland to the town of Venta de Cruces on the Chagres River, from where goods continued by boat downriver to the Caribbean. Roughly 36 kilometres long in its land portion, the stone-paved track — in places several metres wide — crossed steep, jungle-covered terrain and was traversed by mule trains. Its paving of irregular cobbles, laid in the 16th and 17th centuries, survives in sections within what is now the Camino de Cruces National Park and Soberanía National Park.

History

The route was in use from the earliest decades of Spanish colonisation and was the principal alternative to the Camino Real, preferred in the rainy season when the Chagres was navigable. Treasure fleets' cargoes of silver from Peru were unloaded at Panama City and carried over the isthmus by mule; merchants flocked to the great trade fairs at Portobelo. The road was also the path of attackers: in 1671 Henry Morgan's buccaneers marched along it to sack Panama City. Traffic declined after the destruction of the old city, the rise of the Cape Horn route, and finally the construction of the Panama Railroad (1855) and the Panama Canal (1914).

Preservation

Surviving paved segments, bridge abutments, and way-station ruins are protected within national parks near modern Panama City. The route forms part of Panama's colonial heritage and was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the "Colonial Transisthmian Routes of Panama".
