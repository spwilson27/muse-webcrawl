Sipán

The Huaca Rajada platform at Sipán

Sipán is an archaeological site of the Moche culture located in the Zaña valley, near the modern town of Sipán in the Lambayeque Region of northern Peru. It became world famous in 1987 with the discovery of the intact royal tombs of the Lord of Sipán, one of the most important archaeological finds in the Americas.

The site

The site consists of the Huaca Rajada, an adobe platform complex built by the Moche, who flourished on the north coast of Peru from about AD 100 to 800. The platform contained a series of elite burials that escaped the looting (huaqueo) that had destroyed most Moche tombs.

Discovery

In February 1987 local looters quarrelled over gold objects taken from the site, and the police, alerted to the dispute, recovered spectacular artifacts including a golden mask. Archaeologist Walter Alva of the Brüning Museum in Lambayeque then led emergency excavations and uncovered the unlooted tomb of a high-ranking Moche ruler, dubbed the Lord of Sipán, dated to around AD 250–300.

The Lord of Sipán

The principal burial contained a man of about 35–45 years old, accompanied by sacrificed attendants, a child, two llamas, and a dog, along with hundreds of objects of gold, silver, copper, and turquoise: ear ornaments, nose ornaments, ceremonial knives (tumi), banners, and necklaces of peanut-shaped beads, half gold and half silver, symbolising the duality central to Andean thought. Further royal tombs were found in subsequent seasons, including those of the Priest of Sipán and the Old Lord of Sipán.

Museum

The finds are displayed in the Royal Tombs of Sipán Museum (Museo Tumbas Reales de Sipán), opened in 2002 in the city of Lambayeque, in a building evocative of a Moche truncated pyramid.
