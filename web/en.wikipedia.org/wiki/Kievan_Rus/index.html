Kievan Rus' was a loose federation of East Slavic and Finnic peoples in Eastern Europe from the late 9th to the mid-13th century, ruled by the Rurik dynasty. Encompassing a variety of polities and peoples, it was the largest state in Europe at the time, stretching from the Baltic Sea in the north to the Black Sea in the south.

According to the Primary Chronicle, the first ruler to unite the East Slavic lands was Oleg of Novgorod, who seized Kiev in 882 and made it the capital. The state's golden age came under Vladimir the Great (r. 980–1015), who adopted Orthodox Christianity from the Byzantine Empire in 988, and his son Yaroslav the Wise (r. 1019–1054), under whom the first East Slavic law code, the Russkaya Pravda, was compiled.

History

The origins of the Rus' are debated; the "Normanist" theory holds that Varangians (Vikings), called Rus', founded the state after being invited by the Slavic and Finnic tribes in 862. Rurik's successor Oleg moved the capital to Kiev. Trade along the route "from the Varangians to the Greeks" — down the Dnieper to Constantinople — made Kiev wealthy.

After Yaroslav's death in 1054, the federation fragmented into competing principalities including Galicia-Volhynia, Novgorod, and Vladimir-Suzdal. The Mongol invasion of 1237–1240 destroyed Kiev and ended the federation; many principalities became tributaries of the Golden Horde.

Legacy

Kievan Rus' is considered the cultural ancestor and point of origin for the modern East Slavic states of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus, all of which claim its heritage. Its conversion to Orthodoxy shaped the religion, alphabet (Cyrillic), art, and architecture of the region.
