Carrack

A Portuguese carrack of the 16th century

A carrack (Portuguese: nau; Spanish: nao) is a three- or four-masted ocean-going sailing ship that was developed in the 14th to 15th centuries in Europe, most notably in Portugal. Evolved from the single-masted cog, carracks were among the first vessels capable of extended ocean voyages in heavy seas, and they became the workhorses of the early Age of Discovery.

Characteristics

Carracks were square-rigged on the fore and main masts and lateen-rigged on the mizzenmast. Their hulls were carvel-built, with a characteristically high rounded stern, a large aftcastle, and a projecting forecastle overhanging the bow. These high castles gave fighting advantages in boarding actions but made the ships crank and poor at sailing to windward. Sizes grew over time: early carracks displaced a few hundred tons, while the great Portuguese India naus of the 16th century could exceed 1,000 tons, and some, such as the Madre de Deus, reportedly reached around 1,600 tons.

History

Portuguese carracks ran the India route around the Cape of Good Hope, and Spanish naos crossed the Atlantic and, later, the Pacific in the Manila galleon trade. Famous carracks include Columbus's flagship Santa María (often classed as a small carrack, or nao), Vasco da Gama's São Gabriel, and Henry VIII's Mary Rose and Henri Grâce à Dieu. By the late 16th and early 17th centuries the carrack was being superseded by the galleon, which lowered the forecastle and improved sailing qualities, and by the more economical fluyt for cargo.
