Decree of Kutná Hora

The Decree of Kutná Hora (Czech: Dekret kutnohorský) was issued by King Wenceslaus IV of Bohemia on 18 January 1409 at Kutná Hora. It changed the voting structure of Charles University in Prague, granting the Bohemian "nation" of the university three votes and the three other nations one vote each, reversing the previous arrangement in which the foreign (mainly German) nations together held the majority.

Background

Like other medieval universities, Charles University, founded in 1348, was organized into four "nations": the Bohemian, Bavarian, Saxon, and Polish nations, each holding one vote in university elections and decisions. By the early 15th century the university was divided by the religious controversies of the age, particularly over the teachings of John Wycliffe and the reform movement associated with Jan Hus. The Bohemian masters, who largely supported reform, were consistently outvoted by the foreign nations, and disputes intensified during the Western Schism, when King Wenceslaus sought the university's support for his policy of neutrality between the rival popes.

The decree and its consequences

Issued at Kutná Hora, where the king was residing, the decree of January 1409 gave the Bohemian nation three of the four votes in all university affairs. In response, the German masters and students staged a mass exodus from Prague in May 1409; tradition puts their number in the thousands. Many of the departing scholars and students went on to found the University of Leipzig in 1409, and others strengthened universities such as Heidelberg, Erfurt, and Kraków.

Charles University thus became effectively a Bohemian institution dominated by the reform party: Jan Hus was elected its rector in the autumn of 1409. The decree is regarded as a milestone in the rise of Czech national consciousness and as a key episode in the lead-up to the Hussite Wars, as the university became the intellectual centre of the Hussite movement.
