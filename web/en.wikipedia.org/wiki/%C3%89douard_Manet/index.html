Édouard Manet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Édouard Manet (23 January 1832 – 30 April 1883) was a French modernist painter. He was one of the first 19th-century artists to paint modern life, and a pivotal figure in the transition from Realism to Impressionism.

Life and work

Born into an upper-class Parisian family, Manet was steered by his father toward a career in law but turned instead to art, studying under Thomas Couture. His early masterworks, Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (1863) and Olympia (1863, exhibited 1865), caused enormous controversy: both confronted the viewer with direct gazes and contemporary figures placed in compositions borrowed from Old Masters such as Titian and Raimondi.

Manet's style — broad areas of contrasting tone, sketch-like brushwork, flattened space, and modern Parisian subjects from café-concerts to the execution of Emperor Maximilian — deeply influenced the younger Impressionists, though he never exhibited with them. His last major work, A Bar at the Folies-Bergère (1882), is a shimmering, ambiguous portrait of modern nightlife.

Legacy

Manet is widely regarded as the father of modern painting. His frank engagement with the present, and his self-conscious quotations of art history, made him a touchstone for artists from Monet and Cézanne to Picasso, and his estate sale and retrospective after his death in 1883 confirmed his central place in French art.
