Microburin

A microburin is a characteristic waste product of the manufacture of microliths, small stone tools of the Mesolithic and late Upper Palaeolithic. It is a small fragment of a flint blade or bladelet, typically triangular or trapezoidal in section, bearing a distinctive scar where it was snapped from the rest of the blade.

The microburin technique

To make a microlith of a desired length, a knapper first struck a shallow notch into the edge of a blade. The blade was then snapped at the notch — often over an anvil edge — producing a short, standardized segment. The discarded end piece, bearing the notch and part of the fracture surface, is the microburin. The retained segment was then retouched, often with an abrupt ("backed") edge, to form geometric microliths such as crescents, trapezes, and triangles used as components of composite tools and projectile points.

Significance

Because microburins are waste rather than finished tools, their presence at a site is strong evidence that microliths were manufactured there rather than merely used or lost. The technique is documented widely in Mesolithic Europe and in Epipalaeolithic industries of the Near East, such as the Kebaran and Natufian, where it was used to produce lunates and other geometric forms. Archaeologists distinguish proximal and distal microburins depending on which end of the blade was discarded.
