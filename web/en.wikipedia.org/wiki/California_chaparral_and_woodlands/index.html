California chaparral and woodlands

The California chaparral and woodlands is a terrestrial ecoregion of Mediterranean forests, woodlands and scrub covering much of the U.S. state of California and the north-western part of Baja California, Mexico. Occupying around 164,000 square kilometres, it forms part of the California Floristic Province, one of the world's recognised biodiversity hotspots, with thousands of endemic plant species. The ecoregion is characterised by a Mediterranean climate of hot, dry summers and cool, wet winters.

Plant communities

The ecoregion comprises a mosaic of communities. Chaparral is a dense shrubland of evergreen, sclerophyllous (hard-leaved) shrubs such as chamise, manzanita, ceanothus, scrub oak and toyon, adapted to summer drought and recurrent fire. Oak woodlands and savannas feature valley oak, blue oak and coast live oak over grassland understories. Coastal sage scrub grows near the shore in Southern California, while montane conifer forests of ponderosa pine, sugar pine and white fir occur at higher elevations, and closed-cone pine forests, cypress groves and serpentine barrens add further diversity.

Fire ecology

Chaparral is a fire-adapted ecosystem. Many shrubs resprout from root crowns after burning, while others germinate only after their seeds are exposed to fire or smoke. Historically burning at intervals of several decades, chaparral landscapes now face altered fire regimes: too-frequent fire, often set by human ignitions near growing cities, can convert chaparral to non-native grassland, while the wildfire risk at the wildland–urban interface is a major public-policy concern in California. Indigenous peoples managed these landscapes with cultural burning for millennia before European settlement.
