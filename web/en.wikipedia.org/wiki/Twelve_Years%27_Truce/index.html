Twelve Years' Truce

Signed9 April 1609, Antwerp
PartiesSpain and the Archdukes of the Southern Netherlands; the Dutch Republic
EffectSuspension of hostilities in the Eighty Years' War until 1621

The Twelve Years' Truce was a ceasefire in the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic, signed in Antwerp on 9 April 1609 and lasting until 1621. Spain, financially exhausted, treated the Republic as a sovereign state for the duration of the truce, an implicit recognition of Dutch independence.

Background and terms

Negotiations, mediated by France and England, were driven by Spanish bankruptcy and Dutch war-weariness. Under the terms, fighting in Europe ceased for twelve years; Spain dropped its demand that the Dutch cease trading in the Indies in practice, and removed restrictions on Catholic worship quietly shelved. The truce allowed the Dutch Republic to expand its overseas commerce enormously while Spain recovered.

Consequences

The truce sharpened internal Dutch divisions, contributing to the political crisis between Johan van Oldenbarnevelt and Maurice of Nassau that ended with Oldenbarnevelt's execution in 1619. When the truce expired in 1621, war resumed and continued until the Peace of Münster in 1648, in which Spain formally recognised Dutch independence.
