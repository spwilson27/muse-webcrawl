Headscarf

A headscarf is a scarf covering most or all of the top of a person's — usually a woman's — hair and head, leaving the face uncovered.

A headscarf is a scarf covering most or all of the top of the head and hair, leaving the face uncovered. Headscarves are worn for many reasons: religious observance, modesty, fashion, protection from sun, wind and dust, and cultural tradition. They may be made of light or heavy fabric, plain or elaborately patterned, and are tied or pinned in numerous regional styles.

Religious and cultural use

In Islam, the hijab is a headscarf worn by many Muslim women as an expression of modesty and faith; related garments include the niqab and chador. Orthodox Jewish women may wear a tichel or sheitel after marriage; Christian traditions include the mantilla in Spain and the head coverings of Anabaptist and some Orthodox women. In Eastern Europe the babushka is a traditional kerchief tied under the chin.

Fashion and practical use

In the 20th century the headscarf became a fashion accessory, popularised by figures such as Queen Elizabeth II, who wore silk scarves (notably by Hermès) for horse riding and country pursuits, and by film stars of the 1950s and 1960s. Wartime factory workers in Britain and America wore scarves to protect their hair from machinery. Headscarves remain common workwear in agriculture and food production and are used for protection during chemotherapy hair loss.

Controversies

Laws restricting religious headscarves in schools and public employment — notably in France, which banned conspicuous religious symbols in state schools in 2004 — have been politically contentious, while in Iran and Afghanistan the compulsory wearing of the hijab has generated protest movements, especially following the death of Mahsa Amini in 2022.
