Genetic rescue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic rescue is a conservation strategy in which the introduction of new genetic variation — through immigration or deliberate translocation of individuals into a small, inbred population — reduces inbreeding depression and increases the population's fitness and adaptive potential. The influx of immigrants masks deleterious recessive alleles, raises heterozygosity, and can reverse declining population trends.

Mechanism

Small, isolated populations lose genetic diversity through genetic drift and accumulate harmful mutations; mating among relatives then exposes these in homozygous form, causing inbreeding depression — reduced survival, fertility and disease resistance. Even a small number of unrelated immigrants can sharply reduce the frequency of homozygous deleterious genotypes in the next generation. Genetic rescue is distinguished from demographic rescue, in which immigrants help simply by adding numbers; genetic rescue specifically requires an improvement in fitness attributable to new alleles.

Case studies

  • Florida panther — by the early 1990s the population had fallen to roughly 20–30 adults showing kinked tails, heart defects and poor sperm quality. In 1995 eight female cougars from Texas were released in south Florida; hybrid kittens showed higher survival, defects declined, and the population subsequently grew several-fold.
  • Greater prairie chicken in Illinois — translocations from Kansas, Minnesota and Nebraska in the 1990s reversed a decline in egg hatchability caused by accumulated genetic load.
  • Swedish adder — an isolated, inbred population of adders recovered in numbers and offspring viability after the introduction of males from a larger population.
  • Bighorn sheep at the National Bison Range and the Scandinavian wolf — both showed measurable fitness gains following the arrival of one or a few immigrants.

Risks and debate

Genetic rescue must be weighed against the risk of outbreeding depression, in which immigrants are too genetically divergent, breaking up locally adapted gene complexes or introducing maladapted traits; disease transfer and behavioural disruption are additional concerns. Guidelines developed by conservation geneticists recommend using source populations that are genetically similar, historically connected, and from similar environments. Despite these cautions, meta-analyses have found that the fitness benefits of introducing immigrants into inbred populations are large and consistent, and genetic rescue has become a mainstream tool of conservation biology.
