Carnot cycle

The Carnot cycle on a pressure–volume diagram

The Carnot cycle is a theoretical ideal thermodynamic cycle described by the French physicist Sadi Carnot in his 1824 work Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire. It provides an upper bound on the efficiency that any classical thermodynamic engine can achieve during the conversion of heat into work, or conversely the efficiency of a refrigeration system. According to Carnot's theorem, no engine operating between two heat reservoirs can be more efficient than a Carnot engine operating between the same reservoirs.

The four stages

The cycle operating on a fixed amount of working fluid consists of four reversible processes:

  1. Reversible isothermal expansion at the hot temperature TH: the gas absorbs heat and does work on its surroundings.
  2. Reversible adiabatic expansion: the thermally insulated gas continues to expand, cooling to TC.
  3. Reversible isothermal compression at the cold temperature TC: heat is rejected to the cold reservoir.
  4. Reversible adiabatic compression: the gas is compressed back to its initial state, warming to TH.

Efficiency

The efficiency of a Carnot engine depends only on the absolute temperatures of the two reservoirs: η = 1 − TC/TH. This result helped lead Rudolf Clausius and Lord Kelvin toward the second law of thermodynamics and the concept of entropy. A reversed Carnot cycle gives the maximum coefficient of performance for refrigerators and heat pumps operating between the same temperatures.

History

Carnot developed the cycle while analysing the efficiency of steam engines, reasoning within the framework of the caloric theory of heat. Although the caloric theory was later abandoned, Carnot's central conclusions about efficiency survived and were placed on a firm foundation by Clausius and Kelvin around 1850.
