Düsseldorf school of painting

The Düsseldorf school of painting is a term referring to a group of painters who taught or studied at the Düsseldorf Academy of Art (Kunstakademie Düsseldorf) during the 19th century, roughly the 1830s–1860s, when the academy was directed by Wilhelm von Schadow. The school was characterised by finely detailed, often romantic landscapes and narrative paintings, meticulous drawing, and subdued, harmonious colouring.

Characteristics and artists

Under Schadow the academy emphasised academic history painting in the tradition of the Nazarenes. Carl Friedrich Lessing painted romantic historical scenes; Johann Wilhelm Schirmer and the brothers Andreas and Oswald Achenbach developed the school's celebrated landscape painting, based on careful studies from nature along the Rhine and in Italy. Genre painting flourished with artists such as Adolph Tidemand and Rudolf Jordan, and the school attracted many students from Scandinavia, Russia and the United States.

International influence

American students — including Albert Bierstadt, Worthington Whittredge, Sanford Robinson Gifford and Eastman Johnson — carried Düsseldorf's detailed landscape technique back to the United States, where it influenced the Hudson River School. The Düsseldorf Gallery in New York exhibited the school's work to American audiences. In Düsseldorf itself, younger artists founded the progressive Malkasten artists' association in 1848; the academy's dominance waned after Schadow's retirement and the rise of newer movements later in the century.
