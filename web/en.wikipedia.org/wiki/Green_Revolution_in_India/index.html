The Green Revolution in India was a period in the 1960s and 1970s during which Indian agriculture was transformed by the adoption of modern methods and technology, including high-yielding variety (HYV) seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides and expanded irrigation. It is part of the wider global Green Revolution associated with Norman Borlaug.

Led in India by agricultural scientist M. S. Swaminathan, often called the "Father of the Green Revolution in India", the programme ended the country's dependence on food imports — India had been living, in the phrase of the time, a "ship-to-mouth" existence under the US PL-480 food-aid programme — and made it self-sufficient, and eventually a net exporter, in foodgrains.

Background

At independence in 1947, India faced chronic food shortages, a legacy of the Bengal famine of 1943 and low-yielding traditional agriculture. The droughts and the Bihar famine of 1966–67 made reform urgent. In 1966 India imported some 18,000 tonnes of Mexican dwarf wheat seed developed by Borlaug's programme, the largest seed purchase of its kind at the time.

Implementation

The revolution concentrated first on irrigated areas with assured water — Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh — where dwarf wheat varieties, and later high-yielding rice, were introduced together with fertiliser subsidies, minimum support prices, agricultural universities, and expanded canal and tubewell irrigation. Wheat production rose from about 12 million tonnes in 1965 to over 20 million tonnes by 1970 and continued climbing; Punjab became known as the "granary of India".

Effects and criticism

The Green Revolution averted mass famine and underpinned India's food security, but it has been criticised for widening regional and interpersonal inequality (prosperous irrigated regions advanced faster than rain-fed ones), for environmental costs including groundwater depletion, soil salinisation and pesticide pollution, and for narrowing crop diversity by favouring wheat and rice over pulses, millets and oilseeds. Swaminathan himself later called for an "evergreen revolution" — productivity improvement in perpetuity without ecological harm.
