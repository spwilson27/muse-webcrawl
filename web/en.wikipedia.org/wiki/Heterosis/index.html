Heterosis

Heterosis, also called hybrid vigour, is the improved or increased function of any biological quality in a hybrid offspring. An offspring is heterotic if its traits are enhanced as a result of mixing the genetic contributions of its parents. These effects can be measured relative to the average of the parents (mid-parent heterosis) or relative to the better parent (best-parent heterosis). The converse phenomenon, in which hybrids perform worse than either parent, is called outbreeding depression; reduced fitness within a population from inbreeding is inbreeding depression.

History

The phenomenon was observed by plant breeders long before its genetics were understood. Charles Darwin documented the superior growth of cross-fertilised plants in The Effects of Cross and Self Fertilisation in the Vegetable Kingdom (1876). In the early twentieth century, George Harrison Shull and Edward M. East independently investigated heterosis in maize; Shull coined the term "heterosis" in 1914. The effect became the scientific basis of the hybrid seed corn industry, which transformed maize yields in the United States from the 1930s onward.

Genetic basis

Three main hypotheses have been proposed to explain heterosis. The dominance hypothesis attributes it to the masking of deleterious recessive alleles from one parent by dominant alleles from the other. The overdominance hypothesis holds that heterozygosity itself, at certain loci, produces a superior phenotype. The epistasis hypothesis invokes favourable interactions between genes at different loci. Modern genomic studies suggest all three mechanisms contribute, varying by trait and species; no single explanation accounts for all cases.

Applications

Heterosis is exploited commercially in hybrid varieties of maize, sorghum, rice, sunflower, and many vegetables, and in crossbred livestock and poultry. Hybrid rice, promoted extensively in China under Yuan Longping from the 1970s, produced yield gains of roughly 15–20% over conventional varieties and contributed substantially to food security. Because heterosis declines in subsequent generations as segregation breaks up favourable combinations, farmers typically purchase fresh F1 seed each season.
