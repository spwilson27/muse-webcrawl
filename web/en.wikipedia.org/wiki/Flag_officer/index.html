Flag officer

A flag officer is a commissioned officer in a navy or coast guard senior enough to be entitled to fly a personal flag designating the officer's presence and command. The term originated in the age of sail, when an admiral's flag, flown from the flagship, marked the commander of a fleet or squadron; by extension, "flag rank" denotes the admiral grades. In wider usage — particularly in the United States — "flag officer" has come to include general officers of the army, air force and marine corps as well, all of them members of the senior-most officer grades.

Ranks

In most navies the flag ranks, in ascending order, are commodore (in some services a senior appointment rather than a rank), rear admiral, vice admiral and admiral, with fleet admiral or admiral of the fleet as a wartime or honorary grade above them. In NATO's standardised system these correspond to grades OF-6 through OF-10. The United States Navy divides rear admiral into lower and upper halves (one and two stars); the U.S. services as a whole recognise one- to four-star flag and general officers, with five-star grades reserved by law for wartime.

The flag

A flag officer's personal flag typically bears the number of stars of the officer's rank (in American use) or a distinctive device such as the crossed swords and batons of British admirals or the St George's Cross of Royal Navy flag officers. The flag is hoisted aboard the officer's flagship and at shore headquarters, and is hauled down on relief, retirement or death in command. Naval etiquette prescribes gun salutes, side honours and other courtesies calibrated to flag rank.

Usage

Because flag officers command formations — fleets, numbered air forces, corps, major commands — rather than single units, the phrase also has a figurative sense: a "flag officer" of an organisation is one of its most senior figures. Promotion to flag rank is by selection from captains (or colonels) and is sharply competitive; in the modern U.S. services only a small fraction of officers attain it.
