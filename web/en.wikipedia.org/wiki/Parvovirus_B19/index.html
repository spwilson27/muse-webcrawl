Parvovirus B19, also called primate erythroparvovirus 1, is a small, non-enveloped, single-stranded DNA virus in the family Parvoviridae. It was the first (and until 2005 the only) known human parvovirus, discovered by chance in 1975 by Australian virologist Yvonne Cossart while screening blood samples — the name B19 comes from the code of one of the serum samples in the panel, number 19 in row B.

Infection

The virus spreads through respiratory secretions and blood products, and infects red blood cell precursors in the bone marrow via the P antigen (globoside) receptor. In healthy children it commonly causes fifth disease (erythema infectiosum), a mild illness with a characteristic "slapped cheek" rash. In adults, especially women, infection frequently causes acute symmetric joint pain and swelling.

Complications

Because the virus temporarily halts red blood cell production, it can cause a transient aplastic crisis in people with chronic haemolytic anaemias such as sickle cell disease. In immunocompromised patients it may cause chronic anaemia, and infection during pregnancy can lead to hydrops fetalis and miscarriage. Most infections are mild and immunity after infection is generally lifelong; by adulthood, a large proportion of the population carries antibodies.
