Bánh chưng

Bánh chưng is a traditional Vietnamese food made from glutinous rice, mung bean, and pork, wrapped in dong leaves (Phrynium placentarium) and shaped into a square. It is traditionally consumed during Tết, the Vietnamese Lunar New Year, when families prepare and boil the cakes together, often overnight, in the days before the holiday.

Legend

According to legend, bánh chưng was created by Prince Lang Liêu, the youngest son of the sixth Hùng King, who, unable to afford luxurious offerings, made a square cake from rice to represent the Earth, and a round cake (bánh giầy) to represent the Sky. Impressed by the meaning and the humble ingredients, the king chose Lang Liêu as his successor. The square shape of bánh chưng is thus traditionally said to symbolise the Earth in ancient Vietnamese cosmology.

Preparation

Glutinous rice is soaked overnight, then layered with seasoned split mung beans and fatty pork in a mould lined with dong leaves. The package is folded into a tight square, tied with bamboo strings, and boiled for many hours — traditionally eight to twelve — until the rice is cooked and the leaves impart a green tint and fragrance. After boiling, the cakes are pressed under weights to firm them and expel excess water.

Consumption and variations

Bánh chưng is unwrapped, cut into wedges with the tying strings, and eaten with pickled onions, pickled vegetables, or Vietnamese sausage (giò lụa); leftover slices are often pan-fried as bánh chưng rán. In southern Vietnam the analogous Tết cake is the cylindrical bánh tét, wrapped in banana leaves. Vegetarian and sweet versions substitute or omit the pork.
