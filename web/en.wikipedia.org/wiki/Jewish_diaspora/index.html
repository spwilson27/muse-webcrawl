The Jewish diaspora (alternatively, the dispersion) is the worldwide dispersion of Jews outside the Land of Israel. The Hebrew term galut ("exile") carries theological and cultural connotations of forced displacement and longing for return. Jewish communities outside Israel have existed since antiquity — by the Roman period, large diaspora populations lived in Babylonia, Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor, Greece, and Rome — and after the failed revolts against Rome, the centre of Jewish life shifted decisively to the diaspora for nearly two millennia.

Ancient diaspora

The northern Kingdom of Israel fell to Assyria in 722 BC and part of its population was deported — the origin of the "Ten Lost Tribes" tradition. Judah's elite were deported to Babylon after Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple in 586 BC (the Babylonian captivity). Although Cyrus of Persia permitted returns from 538 BC, a large Babylonian community persisted and later produced the Babylonian Talmud. Under the Hellenistic kingdoms, Jews settled widely: Alexandria in Egypt had one of the largest urban Jewish populations of the ancient world and produced the Septuagint translation and the philosophy of Philo. By the 1st century AD, Jews may have numbered several million across the Roman Empire and Parthian Babylonia.

The destruction of the Second Temple (AD 70) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136) deepened dispersion and shifted the demographic balance: Judaea was emptied of much of its Jewish population, and communities in Rome, Asia Minor, Syria, and Babylonia grew in importance.

Medieval dispersion

Major medieval groupings crystallised by region and rite: Ashkenazi Jews of the Rhineland, northern France, and later Eastern Europe (Yiddish-speaking); Sephardi Jews of Iberia and, after 1492, the Ottoman world (speakers of Judaeo-Spanish/Ladino); Mizrahi (Eastern) Jews of the Middle East and North Africa; and smaller communities including Romaniotes in Greece, Italkim in Italy, Yemenite Jews, Persian and Bukharan Jews, the Beta Israel of Ethiopia, and the Bene Israel and Cochin Jews of India.

Medieval diaspora life was marked by both achievement and precarity: the Golden Age of Jewish culture in Muslim Spain, the rabbinic academies of France and Germany (Rashi, the Tosafists), Maimonides in Egypt — alongside blood libels (beginning Norwich 1144), Crusader massacres in the Rhineland (1096), and expulsions from England (1290), France (1306, 1394), and many German cities.

Expulsions and migrations

The Alhambra Decree (1492) expelled Jews from Spain, followed by Portugal (1497); Sephardim resettled in North Africa, Italy, Amsterdam, and the Ottoman Empire (Salonica, Istanbul, Safed), where Safed became a centre of Kabbalah. In Eastern Europe, the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth hosted the world's largest Jewish population until the partitions; the Russian Empire later confined most Jews to the Pale of Settlement. The Khmelnytsky massacres (1648) were a major trauma in Eastern Europe.

Emancipation across Western Europe after the French Revolution gradually granted citizenship, while Eastern European Jews emigrated massively between 1881 and 1924 — over two million to the United States alone — fleeing pogroms and poverty. The Holocaust (1941–45) murdered about six million Jews and destroyed the great majority of Europe's diaspora communities. After 1948, most Jews of Arab and Muslim countries (about 850,000) emigrated or were expelled, chiefly to Israel and France.

Modern diaspora

Today the largest diaspora population is in the United States (roughly 6 million, centred on New York), followed by France, Canada, the United Kingdom, Argentina, Russia, Germany, and Australia. Israel's Law of Return (1950) grants Jews worldwide the right to immigrate (aliyah), and relations between Israel and the diaspora — religious pluralism, political support, demographic trends — remain a central theme of modern Jewish life. Since 1967, Israel and the diaspora each hold roughly half of the world's Jews.

Culture in diaspora

Diaspora generated distinctive languages (Yiddish, Ladino/Judaeo-Spanish, Judaeo-Arabic, Judaeo-Persian), liturgical rites, cuisines, and literatures, while sustained by institutions — synagogue, communal councils, rabbinic courts — that preserved continuity across dispersion. The concept of diaspora itself became influential in cultural studies, where the Jewish case is a paradigmatic example of a diasporic people maintaining identity without territorial sovereignty.
