The North American High (also called the Canadian High) is a large, semi-permanent continental anticyclone that forms over the interior of North America during winter. It is the North American counterpart of the Siberian High of Eurasia, and a counterpart in the opposite sense to the Icelandic Low and Aleutian Low of the surrounding oceans.

Formation

The high develops as intense radiational cooling over the snow-covered continental interior chills the lower atmosphere, increasing surface density and pressure. Cold, dense air accumulates over Canada and the northern United States, often with central pressures exceeding 1,050 hPa in the strongest events. Because it is a cold-core, shallow system, the high weakens with height and tends to disappear in the middle troposphere.

Weather effects

Within the high, weather is typically clear, calm and bitterly cold. On its eastern and southern flanks, clockwise circulation drives Arctic and polar air masses southward into the central and eastern United States, producing cold waves, freezes in the Deep South, and — where the cold air overruns moist Gulf air — snow and ice storms. The movement of the high and its embedded air masses strongly influences winter storm tracks and the development of nor'easters along the East Coast.

Seasonal behaviour

The North American High is strongest from December to February. In summer it retreats and is replaced by thermal low pressure over the heated interior, while the subtropical Bermuda–Azores High dominates the circulation around the continent's southeastern flank.
