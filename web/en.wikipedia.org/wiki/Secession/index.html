Secession

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Secession is the withdrawal of a group from a larger entity, especially a political entity, or of a political entity from a confederation or state — the creation of a new polity by detaching territory and population from an existing state. It differs from revolution (which aims at the whole state's government) and from decolonization (withdrawal from a colonial power), though real cases blend all three.

Law and cases

International law neither grants a general right of unilateral secession outside colonial and analogous contexts nor prohibits declarations of independence as such (ICJ, Kosovo opinion, 2010), leaving outcomes to effectiveness and recognition: successful secessions (Eritrea 1993, East Timor 2002, South Sudan 2011 — the latter two by negotiated referendums; Bangladesh 1971 by war) against failed or contested ones (Biafra, Katanga, Somaliland's three decades of unrecognised de facto statehood, Catalonia's suppressed 2017 bid, the Confederate States whose defeat and Texas v. White (1869) settled US law against unilateral secession). Democratic theory debates remedial-right theories (secession justified by grave injustice) versus primary-right theories (any territorial majority may choose) versus constitutionalised middle paths — Canada's Clarity Act after the Supreme Court's Quebec Secession Reference (1998), and the agreed Edinburgh process for Scotland's 2014 referendum, are the liberal models of lawful exit by negotiation and clear majority on a clear question.
