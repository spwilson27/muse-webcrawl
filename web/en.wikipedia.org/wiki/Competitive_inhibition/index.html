Competitive inhibition

Competitive inhibition is a form of enzyme inhibition in which a molecule similar in structure to the substrate binds to the enzyme's active site, preventing the substrate from binding. Because inhibitor and substrate compete for the same site, the inhibition can be overcome by increasing the substrate concentration.

Mechanism

The inhibitor (I) binds reversibly to the free enzyme (E) to form an EI complex, which cannot bind substrate. In the classic Michaelis–Menten treatment, competitive inhibition increases the apparent Michaelis constant Km (more substrate is needed to reach half-maximal rate) while leaving the maximum velocity Vmax unchanged, since at saturating substrate concentrations the substrate outcompetes the inhibitor.

v = Vmax[S] / (Km(1 + [I]/Ki) + [S])

Examples

Many drugs act as competitive inhibitors. Statins competitively inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis. Methotrexate competes with folate at dihydrofolate reductase. The classic textbook example is the inhibition of succinate dehydrogenase by malonate, a structural analogue of succinate.

Contrast with other inhibition types

Unlike non-competitive inhibition, where the inhibitor binds an allosteric site and lowers Vmax, competitive inhibition is reversible by excess substrate and produces characteristic intersecting patterns on Lineweaver–Burk plots at the 1/v axis.
