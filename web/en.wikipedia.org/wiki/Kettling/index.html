Kettling

A police kettle during a protest

Kettling (also known as containment or corralling) is a police tactic for controlling large crowds during demonstrations or protests. It involves the formation of large cordons of police officers who then move to contain a crowd within a limited area. Protesters are prevented from leaving the "kettle" — sometimes for several hours — or leave only through an exit controlled by the police, who may record the identities of those released.

History and use

A precursor of the tactic was used by German police in Hamburg in 1986 (the Hamburger Kessel), which a court later ruled unlawful. British police used kettling extensively in the 2000s, including at the 2001 May Day protests in London and the 2009 G20 London summit protests, where the death of bystander Ian Tomlinson after being struck by an officer drew national scrutiny. The tactic has been used in Canada, the United States, Germany, and other countries.

Criticism and legality

Critics argue that kettling indiscriminately detains peaceful protesters and bystanders, denies access to food, water, and toilets, and can provoke the disorder it claims to prevent. Supporters say it prevents violence and property damage with less force than dispersal. In the 2012 case Austin v United Kingdom, the European Court of Human Rights held that a seven-hour kettle at Oxford Circus did not breach Article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights in the specific circumstances, while courts in some jurisdictions have found particular kettles unlawful.
