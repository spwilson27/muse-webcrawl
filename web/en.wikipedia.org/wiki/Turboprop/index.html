Turboprop

Turboprop
TypeGas turbine aircraft engine
Power sourceTurbine driving a propeller via reduction gearbox

A turboprop is a turbine engine that drives an aircraft propeller. A turboprop consists of an intake, reduction gearbox, compressor, combustor, turbine, and a propelling nozzle. Air is drawn into the intake and compressed by the compressor. Fuel is then added to the compressed air in the combustor, where the fuel–air mixture burns. The hot combustion gases expand through the turbines, which drive both the compressor and, through the reduction gearbox, the propeller.

Unlike a turbojet, the turboprop's exhaust gases do not generally contain enough energy to create significant thrust, since almost all of the engine's power is used to drive the propeller. Turboprops are most efficient at flight speeds below about 725 km/h (450 mph) and are widely used on small and medium-sized aircraft.

History

The first turboprop engine was designed by Hungarian mechanical engineer György Jendrassik in 1938; his Cs-1 engine was tested in 1940. The world's first turboprop-powered aircraft to fly was a Gloster Meteor fitted with Rolls-Royce Trent engines in 1945. The Rolls-Royce Dart powered the Vickers Viscount, the first turboprop airliner, which entered service in 1953.

Technology

Most turboprops use a reduction gearbox between the turbine and the propeller, because the turbine performs best at rotational speeds far higher than a propeller can tolerate. Many designs use a free turbine, in which a separate turbine stage, not mechanically connected to the compressor, drives the propeller. Prominent examples include the Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6, one of the most popular turboprop engines ever produced, and the Europrop TP400, which powers the Airbus A400M.

Applications

Turboprops power regional airliners such as the ATR 72 and the De Havilland Canada Dash 8, military transports such as the Lockheed C-130 Hercules, and countless utility and agricultural aircraft. Their fuel efficiency at low altitude and speed, together with the ability to operate from short, unprepared runways, keeps them in production despite competition from regional jets.
