Territory

Related concepts: State, dependent territory, territorial integrity, jurisdiction

A territory is an area of land, sea or space that belongs to or is administered by a state, an organisation or another authority. In international law, territory is one of the constitutive elements of a state, together with a permanent population, a government and the capacity to enter into relations with other states (as set out in the 1933 Montevideo Convention).

State territory

A state's territory comprises its land area, internal waters and territorial sea (generally up to 12 nautical miles from the baseline), together with the airspace above them. Sovereignty over territory is protected by the principle of territorial integrity, which prohibits the acquisition of territory by force; changes normally occur through cession, accession, prescription, or the emergence of new land. Doctrines such as uti possidetis have been used to settle the boundaries of newly independent states by preserving former administrative lines.

Dependent and administered territories

A dependent territory is an area that does not possess full political independence or sovereignty as a state but remains politically outside the controlling state's integral area. Examples include the British Overseas Territories (such as Gibraltar and Bermuda), the French overseas collectivities, the U.S. territories (such as Puerto Rico and Guam), and the realms of New Zealand. Historically, the League of Nations mandates and United Nations trust territories were administered by states on behalf of the international community.

Other uses

Within federal states, "territory" may denote a first-order subdivision that is not a state or province, such as Australia's Northern Territory, Canada's Yukon, Northwest Territories and Nunavut, or India's union territories. The word is also used in ecology, for the area defended by an animal or group, and in commerce for sales regions assigned to agents.
