Saliva

Saliva
Produced by: Salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual)
Daily volume: ~0.5–1.5 litres (humans)
pH: ~6.2–7.6
Key components: Water, amylase, mucins, lysozyme, electrolytes

Saliva (commonly referred to as spit) is an extracellular fluid produced and secreted by salivary glands in the mouth. In humans, saliva is around 99% water and contains electrolytes, mucus, white blood cells, epithelial cells, enzymes such as amylase and lipase, and antimicrobial agents including secretory IgA and lysozyme.

Functions

Saliva lubricates food to form a bolus for swallowing, begins starch digestion via salivary amylase, protects the teeth by buffering acids and supplying calcium and phosphate for remineralisation, aids taste by dissolving food molecules, and supports oral hygiene through its antimicrobial action. Reduced saliva production (xerostomia) increases the risk of dental caries and oral infection.

Production

Humans produce roughly 0.5 to 1.5 litres of saliva daily, mostly from the submandibular glands at rest and the parotid glands when stimulated by eating.
