In statistics, originally in geostatistics, kriging or Kriging, also known as Gaussian process regression, is a method of interpolation based on Gaussian processes governed by prior covariances. Under suitable assumptions on the priors, kriging gives the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) at unsampled locations. The method is widely used in the domain of spatial analysis and computer experiments.

History

The technique is named after South African mining engineer Danie G. Krige, who developed empirical methods for estimating ore grades from drill-hole samples in the 1950s. The French mathematician Georges Matheron formalised Krige's approach in the 1960s as the theory of regionalised variables, founding modern geostatistics.

Method

Kriging assumes that the distance or direction between sample points reflects a spatial correlation that can be used to explain variation in the surface. The spatial correlation structure is modelled by a variogram or covariance function, characterised by parameters including the nugget, sill, and range. The interpolated value at a target location is a weighted linear combination of the observed values; the weights are chosen so that the predictor is unbiased and its variance is minimised, which requires solving a linear system derived from the variogram model.

Types of kriging

Several variants exist. Simple kriging assumes a known, constant mean. Ordinary kriging assumes an unknown constant mean and is the most common form. Universal kriging allows the mean to vary as a polynomial trend surface. Indicator kriging works with binary variables to estimate exceedance probabilities, while cokriging exploits correlations between two or more related variables. In machine learning, the equivalent method, Gaussian process regression, is used for surrogate modelling, optimisation, and probabilistic prediction.

Applications

Kriging is applied in mining (ore reserve estimation), hydrogeology, soil science, meteorology, environmental monitoring, petroleum engineering, precision agriculture, and the design and analysis of computer experiments.
