Decius Mus

[Image: Publius Decius Mus performing the devotio]
Stoffer's 17th-century painting of the devotio of Decius Mus

Publius Decius Mus is the name borne by several Roman politicians of the plebeian gens Decia, most famously three generations of consuls who are said to have performed the ritual of devotio — sacrificing their own lives in battle to ensure a Roman victory. The name is most strongly associated with the consul of 340 BC, who died at the Battle of Vesuvius during the Latin War.

The devotio legend

According to the historian Livy, Publius Decius Mus, consul in 340 BC alongside Titus Manlius Torquatus, found his army hard pressed by the Latins near Mount Vesuvius. Following a dream that told both consuls that the side whose commander devoted himself to the gods of the underworld would win, Decius summoned the pontifex maximus, recited the prescribed formula with his head veiled, and rode into the enemy ranks, where he was killed. The Roman army, inspired, went on to victory. Livy's account is the classic description of the devotio ritual in Roman religion.

Later Decii

His son, also named Publius Decius Mus, was consul four times (312, 308, 297 and 295 BC) and is said to have devoted himself in the same way at the Battle of Sentinum in 295 BC against the Samnites, Gauls and Umbrians, dying as his father had. A grandson of the same name was consul in 279 BC and, by some accounts, performed a third devotio at the Battle of Asculum against Pyrrhus of Epirus, though the historicity of this third sacrifice is more doubtful.

Career of the first Decius

The elder Decius first distinguished himself as a military tribune in 343 BC during the First Samnite War, when he led a detachment that seized a height and saved the army of Aulus Cornelius Cossus from a dangerous position in a Samnite defile. Elected consul for 340 BC, he marched with Manlius against the Latins and Campanians in the Latin War, culminating in the battles of Vesuvius and Trifanum that broke Latin resistance.

Historical assessment

Modern historians debate how much of the devotio story is historical. The triple repetition of the same sacrifice across three generations suggests legendary elaboration, but the core tradition — that a Decius Mus died heroically at Sentinum in 295 BC — is generally accepted, and the devotio ritual itself is well attested as a genuine Roman religious practice.
