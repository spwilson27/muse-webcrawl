Trigeminal autonomic cephalgia

Trigeminal autonomic cephalgias (TACs) are a group of primary headache disorders characterised by severe unilateral pain in the distribution of the trigeminal nerve, accompanied by prominent ipsilateral cranial autonomic features such as conjunctival injection, lacrimation, nasal congestion, rhinorrhoea, eyelid oedema, forehead sweating, miosis or ptosis. They are classified as group 3 in the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3).

Types

  • Cluster headache — attacks of excruciating pain lasting 15 minutes to 3 hours, occurring in bouts ("clusters") separated by remissions, or chronically.
  • Paroxysmal hemicrania — shorter (2–30 minute), more frequent attacks that respond absolutely to indomethacin.
  • Short-lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache attacks — SUNCT and SUNA, with very brief (seconds to minutes) attacks occurring many times a day.
  • Hemicrania continua — a continuous unilateral headache with exacerbations, also indomethacin-responsive.

Mechanism and treatment

The TACs are thought to involve activation of the trigeminovascular system with reflex activation of cranial parasympathetic outflow, and posterior hypothalamic dysfunction is implicated particularly in cluster headache. Acute treatments vary by type — high-flow oxygen and subcutaneous sumatriptan for cluster attacks, indomethacin for paroxysmal hemicrania and hemicrania continua — while preventive therapies include verapamil, lithium and newer agents targeting the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) pathway.
