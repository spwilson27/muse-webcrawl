Exploded-view drawing

An exploded-view drawing of a complex mechanism or device is a diagram, picture, or technical drawing of an object that shows the relationship or order of assembly of various parts. It does this by showing the components of an object slightly separated by distance, or suspended in surrounding space in the case of a three-dimensional exploded diagram, as if there had been a small controlled explosion emanating from the middle of the object, causing the parts to move apart an equal distance away from their original locations.

Description

In an exploded view, the parts of an assembly are spread out — usually along one or more axes, and often along the axis of assembly — so that each part remains recognisable and its position relative to its neighbours can be clearly seen. The separation is normally uniform or proportional, preserving the correct order in which the parts fit together, so that a viewer can mentally (or in practice) collapse the drawing back into the assembled object. Dashed "trail" lines are often drawn between parts to indicate how they align and in which direction they slide together; fasteners such as bolts and screws are shown floating along the same axis as the parts they secure.

The exploded-view drawing is one of the standard pictorial representations in engineering drawing, alongside orthographic projections and sectional views. Unlike a section, which cuts the object to reveal its interior, the exploded view leaves every part intact and is principally concerned with the structure of an assembly rather than with the shape of an individual part.

Uses

Exploded diagrams are extensively used in:

  • Assembly and disassembly instructions, such as furniture assembly manuals and consumer-product guides.
  • Repair and maintenance manuals, where they show the order in which components must be removed and replaced.
  • Parts catalogues, where numbered parts in the diagram are keyed to a parts list giving names and ordering numbers.
  • Patent illustrations, to explain how the components of an invention cooperate.
  • Marketing and museum displays, where exploded models of engines, watches, and other mechanisms make their internal organisation legible at a glance.

History

The exploded view is one of the many graphic inventions of the Renaissance. Early examples appear in the notebooks of engineers such as Mariano Taccola and Francesco di Giorgio Martini, and Leonardo da Vinci used exploded drawings in his studies of mechanisms, including his drawings of a flying machine and of gearing. The representation was refined during the early fifteenth century and was eventually codified in the engineering drawing standards of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Modern computer-aided design software can generate exploded views automatically from a three-dimensional model by displacing parts along chosen axes, and exploded-view animations are now common in product presentations.
