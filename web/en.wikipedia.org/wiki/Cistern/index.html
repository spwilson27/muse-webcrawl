Cistern

A cistern is a waterproof receptacle for holding liquids, usually water, often built to catch and store rainwater.

A cistern (from Middle English cisterne, from Latin cisterna, from cista, 'box', from Greek κίστη kistē, 'basket') is a waterproof receptacle for holding liquids, usually water. Cisterns are often built to catch and store rainwater. They are distinguished from wells by their waterproof linings. Modern cisterns range in capacity from a few litres to thousands of cubic metres, effectively forming covered reservoirs.

Early history

Waterproof lime plaster cisterns in the floors of houses are features of Neolithic villages of the Levant at, for instance, Ramad and Lebwe, and by the late fourth millennium BC, as at Jawa in northeastern Lebanon, cisterns were essential elements of emerging water management techniques in dry-land farming communities.

The ancient Roman impluvium, a standard feature of the domus, collected rainwater from the compluvium roof and channelled it into an underground cistern, from which household water could be drawn. Large imperial cisterns, such as the Basilica Cistern in Constantinople (Istanbul), could hold tens of thousands of cubic metres of water and were supported by forests of marble columns.

Modern use

Cisterns are commonly prevalent in areas where water is scarce, either because it is rare or because it has been depleted due to heavy use. Early on, the water was used for many purposes including cooking, irrigation, and washing. Present-day cisterns are often only used for irrigation due to concerns over water quality. Cisterns today can also be outfitted with filters or other water purification methods when the water is intended for drinking. Household toilet cisterns — the tank storing water for flushing — are another common modern use of the word.
