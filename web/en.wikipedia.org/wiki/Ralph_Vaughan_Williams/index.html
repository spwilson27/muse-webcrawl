Ralph Vaughan Williams (12 October 1872 – 26 August 1958) was an English composer. His works include operas, ballets, chamber music, secular and religious vocal pieces, and orchestral compositions including nine symphonies, written over sixty years. Strongly influenced by Tudor music and English folk-song, his output marked a decisive break in British music from its German-dominated style of the 19th century.

Life

Born into a well-connected family — Charles Darwin was his great-uncle — Vaughan Williams was educated at Charterhouse, the Royal College of Music, and Trinity College, Cambridge, and later studied briefly with Maurice Ravel in Paris. From 1903 he collected English folk songs in the countryside, an activity that profoundly shaped his musical language. He also edited the music of The English Hymnal (1906), composing tunes such as "Sine Nomine" ("For All the Saints"). Despite his background and age, he enlisted and served in the First World War, first in the Royal Army Medical Corps and then as an artillery officer, an experience that left him with growing deafness and influenced works such as the Pastoral Symphony.

Music

His best-loved works include the Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis (1910), written for the resonant acoustic of Gloucester Cathedral; The Lark Ascending (1914/1920), a romance for violin and orchestra evoking George Meredith's poem; the Fantasia on "Greensleeves"; and the masque Job. His nine symphonies range from the choral A Sea Symphony and the urban A London Symphony to the stark Sixth and the film-derived Sinfonia antartica. He remained creatively active into his eighties and declined a knighthood, though he accepted the Order of Merit.
