The age of criminal responsibility is the age below which a person is deemed incapable of committing a criminal offence and therefore cannot be prosecuted through the criminal courts. It reflects a legal judgement about children's capacity to understand wrongfulness and to participate in a trial, and it varies enormously between countries — from as low as 7 in some jurisdictions to 18 in others, where younger offenders are dealt with only through welfare or juvenile-justice systems.

International standards and debate

The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child requires states to set a minimum age, and the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child has urged that it be no lower than 14. Critics of low ages (such as England and Wales's 10) argue they criminalise children who lack developmental maturity, citing neuroscience on adolescent brain development; defenders argue that serious offences require a justice response and that the age is only a threshold for a separate youth court system, not adult punishment.

Common law and doli incapax

In common law tradition, children under 7 were conclusively presumed incapable (doli incapax), and those aged 7–14 were rebuttably presumed incapable unless the prosecution proved they knew the act was wrong. Several jurisdictions have abolished or modified this presumption, while others retain it as a safeguard alongside a low statutory age.

Variation

Separate ages often apply for different purposes: the age at which a young person can be tried as an adult, detained, or given a criminal record typically exceeds the minimum age of responsibility itself.
