Castile (Spanish: Castilla) is a historical region of Spain. Its exact boundaries have varied, but it traditionally comprised much of the central Iberian plateau (the Meseta), including the lands of the medieval Kingdom and Crown of Castile. Today its territory is divided principally among the autonomous communities of Castile and León, Castile–La Mancha, and the Community of Madrid, with smaller parts in Cantabria, La Rioja, Aragon, Extremadura and Valencia.

History

The name Castile derives from the many castles (castillos) raised along the frontier with Muslim al-Andalus. It emerged in the 9th century as a frontier county of the Kingdom of Asturias–León, first mentioned in documents around 800. Under Count Fernán González in the 10th century the county became effectively hereditary and autonomous. In 1035 Ferdinand I united Castile with León and raised it to a kingdom, and in 1230 Ferdinand III permanently united the two crowns. The Crown of Castile expanded southward during the Reconquista, conquering Toledo (1085), Córdoba (1236) and Seville (1248), and later Granada (1492). Under Isabella I and Ferdinand II, Castile was dynastically joined with Aragon (1479), laying the foundations of the modern Spanish state, and Castilian — Spanish — became the dominant language of the peninsula and of Spain's overseas empire.

Geography

Castile occupies the high central plateau of Iberia, divided by the Sistema Central mountain range into Old Castile to the north and New Castile to the south. The Douro, Tagus and Guadiana rivers drain the region. Its continental climate, cereal-growing plains and stock-raising traditions shaped its historic economy and culture.

Language and identity

Castilian Spanish originated as the Romance dialect of the region and became the standard language of Spain. The historical region has no single modern administration, and "Castile" continues to be used culturally and geographically rather than politically.
