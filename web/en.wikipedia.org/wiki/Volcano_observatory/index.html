Volcano observatory

A volcano observatory is an institution that conducts monitoring of a volcano or group of volcanoes and research into volcanic processes, in order to understand potentially hazardous behavior and to provide early warning of eruptions to civil authorities and the public.

Functions

Observatories track seismic activity, ground deformation (using GPS, tiltmeters, and satellite radar interferometry), gas emissions, thermal anomalies, and changes in crater lakes and streams. By combining these data, scientists assess the state of a volcano, assign alert levels, and advise emergency managers on evacuations. Observatories also maintain long-term geological studies, hazard maps, and archives of eruptive history.

History

The first volcano observatory, the Vesuvius Observatory, was founded in 1841 on the slopes of Mount Vesuvius in Naples and directed by Macedonio Melloni; it is the oldest volcanological institution in the world. The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory followed in 1912, founded by Thomas Jaggar after the devastating 1908 Messina earthquake. The success of monitoring at volcanoes such as Mount St. Helens (1980) and Pinatubo (1991) — where forecasts enabled evacuations that saved tens of thousands of lives — demonstrated the value of permanent observatories.

Organization

In the United States, the U.S. Geological Survey operates observatories covering Hawaii, Alaska, the Cascades, Yellowstone, and California, and issues alert levels and weekly updates. Many volcano-hosting nations run their own observatories, coordinated internationally through the World Organization of Volcano Observatories (WOVO). Modern observatories increasingly rely on automated telemetry and satellite remote sensing, allowing continuous surveillance of remote volcanoes in places like Alaska's Aleutian Islands and Kamchatka.
