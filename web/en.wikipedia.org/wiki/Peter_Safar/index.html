Peter J. Safar (April 12, 1924 – August 3, 2003) was an Austrian-American anaesthesiologist and resuscitation pioneer, widely regarded as the father of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). He combined rescue breathing with closed-chest compression into modern CPR, founded the first multidisciplinary intensive care unit in the United States, and established academic critical care medicine and paramedic training as disciplines.

Early life and career

Safar was born in Vienna, where his parents were physicians; he opposed Nazism as a youth and emigrated to the United States in 1949 after medical school. After training in surgery, pathology and anaesthesiology (including at the University of Pennsylvania and Yale), he became chief of anaesthesiology at Baltimore City Hospital in 1955, where in 1958 he opened the first American multidisciplinary intensive care unit and the first ICU fellowship programme.

CPR and emergency medicine

In the 1950s Safar proved experimentally that exhaled air could sustain oxygenation in apnoeic patients, championing mouth-to-mouth ventilation against prevailing manual methods, and he promoted the head-tilt/chin-lift airway manoeuvre. When Kouwenhoven and colleagues demonstrated closed-chest cardiac compression in 1960, Safar merged the techniques into the ABC (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) protocol of CPR and wrote The ABC of Resuscitation and the textbook Cardiopulmonary Cerebral Resuscitation. In Pittsburgh, where he chaired anaesthesiology from 1961, he founded Freedom House Ambulance Service in 1967, the first community paramedic programme in the U.S., staffed by African-American recruits from the Hill District, and helped design modern ambulance standards. He persuaded Norwegian toymaker Asmund Laerdal to produce the Resusci Annie training manikin.

Later work and legacy

Safar founded the Safar Center for Resuscitation Research at the University of Pittsburgh (1979), researching brain resuscitation, therapeutic hypothermia and suspended animation for trauma. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Medicine three times, co-founded the World Association for Disaster and Emergency Medicine, and campaigned for nuclear disarmament. Guidelines bodies such as the European Resuscitation Council continue the standards tradition he created. He died of cancer in Pittsburgh in 2003.
