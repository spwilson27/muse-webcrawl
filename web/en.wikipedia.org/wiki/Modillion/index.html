Modillion

TypeArchitectural ornament
LocationUnder a cornice (Corinthian and Composite orders; also Ionic)
FormSmall ornamental bracket, often with acanthus leaf and volute

A modillion is an ornamental bracket used in classical architecture to support a projecting cornice. Modillions are arranged in a regular row beneath the corona of the cornice, especially in the Corinthian and Composite orders, and are typically more elaborate than the block-like dentils used in the Ionic order. Each modillion is usually shaped as a small scroll-like bracket, its sides decorated with an acanthus leaf and its underside sometimes with a rosette or leaf in the intervals between brackets.

Origin and use

The modillion derives from Roman practice; Vitruvius described the projecting brackets under a cornice as representing, in stone, the ends of rafters — an explanation he also gave for dentils and mutules. In Roman Corinthian temples and basilicas, carved modillions became a hallmark of a rich cornice. Renaissance architects such as Palladio codified their proportions and spacing, typically placing one modillion over the centre of each column and spacing the rest at regular intervals, and the ornament passed into Baroque, Neoclassical, Beaux-Arts and Colonial Revival architecture. In later, less formal work, simpler block modillions — plain rectangular blocks — were often substituted and are sometimes themselves loosely called modillions or "block modillions".

Distinctions

Modillions differ from related ornaments by position and form: dentils are small tooth-like blocks in the bed-moulding of Ionic (and sometimes Corinthian) cornices; mutules are flat slabs on the soffit of the Doric cornice, studded with guttae; corbels are structural brackets carrying real loads at various positions in a building, whereas classical modillions are essentially decorative, "supporting" the cornice only visually.
