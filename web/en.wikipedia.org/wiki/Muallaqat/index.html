The Mu'allaqat (Arabic: المعلقات, "The Suspended Odes") are a collection of seven — in some traditions ten — long pre-Islamic Arabic poems, regarded as the finest works of early Arabic literature. The name is traditionally explained by the story that the poems, written in gold, were suspended on the walls of the Kaaba in Mecca, though this account is doubted by modern scholars; the name may instead mean poems "hung up" as prized treasures.

The poems and poets

The seven poets most commonly listed are Imru' al-Qais, Tarafa, Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma, Labid, 'Amr ibn Kulthum, Antara ibn Shaddad, and al-Harith ibn Hilliza. Some lists add al-Nabigha, al-A'sha, and 'Abid ibn al-Abras, making ten. Each poem is a qasida (ode) following the classical form: an elegiac opening at the abandoned campsite, a journey section describing the poet's mount and the desert, and a concluding purpose such as praise, boast, or satire.

The most famous of the set, the Mu'allaqa of Imru' al-Qais, opens with the celebrated line "Halt, both of you — let us weep for the memory of a beloved and a dwelling-place," and set patterns imitated by Arabic poets for centuries.

Preservation and study

The collection was compiled in the eighth century, traditionally by the scholar Hammad al-Rawiya. The poems were preserved through oral transmission and early manuscripts, and have been translated into many languages; they remain foundational texts in the study of Arabic language and literature.
