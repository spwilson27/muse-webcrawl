Hyperkalemic periodic paralysis

Hyperkalemic periodic paralysis (HYPP, HyperKPP) is an inherited autosomal dominant disorder characterised by episodes of muscle weakness or paralysis associated with elevated serum potassium. It is a channelopathy caused by mutations in the SCN4A gene, which encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.4 of skeletal muscle.

HYPP results from gain-of-function mutations in the SCN4A sodium channel.

Signs and symptoms

Attacks typically begin in the first decade of life. They are precipitated by rest after exercise, potassium-rich foods, fasting, cold exposure and stress. Weakness usually affects the limbs and trunk while sparing the respiratory and facial muscles in most cases, and episodes last from tens of minutes to a few hours. Between attacks, some patients develop mild myotonia, and later in life a fixed proximal weakness may develop.

Mechanism

The mutant sodium channels show impaired fast inactivation, producing a persistent sodium current. When extracellular potassium rises, the muscle membrane depolarises; at moderate depolarisation this causes myotonia (hyperexcitability), and at greater depolarisation the fibres become inexcitable, producing paralysis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical history, serum potassium measured during an attack (which may be only high-normal), electromyography showing myotonia, and genetic testing. Acute attacks often respond to carbohydrate intake or inhaled salbutamol; preventive treatment includes thiazide diuretics, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide, and avoidance of triggers. The disorder also occurs in American Quarter Horses, where it traces to the stallion Impressive.
