Export–Import Bank of the United States

EXIM headquarters in Washington, D.C.

The Export–Import Bank of the United States (EXIM) is the official export credit agency of the United States federal government. Operating as a wholly owned federal government corporation, EXIM's mission is to support American jobs by facilitating the export of U.S. goods and services. When private lenders are unable or unwilling to provide financing, the bank fills gaps for American businesses by equipping them with tools to compete for global sales.

History

The bank was established by executive order of President Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1934, initially to finance trade with the Soviet Union and Cuba. It became an independent agency in 1945 under the Export-Import Bank Act. Its charter is periodically reauthorised by Congress; a lapse in 2015 briefly suspended its authority to approve new transactions, and the board lacked a quorum from 2015 to 2019. Congress reauthorised the bank in 2019 for seven years, through 2026.

Programs

EXIM provides export credit insurance, working capital guarantees, and direct loans and loan guarantees to foreign buyers of U.S. exports. Major beneficiary sectors have historically included aircraft manufacturing — leading critics to dub it the "Boeing Bank" — power generation, and small-business exporters, for whom the bank reserves a statutory share of its financing.

Criticism and support

Critics, including some free-market groups and members of both parties, characterise the bank as corporate welfare favouring large firms. Supporters argue that it levels the playing field against heavily subsidised foreign export credit agencies, sustains U.S. manufacturing jobs, and has historically returned money to the Treasury through fees.
