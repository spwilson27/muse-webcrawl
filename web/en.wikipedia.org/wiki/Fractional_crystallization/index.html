Fractional crystallization

Fractional crystallization is a process by which, during the cooling of a liquid, crystals form and are removed from the liquid, changing the composition of the remaining melt. The term is used both in chemistry — for separating substances by differences in solubility — and in geology, where it is a principal mechanism of magmatic differentiation.

In geology

As a magma cools, the minerals that crystallize first are those with the highest melting points, such as olivine and pyroxene in mafic magmas (Bowen's reaction series). If these early crystals settle out or are otherwise isolated from the melt, the residual liquid becomes progressively enriched in silica, alkalis and incompatible elements, evolving from basaltic toward andesitic and rhyolitic compositions. Fractional crystallization explains much of the compositional diversity of igneous rocks and is important in forming layered intrusions and some ore deposits.

In chemistry

In chemistry, fractional crystallization separates a mixture of substances based on differences in solubility, typically by slowly cooling a saturated solution, or by successive recrystallization. It is used to purify compounds and to separate rare-earth elements and isotopes of similar chemistry.
