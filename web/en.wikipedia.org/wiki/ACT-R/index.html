ACT-R (pronounced "act-are") is a cognitive architecture — a computational theory of human cognition — developed principally by John Robert Anderson and colleagues at Carnegie Mellon University. The name derives from "Adaptive Control of Thought—Rational". ACT-R models are computer programs that simulate the mechanisms by which perception, memory and motor action interact to produce behaviour.

Architecture

ACT-R divides cognition into specialised modules — among them a declarative memory module, a goal module, visual and manual modules — each communicating through limited-capacity buffers. A central procedural system, implemented as production rules ("if–then" rules), fires on the contents of the buffers, with rule selection taking about 50 milliseconds per cycle. Memory is represented in chunks, whose activation levels govern retrieval speed and probability according to equations derived from the statistics of past use.

Learning

Learning in ACT-R includes the strengthening of chunks through use (base-level learning), the compilation of declarative knowledge into production rules through practice (production compilation), and the adjustment of rule utilities by reinforcement. These mechanisms reproduce empirical regularities such as the power law of practice and the fan effect.

Applications

ACT-R has been used to build models of memory, problem solving, language processing, driving, human–computer interaction and intelligent tutoring systems; Anderson's algebra tutors built on the theory were commercialised as Cognitive Tutors. The architecture has also been linked to neuroimaging data by mapping modules to brain regions such as the basal ganglia (procedural) and prefrontal cortex (goal buffer).
