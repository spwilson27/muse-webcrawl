Southern Shaolin Monastery

The Southern Shaolin Monastery (Chinese: 南少林寺) is a name given to a Buddhist monastery said to have existed in Fujian province, China, as a southern counterpart to the famous Shaolin Monastery on Mount Song in Henan. Southern Shaolin traditions credit the monastery as the birthplace of the Southern Chinese martial arts, collectively called Nanquan ("Southern Fist"), including Hung Ga, Choy Li Fut, Wing Chun, and the Fujian White Crane style.

Legend and history

According to popular tradition, monks from the northern Shaolin Temple established a southern temple during the Tang dynasty, and it later became a refuge for Ming loyalists resisting the Qing dynasty. Legends tell that the Qing government burned the monastery and massacred its monks, with only five elders — the Five Elders of Shaolin, including the abbess Ng Mui and the monk Jee Sin — escaping to spread the arts across southern China. These stories underpin the founding myths of many southern styles and of secret societies such as the Tiandihui (Heaven and Earth Society).

Historians treat much of this narrative as folklore that crystallised in martial arts novels and secret-society mythology of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; documentary evidence for a historical Southern Shaolin Temple comparable to the Henan monastery is thin, and the tales combine kernels of fact — such as the real destruction of the Henan temple in 1647 and again in the 1920s — with invention.

Claimed locations and revival

Several Fujian localities, including Putian, Quanzhou, and Fuqing, claim to be the site of the Southern Shaolin Temple. Ruins identified at Putian in the 1990s were developed into a reconstructed temple complex that now teaches martial arts and receives tourists, and Shaolin-affiliated schools in the region train students in both northern and southern forms. Regardless of the historical debate, the Southern Shaolin idea remains a central cultural symbol of southern kung fu and of resistance mythology in Chinese popular culture.
