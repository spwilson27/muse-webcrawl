Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen

Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (born Constantin Georgescu; 4 February 1906 – 30 October 1994) was a Romanian-American mathematician, statistician and economist. He is regarded as the founder of bioeconomics and ecological economics, best known for arguing that the second law of thermodynamics — the entropy law — governs economic processes and places ultimate limits on material growth.

Education and early career

Georgescu-Roegen studied mathematics at the University of Bucharest and took his doctorate in statistics at the Sorbonne in Paris, then worked at University College London and, on a Rockefeller fellowship, at Harvard University, where he came under the influence of Joseph Schumpeter. His early papers of the 1930s on consumer choice and production theory were praised by leading economists of the day. Returning to Romania, he held senior posts in statistics and economic administration before fleeing the communist takeover in 1948.

Vanderbilt and the entropy law

He spent the rest of his career at Vanderbilt University in Nashville. In Analytical Economics (1966) and above all in his magnum opus The Entropy Law and the Economic Process (1971), he argued that neoclassical economics had modelled production as a circular, reversible flow while ignoring the irreversible transformation of low-entropy energy and materials into high-entropy waste. The production process, on his account, is fundamentally the transformation of valuable low entropy into worthless high entropy, and no technology can repeal this constraint.

Bioeconomics and influence

From this critique Georgescu-Roegen developed a "bioeconomic" program advocating a steady-state economy, population stabilization, and drastic frugality in the use of energy and materials. His ideas strongly influenced the economist Herman Daly, who studied with him, and through Daly the modern fields of ecological economics and the degrowth movement. Although largely marginalized by mainstream economics during his lifetime, his work has been repeatedly rediscovered as environmental limits have become central to economic debate.
