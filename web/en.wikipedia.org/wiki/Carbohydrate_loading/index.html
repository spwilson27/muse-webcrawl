Carbohydrate loading

A pre-race pasta meal is the classic image of carbohydrate loading. (Image placeholder)

Carbohydrate loading, commonly known as carb-loading, is a nutritional strategy used by endurance athletes to maximise the storage of glycogen in the muscles and liver before an event, typically one lasting more than about 90 minutes, such as a marathon. By increasing glycogen stores — the body's readily available carbohydrate fuel — athletes can delay the onset of fatigue associated with glycogen depletion, colloquially known as "hitting the wall" or "bonking".

Method

The classical regimen, developed from Scandinavian research in the 1960s, combined several days of hard training and a very low-carbohydrate diet to deplete glycogen, followed by several days of rest and a high-carbohydrate diet, during which the muscles would "supercompensate" and store glycogen above normal levels. Modern practice has largely dropped the harsh depletion phase: studies showed that trained athletes can achieve near-maximal stores simply by tapering training while eating a high-carbohydrate diet (around 10–12 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight per day) for the final 36–72 hours before competition.

Evidence and effects

Carbohydrate loading can roughly double muscle glycogen stores and has been shown to improve endurance performance by about 2–3% and to delay fatigue in events lasting over 90 minutes. It offers little benefit for shorter events, in which glycogen is not limiting. Because each gram of glycogen is stored with about three grams of water, loading typically adds body mass, which some athletes perceive as heaviness; gastrointestinal discomfort from very large carbohydrate intakes is also common. The pre-race "pasta party" held by many marathons is a social tradition built around the practice.
