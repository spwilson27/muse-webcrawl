Morris water navigation task

Morris water navigation task
FieldBehavioural neuroscience
Devised byRichard Morris (1984)
SubjectsMainly rats and mice
MeasuresHippocampus-dependent spatial learning and memory

The Morris water navigation task, also known as the Morris water maze, is a behavioural procedure widely used in psychology and neuroscience to study spatial learning and memory. Developed by Richard G. M. Morris at the University of St Andrews in the early 1980s, it has become one of the standard tools for testing hippocampal function and for evaluating rodent models of neurological disorders such as Alzheimer's disease.

Procedure

An animal is placed in a large circular pool of opaque (milky) water from which it cannot see or smell an escape platform submerged just below the surface. Released from varying start positions over repeated trials, the animal must learn the platform's location using distant visual cues around the room. Performance is measured by escape latency and path length, which normally fall steeply over days of training. In a probe trial, the platform is removed and the time the animal spends searching in the target quadrant is taken as a measure of memory.

Interpretation

Rats with hippocampal lesions are severely impaired on the hidden-platform version but perform normally when the platform is visible, implicating the hippocampus in the formation of a cognitive map of space. The task is sensitive to NMDA receptor blockade, which also impairs long-term potentiation, historically linking the maze to theories of synaptic plasticity and memory. Variants include the dry-land Barnes maze and radial arm maze.

Criticisms

Swimming is stressful and involves strong motivations other than spatial cognition; performance can be affected by motor, visual, or anxiety differences between strains or treatments, so careful controls — especially visible-platform trials — are essential.
