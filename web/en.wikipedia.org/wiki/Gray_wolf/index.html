The gray wolf (Canis lupus), also spelled grey wolf, is a large canine native to Eurasia and North America. More than thirty subspecies of Canis lupus have been recognized, including the dingo and the domestic dog, though genetic studies continue to revise the taxonomy. The wolf is the largest extant member of the family Canidae, and is further distinguished from other Canis species by its less pointed ears and muzzle, shorter torso and longer tail.

The gray wolf is a highly social animal, living in nuclear families — a mated pair and their offspring — sometimes forming larger packs. It is an apex predator throughout most of its range, feeding mainly on large ungulates such as deer, elk, moose and bison, supplemented by smaller prey and livestock.

Description

Adults measure 105–160 cm in length and 80–85 cm at the shoulder, weighing on average 30–50 kg, with Eurasian wolves generally larger than North American ones. The thick winter coat is typically grizzled grey but ranges from nearly pure white in the Arctic to brown, cinnamon and black. Wolves have powerful jaws and keen senses of smell and hearing adapted to locating prey over long distances.

Behaviour and ecology

Wolf packs defend territories that can span hundreds of square kilometres. Communication includes howling, scent marking and body postures. Breeding usually occurs in late winter; litters of four to six pups are born in a den after a gestation of about 63 days. Wolves play a major ecological role by regulating herbivore populations — the 1995 reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park is often cited in studies of trophic cascades.

Relationship with humans

The wolf was domesticated into the dog more than 15,000 years ago. Historically feared and persecuted because of livestock predation and rare attacks on humans, wolves were eradicated across much of Western Europe and the contiguous United States by the mid-20th century. Legal protection and reintroduction programmes have since allowed populations to recover in parts of both continents, bringing continuing debate among conservationists, hunters and farmers. The species is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN because of its wide remaining distribution, although many local populations are threatened.

Subspecies

Recognised subspecies include the Eurasian wolf (C. l. lupus), the Arctic wolf (C. l. arctos), the Mexican wolf (C. l. baileyi), the Indian wolf (C. l. pallipes) and the Himalayan wolf (C. l. chanco). The eastern wolf and red wolf, sometimes treated as separate species, remain taxonomically debated.
