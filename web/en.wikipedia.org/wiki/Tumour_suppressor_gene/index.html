Tumour suppressor gene

A tumour suppressor gene (also spelled tumor suppressor gene in American English) is a gene whose protein product normally restrains cell division, repairs DNA damage, maintains genomic stability, or triggers programmed cell death (apoptosis). When both copies of such a gene are inactivated by mutation, the loss of these controls can allow a cell to grow uncontrollably and contribute to the development of a tumor. Tumour suppressor genes thus act as the "brakes" of cell growth, in contrast to oncogenes, whose activation acts as an "accelerator".

Two-hit hypothesis

Alfred Knudson's "two-hit" hypothesis (1971), developed from study of the childhood eye cancer retinoblastoma, proposed that both alleles of a tumour suppressor gene must be inactivated for cancer to result. Individuals who inherit one defective copy — as in hereditary retinoblastoma (RB1) or Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53) — are predisposed to cancer because only a single additional somatic mutation is needed in any susceptible cell.

Examples

  • TP53 — encodes the p53 protein, the "guardian of the genome", which halts the cell cycle and induces apoptosis in response to DNA damage; it is the most commonly mutated gene in human cancers.
  • RB1 — encodes the retinoblastoma protein, a master regulator of the G1/S cell-cycle checkpoint; the first tumour suppressor gene identified.
  • BRCA1 and BRCA2 — involved in DNA double-strand break repair; inherited mutations greatly raise breast and ovarian cancer risk.
  • APC — regulates the Wnt signalling pathway; its loss initiates most colorectal cancers.
  • PTEN, VHL, NF1 and CDKN2A (p16) — other widely studied examples.

Functions

Tumour suppressors are sometimes divided into "gatekeepers", which directly control cell proliferation and death (for example RB1 and APC), and "caretakers", which maintain genome integrity through DNA repair (for example BRCA1/2 and the mismatch-repair genes of Lynch syndrome). Loss of function may occur through point mutations, deletions, loss of heterozygosity, or epigenetic silencing such as promoter methylation.
