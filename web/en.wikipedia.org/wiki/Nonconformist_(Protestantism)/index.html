Nonconformist (Protestantism)

Nonconformist is a term used in England and Wales for a Protestant Christian who does not "conform" to the governance and usages of the established church — historically the Church of England. Nonconformists include Presbyterians, Congregationalists, Baptists, Quakers, Methodists, and members of the United Reformed Church, among others. In Scotland, the term applies in relation to the established Church of Scotland.

History

The category was created by the religious settlement after the English Restoration. The Act of Uniformity 1662 required clergy to assent to the Book of Common Prayer; around 2,000 ministers who refused were ejected from their livings on St Bartholomew's Day 1662 — the "Great Ejection" — and their followers formed separate congregations. The Clarendon Code penalised dissenters, restricting their meetings, preaching and office-holding. The Toleration Act 1689 granted freedom of worship to most Protestant dissenters, though civil disabilities — such as exclusion from Oxford and Cambridge degrees and from municipal office — persisted until the repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828 and the university reforms of the nineteenth century.

Influence

Nonconformity became a powerful force in British social and political life, prominent in the temperance movement, abolitionism, and the Liberal Party — the so-called "Nonconformist conscience". Eminent Nonconformists ranged from John Bunyan and Isaac Watts to the chocolate-making Quaker families (Cadbury, Fry, Rowntree) whose philanthropy shaped their model towns.
