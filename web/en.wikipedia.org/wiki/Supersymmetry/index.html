Supersymmetry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Chart pairing each Standard Model particle with its hypothetical superpartner]
Every fermion gains a bosonic partner, and vice versa.

Supersymmetry (SUSY) is a symmetry of spacetime that relates the two classes of elementary particles: bosons and fermions. In a supersymmetric theory, each particle from one class has a superpartner in the other class whose spin differs by one half-unit — the electron a scalar 'selectron', the photon a spin-½ 'photino', and so on. If the symmetry were exact, partners would share their partners' masses; none has been observed, so supersymmetry, if real, must be broken at an energy scale above current reach.

Developed in the early 1970s (Golfand and Likhtman; Volkov and Akulov; Wess and Zumino), supersymmetry became central to theory because it stabilises the Higgs boson mass against quantum corrections (the hierarchy problem), makes the three gauge couplings unify almost exactly (Grand Unified Theory), supplies dark-matter candidates (the lightest superpartner) and is required by String theory. Incorporating gravity gives Supergravity. Four decades of searches, culminating in the LHC's null results through the 2010s–2020s, have excluded the simplest, most 'natural' versions and pushed theorists toward higher scales or subtler spectra — the field's defining disappointment and continuing debate.

Status

The Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model (MSSM) doubles the particle spectrum and predicts superpartner masses from hundreds of GeV to several TeV. Collider limits now exceed a TeV for coloured partners; whether supersymmetry is merely heavy ('split SUSY'), hidden, or absent from nature is unresolved. Its mathematical role in string theory and holography survives regardless of its low-energy fate.
