Fundamentalism

Fundamentalism
Origin of termUnited States, c. 1920
Named afterThe Fundamentals (1910–1915)
CharacteristicsStrict adherence to doctrinal fundamentals; reaction against theological modernism

Fundamentalism is a tendency among certain groups — mainly religious, but also political and ideological — characterised by a markedly strict literalism as applied to certain specific scriptures, dogmas, or ideologies, and a strong sense of the importance of maintaining ingroup and outgroup distinctions. The term originated in early-20th-century American Protestantism, for adherents of a movement that insisted on the "fundamentals" of the Christian faith against theological modernism and biblical criticism; it has since been applied, sometimes controversially, to analogous movements in other religions.

Christian fundamentalism

The movement took its name from The Fundamentals, a twelve-volume series of essays published between 1910 and 1915 affirming five points: the inerrancy of Scripture, the Virgin Birth, the substitutionary atonement, the bodily resurrection of Christ, and the authenticity of miracles (later lists added Christ's second coming). The term "fundamentalist" was coined in 1920 by Baptist editor Curtis Lee Laws. The movement gained national attention at the Scopes "Monkey Trial" of 1925 over the teaching of evolution in Tennessee, after which American fundamentalists largely withdrew from mainstream cultural institutions, building their own churches, schools and colleges, before re-emerging as a political force in the late 1970s.

Use for other movements

Scholars such as those of the Fundamentalism Project (Martin Marty and R. Scott Appleby) have compared movements in Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, Sikhism and other traditions that combine scriptural literalism, selective retrieval of tradition and reaction against secular modernity. Critics of the broader usage argue the term carries pejorative connotations of extremism and may obscure important differences between movements; "Islamic fundamentalism", for instance, is often used loosely for Islamism. The word is also applied metaphorically in non-religious contexts, e.g. "market fundamentalism".
