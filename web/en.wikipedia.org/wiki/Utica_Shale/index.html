Utica Shale


Outcrop of the Utica Shale, New York

The Utica Shale is a Late Ordovician geological formation of black, organic-rich shale underlying much of the Appalachian Basin and the St. Lawrence Lowlands in the eastern United States and Canada. Named for outcrops near Utica, New York, it lies a few thousand feet beneath the younger, better-known Marcellus Shale and reaches thicknesses of several hundred feet in places.

Geology

Deposited about 450 million years ago in a deep foreland basin formed during the Taconic orogeny, the Utica consists of calcareous black shale with high total organic carbon. Its Point Pleasant interval in Ohio is the most productive zone. Depth increases southeastward: where it is deeply buried in Pennsylvania it is thermally mature for dry gas; in Ohio it yields oil, wet gas and natural-gas liquids; in New York it lies shallower.

Development

Horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing opened the Utica to production from about 2011, centred on Ohio, where it drove a drilling boom in the state's east. It also underlies parts of Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, New York and the Canadian provinces of Quebec and Ontario, though Quebec and New York banned high-volume fracking. The U.S. Geological Survey has estimated mean undiscovered resources in the tens of trillions of cubic feet of gas plus oil and liquids.
