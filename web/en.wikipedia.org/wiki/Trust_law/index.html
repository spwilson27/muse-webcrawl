Trust law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trust is a legal arrangement in which property is held by one party (the trustee) for the benefit of another (the beneficiary), under duties enforceable in equity. The settlor transfers assets to trustees, who hold legal title but must administer them solely for the beneficiaries or purposes specified; the beneficiaries hold the equitable interest. Trusts arose in England from medieval “uses” and were developed by the Court of Chancery.

Trusts require certainty of intention, subject matter and objects, and arise expressly, by implication (resulting trusts), or by operation of law (constructive trusts). Their uses are legion: family wealth management and wills, pension funds, unit trusts and investment vehicles, charities, and commercial structures (debenture trusts, securitisations). Trustee duties — loyalty, prudence, no unauthorised profit — are strict, and breach of trust gives personal and proprietary remedies, including tracing into substituted assets. Civil-law countries long lacked the trust but many have adopted equivalents (fiducie, Treuhand) or recognised foreign trusts under the Hague Trusts Convention (1985).
