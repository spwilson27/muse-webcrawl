Tax Cuts and Jobs Act

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA), formally "An Act to provide for reconciliation pursuant to titles II and V of the concurrent resolution on the budget for fiscal year 2018" (Public Law 115-97), is a United States federal law enacted in December 2017 that made the most sweeping changes to the Internal Revenue Code since the Tax Reform Act of 1986. It was signed by President Donald Trump on 22 December 2017 after passing Congress on party-line votes through the budget reconciliation process.

Main provisions

For corporations, the Act permanently cut the top corporate income tax rate from 35% to a flat 21%, repealed the corporate alternative minimum tax, and moved the United States toward a territorial system for foreign profits, with a one-time transition tax on accumulated offshore earnings and new provisions (GILTI, BEAT and FDII) affecting multinational taxation. For individuals, it lowered most marginal rates (the top rate fell from 39.6% to 37%), nearly doubled the standard deduction, doubled the child tax credit, capped the state and local tax (SALT) deduction at $10,000, limited the mortgage interest deduction, and repealed the personal exemption; most individual provisions were scheduled to expire after 2025. The Act also reduced the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate penalty to zero from 2019, doubled the estate tax exemption, created a 20% deduction for pass-through business income (section 199A), and introduced Opportunity Zones.

Effects and debate

The Congressional Budget Office estimated the Act would add roughly $1.9 trillion to federal deficits over a decade including interest and macroeconomic feedback. Supporters credited it with boosting business investment, wages and repatriation of foreign profits; critics argued the benefits flowed disproportionately to corporations and high earners, pointing to large share buybacks, and disputed the size of wage and investment effects. The SALT cap proved especially contentious in high-tax states. The scheduled expiry of the individual provisions at the end of 2025 made the Act's future a central issue in subsequent tax legislation debates.
