South Sea Company

The South Sea Company (officially the Governor and Company of the merchants of Great Britain, trading to the South Seas and other parts of America, and for the encouragement of fishing) was a British joint-stock company founded in January 1711, created as a public-private partnership to consolidate and reduce the cost of the national debt, as well as to revive and encourage trade with South America. In 1720 the company's stock became the centre of the South Sea Bubble, one of the most famous speculative manias in financial history.

Foundation and trade

The company received a monopoly on British trade with Spanish America, including the asiento granted by the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713 to supply enslaved Africans to Spain's colonies, together with the right to send one trading ship a year. Its actual trading profits were modest, but the company's financial role — taking over portions of the government debt in exchange for a guaranteed income from the state — made its stock attractive.

The Bubble of 1720

In 1720 the company proposed to assume the bulk of the entire national debt. A speculative frenzy followed: the share price rose from about £128 in January 1720 to a peak over £1,000 in the summer, drawing in investors from every level of society and spawning dozens of rival "bubble companies". The price then collapsed to under £200 by the end of the year, ruining many investors and triggering a political scandal that reached the Chancellor of the Exchequer, John Aislabie, who was expelled from the Commons and imprisoned. Robert Walpole's management of the aftermath helped establish him as Britain's first prime minister.

Later history

The company was restructured, its debt largely taken over by the Bank of England and the East India Company. It continued in being — trading, whaling in Greenland waters in the 1720s–30s, and managing its remaining government securities — until it was finally wound up in the 1850s.
