Space settlement

A space settlement (also called a space habitat or orbital colony) is a hypothetical permanent human settlement located in outer space or on a celestial body, as distinct from a temporary space station. No space settlement has yet been built; the concept has been developed extensively in engineering studies and science fiction.

Designs

Classic 1970s studies, notably Gerard K. O'Neill's proposals and the 1975 NASA Ames summer study, envisioned vast rotating structures — cylinders, toruses (such as the Stanford torus) and spheres (Bernal spheres) — that simulate gravity through rotation, house thousands to millions of residents, and use sunlight reflected by mirrors for light and agriculture. Materials would come largely from the Moon or asteroids, avoiding the cost of lifting them from Earth. Settlers would need closed ecological life-support, radiation shielding several metres thick, and protection from micrometeorites.

Rationale and criticism

Advocates cite survival insurance for humanity, access to solar energy and raw materials, and room for population growth. Critics question the enormous cost, unproven closed ecosystems, the physiological effects of partial gravity, and the social and ethical questions of governing off-world communities. Alternative settlement sites include the surfaces of Mars and the Moon.
