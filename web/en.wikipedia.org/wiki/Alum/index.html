An alum is a type of chemical compound, usually a hydrated double sulphate salt of aluminium with another cation. The general formula is XAl(SO4)2·12H2O, where X is a monovalent cation such as potassium or ammonium. By itself, the word "alum" most often refers to potash alum, KAl(SO4)2·12H2O, a substance known and traded since antiquity. Other alums substitute chromium or iron for aluminium (chrome alum, ferric alum) or sodium or ammonium for potassium.

Properties and production

Alums crystallise in regular octahedra and dissolve readily in water, giving acidic solutions. Potash alum is produced by treating bauxite or alum shale with sulphuric acid and adding potassium sulphate, then crystallising the product. Historically it was mined from alunite rock and from alum schists, notably at Tolfa in Italy and Whitby in England, and control of alum mines was once of strategic economic importance because of the dyeing industry.

Uses

Alum has been used since ancient Egypt and Rome. Its most important historical role was as a mordant that fixes dyes to cloth; it also served in tanning leather, sizing paper, and making parchment transparent. In water treatment, aluminium sulphate ("filter alum") is still used as a flocculant to clarify drinking water. Potash alum is mildly antiseptic and astringent, used in styptic pencils, as an after-shave and deodorant crystal, as a firming agent in pickling, and as a component of some baking powders and vaccine adjuvants.
