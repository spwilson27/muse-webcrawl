Atrium (heart)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The atrium (plural: atria) is one of the two upper chambers of the heart that receives blood from the circulatory system. The blood in the atria is pumped into the ventricles through the atrioventricular valves: the tricuspid valve on the right and the mitral valve on the left.

Structure

Right atrium

The right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the systemic circulation via the superior and inferior venae cavae and from the heart itself via the coronary sinus. It contains the sinoatrial node, the heart's natural pacemaker, and the fossa ovalis, a remnant of the fetal foramen ovale, on its medial wall. Its muscular ridges are called pectinate muscles.

Left atrium

The left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs through the four pulmonary veins. It is the most posterior of the heart chambers, lying directly in front of the oesophagus — a relationship of clinical importance in ablation procedures for atrial fibrillation. Its appendage is a common site of thrombus formation.

Function

The atria act as reservoirs and conduits during ventricular systole and as booster pumps in late diastole; atrial contraction contributes roughly 20–30% of ventricular filling, the "atrial kick" whose loss in atrial fibrillation can reduce cardiac output. The interatrial septum separates the two atria; defects in it produce atrial septal defects.

Development

The atria develop from the primitive atrium of the embryonic heart tube, which is divided by the septum primum and septum secundum, leaving the foramen ovale that normally closes after birth.
