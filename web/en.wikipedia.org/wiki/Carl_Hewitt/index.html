Carl Hewitt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Carl Eddie Hewitt (born 1944) is an American computer scientist who designed the Planner programming language and originated the actor model of concurrent computation, one of the most influential frameworks for distributed systems. He was a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT).

Planner and knowledge-based systems

Hewitt received his PhD from MIT in 1971 under Seymour Papert, Marvin Minsky and Mike Paterson. His work on Planner (1969) proposed programming in terms of goals, assertions and pattern-directed invocation of procedures — an early architecture for knowledge-based and expert systems that influenced later languages such as Prolog (by reaction against Planner's procedural embedding of knowledge) and the micro-worlds research at the MIT AI Lab.

The actor model

In 1973, with Peter Bishop and Richard Steiger, Hewitt published the actor model: a mathematical model of concurrent computation in which "actors" are the universal primitives — computational entities that, in response to a message, can make local decisions, create more actors, and send messages to others. The model treated computation as inherently distributed and asynchronous, motivated partly by the physics of computation and by Hewitt's vision of open, networked systems ("open systems" and his "ViewFinder" sociology-of-science work). Actor ideas influenced the design of languages and systems including Smalltalk, Erlang's processes, the Akka toolkit, Microsoft's Orleans, and modern microservice architectures.

Logic and inconsistency

Hewitt later argued that large-scale real-world information systems are inherently inconsistent, and developed "direct logic" and the iConsult/iOrgs programme on inconsistency-robust information integration — a departure from classical-logic orthodoxy that has been debated within the logic-programming community.
