Isomerism

Isomerism is the phenomenon in which molecules or polyatomic ions have the same chemical formula — the same number of atoms of each element — but different arrangements of atoms in space, and therefore different properties. A molecule or ion exhibiting isomerism is known as an isomer. There are two broad categories: structural isomerism (also called constitutional isomerism) and stereoisomerism.

Structural isomers

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but differ in how their atoms are connected. Butane and isobutane, both C4H10, are the classic example: butane has a straight four-carbon chain, whereas isobutane has a branched chain with a methyl group on the central carbon. Structural isomerism is subdivided into chain isomerism, position isomerism, and functional group isomerism.

Stereoisomers

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but differ in the spatial arrangement of their atoms. They include enantiomers, which are non-superimposable mirror images of one another (as in chiral molecules), and diastereomers, which are not mirror images. Geometric (cis–trans) isomerism arises from restricted rotation, as about a carbon–carbon double bond.

History

The concept was introduced by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1830 after Friedrich Wöhler and Justus von Liebig found that silver fulminate and silver cyanate had identical compositions but strikingly different properties.
