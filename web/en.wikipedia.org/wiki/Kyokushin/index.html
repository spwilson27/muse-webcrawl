Kyokushin (極真, "the ultimate truth") is a style of stand-up, full-contact karate founded in 1964 by Korean-Japanese master Mas Oyama (Masutatsu Oyama). It is rooted in a philosophy of self-improvement and hard, realistic fighting: tournaments are fought bare-knuckle under knockdown rules, in which punches to the head are prohibited but kicks to the head, and punches and kicks to the body and legs, are delivered at full force without protective padding.

History

Oyama, born Choi Yeong-eui in Korea, trained in Shotokan under Gichin Funakoshi and in Goju-Ryu under So Nei Chu, and became famous for feats such as fighting bulls and completing the hyakunin kumite (100-man kumite) challenge. He opened his first dojo in Tokyo in 1953 and formally established the International Karate Organization (IKO) Kyokushinkaikan in 1964. From the 1970s Kyokushin spread worldwide, with world open tournaments held in Tokyo drawing large audiences. After Oyama's death in 1994, disputes over succession fragmented the movement into numerous organizations, including the IKO led by Shokei Matsui, the WKO (Shinkyokushinkai) led by Kenji Midori, the IFK, Kyokushin-kan and others.

Training

Training follows the classic three pillars of karate: kihon (basics), kata (forms, drawn largely from Shotokan and Goju-Ryu) and kumite (sparring), supplemented by rigorous physical conditioning, body hardening and breaking (tameshiwari). The emblem of Kyokushin is the kanku, taken from the Kanku kata. The ultimate demonstration of endurance is the 100-man kumite, completed by only a small number of practitioners, including Steve Arneil, Akira Masuda and Francisco Filho.

Influence

Kyokushin strongly influenced full-contact kickboxing and mixed martial arts; notable fighters with Kyokushin backgrounds include Georges St-Pierre, Bas Rutten, Semmy Schilt and Andy Hug (via the offshoot Seidokaikan). Offshoot styles such as Ashihara, Enshin, Shidokan and Kudo descend from it.
