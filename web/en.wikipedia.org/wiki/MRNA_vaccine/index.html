MRNA vaccine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An mRNA vaccine is a vaccine that uses messenger RNA to instruct the recipient's own cells to produce an antigen — typically a viral protein — provoking an immune response without using any live pathogen. The mRNA is delivered inside lipid nanoparticles and degrades within days.

Development

The approach rests on decades of research: mRNA was discovered in 1961, liposome delivery explored from the 1970s, and the key breakthrough — modifying nucleosides (notably pseudouridine) so synthetic mRNA evades destructive innate immune reactions — was made by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman in 2005, work recognised by the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The first mRNA vaccines to be authorised were the COVID-19 vaccines from Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna in December 2020 (see COVID-19), produced at unprecedented speed because the platform only needs the pathogen's genetic sequence.

Prospects

mRNA platforms are now being tested against influenza, RSV, HIV and other infections, and in personalised cancer vaccines that encode a patient's tumour-specific antigens (see antigen). Advantages include rapid redesign and cell-free manufacturing; disadvantages include cold-chain requirements and reactogenicity.
