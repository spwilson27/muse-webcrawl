Progressive rock (also known as prog rock or simply prog) is a broad genre of rock music that developed in the United Kingdom in the mid-to-late 1960s. Initially termed "progressive pop", the style was an outgrowth of psychedelic bands who abandoned standard pop traditions in favour of instrumentation and compositional techniques more frequently associated with jazz, folk, or classical music. Additional elements included concept albums, elaborate lyrics — often dealing with science fiction, fantasy and philosophy — extended instrumental passages, odd time signatures, and virtuoso musicianship.

Characteristics

Progressive rock bands pushed rock's technical and compositional boundaries: songs were frequently arranged as multi-part suites lasting an entire album side, time signatures shifted between bars, and instrumentation expanded beyond guitars, bass and drums to include Hammond organ, Mellotron, synthesizers, flute and full orchestral textures. Albums were often conceived as unified works (concept albums) with elaborate gatefold artwork.

History

The genre crystallized around 1969 with albums such as King Crimson's In the Court of the Crimson King. In the early 1970s bands including Pink Floyd, Yes, Genesis, Emerson, Lake & Palmer, Jethro Tull and Rush achieved major commercial success, culminating in works like The Dark Side of the Moon (1973) and Close to the Edge (1972). By the late 1970s, punk rock's backlash against perceived excess, along with changing tastes, pushed prog out of the mainstream, though a neo-progressive revival (Marillion and others) followed in the 1980s, and the genre has remained influential on metal, alternative and modern progressive bands.

Legacy

Progressive rock is frequently cited in polls of the greatest albums of all time and has spawned subgenres including progressive metal, symphonic prog, and the Canterbury scene. Its emphasis on the album as an artistic statement helped shape rock criticism and the music industry of the 1970s.
