Gobies are small fishes of the family Gobiidae, one of the largest fish families, with more than 2,000 species in over 200 genera. Most gobies are relatively small — typically less than 10 cm (4 in) long — and are bottom-dwelling fishes of shallow coastal waters, coral reefs, estuaries and rivers around the world. The family includes some of the smallest vertebrates known, such as Trimmatom nanus and the dwarf pygmy goby Pandaka pygmaea.

Characteristics

Gobies are distinguished by their fused pelvic fins, which form a suction-cup-like disc used to cling to rocks and coral in surge zones. Most lack a swim bladder and spend their lives on or near the bottom. Many species dig burrows or occupy burrows made by other animals.

Ecology

Some gobies live in remarkable symbioses: shrimp gobies share burrows with alpheid pistol shrimps, the shrimp maintaining the burrow while the nearly blind goby keeps watch; cleaner gobies of the genus Elacatinus pick parasites from larger reef fish; and mudskippers are amphibious gobies that move on land using their pectoral fins. The round goby (Neogobius melanostomus) from the Black and Caspian seas has become a notorious invasive species in the North American Great Lakes and European rivers. Many colourful species, such as clown and neon gobies, are popular in marine aquaria.
