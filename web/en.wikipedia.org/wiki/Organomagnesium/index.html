Organomagnesium

A Grignard reagent, the best-known class of organomagnesium compounds.

Organomagnesium chemistry comprises the study of compounds containing a carbon–magnesium bond. The most famous organomagnesium compounds are the Grignard reagents, organomagnesium halides of the general formula RMgX (X = Cl, Br, I), discovered by Victor Grignard around 1900 and recognised by the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1912.

Grignard reagents

Grignard reagents are prepared by reacting an alkyl or aryl halide with magnesium metal in a dry ether solvent, typically diethyl ether or tetrahydrofuran. They behave as strong bases and as nucleophiles equivalent to carbanions, adding to carbonyl groups of aldehydes, ketones, esters and carbon dioxide to form new carbon–carbon bonds; hydrolysis then yields alcohols or carboxylic acids. Because they react violently with water and air, they must be handled under anhydrous, inert conditions.

Other organomagnesium compounds

Dialkylmagnesium compounds (R2Mg), magnesacycles and mixed "turbo-Grignard" reagents (such as i-PrMgCl·LiCl) extend the family. In solution, Grignard reagents participate in the Schlenk equilibrium between RMgX, R2Mg and MgX2. Organomagnesium reagents are also used industrially, for example in the preparation of pharmaceuticals and in Ziegler–Natta-type polymerisation chemistry.
