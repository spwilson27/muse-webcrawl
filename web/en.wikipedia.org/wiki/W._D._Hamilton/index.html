William Donald Hamilton

Born: 1 August 1936, Cairo, Egypt

Died: 7 March 2000, London, England

Fields: Evolutionary biology

Known for: Kin selection, Hamilton's rule, inclusive fitness

William Donald Hamilton FRS (1 August 1936 – 7 March 2000) was a British evolutionary biologist. He is widely regarded as one of the most significant evolutionary theorists of the 20th century, best known for his mathematical theory of kin selection and inclusive fitness, which explained the evolution of altruistic behaviour in terms of the genes shared between relatives.

Kin selection and Hamilton's rule

In two papers published in 1964, "The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour" I and II, Hamilton formalised the concept that a gene can spread through a population not only by increasing the reproductive success of its bearer, but also by increasing the success of relatives who are likely to carry copies of the same gene. This led to Hamilton's rule, expressed as rB > C: an altruistic act evolves when the benefit B to the recipient, weighted by the coefficient of relatedness r, exceeds the cost C to the actor. The theory elegantly explained the evolution of sterile worker castes in ants, bees and wasps (Hymenoptera), whose haplodiploid sex-determination system produces unusually high relatedness among sisters.

Other work

Hamilton also made major contributions to the evolutionary theory of sex, proposing with colleagues that sexual reproduction is maintained because it helps hosts stay ahead of rapidly evolving parasites — the "Red Queen" hypothesis. He worked on sex ratios, ageing and senescence, extraordinary sex ratios in fig wasps and other insects, and the evolution of social behaviour in general. His collected papers were published in the volumes Narrow Roads of Gene Land.

Career and death

Hamilton taught at Imperial College London and the London School of Economics before becoming a professor at the University of Michigan and, from 1984, a Royal Society research professor at the University of Oxford. He died in 2000 of complications following an expedition to the Congo, where he had been collecting data on the origins of HIV. He was awarded the Darwin Medal in 1988 and the Crafoord Prize in 1993.
