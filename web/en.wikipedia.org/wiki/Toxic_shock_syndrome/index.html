Toxic shock syndrome (TSS) is a condition caused by bacterial toxins. Symptoms may include fever, rash, skin peeling, and low blood pressure. There may also be symptoms related to the specific underlying infection such as mastitis, osteomyelitis, necrotising fasciitis, or pneumonia. TSS is typically of two types, caused by Staphylococcus aureus or by Streptococcus pyogenes; streptococcal TSS is sometimes referred to as toxic shock-like syndrome (TSLS).

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms of TSS arise suddenly and progress rapidly. They include a high fever, a diffuse "sunburn" rash, hypotension (low blood pressure), malaise and confusion, vomiting and diarrhoea, and involvement of multiple organ systems — kidney, liver, blood, mucous membranes and the central nervous system. One to two weeks after onset, the skin of the palms and soles characteristically peels (desquamation). Staphylococcal TSS is defined by clinical criteria originally set out by the CDC; blood cultures are usually negative in staphylococcal TSS because the illness is caused by toxin rather than by bacteraemia.

Causes and mechanism

TSS is caused by exotoxins that act as superantigens. In staphylococcal TSS the principal toxin is toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1), produced by S. aureus colonising a mucosal site such as the vagina or a wound; in streptococcal TSS, streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins produced during invasive S. pyogenes infection are responsible. Superantigens bind directly to MHC class II molecules and T-cell receptors, bypassing normal antigen processing and activating a large fraction of T cells at once. The resulting massive release of cytokines produces capillary leak, fever, rash and shock.

Risk factors

In 1980 TSS was famously associated with the use of super-absorbent tampons, especially the Rely tampon, which was withdrawn from the market; cases fell sharply after changes in tampon materials and absorbency labelling. Menstrual TSS is now less common, and roughly half of staphylococcal cases are non-menstrual, following wounds, surgery, burns, childbirth, nasal packing or localised infections. Streptococcal TSS usually complicates invasive soft-tissue infections such as necrotising fasciitis.

Treatment

TSS is a medical emergency. Treatment includes removal of the source (for example, a retained tampon or infected packing), aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, antibiotics that suppress toxin production such as clindamycin together with a beta-lactam or vancomycin, and intensive care support of failing organs. Intravenous immunoglobulin is sometimes used, particularly in streptococcal TSS. Even with treatment, staphylococcal TSS has a mortality of under 5%, while streptococcal TSS kills roughly 30–70% of patients.

History

The term was coined in 1978 by the paediatrician James K. Todd, who described the syndrome in seven children. The 1980 epidemic among menstruating women in the United States, linked to high-absorbency tampons, brought TSS to wide public attention and led to regulatory changes and warning labels on tampon packaging.
