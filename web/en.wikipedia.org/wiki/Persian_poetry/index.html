Persian poetry (Persian: شعر فارسی) is one of the world's great literary traditions, extending for more than a thousand years across Persia (modern Iran), Afghanistan, Tajikistan and the wider Persianate world. Written in the Persian language, it flourished at courts from Samarkand to Delhi and Constantinople, and its imagery, forms and metres profoundly influenced Ottoman Turkish, Urdu and other literatures.

Forms

Classical Persian poetry is quantitative, built on metres derived from Arabic prosody (ʿarūż). Major forms include the masnavi, rhyming couplets used for long narrative and mystical poems; the ghazal, a short lyric of autonomous couplets unified by rhyme and refrain, typically on love and wine; the qasida, a longer ode of praise or satire; and the ruba'i, the quatrain made famous by Omar Khayyam.

History

The earliest major figure is Rudaki (d. 941), "father of Persian poetry", at the Samanid court. Ferdowsi's Shahnameh (Book of Kings, completed 1010) preserved Persian epic and language. The mystical masnavis of Attar and Rumi's Masnavi — some 26,000 couplets — made poetry a vehicle of Sufi teaching. Saadi's Gulistan and Bustan (13th century) set the standard for ethical prose-poetry, while Hafez of Shiraz (14th century) perfected the ghazal; his Divan remains a household text in Iran, consulted for bibliomancy (fāl).

Modern poetry

In the 20th century Nima Yushij founded "New Poetry" (she'r-e now), breaking classical metre and form. Later poets such as Forugh Farrokhzad, Ahmad Shamlou and Sohrab Sepehri carried Persian poetry into free verse and contemporary themes.
