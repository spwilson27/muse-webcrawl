Mercat cross

The mercat cross of a Scottish burgh marked its right to hold a market.

A mercat cross is the Scots name for the market cross found in many Scottish towns, cities and villages. It symbolised a burgh's right — granted by the monarch, a bishop or a baron — to hold a regular market or fair, and it served as the focal point of civic life: the place where royal proclamations were read, merchants displayed goods, public punishments were inflicted, and contracts were sealed.

Design

Typically a mercat cross consists of a tall stone shaft rising from a stepped octagonal base, surmounted by a finial — a cross, a carved capital, the royal unicorn of Scotland, or the arms of the burgh or its founder. The crosses date from the medieval period onward; many were restored or rebuilt in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and again in the Victorian era. Scotland retains around 126 known mercat crosses, the largest concentration of market crosses in Britain.

Functions

Beyond marking the market, the cross was the town's legal and ceremonial centre. Proclamations of new monarchs are still made at the Mercat Cross in Edinburgh, where royal heralds read announcements and where the return of the Stone of Destiny was announced. Executions and punishments took place at crosses; trade was required to be conducted "at the cross" under standard weights; and in many burghs the cross was the point from which distances were measured.

Notable examples

Famous surviving crosses include those of Edinburgh (rebuilt 1885 incorporating the original shaft capital), Glasgow, Aberdeen (1686, one of the finest, with portraits of Scottish monarchs), Prestonpans, Culross, Dunfermline and Clackmannan — the last associated with Robert the Bruce. Many crosses were removed in the eighteenth century as obstructions to traffic and later restored.
