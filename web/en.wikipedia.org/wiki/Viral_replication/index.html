Viral replication

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stages
Attachment · Penetration · Uncoating · Replication · Assembly · Release

Viral replication is the formation of biological viruses during the infection process in the target host cells. Viruses must first get into the cell before viral replication can occur. Through the generation of abundant copies of its genome and packaging these copies, the virus continues infecting new hosts. Replication between viruses is greatly varied and depends on the type of genes involved in them. Most DNA viruses assemble in the nucleus while most RNA viruses develop solely in the cytoplasm.

Stages

Attachment. The virus binds to specific receptor molecules on the surface of a susceptible host cell. The specificity of this interaction determines the host range and cell tropism of the virus.

Penetration. The virion enters the cell, by receptor-mediated endocytosis, membrane fusion, or—in bacteriophages—injection of the genome.

Uncoating. The viral capsid is removed or degraded, releasing the viral genome inside the cell.

Replication and protein synthesis. The viral genome is copied and viral proteins are made using the host cell's machinery. The strategy depends on the genome type (see Baltimore classification): double-stranded DNA viruses replicate much like cellular DNA; positive-sense RNA viruses use their genome directly as mRNA; negative-sense RNA viruses must first transcribe it using a viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase; and retroviruses reverse-transcribe their RNA into DNA, which is integrated into the host genome.

Assembly. Newly synthesized genomes and proteins assemble into progeny virions.

Release. Progeny viruses leave the cell by budding (commonly used by enveloped viruses, which acquire their envelope from host membranes) or by lysing the cell (commonly used by non-enveloped viruses and bacteriophages).

Growth cycle outcomes

In a productive (lytic) infection, the cycle is completed and new infectious virions are released, often killing the host cell. In latent infections, the viral genome persists without producing virions, as in herpesviruses and in the lysogenic cycle of temperate bacteriophages, where the viral DNA integrates as a prophage and is copied with the host genome until induction triggers the lytic cycle. Some infections are persistent or transforming, as when certain viruses promote cell proliferation and cancer.

Replication fidelity

RNA viruses generally replicate with low fidelity because their polymerases lack proofreading, which produces large mutant populations ("quasispecies") and rapid evolution. DNA viruses and retroviruses differ: retroviral reverse transcriptase is also error-prone. This variability is a major challenge for vaccine and antiviral drug design, although it also constrains viral fitness through error thresholds.
