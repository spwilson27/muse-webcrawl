Imari ware

Imari ware (Japanese: 伊万里焼, Imari-yaki) is a type of Japanese porcelain made in the town of Arita, in the former Hizen Province (present-day Saga Prefecture), northwestern Kyūshū. The ware takes its name from the port of Imari, from which it was shipped to Europe and elsewhere. In the West, "Imari" traditionally refers especially to the colourful export wares decorated in underglaze blue with overglaze red, green and gold enamels.

History

Porcelain production in Arita began in the early 17th century after Korean potters, brought to Japan during the invasions of Korea in the 1590s, discovered kaolin clay at Izumiyama. When the Chinese porcelain trade was disrupted by the Ming–Qing transition in the 1650s, the Dutch East India Company turned to Japanese porcelain, and Imari ware flooded European markets, where it was eagerly collected and imitated — notably at Meissen and later by English factories.

Styles

Several decorative styles are distinguished: Ko-Imari ("old Imari"), the earliest export style; Kakiemon ware, with delicate enamel painting over a milky-white body; Nabeshima ware, made for the ruling Nabeshima clan under strict secrecy; and the brocade-like Kinrande style, lavishly decorated with gold. Chinese potters in turn imitated Imari palettes in "Chinese Imari" export ware.

Decline and legacy

As Chinese exports resumed and European porcelain manufacture developed, Japanese exports declined in the 18th century. Arita remains a major porcelain centre today, and antique Imari is widely collected and displayed in museums worldwide.
