Parathyroid glands are small endocrine glands in the neck of humans and other tetrapods. Humans usually have four parathyroid glands, located on the back of the thyroid gland in variable places. The parathyroid glands produce and secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH) in response to low blood calcium, which plays a key role in regulating the amount of calcium in the blood and within the bones.

The parathyroid glands share a similar blood supply, venous drainage and lymphatic drainage to the thyroid glands. They are derived embryologically from the endoderm of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches. Each gland is roughly the size of a grain of rice, about 6 mm in length, and weighs about 30–40 mg in adults.

Structure

The two pairs of glands are usually designated the superior and inferior parathyroid glands. The superior pair arises from the fourth pharyngeal pouch and is usually found at the posterior aspect of the upper thyroid; the inferior pair arises from the third pouch and is more variable in position because it migrates with the thymus. The glands contain chief cells, which secrete PTH, and oxyphil cells, whose function is less clear.

Function

The main function of the parathyroid glands is to maintain the body's calcium and phosphate levels within a very narrow range. When blood calcium falls, chief cells sense the change via calcium-sensing receptors and increase PTH secretion. PTH raises blood calcium by stimulating bone resorption, increasing renal calcium reabsorption, and promoting activation of vitamin D, which increases intestinal absorption of calcium.

Clinical significance

Overactivity of one or more glands causes hyperparathyroidism, most often due to a benign adenoma, leading to hypercalcaemia with symptoms sometimes summarised as "stones, bones, groans and moans" — kidney stones, bone pain and demineralisation, abdominal complaints, and psychiatric symptoms. Underactivity, hypoparathyroidism, most commonly follows thyroid surgery and causes hypocalcaemia with tetany.

History

The parathyroid glands were first described in the Indian rhinoceros by Richard Owen in 1850 and in humans by the Swedish medical student Ivar Victor Sandström in 1880; their function in calcium metabolism was worked out in the early 20th century.
