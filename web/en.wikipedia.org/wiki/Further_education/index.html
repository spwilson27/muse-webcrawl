Further education

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Further education (often abbreviated FE) in the United Kingdom and Ireland is education in addition to that received at secondary school, distinct from the higher education offered at universities. It is usually taken after the age of 16 — the minimum school leaving age — and includes vocational and technical qualifications as well as the academic courses (such as A-levels) that can also be taken in schools. Further education is delivered mainly by dedicated FE colleges, sixth-form colleges, and adult and community learning providers.

Further education colleges combine academic courses with hands-on vocational training.

Provision in England

English FE colleges offer courses from basic literacy and numeracy and entry-level qualifications through GCSEs and A-levels to BTECs, apprenticeships, National Vocational Qualifications (NVQs), foundation degrees and, in some colleges, full degrees. Since 2015–16 young people in England have been required to remain in education or training until 18, which most do in school sixth forms, sixth-form colleges or FE colleges, or through apprenticeships. The sector was incorporated — removed from local authority control — under the Further and Higher Education Act 1992, and colleges are funded through agencies of central government, a model long criticised for instability and underfunding compared with schools and universities.

Elsewhere

In Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, FE is devolved and organised through regional colleges; Scotland's colleges were reorganised into 13 regions in the 2010s. In Ireland, further education includes Post-Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses and the training functions formerly run by FÁS, now within Education and Training Boards and SOLAS. Analogous sectors elsewhere include American community colleges, Australian TAFE institutes and German vocational schools (Berufsschulen), though the UK term "further education" is specific to its own system.
