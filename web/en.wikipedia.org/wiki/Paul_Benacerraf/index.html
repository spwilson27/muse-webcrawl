Paul Benacerraf

Paul Joseph Salomon Benacerraf (born 4 March 1930) is a French-born American philosopher working in the philosophy of mathematics. He is the James S. McDonnell Distinguished University Professor of Philosophy Emeritus at Princeton University, where he spent his teaching career and also served as provost. His two papers "What Numbers Could Not Be" (1965) and "Mathematical Truth" (1973) are among the most influential works in modern philosophy of mathematics.

Benacerraf's identification problem and epistemological dilemma reshaped debate about mathematical objects.

Work

In "What Numbers Could Not Be," Benacerraf considered the competing set-theoretic reductions of the natural numbers — Zermelo's and von Neumann's — and argued that since the two reductions disagree about which sets the numbers are, yet both serve equally well, numbers cannot be any particular sets: what matters is only the structure of the progression. The argument became a founding motivation for mathematical structuralism. In "Mathematical Truth," he posed a dilemma: a satisfactory philosophy of mathematics must reconcile a plausible (causal) theory of knowledge with a semantics for mathematics that treats its statements as referring to abstract objects — but abstract objects, being outside space and time, cannot be causally known. This "epistemological problem" for Platonism set the agenda for decades of subsequent work, including responses by Hartry Field and others. With Hilary Putnam he edited the standard anthology Philosophy of Mathematics: Selected Readings (1964; 2nd ed. 1983).
