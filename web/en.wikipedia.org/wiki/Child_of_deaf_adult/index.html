Child of deaf adult

A child of deaf adult, often known by the acronym CODA (for "child of deaf adults"), is a person who was raised by one or more deaf parents or guardians. The term is most often applied to hearing children of deaf parents, who grow up between two languages and cultures: the spoken language of the hearing majority and the signed language and Deaf culture of their parents. The majority of children born to deaf parents are hearing — around 90 per cent.

CODAs typically grow up bilingual in a signed and a spoken language

Terminology

The acronym was coined by Millie Brother, who in 1983 founded the organisation CODA International to support children of deaf adults. Related terms include "mother-father deaf" (MFD) for a person with deaf parents on both sides, "CODA" being capitalised when referring to the cultural identity and organisation, and "koda" for a kid of deaf adults. Deaf children of deaf parents are sometimes called "Deaf of Deaf" or DoD.

Experience

CODAs typically acquire a sign language, such as American Sign Language, as a first or joint-first language at home, and spoken English from the wider environment. From an early age many act as informal interpreters and cultural brokers for their parents in interactions with hearing institutions — doctors, teachers, officials — a role that can bring maturity and bilingual skill but also burdens sometimes described as "parentification" or language brokering.

The CODA experience is a theme of growing cultural attention. The 2021 film CODA, about a hearing teenager in a deaf fishing family in Massachusetts, won the Academy Award for Best Picture and brought the term to wide public awareness; memoirs and plays by CODAs explore the bicultural identity further.

Community and research

CODA International holds annual conferences and supports networks for adult children of deaf parents. In linguistics, CODAs are studied as heritage signers whose bilingual development sheds light on language acquisition in two modalities.
