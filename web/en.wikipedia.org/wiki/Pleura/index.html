Pleura

The pleurae (singular: pleura) are the two membranes surrounding the lungs and lining the thoracic cavity. The visceral pleura covers the surface of each lung, while the parietal pleura lines the inner surface of the chest wall, the diaphragm, and the mediastinum. Between the two layers lies the pleural cavity, a potential space containing a thin film of pleural fluid that lubricates the surfaces and allows the lungs to slide smoothly during breathing, while its surface tension helps keep the lungs expanded against the chest wall.

Structure

Visceral pleura

The visceral pleura is a delicate serous membrane that covers the surface of each lung and extends into the interlobar fissures. It is intimately adherent to the lung tissue and cannot be separated from it. Unlike the parietal pleura, it receives its blood supply from the bronchial circulation and is insensitive to pain, temperature, and touch, responding mainly to stretch.

Parietal pleura

The parietal pleura lines the internal surface of the thoracic wall and is named according to the region it covers: costal pleura (against the ribs and intercostal spaces), diaphragmatic pleura (over the diaphragm), mediastinal pleura (against the mediastinum), and the cervical pleura or cupula, which projects into the neck above the first rib. It is richly supplied with pain fibres via the intercostal nerves and the phrenic nerve, which is why pleural inflammation — pleurisy — produces sharp, localised chest pain.

Recesses

Where the parietal pleura reflects from one surface to another, potential spaces called recesses form, into which the lungs do not fully extend during quiet breathing. The most important are the costodiaphragmatic recesses, the lowest points of the pleural cavity, where excess fluid tends to collect in a standing patient.

Function

The pleural fluid, secreted and absorbed by the pleural membranes, provides lubrication and creates surface tension that couples the lungs mechanically to the chest wall and diaphragm. This coupling ensures that when the thoracic cavity expands during inspiration, the lungs expand with it. If air or fluid separates the two layers — a pneumothorax or pleural effusion — this coupling is lost and the lung recoils toward collapse.

Clinical significance

  • Pleurisy (pleuritis) — inflammation of the pleura, often causing a friction rub and sharp pain on inspiration.
  • Pleural effusion — abnormal accumulation of fluid in the pleural cavity, from transudates, exudates, blood (haemothorax), lymph (chylothorax), or pus (empyema).
  • Pneumothorax — air in the pleural cavity causing partial or complete lung collapse.
  • Mesothelioma — a malignancy of the pleural mesothelium, strongly associated with asbestos exposure.
