IUCN Red List

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The IUCN Red List of Threatened Species, founded in 1964 by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), is the world's most comprehensive inventory of the conservation status of biological species. It uses a set of quantitative criteria to evaluate extinction risk and classifies species into categories: Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered, Extinct in the Wild, and Extinct (with Data Deficient and Not Evaluated for incompletely assessed species). Species in the Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered categories are collectively described as "threatened".

Scope and findings

By the mid-2020s the Red List had assessed more than 160,000 species, of which over 45,000 were classified as threatened with extinction — including about a quarter of mammals, roughly one in seven birds, over a third of amphibians (the most threatened vertebrate group), and around a third of reef-building corals and conifers. The assessments inform conservation funding, protected-area planning and international agreements such as CITES. The Red List Index tracks trends over time and consistently shows species sliding toward extinction faster than conservation action reverses the slide, a key indicator for the World Health Organization-era goals and the UN biodiversity targets. Habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species and climate change are the leading drivers recorded.
