Aramid

Aramid fibres
TypeSynthetic polymer fibre
Key examplesKevlar, Twaron (para-aramid); Nomex, Teijinconex (meta-aramid)
First produced1960s–1970s (DuPont)
PropertiesHigh strength-to-weight ratio, heat resistance, no melting point

Aramid fibres are a class of strong, heat-resistant synthetic fibres made from aromatic polyamides — long-chain polymers in which at least 85% of the amide linkages are attached directly to two aromatic rings, per the U.S. Federal Trade Commission definition. The name is a contraction of "aromatic polyamide".

History

Para-aramid fibres were developed at DuPont in the 1960s by a team led by Stephanie Kwolek, whose discovery of liquid crystalline polymer solutions in 1965 led to the commercial introduction of Kevlar in the early 1970s. The meta-aramid Nomex had been introduced in 1967 for flame-resistant clothing. The Dutch company Akzo later developed the competing para-aramid Twaron, leading to prolonged patent litigation with DuPont in the 1980s.

Types

Para-aramids

Para-aramids such as Kevlar and Twaron have very high tensile strength (roughly five times that of steel on an equal-weight basis), high modulus, and good toughness. They are used in ballistic body armour, helmets, cut-resistant gloves, tyre reinforcement, ropes and cables, fibre-optic cable strength members, and aerospace and sporting composites.

Meta-aramids

Meta-aramids such as Nomex are valued chiefly for thermal stability: they do not melt, drip, or support combustion in air, and decompose only above about 370 °C. They are used in firefighting turnout gear, racing drivers' suits, electrical insulation paper, and honeycomb cores for aircraft panels.

Properties and limitations

Aramid fibres have low compressive strength compared with carbon fibre, are degraded by ultraviolet light, and absorb moisture. They are difficult to cut or machine because of their toughness. Fibres are typically bright yellow-gold for para-aramids and white for meta-aramids.

Production

The polymer is dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid and extruded through spinnerets in a dry-jet wet-spinning process; the liquid-crystalline solution aligns the polymer chains, giving the spun fibre its exceptional axial strength.
