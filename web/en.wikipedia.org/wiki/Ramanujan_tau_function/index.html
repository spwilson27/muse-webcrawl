The Ramanujan tau function, studied by Srinivasa Ramanujan in his 1916 paper "On certain arithmetical functions", is the arithmetic function τ(n) defined by the values of the coefficients in the power series expansion of the modular discriminant Δ(z).

Δ(z) = q ∏n≥1(1 − qn)24 = Σn≥1 τ(n) qn,   where q = e2πiz.

Properties and conjectures

Ramanujan observed that τ is a multiplicative function and conjectured three properties that shaped 20th-century number theory: the multiplicativity relations for prime powers, the bound |τ(p)| ≤ 2p11/2 for primes p, and congruences such as τ(n) ≡ σ11(n) (mod 691). The multiplicativity was proved by Louis Mordell in 1917 using what became the theory of Hecke operators. The bound — the deepest of the three, and the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis for the modular form Δ — was proved by Pierre Deligne in 1974 as a consequence of his proof of the Weil conjectures, work that earned him the Fields Medal. The congruences are explained by the fact that the Eisenstein series E12 and Δ differ by a multiple of 691.

Lehmer's conjecture

D. H. Lehmer conjectured that τ(n) never vanishes; this remains unproven, though it has been verified computationally for n up to enormous bounds, and it is known that a counterexample, if one exists, must be very large. The values of τ grow roughly like n11/2 on average, as predicted by the Sato–Tate distribution for the associated modular form.
