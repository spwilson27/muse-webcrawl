Unitarianism

A Unitarian chapel: historically characterised by plain worship and rational preaching.

Unitarianism (from Latin unitas, "unity") is a Nontrinitarian branch of Christianity. Unitarians affirm the unitary nature of God as a single being and reject the doctrine of the Trinity — that God exists as three persons — as well as other traditional doctrines including original sin and predestination. Jesus is regarded as a great teacher and saviour figure but not as God incarnate.

History

Unitarian ideas surfaced in the Radical Reformation of the 16th century: Michael Servetus, burned at Geneva in 1553, denied the Trinity, and the Socinians of the Polish Brethren and the Unitarian Church of Transylvania — recognised by the Edict of Torda in 1568 — formed the first Unitarian churches. In England, Theophilus Lindsey opened the Essex Street Chapel in London in 1774, and Joseph Priestley was a leading Unitarian minister and theologian. In the United States, Unitarianism emerged from the liberal wing of New England Congregationalism; Harvard Divinity School became a Unitarian stronghold, and figures such as Ralph Waldo Emerson and Theodore Parker pushed the movement toward transcendentalism.

Beliefs

Historically Unitarians held the Bible to be authoritative but interpreted it by reason, emphasised the humanity of Jesus and his moral example, and stressed human freedom and progress. Many modern Unitarians — particularly within Unitarian Universalism — are religiously pluralist, and individual beliefs range from liberal Christianity to humanism; other national Unitarian churches, such as those in Transylvania and Hungary, remain explicitly Christian and churchly.

Organisation

Unitarian churches are congregational in polity. National bodies include the General Assembly of Unitarian and Free Christian Churches in the United Kingdom, the Unitarian Church of Transylvania, and (through its Unitarian heritage) the Unitarian Universalist Association in the United States. Famous Unitarians have included Isaac Newton (privately), John Adams, Thomas Jefferson, Charles Dickens, Florence Nightingale and Tim Berners-Lee.
