Pierre Laurent Wantzel (5 June 1814 – 21 May 1848) was a French mathematician who proved that two of the classical problems of antiquity — trisecting an angle and doubling the cube — are impossible to solve using only compass and straightedge.

The 1837 proof

In a paper published in 1837 in the Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées, Wantzel characterised the numbers constructible with compass and straightedge: a length is constructible only if it can be obtained from the rationals by a finite sequence of operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and square roots — equivalently, if it lies in a tower of quadratic field extensions, so its degree over the rationals is a power of two. Doubling the cube requires constructing ∛2, which has degree three, and trisecting a 60° angle requires a root of an irreducible cubic; hence both are impossible. In the same work Wantzel gave the necessary condition for a regular polygon to be constructible, completing the criterion associated with Gauss, and he settled the construction of regular polygons such as the heptagon (impossible) rigorously.

Life

Wantzel studied at the École Polytechnique, where he later taught analysis and mechanics, and also taught at the École des Ponts et Chaussées. Despite the importance of his 1837 result, his proof was little noticed for decades and is sometimes mistakenly credited to others; he died in Paris at the age of 33, reportedly worn down by overwork.
