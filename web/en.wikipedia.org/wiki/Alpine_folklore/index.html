Alpine folklore

[Image: A traditional Alpine Perchten mask]
A carved Perchten mask used in winter processions.

Alpine folklore comprises the traditional narratives, customs, and beliefs of the peoples of the Alps — spanning Austria, Switzerland, Bavaria, northern Italy, Slovenia, and Alpine France. Shaped by the hazards and rhythms of mountain agriculture, transhumance, avalanches, and long winters, it preserves pre-Christian elements interwoven with Catholic and Protestant practice, and continues to be enacted in masked processions, seasonal festivals, and storytelling.

Beings and legends

The Alpine supernatural world is densely populated. Krampus, the horned companion of St. Nicholas, punishes naughty children in early December, while the masked Perchten run through villages in winter to drive out the spirits of the cold season. Mountain dwarfs and mining spirits (Bergmännlein) guard buried treasure; the Tatzelwurm, a serpent-cat creature, haunts remote valleys in travellers' tales. Wild processions of the dead, such as the Wilde Jagd (Wild Hunt), sweep across stormy skies, and local saints and witches populate countless village legends.

Prominent legendary figures include William Tell, the crossbowman of Swiss national myth, and mountain hermits and dragon-slayers of local tradition. Tales of hidden hoards guarded by "Venetian" prospectors, of sunken bells, and of alpine dairymen punished for greed reflect the moral landscape of herding communities.

Customs

The folklore is embedded in the agrarian calendar: the spring driving of cattle to high pastures (Alpaufzug) and their autumn return (Alpabzug), often with decorated lead cows; midsummer fires lit on the peaks; carnival processions such as the Scheibenschlagen (throwing of burning discs); and bell-ringing to drive away winter. Yodelling, alphorn playing, and folk costume (Tracht) are folkloric expressions in sound and dress.

Study and survival

Nineteenth-century romantics and folklorists collected Alpine tales extensively, and the traditions remain vigorous, sustained by village associations and tourism alike, even as scholars caution that some "ancient" customs are documented reconstructions of more recent date.
