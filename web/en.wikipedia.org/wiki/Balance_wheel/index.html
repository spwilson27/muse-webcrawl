The balance wheel is the timekeeping device used in mechanical watches and small clocks, analogous to the pendulum in pendulum clocks. It is a weighted wheel that rotates back and forth, controlled by a spiral torsion spring known as the balance spring or hairspring. The wheel and spring together form a harmonic oscillator whose resonant frequency regulates the release of energy from the escapement, and hence the rate at which the watch's gears advance.

Operation

The escapement gives the balance a small impulse each swing, replacing energy lost to friction, and in return the balance's rotation unlocks the escapement so the gear train advances by a fixed amount. The period of oscillation depends on the wheel's moment of inertia and the stiffness of the spring. Most modern wristwatches beat at 28,800 vibrations per hour (4 Hz), giving eight ticks per second.

History

Balance wheels appeared in clocks in the 15th century, but accuracy improved dramatically when Christiaan Huygens and Robert Hooke independently added the spiral balance spring around 1675, making portable timepieces practical. Compensation balances, made of bimetallic rims, were developed in the 18th century to cancel the effect of temperature on the spring's elasticity — a key advance for marine chronometers. In the 20th century, alloys such as Elinvar and Glucydur, and later silicon hairsprings, improved stability, magnetism resistance and precision.

Regulation

A watch's rate is adjusted by altering the effective length of the hairspring (via a regulator index) or the inertia of the wheel (via timing screws or laser trimming). Precise regulation and adjustment in multiple positions distinguish chronometer-grade movements.
