Ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency

Ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency (OTCD) is an X-linked genetic disorder of the urea cycle caused by mutations in the OTC gene, which encodes the enzyme ornithine transcarbamylase. It is the most common urea cycle disorder. The enzyme catalyses the reaction of ornithine with carbamoyl phosphate to form citrulline in the mitochondria of liver cells; its deficiency blocks the urea cycle, so nitrogen from protein breakdown accumulates as toxic ammonia (hyperammonaemia).

The urea cycle: OTCD blocks the conversion of ornithine and carbamoyl phosphate to citrulline.

Presentation

Because the gene is on the X chromosome, severely affected males typically present in the newborn period: after an initially normal birth, the infant becomes lethargic, feeds poorly, hyperventilates and may progress to seizures, coma and death if untreated. Females heterozygous for the mutation have a variable course because of random X-inactivation; many are asymptomatic, but a substantial minority experience episodes of hyperammonaemia — sometimes triggered by childbirth, illness or the drug valproate — which can be life-threatening. Milder or late-onset presentations in both sexes include vomiting, migraines, behavioural changes, protein intolerance and episodes of confusion after protein loads.

Diagnosis

Biochemical findings include elevated blood ammonia and glutamine, low citrulline and arginine, and a distinctive elevation of orotic acid in the urine, reflecting diversion of carbamoyl phosphate into pyrimidine synthesis. Confirmation is by genetic testing of the OTC gene; historically, liver enzyme assay was used.

Treatment

Acute hyperammonaemia is a medical emergency treated by stopping protein intake, giving intravenous glucose to suppress catabolism, nitrogen-scavenging drugs (sodium benzoate, sodium phenylacetate or glycerol phenylbutyrate), supplementation with arginine or citrulline, and haemodialysis or haemofiltration when ammonia is dangerously high. Long-term management combines a protein-restricted diet, essential amino acid supplements and chronic nitrogen scavengers. Liver transplantation is curative of the metabolic defect. The condition received wide attention in 1999 when Jesse Gelsinger died in an early gene-therapy trial for OTCD, a landmark event in the ethics and oversight of human gene therapy.
