Frozen food

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A supermarket freezer aisle stacked with frozen vegetables and ready meals]

Frozen food is food preserved by freezing — the oldest preservation instinct (northern peoples always froze their catch) turned into an industry in the 1920s–30s by Clarence Birdseye's quick-freezing methods. Modern frozen food spans peas and berries frozen within hours of harvest, fish frozen at sea, ready meals, pizzas, ice cream and whole cuisines in the supermarket's freezer aisle. Freezing halts microbial growth and slows chemistry almost to a stop without the nutrient and texture costs of canning, at the price of an unbroken cold chain from factory to home freezer.

History

Ice itself was big business first: Frederic Tudor, Boston's "Ice King", shipped New England pond ice to the Caribbean, India and Australia from 1806, and ice houses and iceboxes conditioned cities to cold food. Mechanical refrigeration industrialised meat packing in Chicago and shipped Argentine beef and New Zealand lamb to Europe. Birdseye added the missing piece — speed — and retail followed: the 1930 Springfield launch of Birds Eye Frosted Foods, the growth of home freezers after the Second World War, and the Swanson TV dinner (1954), sold on the tray-table of the television age, completed the frozen kitchen.

Quality and questions

Blanching before freezing protects vegetables' colour and enzymes; IQF (individually quick frozen) technology keeps peas and prawns free-flowing. Frozen-at-harvest vegetables routinely match or beat tired "fresh" produce in vitamin content — a finding that surprises shoppers raised on fresh-is-best. The frozen sector's costs are energy and packaging, and its frontier keeps moving: premium frozen meals, frozen doughs and bakery, and restaurant-grade fish have upgraded the aisle's reputation from cheap substitute to deliberate choice.
