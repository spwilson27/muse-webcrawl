Perso-Arabic script

The Perso-Arabic script is a writing system based on the Arabic script that was adapted for the Persian language following the Muslim conquest of Persia in the seventh century. Persian scribes added four letters to the Arabic alphabet to represent sounds not found in Arabic — /p/, /tʃ/, /ʒ/, and /ɡ/ — and modified the shapes or uses of several others. The script was subsequently adopted, with further additions, for many languages of the Islamic world, including Urdu, Ottoman Turkish, Kurdish, Pashto, Sindhi, Malay (as Jawi), and numerous others.

Characteristics

Like Arabic, the Perso-Arabic script is written from right to left, is cursive, and represents primarily consonants, with short vowels optionally indicated by diacritics. Persian developed the elegant nastaʿlīq style of calligraphy, still preferred for Urdu, distinct from the naskh style common in Arabic printing. Persian also uses different forms of the letters kâf and yâ compared with Arabic usage, and includes the letter gaf with its distinctive stroke.

Spread and modern use

Perso-Arabic script spread across Central and South Asia with Persianate culture. Today it remains the official script of Persian (Iran, Afghanistan as Dari, and Tajikistan historically until the adoption of Cyrillic), Urdu in Pakistan and India, and several regional languages. Many languages formerly written in Perso-Arabic — such as Turkish (1928), Azerbaijani, and the Central Asian languages under the Soviet Union — have since switched to Latin or Cyrillic alphabets.
