A syllable is a unit of organisation for a sequence of speech sounds. It is typically made up of a syllable nucleus (most often a vowel) with optional initial and final margins (typically consonants). Syllables are often considered the phonological "building blocks" of words. They can influence the rhythm of a language, its prosody, its poetic metre, and its rules for where stress falls.

Structure

A syllable is conventionally divided into the onset (the consonants that begin it), the nucleus (its sonorous core, normally a vowel or a syllabic consonant such as the "l" in English bottle), and the coda (the consonants that follow the nucleus). The nucleus and coda together form the rime (or rhyme), the part of the syllable used in rhyming. A syllable with no coda is an open syllable; one with a coda is closed. Languages differ greatly in the syllable structures they permit: Japanese largely restricts syllables to (C)V(N) shapes, while English permits complex clusters, as in strengths /strɛŋkθs/ — a single syllable.

Syllables and writing

Some writing systems are organised around syllables: in a syllabary, such as Japanese kana or the Cherokee syllabary, each character represents a syllable. Other scripts, such as the Devanagari abugida, are built on consonant characters with inherent vowels. In alphabetic writing, rules for dividing words into syllables are used in hyphenation at line breaks.

Counting syllables

The number of syllables in a word is a basic measure in poetry and readability formulas. English examples: cat has one syllable, water has two, and banana has three. Poetic metres such as the haiku (traditionally counted in on, similar to morae) and the classical metres of Greek and Latin verse are built on syllable counts and syllable weight.

Syllable weight

In many languages syllables are classed as heavy or light. A syllable is typically heavy if it has a long vowel or a coda consonant, and light if it is open with a short vowel. Syllable weight conditions stress placement in languages such as Latin and Arabic and underlies quantitative verse metres.
