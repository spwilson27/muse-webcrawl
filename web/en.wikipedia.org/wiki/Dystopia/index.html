Dystopia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A grey totalitarian cityscape: endless concrete towers, surveillance cameras and a giant screen addressing a silent crowd]

A dystopia (from Ancient Greek dys- 'bad' and topos 'place'; also called a cacotopia) is a speculated community or society that is undesirable or frightening. Dystopian fiction (including a subgenre, 'anti-utopian' literature) explores societies ruled by oppressive governments, environmental collapse, technological control or the loss of individual freedom, usually as a warning extrapolated from present trends.

The genre's classics are Yevgeny Zamyatin's We (1924), Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932) and George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949), joined later by Ray Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451 (1953), Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale (1985) and a vast modern literature for young adults. Dystopia is the dark mirror of utopia — the word itself coined by analogy with Thomas More's Utopia (1516).
