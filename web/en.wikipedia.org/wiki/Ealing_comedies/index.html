The Ealing comedies are a celebrated series of comedy films produced by Ealing Studios in London between roughly 1947 and 1957. Made under the leadership of producer Michael Balcon, the films are remembered for their gentle, whimsical humour, their affectionate portrayal of British life and institutions, and their recurring theme of ordinary little people banding together to outwit authority, bureaucracy, or big business.

History

Ealing Studios, based in the west London suburb of Ealing, had produced films since the early twentieth century. Under Balcon, who took over in 1938, the studio developed a strong sense of corporate identity — Balcon's name appeared on a celebrated roundel above the studio gate. After the Second World War, the studio turned increasingly to comedy, beginning with Hue and Cry (1947), often regarded as the first of the Ealing comedies.

Notable films

  • Passport to Pimlico (1949) — residents of a London street discover an ancient charter making them part of Burgundy and declare independence.
  • Whisky Galore! (1949) — Scottish islanders salvage whisky from a wrecked ship, based on Compton Mackenzie's novel.
  • Kind Hearts and Coronets (1949) — Alec Guinness plays eight members of an aristocratic family murdered by a disinherited relative.
  • The Lavender Hill Mob (1951) — a mild-mannered bank clerk plans a bullion robbery; won an Academy Award for its screenplay by T. E. B. Clarke.
  • The Man in the White Suit (1951) — a chemist invents an indestructible fabric, threatening the textile industry.
  • The Ladykillers (1955) — a criminal gang lodges with a sweet old lady; the last and among the most famous of the cycle.

Style and legacy

Directors associated with the comedies include Charles Crichton, Alexander Mackendrick, Robert Hamer, Henry Cornelius and Charles Frend. The films combined location realism with farce and satire, and launched or consolidated the careers of actors such as Alec Guinness, Stanley Holloway, Sid James and Peter Sellers. Ealing Studios was sold to the BBC in 1955, and the comedy cycle wound down by the late 1950s, but the films remain central to the canon of British cinema; several have been remade, most notably The Ladykillers (2004) by the Coen brothers.
