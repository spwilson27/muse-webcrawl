Gris-gris (talisman)

TypeAmulet / talisman
OriginWest Africa; carried to the Americas via the African diaspora
TraditionsLouisiana Voodoo, Hoodoo, Vodou, Santería-adjacent folk practice
FormSmall cloth or leather bag containing herbs, roots, stones, written verses and other charged objects

A gris-gris (pronounced gree-gree) is a talisman or amulet originating in West Africa and carried throughout the African diaspora, most famously in Louisiana Voodoo and in the African American folk-magic tradition known as Hoodoo. In its classic form it is a small bag of cloth or leather, filled with a ritually prepared mixture of herbs, roots, minerals, animal parts, personal items and sometimes written prayers or verses, and worn or carried for protection, luck, love, healing or power.

Origins

The practice derives from West African traditions, particularly among the Mandé and other peoples of the Senegambia region, where Muslim marabouts and traditional healers prepared protective amulets — often leather pouches containing Qur'anic verses written on paper — for warriors, hunters and travellers. Enslaved Africans carried the practice to the Caribbean and to Louisiana, where it blended with Catholic sacramentals, Native American herbal lore and European folk magic.

Use in Louisiana Voodoo and Hoodoo

In nineteenth-century New Orleans, gris-gris bags became a signature of Voodoo practitioners such as Marie Laveau, who was reputed to prepare them for clients seeking love, money, legal success or protection from enemies. In Hoodoo the gris-gris is closely related to the mojo hand or nation sack: a personal charm bag that must be "fed" with oils, whiskey or perfume and kept hidden from the touch of others, which is said to destroy its power.

Preparation

A traditional gris-gris is assembled with intent: ingredients are chosen by sympathetic correspondence (for example, lodestone for attraction, graveyard dirt for matters of the dead, or red pepper for protection), blessed or prayed over, and sewn into the bag, which is often anointed and tied with a number of knots significant to the maker. The exact contents are traditionally secret and specific to the person for whom the bag was made.

In popular culture

The gris-gris entered wider American culture through blues and rhythm-and-blues songs about mojo hands and through Dr. John's 1968 album Gris-Gris, which brought the term to a rock audience. The word is also used more loosely in Louisiana French to mean a spell, a curse or bad luck itself.
