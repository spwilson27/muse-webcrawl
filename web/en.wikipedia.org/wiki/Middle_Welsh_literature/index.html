Middle Welsh literature is the body of literature written in the Middle Welsh language (Cymraeg Canol), roughly between the twelfth and the end of the fourteenth century. It is best known for its prose tales — above all the collection now called the Mabinogion — but it also includes court poetry, chronicles, laws, religious prose, translations and the Triads that catalogue the traditions of Britain.

Prose tales

The outstanding achievement of Middle Welsh prose is the group of eleven tales preserved in the White Book of Rhydderch (c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (c. 1382–1410), published in modern times under the title The Mabinogion. They include the Four Branches of the Mabinogi (Pwyll Prince of Dyfed, Branwen Daughter of Llŷr, Manawydan Son of Llŷr and Math Son of Mathonwy), the Arthurian tale Culhwch and Olwen — generally regarded as the earliest surviving Arthurian prose narrative — the dream-vision The Dream of Macsen Wledig, the allegorical Lludd and Llefelys, and the three Welsh romances Owain, Peredur and Geraint and Enid.

Poetry

The court poets (gogynfeirdd, "the early poets") composed elaborate praise poetry for the Welsh princes in strict metres, using the intricate system of consonance and rhyme known as cynghanedd. Their work survives in manuscripts such as the Hendregadredd Manuscript. Poets of the period include Meilyr Brydydd, Gwalchmai ap Meilyr, Cynddelw Brydydd Mawr and Llywelyn Fardd.

Chronicles, laws and learning

The prose chronicle Brut y Tywysogion ("Chronicle of the Princes") continues the history of Wales from the seventh century to 1282. Legal texts transmit the laws attributed to Hywel Dda. Learned prose includes the Welsh Triads (Trioedd Ynys Prydein), medical writings associated with the Physicians of Myddfai, and translations of French and Latin works such as the Grail romances and the Charlemagne cycle.
