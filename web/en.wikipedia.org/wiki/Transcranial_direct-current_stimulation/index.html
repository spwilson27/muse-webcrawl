Transcranial direct-current stimulation

Transcranial direct-current stimulation (tDCS) is a form of neuromodulation that uses constant, low direct current delivered via electrodes on the head. It is a non-invasive technique: the weak current (typically 1–2 mA) is not strong enough to trigger action potentials directly, but shifts resting membrane potentials and thereby the excitability of cortical neurons under the electrodes.

How it works

Anodal stimulation generally increases excitability in the underlying cortex, while cathodal stimulation decreases it; effects can outlast stimulation by an hour or more and are thought to involve NMDA-receptor-dependent plasticity. Sessions usually last 10–30 minutes with saline-soaked sponge electrodes.

Uses and evidence

tDCS has been investigated for depression, chronic pain, stroke rehabilitation, schizophrenia and cognitive enhancement. Results are mixed and often small, and replication has been uneven; regulators have not approved it broadly, and unsupervised do-it-yourself use raises safety concerns. Common side effects are mild: tingling, itching and skin redness under the electrodes.

History

Electrical stimulation of the brain dates to antiquity, but modern tDCS research began around 2000 with studies by Michael Nitsche and Walter Paulus demonstrating polarity-dependent excitability changes in human motor cortex.
