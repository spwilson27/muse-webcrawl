Neurolaw

Neurolaw is an emerging field of interdisciplinary study that explores the effects of discoveries in neuroscience on legal rules and standards. Drawing on neuroscience, psychology, social psychology and the law itself, it addresses questions such as criminal responsibility, the reliability of memory and testimony, addiction, adolescent development, and the use of brain imaging evidence in court.

History

The term came into use in the early 1990s, and the field grew rapidly with the spread of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and other neurotechnologies. Major initiatives include the MacArthur Foundation's Law and Neuroscience Project in the United States.

Applications

Neuroscience evidence has been offered in criminal cases concerning culpability and sentencing — for example, evidence of brain tumours, traumatic brain injury or adolescent brain development. In Roper v. Simmons (2005) and later cases, developmental neuroscience informed the United States Supreme Court's reasoning on juvenile punishment. Neuroscience has also been proposed for lie detection, prediction of dangerousness, and assessment of pain and damages in civil litigation.

Criticism

Critics caution that brain images can be unduly persuasive to juries, that group-level research findings may not apply to individuals, and that neuroscience does not by itself answer the law's normative questions about responsibility and free will. Scholars debate whether the field will revolutionise legal doctrine or mainly refine existing concepts.
