Stone–Čech compactification

In the mathematical discipline of general topology, Stone–Čech compactification is a technique for constructing a universal map from a topological space X to a compact Hausdorff space βX. The Stone–Čech compactification βX of a topological space X is the "largest", most general compact Hausdorff space "generated" by X. A compact Hausdorff space has the property that every continuous function from it to another topological space extends uniquely, which makes βX particularly useful.

History

The Stone–Čech compactification was first described by Marshall Harvey Stone and Eduard Čech, independently, in the years 1930 and 1937 respectively. When X is a Tychonoff space, the space βX is a compactification of X that has the universal property that any continuous function from X to another compact Hausdorff space Y extends to a continuous function on βX uniquely.

Universal property and functoriality

The Stone–Čech compactification is the compact Hausdorff space βX together with a continuous map iX : X → βX such that if f : X → K is any continuous map from X to a compact Hausdorff space K, then there exists a unique continuous map βf : βX → K such that f = βf ∘ iX. This universal property can be used to define βX uniquely, up to homeomorphism.

In the special case where X is discrete, βX can be identified with the set of all ultrafilters on X, with a topology making it the space of maximal ideals of the ring of bounded real-valued functions on X. The Stone–Čech compactification of the natural numbers, βN, is a large space of fundamental importance in set-theoretic topology and in the theory of ultrafilters.
