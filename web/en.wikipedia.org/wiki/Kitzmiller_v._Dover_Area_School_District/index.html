Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District, 400 F. Supp. 2d 707 (M.D. Pa. 2005) was the first direct challenge in the United States federal courts to a public school district policy requiring the teaching of intelligent design (ID) as an alternative to evolution in high school science classrooms. The trial, held in 2005 in Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, is often referred to as the Dover Panda Trial, after the intelligent design textbook Of Pandas and People.

Background

In October 2004, the school board of the Dover Area School District in York County, Pennsylvania, voted 6–3 to add a one-paragraph statement to the biology curriculum noting that evolution is a theory with gaps, and directing students to Of Pandas and People for an alternative explanation based on intelligent design. Eleven parents of Dover students, led by Tammy Kitzmiller and represented by the ACLU and Americans United for Separation of Church and State, sued, arguing the policy violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment.

Trial and ruling

The bench trial ran from September 26 to November 4, 2005, before Judge John E. Jones III. Expert witnesses for the plaintiffs included biologist Kenneth Miller and philosopher Barbara Forrest, whose testimony traced the history of the intelligent design movement and documented that early drafts of Of Pandas and People had simply replaced the word "creation" with "intelligent design" after the Supreme Court's 1987 decision in Edwards v. Aguillard — including the transitional typo "cdesign proponentsists". On December 20, 2005, Jones ruled for the plaintiffs, holding that intelligent design is not science, that it is a religious view — a mere relabelling of creationism — and that the school board's policy violated the Establishment Clause. The decision, 139 pages long, also found that several board members had lied under oath about their motives.

Aftermath

Before the ruling, Dover voters had already replaced eight of the nine school board members who supported the policy. The new board declined to appeal, and the plaintiffs were awarded over $1 million in legal fees, later negotiated down. The ruling has been highly influential in subsequent disputes over teaching evolution and remains the leading judicial treatment of whether intelligent design qualifies as science.
