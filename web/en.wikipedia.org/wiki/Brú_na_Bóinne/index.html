Brú na Bóinne (Irish for "palace/mansion of the Boyne", pronounced roughly "broo na BOY-nya"), also called the Bend of the Boyne, is a Neolithic monumental landscape in County Meath, Ireland, situated in a bend of the River Boyne between Drogheda and Slane. It contains one of the world's most important prehistoric landscapes, with some 40 passage tombs and other monuments built around 3200 BC — older than Stonehenge and the Egyptian pyramids. The three principal sites are the great passage tombs of Newgrange, Knowth and Dowth, surrounded by smaller satellite tombs, standing stones and henges. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1993.

Newgrange

Newgrange is the most famous of the tombs: a large circular mound with a corbelled-roof chamber reached by a 19-metre passage, fronted by a reconstructed facade of white quartz. Above the entrance is a "roof box" through which the rising sun at the winter solstice shines down the passage to illuminate the chamber floor. Its kerbstones are decorated with megalithic art — spirals, lozenges and concentric circles.

Archaeology and myth

Excavations led by Michael J. O'Kelly (Newgrange) and George Eogan (Knowth) revealed cremated human remains, grave goods and rich carved art. In Irish mythology Brú na Bóinne is the dwelling of the Tuatha Dé Danann, especially the god the Dagda and his son Aengus Óg, who won the mound by trickery, asking for it "for a day and a night" — which, in the tale, means forever.
