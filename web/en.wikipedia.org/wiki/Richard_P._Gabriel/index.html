Richard P. Gabriel (born 1949) is an American computer scientist known for his work on the Lisp programming language, for the design philosophy known as "worse is better," and for his role in the standardization of Common Lisp.

Career

Gabriel earned his PhD from Stanford University, where he worked on Lisp systems. In 1984 he founded Lucid Inc., which commercialised Common Lisp environments and compilers; he later worked at Sun Microsystems and IBM Research, and has been a Distinguished Engineer at IBM and a research associate connected with Stanford and the Hasso Plattner Institute. He was a central figure in the ANSI Common Lisp standardization effort and a long-serving figure in the object-oriented and patterns communities.

"Worse is better"

In his widely circulated 1989–1991 essays, beginning with "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big," Gabriel contrasted an "MIT/Stanford" philosophy of design — correctness and completeness above all — with a "New Jersey" style exemplified by C and Unix, in which simplicity of implementation comes first. He argued that the simpler, somewhat less correct approach ("worse is better") spreads faster and ultimately wins, because a working, portable system that is easy to adopt will out-compete a perfect one. The essays became a staple text in software engineering culture.

Writing

Gabriel is also a poet, with an MFA in creative writing, and author of Patterns of Software (1996) and Writers' Workshops and the Work of Making Things (2002). He received the ACM Software System Award in 2010 as part of the group honoured for the development of Lisp.
