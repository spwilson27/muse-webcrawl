Lenape

The Lenape (English: /ləˈnɑːpi, ˈlɛnəpi/; Lenape languages: Lënape), also called the Lenni Lenape and Delaware people, are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands. Their historical homeland, Lenapehoking, encompassed present-day New Jersey, eastern Pennsylvania, the northern part of Delaware, and the lower Hudson Valley and western Long Island in New York.

History

The Lenape spoke two closely related Eastern Algonquian languages, Unami and Munsee. From the early 17th century they traded with Dutch, Swedish and English colonists. A series of land transactions, including the notorious Walking Purchase of 1737, together with disease and war, dispossessed them of their homeland. During the 18th and 19th centuries most Lenape were progressively removed westward to Ohio, Indiana, Missouri, Kansas and finally Indian Territory (Oklahoma), while other communities resettled in Canada.

Contemporary communities

Federally recognised Lenape nations in the United States include the Delaware Nation and the Delaware Tribe of Indians in Oklahoma and the Stockbridge–Munsee Community in Wisconsin; in Canada, Lenape communities live at Moraviantown and Munsee-Delaware in Ontario. Other descendants maintain state-recognised and community organisations in New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware, and language revitalisation programmes teach Unami and Munsee.

Culture

Traditional Lenape society was organised in matrilineal clans — Turtle, Wolf and Turkey. Subsistence combined maize-bean-squash agriculture with hunting, fishing and gathering. The Big House Church (Gamwing) ceremony preserved elements of the traditional religion in Oklahoma communities into the 20th century.
