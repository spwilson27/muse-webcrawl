Cylinder

A cylinder (from Ancient Greek κύλινδρος (kúlindros) 'roller, tumbler') has traditionally been a three-dimensional solid, one of the most basic of curvilinear geometric shapes. In elementary geometry, it is considered a prism with a circle as its base.

A cylinder may also be defined as an infinite curvilinear surface in various modern branches of geometry and topology. The shift in the basic meaning—solid versus surface (as in ball and sphere)—has created some ambiguity with terminology. The two meanings are distinguished by whether a "solid cylinder" or a "cylindrical surface" is meant.

Types

The definitions and results in this section are taken from the 1913 text Plane and Solid Geometry by George Wentworth and David Eugene Smith, which uses "cylinder" for what is now called a right circular cylinder when the context is clear.

A right circular cylinder is a solid bounded by two congruent circular bases in parallel planes and a lateral surface generated by a line segment perpendicular to the bases. An oblique cylinder has its lateral edges not perpendicular to the bases. More generally, a cylinder may be generated by translating any plane curve along a straight line.

Volume and surface area

For a right circular cylinder of radius r and height h, the volume is V = πr2h and the total surface area is A = 2πr2 + 2πrh = 2πr(r + h).

Cylindrical surfaces and sections

A cylindrical surface is a surface consisting of all the points on all the lines which are parallel to a given line and pass through a fixed plane curve in a plane not parallel to the line. Sections of a right circular cylinder by planes are conics: circles, ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas, or degenerate cases.

Applications

Cylindrical shapes appear throughout engineering: engine cylinders housing pistons, hydraulic and pneumatic cylinders, pipes, tanks, rollers and printing cylinders.
