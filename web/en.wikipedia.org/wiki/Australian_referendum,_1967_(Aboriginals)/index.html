1967 referendum (Aboriginals)
Date27 May 1967
ProposalAmend the Constitution to include Aboriginal people in the census and allow the Commonwealth to make laws for them
ResultApproved — 90.77% Yes, carried in all six states
GovernmentHolt government (Liberal–Country coalition)

The 1967 Australian referendum on Aboriginal affairs was held on 27 May 1967. Voters were asked to approve the Constitution Alteration (Aboriginals) 1967, which amended the Constitution of Australia in two ways: it repealed section 127, which had excluded "aboriginal natives" from being counted in the census, and it amended section 51(xxvi) to remove the words "other than the aboriginal race in any State", allowing the Commonwealth Parliament to make special laws for Aboriginal people.

Campaign

The referendum followed a decade of activism by Aboriginal and non-Aboriginal campaigners, including the Federal Council for the Advancement of Aborigines and Torres Strait Islanders (FCAATSI) and campaigners such as Faith Bandler. The Holt government supported the change, no official "No" case was published, and campaigners framed a Yes vote as a matter of fairness and national conscience.

Result and significance

The proposal passed with 90.77% of the national vote — still the highest Yes vote in any Australian referendum — and a majority in every state. Contrary to a common myth, the referendum did not itself grant Aboriginal people citizenship or the vote (most already had them under state and Commonwealth law by 1967). Its real effects were constitutional and symbolic: the Commonwealth could legislate for Aboriginal people and they were counted in the population, enabling federal funding and responsibility. The referendum remains a landmark in Australian history and in the reconciliation movement.
