Viceroyalty of New Spain

New Spain at its maximal extent, including Central America, the Caribbean, the Philippines, and territories in North America.

New Spain, officially the Viceroyalty of New Spain (Spanish: Virreinato de Nueva España), was an integral territorial entity of the Spanish Empire, established by the Spanish Crown during the Spanish conquest of the Americas. Its capital was Mexico City, built on the ruins of Aztec Tenochtitlan. The viceroyalty, created in 1535 with Antonio de Mendoza as its first viceroy, at its height covered Mexico, Central America (except Panama), the Spanish Caribbean, much of the present-day southwestern and central United States, Florida, the Philippines, and various Pacific islands.

Administration

The viceroy governed as the king's representative, advised by the Audiencia, with the Council of the Indies in Spain overseeing colonial affairs. Society was organised in a casta hierarchy, with peninsulares (Spanish-born) at the top, followed by creoles (American-born Spaniards), mestizos, indigenous peoples, and Africans and their descendants. Silver mining at Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and Potosí (in Peru) financed the empire, and the Manila galleon linked Acapulco to the Philippines and the China trade.

End of the viceroyalty

After the Bourbon Reforms and the upheavals of the Napoleonic Wars, the Mexican War of Independence (1810–1821) ended Spanish rule: the Army of the Three Guarantees entered Mexico City in September 1821 and the viceroyalty was dissolved into the Mexican Empire, followed by the federal republic, while Central America separated into its own provinces.
