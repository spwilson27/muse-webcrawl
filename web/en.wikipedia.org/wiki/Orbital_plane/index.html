Orbital plane

The orbital plane of a revolving body is the geometric plane in which its orbit lies. A sufficiently large number of points in the plane can be defined by three non-collinear points; in practice the position and velocity vectors of the orbiting body and the central body define the plane. By definition, the orbital plane contains both bodies' centres at all times in an ideal two-body orbit, because the gravitational force acts along the line joining them and angular momentum — perpendicular to the plane — is conserved.

Reference planes

The orientation of an orbital plane is described relative to a chosen reference plane. For objects in the Solar System the ecliptic — the plane of Earth's orbit around the Sun — is customary. For artificial Earth satellites the equatorial plane is usually used, and inclination is measured from it. The invariable plane, perpendicular to the total angular momentum of the Solar System, provides the most fundamental reference and is tilted about 1.57° to the ecliptic, chiefly because of Jupiter's dominance.

Describing the plane

Two classical orbital elements fix the plane: the inclination (i), the angle between the orbital plane and the reference plane, and the longitude of the ascending node (Ω), the angle in the reference plane from a reference direction to the ascending node, where the orbiting body crosses the reference plane moving northward. Prograde orbits have inclinations below 90°; polar orbits are near 90°; retrograde orbits exceed 90°.

Plane changes

Because a spacecraft's velocity lies within its orbital plane, changing the plane requires a velocity component perpendicular to it, making plane-change maneuvers among the most propellant-expensive in astrodynamics: rotating the plane by an angle θ at speed v costs Δv = 2v sin(θ/2). Plane changes are therefore best performed where orbital speed is lowest, such as at apoapsis.
