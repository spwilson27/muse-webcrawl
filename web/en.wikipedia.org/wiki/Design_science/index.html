Design science

Design science links the building of artifacts with their evaluation

Design science is a research paradigm and field of study concerned with the systematic creation and evaluation of artifacts — systems, methods, models and constructs — intended to solve practical problems. It is distinguished from the descriptive sciences, which seek to explain natural phenomena, by its orientation toward the artificial: how things ought to be in order to attain goals, rather than how they are.

Origins

The intellectual foundations of design science were laid by Herbert A. Simon in The Sciences of the Artificial (1969), which argued that design — devising courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones — is a legitimate object of scientific study central to professions such as engineering, architecture, medicine and management. R. Buckminster Fuller also used the term "design science" for a systematic approach to global problem solving.

Design science research

In information systems and engineering, design science research (DSR) follows an iterative cycle of problem identification, objective definition, design and development of an artifact, demonstration, evaluation and communication. Hevner and colleagues articulated widely used guidelines for conducting and judging such research, emphasising both rigour — grounding in existing knowledge — and relevance to real problems.

Relation to design studies

Design science overlaps with, but is distinct from, design studies and design theory, which examine design activity, cognition and practice more broadly, and from applied research and development, which may produce artifacts without the explicit knowledge contribution design science requires.
