The Ngô dynasty (Vietnamese: Nhà Ngô) was a Vietnamese dynasty that ruled from 939 to 965. It is conventionally regarded as the first dynasty of an independent Vietnam after roughly a thousand years of Chinese domination, founded by Ngô Quyền following his victory over the Southern Han at the Battle of Bạch Đằng River.

Founding

In 938 (the battle is traditionally dated to late 938), the Vietnamese general Ngô Quyền defeated an invading Southern Han fleet at the Battle of Bạch Đằng by driving iron-tipped stakes into the riverbed, impaling the enemy ships as the tide fell — a tactic in the tradition of earlier victories on the same river. In 939 he proclaimed himself king and established his capital at Cổ Loa, the ancient citadel near modern Hanoi. He took the title Ngô Vương ("King Ngô") rather than emperor, and organised a court modelled in part on Chinese practice.

Decline

Ngô Quyền died in 944 after a short reign. Power passed first to his brother-in-law Dương Tam Kha, who usurped the throne, and then, after 950, to Ngô Quyền's sons Ngô Xương Ngập and Ngô Xương Văn, who ruled jointly. Their authority steadily weakened as regional lords asserted independence, and after Ngô Xương Văn was killed in battle in 965 the country dissolved into the Anarchy of the Twelve Warlords, which lasted until Đinh Bộ Lĩnh reunified Vietnam in 968 and founded the Đinh dynasty.

Legacy

Despite its brevity, the Ngô dynasty is celebrated in Vietnamese historiography for ending the "Bắc thuộc" (northern domination) period and opening the era of independent Vietnamese dynasties.
