Normativity

Normativity is the phenomenon in human societies of designating some actions or outcomes as good, desirable, or permissible and others as bad, undesirable, or impermissible. A norm in this sense means a standard for evaluating or making judgments about behaviour or outcomes.

In philosophy

Normative claims concern what ought to be the case, as opposed to descriptive claims about what is the case — a contrast drawn sharply by David Hume's is–ought problem. Normativity is central to ethics (moral norms), epistemology (norms of rational belief), logic, aesthetics, and philosophy of language, where meaning itself is sometimes held to be normative (following Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein).

Types of norms

Philosophers distinguish moral, prudential, epistemic, legal, and conventional norms, and debate whether normative facts are reducible to natural facts (normative naturalism) or form an irreducible domain — the "normative problem" in metaethics.
