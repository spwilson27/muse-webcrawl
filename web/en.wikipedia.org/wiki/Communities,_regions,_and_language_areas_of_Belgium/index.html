Communities, regions, and language areas of Belgium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Belgium is a federal state made up of two kinds of federated entities: three Communities, organised around language and culture, and three Regions, organised around territory and economic matters. These divisions overlay each other, and together with four constitutionally defined language areas they form the framework of Belgian federalism.

Communities

The three Communities are:

  • the Flemish Community, covering Dutch speakers in Flanders and Brussels;
  • the French Community (French Community of Belgium, often called Wallonia-Brussels), covering French speakers in Wallonia and Brussels;
  • the German-speaking Community, covering the small German-speaking area in the east of the province of Liège.

Communities are responsible for matters such as education, culture, language use, health policy and assistance to individuals. Each has its own parliament and government. In Flanders, the Community and Region institutions were merged into a single Flemish Parliament and Government.

Regions

The three Regions are:

  • the Flemish Region (Flanders) in the north;
  • the Walloon Region (Wallonia) in the south;
  • the Brussels-Capital Region, an enclave within Flanders containing the capital.

Regions are responsible for territorial and economic matters, including regional economic policy, employment, agriculture, water and energy policy, housing, transport, environment and town and country planning.

Language areas

The Belgian constitution defines four language areas: the Dutch-language area, the French-language area, the German-language area, and the bilingual area of Brussels-Capital. The language border, fixed in the early 1960s, determines the official language used by public authorities in each municipality; a small number of municipalities with linguistic facilities grant services to speakers of the other national languages.

Development

This structure emerged through a series of state reforms beginning in 1970, which progressively transferred powers from the unitary Belgian state to the Communities and Regions in response to linguistic and regional tensions, particularly between Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia.
