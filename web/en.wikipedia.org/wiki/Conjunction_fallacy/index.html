Field: Cognitive psychology

Conjunction fallacy

The conjunction fallacy (also known as the Linda problem) is a cognitive bias in which people judge the probability of a conjunction of two events to be greater than the probability of one of the events alone, violating the laws of probability, according to which a conjunction can never be more probable than one of its constituents.

The Linda problem

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman presented subjects with a description of "Linda", a 31-year-old philosophy graduate concerned with social justice. Most respondents ranked "Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement" as more probable than "Linda is a bank teller", even though the second option includes the first. Tversky and Kahneman attributed the error to the representativeness heuristic: the conjunction is more representative of the description.

Explanations and debate

Proposed explanations include misreading "probability" as plausibility, conversational implicature, and frequency formats that reduce the error. The effect remains a staple demonstration of how intuitive judgment departs from the rules of probability taught in logic and formal reasoning.
