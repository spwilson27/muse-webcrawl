Gathas

The Gathas (Avestan: gāθā, "songs" or "hymns") are seventeen hymns in verse embedded in the Yasna liturgy of the Avesta, the sacred texts of Zoroastrianism. They are composed in Old Avestan, the oldest attested stage of an Iranian language, and are traditionally attributed to the prophet Zoroaster (Zarathustra) himself, making them the most ancient and theologically central part of the Zoroastrian canon.

Contents and structure

The seventeen hymns are grouped into five collections, each named for its opening words or metre: the Ahunavaiti, Ushtavaiti, Spenta Mainyu, Vohukhshathra, and Vahishtoishti Gathas. Together with the prose Yasna Haptanghaiti, they form the Old Avestan core of the Avesta. Their language is so archaic that it stands close to the Rigvedic Sanskrit of ancient India.

Themes

The hymns address Ahura Mazda, the supreme God, and meditate on the cosmic struggle between asha (truth/order) and druj (the lie), human free will and moral choice, the Amesha Spentas (divine entities embodying virtues), and the renovation of the world. Their meaning has been debated for centuries because of the compressed, allusive poetic style; translations differ considerably.

Significance

Zoroastrians venerate the Gathas as the words of the prophet. The name "Gatha" was also given to the five intercalary days at the end of the Zoroastrian calendar year, during which the hymns are specially recited.
