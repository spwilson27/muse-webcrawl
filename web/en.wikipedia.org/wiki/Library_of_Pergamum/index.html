The Library of Pergamum was an ancient library in the city of Pergamum (Pergamon) in Asia Minor, one of the most important libraries of the ancient world. Founded and patronised by the Attalid kings who ruled Pergamum in the Hellenistic period, it stood on the city's acropolis beside the Temple of Athena. Ancient sources credit it with around 200,000 scrolls, making it second only to the great Library of Alexandria, with which it was in open rivalry — a rivalry so intense that, according to tradition, it led to the invention of parchment.

History

The library was developed under Attalus I (r. 241–197 BC) and especially Eumenes II (r. 197–159 BC), whose building programme transformed the acropolis. The library comprised a large reading room — about 13.4 by 15 metres, with a slightly raised podium and a colossal statue of Athena — and adjoining storage rooms in which scrolls were kept in niches or on shelves, with a ventilating gap behind the walls to protect against damp. Pergamum's scholars and physicians flourished under royal patronage; the city was also the home of the physician Galen in the Roman period.

The parchment legend

According to Pliny the Elder, drawing on Varro, the Ptolemies of Egypt, jealous of Pergamum's growing library, banned the export of papyrus, forcing the Pergamenes to develop parchment — prepared animal skin — as a writing material; the word "parchment" itself derives from pergamena, "of Pergamum". Modern scholars doubt the story in its simple form (parchment had earlier antecedents and became dominant only centuries later), but Pergamum was certainly a major centre of parchment production, and the codex of parchment sheets eventually replaced the scroll in the Roman world.

Fate of the collection

Plutarch relates that Mark Antony gave Cleopatra the 200,000 scrolls of Pergamum as a gift for the Library of Alexandria. The story is doubted by some historians, but the Pergamene library in any case fades from record in the late Republic, whether through removal, neglect, or the general decline of the roll collection. The rooms on the acropolis were excavated by German archaeologists in the late nineteenth century, and their remains can still be seen at Bergama in modern Turkey.
