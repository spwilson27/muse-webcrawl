Crankshaft

A crankshaft is a mechanical component used in a piston engine to convert the reciprocating motion of the pistons into rotational motion. The crankshaft is a rotating shaft containing one or more crankpins (also called rod bearing journals), offset from the axis of rotation, to which the large ends of the connecting rods from each cylinder attach. It is typically connected to a flywheel to smooth the power delivery and, in many engines, to a harmonic damper intended to reduce torsional vibrations.

Design

Most crankshafts have an integral design: the main journals that run in the engine block's bearings, the crankpins, and the webs (arms) joining them are formed as one piece. Between each pair of webs lies a crankpin; the distance of the crankpin axis from the main axis determines half the piston stroke. A typical inline-four crankshaft has five main journals and four crankpins. Crankshafts also carry features such as oil passages drilled to feed the bearings, a snout at the front driving the camshaft and accessories, and a flange at the rear for the flywheel.

History

The earliest known crank-and-connecting-rod mechanism appears in Roman sources, including the Hierapolis sawmill relief of the 3rd century AD. The Kurdish engineer Ismail al-Jazari described a crankshaft in his water-raising machines in 1206. Crankshafts became central to steam engines and then to the internal combustion engines of the late nineteenth century, which required them to withstand far higher speeds and loads.

Materials and manufacture

Crankshafts are most commonly forged from steel for strength — including micro-alloyed and nitrided steels — or cast from iron (especially ductile iron) for lower-cost applications. High-performance engines may use crankshafts machined from a solid billet of steel. Journal surfaces are hardened and precision-ground, and fillets at the junctions between journals and webs are rolled to induce compressive stresses that resist the fatigue cracking to which crankshafts are prone.

Counterweights and balance

Webs are usually extended opposite the crankpins as counterweights, which offset the mass of the crankpin and part of the connecting rod and piston assembly, reducing vibration and bearing loads. Dynamic balancing of the complete rotating assembly, together with the engine's firing order and crankpin arrangement (for example crossplane versus flat-plane V8 layouts), determines the smoothness of the engine.
