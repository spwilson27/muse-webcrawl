Multiple nuclei model

The multiple nuclei model is an ecological model of urban structure developed by geographers Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman in their 1945 article "The Nature of Cities". It proposes that a city grows not from a single centre, as in the earlier concentric zone model of Ernest Burgess or the sector model of Homer Hoyt, but around several separate nuclei, each of which acts as a growth point for a distinct type of land use.

The model

Harris and Ullman argued that separate nuclei arise because of four factors: certain activities require specialized facilities (e.g., a port, rail yards, or a university); like activities benefit from clustering together (agglomeration); unlike activities are detrimental to one another (e.g., heavy industry and high-class housing); and some activities cannot afford the high rents of the most desirable sites and settle in cheaper areas.

The classic diagram labels districts including the central business district, wholesale and light manufacturing, low-, medium-, and high-class residential areas, heavy manufacturing, an outlying business district, a residential suburb, and an industrial suburb. The number and arrangement of nuclei vary from city to city, and the model is intended as a framework rather than a fixed pattern.

Criticism and legacy

The multiple nuclei model better fits large, complex, polycentric metropolitan areas — especially automobile-era cities such as Los Angeles — than single-centre models, and it anticipates later ideas of edge cities and polycentric urban regions. Critics note that it is largely descriptive, gives little attention to the role of planning, transport policy, or social processes, and can be difficult to test. It remains a standard element of human geography curricula alongside the Burgess and Hoyt models.
