Socially responsible investing (SRI), also known as social investment or sustainable investment, is an investment strategy that considers both financial return and social or environmental good. Investors applying SRI seek to own shares in companies whose practices they judge responsible, avoid those they judge harmful, and use their position as shareholders to press companies for change.

History

The roots of SRI lie in religious investing: Quakers and Methodists in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries avoided profiting from slavery, alcohol and armaments, and John Wesley's sermon "The Use of Money" urged against harming others through business. Modern SRI began in the United States around 1970, with resistance to the Vietnam War — the Pax World Fund (1971) excluded weapons manufacturers — and grew rapidly during the campaign to divest from companies operating in apartheid South Africa. The Sullivan principles (1977) offered a code for firms remaining in South Africa, while the divestment campaign itself became a major pressure on the apartheid regime.

Strategies

Screening is the best-known strategy: negative screens exclude sectors such as tobacco, fossil fuels or weapons, while positive or "best-in-class" screens favour companies with strong environmental, labour or governance records within each industry. Shareholder advocacy uses resolutions, dialogue and proxy votes to change corporate behaviour. Community investing directs capital to underserved communities, for example through community development banks and microfinance. More recently, impact investing has emphasised measurable social outcomes alongside returns.

Relation to ESG and debate

SRI overlaps with, and partly evolved into, ESG investing, which frames environmental, social and governance factors as financial risks and opportunities rather than primarily ethical choices. The field is debated on two fronts: whether screening sacrifices returns (most large studies find little systematic performance difference) and whether labels are meaningful, with regulators in the US and EU moving in the 2020s to police "greenwashing" in fund names and disclosures, even as ESG became politically contested in the United States.
