Criminal procedure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Criminal procedure is the body of law governing the process by which the state investigates, charges, tries and punishes criminal offences — the adjudication process of criminal law, alongside the substantive criminal law that defines offences. It covers arrest, search and seizure, interrogation, bail, charging (indictment or information), arraignment, pre-trial motions, plea bargaining, trial, sentencing and appeal.

Models and rights

Herbert Packer's two models organise the field's argument: the crime control model (an assembly line maximising efficient repression) versus the due process model (an obstacle course policing state power) — every rule from the exclusionary remedy to disclosure duties balances the two. Modern constitutional criminal procedure is built from rights instruments: the US Bill of Rights as incorporated (search, silence, counsel, speedy and public trial, confrontation, double jeopardy), the ECHR's Articles 5 and 6, ICCPR Article 14 — converging texts operating through divergent institutions (adversarial contest versus judicial investigation; plea-dominated versus trial-centred disposition). The mass reality in most systems is process without trial: negotiated guilty pleas, diversion and penal orders disposing of the great majority of cases, which reformers measure against the trial-centric theory.
