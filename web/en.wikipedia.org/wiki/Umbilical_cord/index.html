Umbilical cord

Umbilical cord
ContentsTwo umbilical arteries, one umbilical vein, Wharton's jelly
Typical length50–60 cm at term
FunctionConnects fetus to placenta; carries oxygen and nutrients to the fetus and waste products away

The umbilical cord is the conduit between a developing fetus and the placenta in placental mammals. It normally contains two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein, embedded in a gelatinous substance called Wharton's jelly that protects the vessels from compression. The vein delivers oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus, while the arteries carry deoxygenated blood and waste products back to the placenta.

The human umbilical cord is typically about 50–60 cm long and spiralled, which helps it resist kinking.

Development and physiology

The cord develops from the connecting stalk and yolk sac structures in the fifth week of pregnancy. Blood flows through it at high rates—by term, fetal circulation through the cord is on the order of hundreds of millilitres per minute. The cord has no nerves, so cutting it at birth is painless to mother and baby.

At birth

After delivery the cord is clamped and cut, leaving a stump that dries and falls off within one to three weeks, leaving the umbilicus (navel). Practice has shifted toward delayed cord clamping—waiting at least 30–60 seconds—which improves the newborn's blood volume and iron stores. Blood remaining in the cord and placenta ("cord blood") is rich in hematopoietic stem cells and may be collected for public or private cord blood banking.

Clinical issues

Abnormalities and complications include a single umbilical artery, abnormally short or long cords, knots, cord prolapse during labour (an obstetric emergency), and the cord wrapped around the fetal neck (nuchal cord), which is common and usually harmless. Ultrasound assessment of cord blood flow is used to monitor high-risk pregnancies.
