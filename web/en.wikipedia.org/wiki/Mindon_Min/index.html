Mindon Min (1808–1878) was the penultimate king of Burma (Myanmar), reigning from 1853 to 1878 as the tenth monarch of the Konbaung dynasty. Widely regarded as one of Burma's greatest kings, he is remembered for his modernization efforts, his founding of the city of Mandalay, and his patronage of Buddhism, including convening the Fifth Buddhist Council.

Accession

Mindon came to the throne after the Second Anglo-Burmese War (1852), in which Britain annexed Lower Burma. Opposed to his half-brother Pagan Min's handling of the war, Mindon and his brother Kanaung rebelled and deposed Pagan in February 1853. Mindon sued for peace, though he never formally signed a treaty ceding Lower Burma, and spent his reign trying to modernize his reduced kingdom while avoiding further conflict with the British.

Reforms and Mandalay

With Kanaung as crown prince and chief minister, Mindon introduced administrative, military, and economic reforms: a modernized tax system, standardized coinage, telegraph lines, steamships on the Irrawaddy, and students sent abroad to study. In 1857 he founded a new capital, Mandalay, at the foot of Mandalay Hill, fulfilling a Buddhist prophecy, and built the Mandalay Palace and numerous monasteries. Kanaung's assassination in 1866 was a severe blow to the reform program.

Religious patronage

A devout Buddhist, Mindon convened the Fifth Buddhist Council in 1871, at which the Pali Canon was recited and standardized. He had the entire Tipitaka inscribed on 729 marble slabs at the Kuthodaw Pagoda in Mandalay — often called "the world's largest book". He also donated the Burmah ruby-set crown jewels' counterpart gifts to pagodas and supported missions to Ceylon to restore the Buddhist ordination lineage.

Death and succession

Mindon died in 1878 without clearly designating an heir. Court intrigue brought the young Thibaw Min to the throne, whose reign ended with the Third Anglo-Burmese War and the British annexation of Burma in 1885.
