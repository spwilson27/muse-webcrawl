Graves disease (usually written Graves' disease) is an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system produces antibodies that stimulate the thyroid gland, causing it to produce excess thyroid hormone. It is the leading cause of hyperthyroidism worldwide where iodine intake is sufficient.

Overview

The stimulating antibodies act on the receptor for thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), so the gland enlarges diffusely (a goitre) and overproduces thyroxine. Typical features include unintentional weight loss despite increased appetite, tremor, anxiety, palpitations, heat intolerance and, in many patients, eye signs known as Graves' orbitopathy. Blood tests characteristically show a low TSH with high free T4 and T3, and TSH-receptor antibodies are detectable in most cases.

Management

Options for controlling the overactive gland are antithyroid drugs (methimazole/carbimazole, propylthiouracil), radioactive iodine, and surgical removal of the thyroid. Symptomatic relief with beta blockers is common. Eye involvement may be treated with steroids or, where sight is threatened, decompression surgery. Long-term follow-up of thyroid function is required because both relapse and treatment-induced hypothyroidism are frequent.

History

The disease takes its name from Robert James Graves of Dublin, who published a description in 1835; German physician Karl Adolph von Basedow independently described the same syndrome in 1840, and in German-speaking countries the condition is known as Basedow's disease.
