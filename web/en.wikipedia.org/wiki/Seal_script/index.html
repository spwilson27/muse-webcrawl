Seal script

Characters in small seal script compared with their regular-script descendants.

Seal script (Chinese: 篆書; pinyin: zhuànshū) is an ancient style of Chinese calligraphy and writing. It evolved organically out of the bronze script of the Zhou dynasty and was the first Chinese script to be promulgated as a national standard, under the Qin dynasty. It is named for its enduring use on seals (name chops), where it survives to this day.

Type: Logographic script style

Languages: Old Chinese, Classical Chinese

Period: c. 7th century BC – present (ceremonial and seal use)

Variants: Large seal script (大篆), small seal script (小篆)

Parent systems: Oracle bone script → bronze script

Large and small seal script

Large seal script is a retrospective label for the varied forms of the late Zhou period, including characters on bronze vessels and the Stone Drums. After Qin Shi Huang unified China in 221 BC, his chancellor Li Si standardised the small seal script, eliminating regional variants; small seal became the formal script of the empire for monumental and ceremonial use.

Characteristics

Seal script characters are drawn with strokes of even thickness, are symmetrical and elongated, and tend toward rounded, curving lines. They are considerably more pictographic and less angular than the later clerical and regular scripts, and many forms differ greatly from their modern counterparts, so seal script generally requires specialist study to read.

Modern use

Although superseded for everyday writing by clerical script during the Han dynasty, seal script is still carved on personal and official seals in China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam, and is prized by calligraphers and used decoratively on signage and artworks.
