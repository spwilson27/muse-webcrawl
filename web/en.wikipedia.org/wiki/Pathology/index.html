Pathology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pathology is the study of disease: its causes (aetiology), mechanisms of development (pathogenesis), and the structural and functional changes it produces in cells, tissues and organs. Pathologists diagnose disease by examining tissues (histopathology), cells (cytopathology), blood and body fluids, and — in forensic pathology — by post-mortem examination.

Branches

Anatomical pathology examines surgical specimens and biopsies; clinical pathology runs laboratory tests on blood, urine and microbes (the latter overlapping microbiology); molecular pathology uses genetic tests, increasingly in cancer care. Autopsies remain important for verifying diagnoses and understanding new diseases.

Foundations

The discipline rests on the cellular theory of disease of Rudolf Virchow (1858), who argued that disease arises in individual cells, and on the classical description of Inflammation — redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function. Its core processes are cell injury and death, inflammation and repair, disorders of growth including cancer, and genetic, environmental and nutritional diseases.
