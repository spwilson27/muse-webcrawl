Swing Riots

The Swing Riots were a widespread uprising by agricultural workers in southern and eastern England in 1830, named after the threatening letters sent to farmers, magistrates and landowners signed with the pseudonym "Captain Swing" — apparently a reference to the swinging flail of hand threshing, though no single leader ever existed.

Causes and course

Agricultural labourers faced a miserable combination of low wages, unemployment, church tithes, and the spread of threshing machines, which threatened the winter work that kept families alive. Beginning in Kent in August 1830 with the destruction of a threshing machine, the unrest spread across some fifty counties: labourers smashed machines, burned hay ricks, and demanded higher wages and reduced tithes, generally avoiding violence against persons.

Suppression and legacy

The government responded harshly. Nearly 2,000 protesters were tried by special commissions; 19 were executed, around 500 transported to Australia, and hundreds more imprisoned. Nevertheless the riots helped persuade Parliament of the need for reform: they contributed to the atmosphere behind the Reform Act 1832 and the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834, and threshing machines largely disappeared from English farms for a generation.
