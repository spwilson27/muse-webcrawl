LGBT rights in the Americas

LGBT rights in the Americas vary widely by country and territory, ranging from legal recognition of same-sex marriage and broad anti-discrimination protections in some states to criminal penalties for same-sex sexual activity in a few Caribbean jurisdictions.

Marriage and recognition

Canada became the first country in the Americas to legalise same-sex marriage nationwide in 2005. Argentina followed in 2010—the first in Latin America—followed by Brazil (2013), Uruguay (2013), the United States (nationwide through the 2015 Supreme Court ruling in Obergefell v. Hodges), Colombia (2016), Ecuador (2019), Costa Rica (2020, following an Inter-American Court of Human Rights advisory opinion), Chile (2022), Cuba (2022, via a new family code approved by referendum), Mexico (nationwide by 2022), and Andorra-adjacent European territories aside, as well as several Caribbean territories under European sovereignty. Many other countries recognise civil unions or foreign marriages.

Criminalisation

Consensual same-sex activity is legal in most of the Americas, but a number of English-speaking Caribbean states historically retained colonial-era "buggery" laws. Courts in Belize (2016), Trinidad and Tobago (2018), Antigua and Barbuda, Saint Kitts and Nevis, and Barbados (2022) struck down such provisions; as of the mid-2020s they remained on the books in a shrinking number of jurisdictions such as Jamaica and Guyana.

Other protections

Several countries in the region, including Argentina, Uruguay, and Brazil, have adopted gender-identity laws allowing legal gender change by self-declaration, and Argentina's 2012 Gender Identity Law is often cited as a pioneering model. The Inter-American Commission and Court of Human Rights have played a major role in advancing regional standards, including the 2018 advisory opinion on marriage equality and gender identity recognition, and the Court's judgment in Atala Riffo v. Chile (2012).
