Sir William Wallace (c. 1270 – 23 August 1305) was a Scottish knight who became one of the main leaders during the First War of Scottish Independence. He defeated an English army at the Battle of Stirling Bridge in September 1297 and served as Guardian of Scotland until his defeat at the Battle of Falkirk in July 1298. In August 1305 Wallace was captured and executed for treason in London.

Background

Little is known of Wallace's early life. He is traditionally said to have been born in Elderslie, Renfrewshire, the son of a small landowner. After the deposition of King John Balliol and the English occupation of Scotland in 1296, resistance broke out in several regions. In May 1297 Wallace killed William de Heselrig, the English sheriff of Lanark, an act often treated as the start of his open rebellion.

Stirling Bridge and guardianship

Wallace joined forces with Andrew Moray, leader of the rising in the north. At the Battle of Stirling Bridge (11 September 1297) their forces destroyed the army of the Earl of Surrey as it crossed the narrow bridge over the River Forth. Moray died of wounds soon afterwards. Wallace was knighted and made Guardian of Scotland, ruling in the name of King John. He raided northern England and sought support abroad, including from France and the papacy.

Falkirk and later years

King Edward I invaded Scotland in person in 1298. At Falkirk (22 July 1298) Wallace's schiltrons of spearmen withstood English cavalry but were broken by longbowmen, and he resigned the guardianship. He spent time abroad seeking support, returned to guerrilla warfare, and in August 1305 was captured near Glasgow by the men of John de Menteith. Taken to London, he was tried for treason at Westminster Hall, where he denied the charge on the grounds that he had never sworn allegiance to Edward. He was hanged, drawn and quartered at Smithfield on 23 August 1305.

Legacy

Wallace became a symbol of Scottish national resistance. The late-medieval verse epic The Wallace by Blind Harry shaped his legend, and he is commemorated by the National Wallace Monument near Stirling (completed 1869). The 1995 film Braveheart popularised his story worldwide, though with substantial historical liberties.
