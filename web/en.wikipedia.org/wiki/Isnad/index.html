Isnad

In Islam, isnad (Arabic: إسناد, "support") is the chain of narrators through whom a report — most importantly a hadith of the Prophet Muhammad — has been transmitted. Each hadith consists of two parts: the isnad, the chain of transmitters, and the matn, the actual text of the report. The discipline of evaluating isnads, 'ilm al-rijal (the science of men), developed into a sophisticated biographical and critical method unique to Islamic scholarship.

Structure and evaluation

An isnad lists the transmitters in order, typically beginning with the compiler (e.g., al-Bukhari) and ending with a Companion of Muhammad. A hadith's authenticity was judged by the continuity of the chain, the reliability ('adala), memory and precision (dabt) of each transmitter, and the absence of irregularity or hidden defects. Hadiths were graded as sahih (authentic), hasan (good) or da'if (weak). Compilers such as al-Bukhari and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj applied stringent criteria, and biographical dictionaries recorded the lives, teachers and students of thousands of transmitters.

Significance

The isnad system is one of the earliest systematic methods of source criticism in world history, and Western scholars such as Ignaz Goldziher and Joseph Schacht studied it extensively, debating the extent to which isnads could be projected back in time. Modern hadith criticism continues to use isnad analysis alongside matn criticism.
