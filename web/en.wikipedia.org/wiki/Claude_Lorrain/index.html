Claude Lorrain (French: [klod lɔ.ʁɛ̃]; born Claude Gellée, c. 1600 – 23 November 1682) was a French painter, draughtsman, and etcher of the Baroque era. He spent most of his life in Italy, and is one of the earliest important artists, apart from his contemporaries in Dutch Golden Age painting, to concentrate on landscape painting. His landscapes were typically charged with a Classical or biblical subject, and bathed in a luminous, golden light that made him one of the most imitated painters of the following centuries.

Life

Claude was born in Chamagne in the Duchy of Lorraine. Orphaned young, he traveled to Rome in his teens and worked in the household of the landscape painter Agostino Tassi, from whom he learned perspective and stage-like landscape composition. He also spent time in Naples with Goffredo Wals. By the late 1620s he was established in Rome, where he lived for the rest of his life, receiving commissions from Pope Urban VIII, the Spanish court, and aristocratic patrons across Europe.

Work

Claude's mature paintings combine an idealized vision of the Roman Campagna with carefully observed effects of morning and evening light. His two great late seaports, Seaport with the Embarkation of the Queen of Sheba and Landscape with the Marriage of Isaac and Rebecca (both 1648, National Gallery, London), are landmarks of the classical landscape. To combat forgeries, he recorded his compositions in the Liber Veritatis ("Book of Truth"), a volume of drawings after his paintings now in the British Museum.

Influence

Claude's art profoundly shaped the English landscape tradition: the 18th-century English landscape garden was designed to resemble his paintings, and J. M. W. Turner bequeathed works to hang beside Claude's in the National Gallery. The adjective "Claudean" and the Claude glass, a tinted convex mirror used by artists and tourists to view landscapes in his style, preserve his name.
