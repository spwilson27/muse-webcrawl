Acoustic phonetics is a subfield of phonetics concerned with the physical properties of speech sounds as they travel through the air as sound waves. Together with articulatory phonetics (how sounds are produced) and auditory phonetics (how they are perceived), it forms one of the three classical branches of the discipline.

A wideband spectrogram of an utterance, with time on the horizontal axis and frequency on the vertical.

Concepts

Speech sounds are analysed in terms of frequency (perceived as pitch), amplitude (loudness) and duration, as well as spectral composition. The vocal tract acts as a resonating filter on the sound generated at the larynx (the source–filter model), producing characteristic peaks of energy called formants. The frequencies of the first two or three formants (F1, F2, F3) are the primary acoustic cues distinguishing vowels, while consonants are characterised by features such as voicing onset time, frication noise spectra, bursts and formant transitions.

Methods

The principal instrument of acoustic phonetics is the spectrograph, which produces a spectrogram displaying frequency over time with intensity shown by darkness; digital tools such as Praat are now standard for waveform and spectral analysis. Measurements of formant values, segment durations and voice onset times underpin research in speech perception, phonology, speech technology and forensic speaker comparison.
