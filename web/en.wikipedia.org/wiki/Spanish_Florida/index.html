Spanish Florida (Spanish: La Florida) was the first major European land claim and attempted settlement in the continental United States during the European Age of Discovery. At its greatest extent, the Spanish claim stretched from the Florida peninsula northward along the Atlantic coast and westward along the Gulf of Mexico, though the effectively controlled territory was much smaller.

History

Juan Ponce de León explored and claimed the region for Spain in 1513, naming it La Florida for the Easter season (Pascua Florida) and its flowering landscape. Expeditions by Pánfilo de Narváez (1528) and Hernando de Soto (1539–1543) traversed the southeast, and Tristán de Luna's attempt to settle Pensacola Bay in 1559 failed. Permanent settlement came with Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, who founded St. Augustine in 1565 — the oldest continuously occupied European-established settlement in the continental United States — and destroyed the French Fort Caroline. A network of Franciscan missions later extended across northern Florida and into Georgia.

Transfer to Britain and return

Spain ceded Florida to Great Britain in the 1763 Treaty of Paris ending the Seven Years' War, receiving Havana in return. Britain divided the territory into East Florida and West Florida. Spain regained both Floridas in 1783 after the American Revolutionary War, but its control weakened, and American incursions and filibustering followed.

United States acquisition

Under the Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819, ratified and implemented in 1821, Spain ceded Florida to the United States, ending more than 300 years of Spanish presence. St. Augustine and Pensacola retain substantial Spanish colonial heritage, including the Castillo de San Marcos.
