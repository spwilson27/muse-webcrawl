Black phosphorus

Black phosphorus is the most thermodynamically stable allotrope of phosphorus at room temperature and pressure. It is obtained by heating white phosphorus under high pressures (about 12,000 atmospheres), and was first synthesised by Percy Williams Bridgman in 1914. In appearance, properties, and structure, it resembles graphite, being black and flaky with a layered structure.

Structure and properties

Black phosphorus consists of puckered layers in which each phosphorus atom is bonded to three neighbours; the layers are held together by van der Waals forces and can be exfoliated to produce phosphorene, a two-dimensional material analogous to graphene. Unlike white phosphorus, it does not ignite spontaneously in air and is a semiconductor with a layer-dependent direct band gap (about 0.3 eV in bulk), attracting research interest for electronics and optoelectronics.

Other allotropes

Phosphorus also exists as white, red, and violet allotropes; black phosphorus is the least reactive and densest of the common forms.
