Construction grammar

Construction grammar (often abbreviated CxG) is a family of theories within the field of cognitive linguistics that posit that human language consists of constructions: learned pairings of linguistic forms with functions or meanings, ranging from morphemes and words to idioms and abstract syntactic patterns.

Basic tenets

Construction grammar rejects a strict division between the lexicon and the rules of syntax. Instead, all linguistic knowledge is treated as constructions of varying complexity and schematicity, learned from usage. Meaning is central: every construction pairs a form with a conventionalized function.

Key figures and variants

  • Berkeley Construction Grammar — Charles Fillmore, Paul Kay and colleagues; formalized with unification.
  • Cognitive Construction Grammar — Adele Goldberg; emphasizes argument-structure constructions such as the ditransitive.
  • Radical Construction Grammar — William Croft; argues syntactic categories emerge from constructions.
  • Fluid Construction Grammar — computational implementation by Luc Steels.

Example constructions

"She sneezed the napkin off the table." — the caused-motion construction imposes a transfer meaning even though "sneeze" is not a motion verb.

Other classic examples include the way-construction ("he elbowed his way through the crowd") and the let-alone construction analyzed by Fillmore, Kay and O'Connor.
