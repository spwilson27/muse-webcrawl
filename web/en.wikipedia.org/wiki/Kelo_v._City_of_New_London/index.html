Kelo v. City of New London

Kelo v. City of New London

DecidedJune 23, 2005
Citation545 U.S. 469

QuestionWhether economic development qualifies as a "public use" under the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause

Kelo v. City of New London, 545 U.S. 469 (2005), was a landmark United States Supreme Court decision holding that the use of eminent domain to transfer land from one private owner to another private owner in order to further economic development constitutes a permissible "public use" under the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The 5–4 decision provoked an unusually broad public backlash and led most U.S. states to reform their eminent domain laws.

Background

In 1998, the pharmaceutical company Pfizer announced plans for a research facility in the Fort Trumbull neighborhood of New London, Connecticut. The city authorized a development plan intended to create jobs and increase tax revenues, empowering a private development corporation to acquire the remaining parcels by eminent domain. Susette Kelo, who had bought and renovated a small pink house overlooking the water, and several other homeowners refused to sell. Represented by the Institute for Justice, they sued, arguing that economic development was not a public use. The Connecticut Supreme Court ruled for the city.

Decision

Justice John Paul Stevens, writing for the majority, reaffirmed the Court's broad reading of "public use" as "public purpose," citing Berman v. Parker (1954) and Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff (1984), and held that a comprehensive development plan serving economic growth satisfied the clause so long as it rationally related to a conceivable public purpose. Justice Kennedy's concurrence emphasized protections against purely private favoritism. Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's dissent warned the decision eliminated "any distinction between private and public use of property," and Justice Clarence Thomas argued the holding disproportionately harmed the poor and politically powerless.

Aftermath

Public reaction was overwhelmingly negative across the political spectrum. More than forty states enacted new eminent domain restrictions or constitutional amendments in response. Ironically, the New London project collapsed: Pfizer closed its New London facility in 2009, and the cleared Fort Trumbull lots long sat vacant. Kelo's house was moved to a new location in the city, where it stands as a monument to the case.
