Die-cast toy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Classic die-cast toy vehicles]

A die-cast toy is a toy or collectible model produced by the die casting method of putting molten lead, zinc alloy, or plastic into a mould to make a particular shape. Die-cast toys are usually models of automobiles, aircraft, military vehicles, construction equipment, and trains, and are made most often in scales such as 1:43 and 1:64, though ranges exist from tiny 1:144 models to large 1:12 replicas.

History

Early die-cast toys were cast in lead or iron; production expanded enormously after the Second World War with lightweight zinc alloys such as Mazak (Zamak). The landmark brands were Meccano's Dinky Toys (from 1934), which dominated the quality end of the market; Lesney's Matchbox series (from 1953), sold cheaply in matchbox-style boxes; Mettoy's Corgi Toys (from 1956), "the ones with windows"; and Mattel's Hot Wheels (from 1968), whose low-friction axles and custom-car styling reset the industry. Later years brought specialist makers such as Minichamps and Autoart aimed at adult collectors.

Production and collecting

The moulds ("dies") are expensive, so successful castings are reissued in many liveries; bodies are typically zinc alloy with plastic windows, interiors, and rubber or plastic wheels. Collecting die-cast models is a major hobby, with values driven by rarity, colour variants, condition, and original packaging. Older zinc castings can suffer "zinc pest", an intergranular corrosion that cracks and disintegrates the metal.
