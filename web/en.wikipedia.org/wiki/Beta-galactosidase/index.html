Beta-galactosidase

β-galactosidase (EC 3.2.1.23), also called lactase in its animal digestive form, is a glycoside hydrolase enzyme that catalyses the hydrolysis of β-galactosides into monosaccharides — most famously the splitting of lactose into glucose and galactose. The bacterial enzyme, encoded by the lacZ gene of the lac operon in Escherichia coli, is one of the most studied proteins in molecular biology and a ubiquitous laboratory reporter.

Structure and reaction

The E. coli enzyme is a large tetramer of four identical chains, each of 1,023 amino acids (about 464 kDa in total). It hydrolyses the glycosidic bond of lactose and related substrates through a retaining mechanism with glutamate residues acting as acid/base and nucleophile in the active site. The enzyme also catalyses a transglycosylation that converts some lactose to allolactose, the natural inducer of the lac operon.

Uses in molecular biology

Because its activity is easily measured with chromogenic substrates, β-galactosidase is a standard reporter gene. With X-gal, colonies expressing the enzyme turn blue — the basis of blue-white screening in cloning; with ONPG or MUG, activity can be measured quantitatively or fluorometrically. In mammalian cells, senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity is a widely used marker of cellular senescence. Industrially, fungal and yeast β-galactosidases are used to produce lactose-free dairy products, and human deficiency of the intestinal enzyme causes lactose intolerance.
