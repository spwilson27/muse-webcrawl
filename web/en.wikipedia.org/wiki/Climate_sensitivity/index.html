Climate sensitivity

Climate sensitivity is a measure of how responsive the Earth's climate is to changes in radiative forcing. The most common metric, equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS), is the long-term global-mean surface warming expected after the climate system fully adjusts to a doubling of atmospheric CO2 from pre-industrial levels. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (2021) gives a likely range of 2.5–4.0 °C, with a best estimate of 3 °C.

Related metrics

  • Transient climate response (TCR): warming at the time CO2 doubles under a steady 1%-per-year increase — typically lower than ECS because the deep ocean lags.
  • Transient climate response to cumulative emissions (TCRE): warming per trillion tonnes of carbon emitted, roughly 0.45 °C per 1,000 GtC.
  • Earth system sensitivity: includes very slow feedbacks such as ice-sheet loss, and is higher than ECS.

Why the range narrowed

For decades ECS was quoted as 1.5–4.5 °C. A landmark 2020 assessment (Sherwood et al.) combined evidence from the instrumental record, paleoclimate, and process understanding to narrow the likely range to 2.6–3.9 °C, largely ruling out very low values below 2 °C.

Policy relevance

Higher sensitivity implies that a given emissions budget produces more warming, shrinking the carbon budget for limiting warming to 1.5 or 2 °C.
