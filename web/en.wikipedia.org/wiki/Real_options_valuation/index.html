Real options valuation

Real options valuation applies the mathematics of financial options to capital budgeting decisions. A "real option" is a right — but not an obligation — to undertake a business initiative, such as deferring, expanding, contracting or abandoning a project. Because managers can react to unfolding uncertainty, flexibility itself has value that conventional discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis, which assumes a fixed plan, tends to miss.

Types of real options

  • Option to defer — waiting before committing capital;
  • Option to expand or contract — scaling a successful or failing project;
  • Option to abandon — exiting and recovering salvage value;
  • Option to switch — changing inputs, outputs or processes;
  • Staged (compound) options — as in pharmaceutical R&D, where each phase buys the option on the next.

Methods

Real options are valued with adaptations of the Black–Scholes formula, and more commonly with binomial lattices and Monte Carlo simulation, using the project's value as the underlying "asset" and the investment cost as the exercise price. The term was coined by Stewart Myers in 1977. Applications include natural-resource investments, research and development, real estate development and technology infrastructure, though practitioners note difficulties in estimating volatility and in the non-traded nature of the underlying assets.
