Total factor productivity

Total factor productivity (TFP), also called multi-factor productivity, is a measure of the efficiency with which all inputs — typically labour and capital — are jointly used in production. It is calculated as the portion of output growth not explained by the growth of measured inputs, and so is interpreted as reflecting technological progress, improvements in organisation and skills, and economies of scale.

Growth accounting

In the Solow model, output is written Y = A·F(K, L), where A is total factor productivity. Robert Solow's 1957 growth-accounting study found that most of the growth in United States output per worker in the first half of the twentieth century was attributable to A rather than to capital deepening — the famous "Solow residual", which Moses Abramovitz called "a measure of our ignorance".

Measurement and debate

TFP is estimated as a residual and so absorbs measurement error, capacity utilisation, and omitted inputs such as human capital and research and development. Cross-country studies find large TFP differences are the main explanation of differences in income per capita between rich and poor nations. Productivity slowdowns in advanced economies since the 1970s and mid-2000s remain a central puzzle in macroeconomics.
