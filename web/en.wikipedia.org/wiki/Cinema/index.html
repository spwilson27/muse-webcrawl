Cinema

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cinema is the art and industry of motion pictures — the making, distribution and exhibition of films. The word comes from the French cinéma, short for cinématographe, the camera-projector patented by the Lumière brothers in 1895. Cinema is often called the "seventh art" and encompasses both a mass entertainment industry and an autonomous art form studied by film theory.

History

Preceded by chronophotography (Muybridge, Marey) and Edison's Kinetoscope, projected cinema began publicly with the Lumières' Paris screening of December 1895. Silent film developed a universal visual language; synchronised sound arrived commercially at the end of the 1920s, colour in the 1930s, and widescreen in the 1950s. National film industries grew around Hollywood, India (the world's largest producer by number of films), Europe and East Asia. Digital capture, editing and projection replaced photochemical processes almost entirely in the 2000s–2010s, while streaming has since reshaped distribution and exhibition.

Production and craft

A film is typically created through development, pre-production, principal photography and post-production, involving directors, screenwriters, cinematographers, editors, production designers, composers and many other specialists. Film language — framing, montage, mise-en-scène, sound — is analysed in film studies, while festivals such as Cannes, Venice and Berlin and awards such as the Oscars structure the art's prestige economy.

Exhibition

Films are shown in cinemas (movie theaters) ranging from single screens to multiplexes, as well as on television, physical media and streaming platforms. Box-office revenue, concentrated in the Chinese and North American markets, remains a principal measure of commercial success.
