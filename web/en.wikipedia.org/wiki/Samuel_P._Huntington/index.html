Samuel P. Huntington

Samuel Phillips Huntington
Born: April 18, 1927, New York City
Died: December 24, 2008 (aged 81), Martha's Vineyard, Massachusetts
Known for: The Clash of Civilizations; The Soldier and the State

Samuel Phillips Huntington (April 18, 1927 – December 24, 2008) was an American political scientist, adviser, and academic. He spent more than half a century on the faculty of Harvard University, where he was the Albert J. Weatherhead III University Professor and director of the Center for International Affairs. Huntington is best known for his 1993 theory of the "clash of civilizations," which argued that future wars would be fought not between countries, but between cultures.

Career

Huntington graduated from Yale at eighteen, served briefly in the U.S. Army, and earned his PhD from Harvard in 1951. During the Carter administration he was coordinator of security planning for the National Security Council. He co-founded the journal Foreign Policy. His first major book, The Soldier and the State (1957), examined American civil–military relations and remains a standard work; Political Order in Changing Societies (1968) challenged modernization theory by emphasizing the importance of political institutions and order in developing countries.

The Clash of Civilizations

In a 1993 Foreign Affairs article, expanded into the 1996 book The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order, Huntington argued that after the Cold War, the primary axis of conflict would be cultural and religious — between "civilizations" rather than ideological blocs. The thesis was widely debated and criticized, but became highly influential after the September 11 attacks.

Other works and controversies

Huntington co-authored The Crisis of Democracy (1975) for the Trilateral Commission, arguing that an "excess of democracy" had weakened governance. Who Are We? (2004) warned that large-scale Hispanic immigration threatened American national identity, drawing criticism for nativism. He served as president of the American Political Science Association in 1986–87.
