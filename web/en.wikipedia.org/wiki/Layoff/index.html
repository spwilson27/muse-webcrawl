Layoff

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A layoff (in British and Commonwealth English also called redundancy) is the temporary suspension or permanent termination of employment of an employee or, more commonly, a group of employees for business reasons, such as personnel management or downsizing an organisation. Originally, the term referred exclusively to a temporary interruption in work, as when stand-downs in the dockyards followed a seasonal slack period; in contemporary usage it usually denotes permanent job loss not caused by the employee's fault.

Terminology

Related euphemisms include downsizing, rightsizing, and reduction in force (RIF). A mass layoff involves a large number of employees at one location. Layoffs differ from firing, which is termination for cause such as poor performance or misconduct, and from furlough, which is a mandatory temporary unpaid leave from which employees are expected to return.

Legal framework

Many jurisdictions regulate layoffs. In the United States, the federal WARN Act of 1988 requires employers with 100 or more employees to give 60 days' notice of plant closings and mass layoffs. In the United Kingdom and the European Union, redundancy normally requires a genuine redundancy situation, consultation, fair selection, and statutory or enhanced redundancy pay. Affected workers may qualify for unemployment insurance, severance pay, and retraining support.

Effects

Layoffs can reduce costs and restructure businesses, but research associates them with long-term earnings losses for displaced workers, declines in morale and productivity among remaining staff ("survivor syndrome"), and wider effects on local economies. Downsizing waves were prominent during the recessions of the early 1980s and 1990s, the 2008 financial crisis, and the technology-sector contractions of 2022–2023.
