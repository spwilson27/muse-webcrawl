Multipath propagation

Multipath propagation is the propagation phenomenon that results in radio signals reaching the receiving antenna by two or more paths. Causes of multipath include atmospheric ducting, ionospheric reflection and refraction, and reflection from terrestrial objects such as mountains and buildings, as well as water bodies. Multipath causes signals to interfere with one another, producing fading, distortion, and data errors.

Effects

When copies of a signal arrive with different delays, phases, and amplitudes, they combine at the receiver. Constructive and destructive interference produces frequency-selective fading: some frequencies are enhanced while others are attenuated. As a receiver moves, the interference pattern changes, causing rapid fluctuations in signal strength known as small-scale or Rayleigh fading. In digital systems, the spread of arrival times (delay spread) can cause intersymbol interference, limiting the achievable data rate. In analogue television, multipath produced visible "ghost" images offset from the main picture.

Occurrence

Multipath is significant in virtually all radio communications, including mobile telephony, Wi-Fi, radar, and satellite navigation. In indoor environments, signals reflect from walls, floors, ceilings, and furniture, so no clear line-of-sight path may dominate. Multipath reflections off buildings are a major error source in GPS positioning in urban areas ("urban canyons").

Mitigation and exploitation

Techniques for coping with multipath include diversity reception (spatial, frequency, and time diversity), equalization, spread-spectrum signalling with rake receivers, and orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM), whose long symbol times and cyclic prefixes tolerate delay spread. Modern MIMO systems go further and exploit multipath, using multiple antennas to send independent data streams along different propagation paths, increasing capacity. Radar and sonar systems likewise must distinguish genuine targets from multipath returns.
