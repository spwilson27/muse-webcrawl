Alliance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An alliance is a relationship among people, groups or states that have joined together for mutual benefit or to achieve some common purpose, whether or not explicit agreement has been worked out. In international politics, military alliances are formal agreements for mutual support in case of war — collective-defence commitments such as Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty (an attack on one is an attack on all).

Formation and behaviour

Alliance theory (balance-of-power and balance-of-threat schools) predicts states align against the strongest or most threatening power; alliances also serve to restrain allies, project influence and lock in friendly regimes. Scholars study alliance reliability (most commitments are honoured in the event, with careful counting), the entrapment–abandonment dilemma, burden-sharing disputes (a permanent feature of NATO politics), and alliance institutionalisation — standing commands, integrated forces and consultation machinery distinguishing NATO from looser pacts. The alliance system of 1914 is the classic cautionary case of entangling commitments escalating a Balkan crisis into world war; post-1945 US "hub-and-spokes" alliances in Asia and the treaty network worldwide remain the system's skeleton, now strained and extended by rivalry with China and Russia's war on Ukraine (which brought Finland, 2023, and Sweden, 2024, into NATO).
