Scientific modelling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scientific modelling is the activity of generating models — simplified representations of real systems or phenomena — as part of the scientific method. A model deliberately abstracts away details judged irrelevant to a purpose, allowing scientists to explain observations, explore consequences of hypotheses, make predictions and design experiments and policies. Famous examples include the Bohr model of the atom, the Lotka–Volterra equations of predator–prey dynamics, general circulation models of the climate, and compartmental models of disease spread.

Purposes

  • Explanation: showing how a phenomenon could arise from underlying mechanisms.
  • Prediction: forecasting measurable outcomes — weather, epidemics, election results, material behaviour.
  • Exploration: testing scenarios that cannot be run as physical experiments, such as the effect of a policy or a planet's climate.
  • Design: engineering models (from scale models to digital twins) used to build and test artefacts before construction.

Types of models

Models may be physical (scale models, analogue systems), mathematical (sets of equations, from Newton's laws to differential equations of chemistry), computational (simulations run on computers, including agent-based models and Monte Carlo methods) or conceptual (diagrams and qualitative schemes such as the water cycle). Statistical models infer relationships from data without committing to a mechanism, while machine-learning models are fitted representations prized for predictive accuracy.

Building and evaluating models

Modelling involves choosing variables and assumptions, calibrating parameters against data, and validating the model against observations not used in its construction. A standard checklist asks whether a model is verified (the equations or code are solved correctly) and validated (the outputs match the real system well enough for the intended use). Because all models simplify, evaluation is purpose-relative: as statistician George Box put it, "All models are wrong, but some are useful." Good practice includes sensitivity analysis, uncertainty quantification, and comparison against rival models.

Philosophical issues

Philosophers of science debate what models are (fictions, idealised structures, or partial maps of reality), how they can represent systems they do not resemble, and how idealisation — frictionless planes, infinite populations, rational agents — can nevertheless yield true explanations. Modelling is now sometimes described as a distinct mode of science between theory and experiment.
