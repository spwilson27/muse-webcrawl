Arrow pushing

Curved arrows describing electron movement in a reaction mechanism

Arrow pushing or electron pushing is a technique used to describe the progression of organic chemistry reaction mechanisms. It uses curved ("curly") arrows to track the movement of electrons — in bond breaking and formation, in changes of formal charge, and in resonance structures. The technique was developed in the 1920s, principally by Sir Robert Robinson and Christopher Ingold, and remains the standard formalism taught in organic chemistry.

Conventions

A curved arrow points from the source of the electrons to their destination. An arrow drawn from a bond to an atom indicates that bond's electrons move onto the atom (breaking the bond); an arrow from a lone pair or bond to a position between two atoms indicates formation of a new bond. Double-headed arrows denote movement of electron pairs (the normal case in polar mechanisms). Single-headed "fishhook" arrows denote movement of single electrons in radical (homolytic) processes.

Rules

  • Arrows always indicate electron movement, never the movement of atoms.
  • The tail of the arrow starts at a bond or a lone pair; the head points where the electrons end up.
  • Valence rules must be respected: second-row elements such as carbon, nitrogen and oxygen never exceed an octet in valid structures.
  • Charges must balance across each step of a mechanism.

Applications

Arrow pushing is used to write mechanisms for nucleophilic substitution and addition, elimination, electrophilic aromatic substitution, rearrangements, pericyclic reactions and acid–base reactions, as well as to interconvert resonance forms. Correct electron bookkeeping with arrows allows chemists to predict products, intermediates such as carbocations, and the effect of structure on reactivity.
