Retributive justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Retributive justice is a theory and practice of punishment holding that the morally fitting response to crime is punishment proportionate to the wrongdoing — because it is deserved. In its classic formulations (Kant, Hegel), retributivism treats punishment as an end in itself that respects the offender as a rational agent; its folk ancestor is the lex talionis ("an eye for an eye"), historically a limit on vengeance as much as a licence for it.

Modern role

After a mid-20th-century eclipse (rehabilitation and deterrence dominating penology), desert-based "just deserts" sentencing returned in the 1970s, shaping sentencing guidelines and proportionality review (a constitutional standard in many systems and in international criminal law). Philosophical debate continues over whether retributive intuitions are moral insight or dressed-up vengeance ("expressive" theories split the difference: punishment as communal denunciation), over retribution's ability to set cardinal scales (how many years is a burglary "worth"?), and over its compatibility with restorative and abolitionist programmes. In public opinion, retributive motives consistently predict support for harsher penalties, including capital punishment.
