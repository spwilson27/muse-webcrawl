David Joseph Bohm (20 December 1917 – 27 October 1992) was an American scientist who has been described as one of the most significant theoretical physicists of the 20th century, best known for developing the de Broglie–Bohm interpretation of quantum mechanics, in which particles have definite trajectories guided by a pilot wave.

Biography

Bohm studied at Pennsylvania State College and completed his PhD at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1943 under J. Robert Oppenheimer. He worked on the Manhattan Project at Berkeley and taught at Princeton. Summoned before the House Un-American Activities Committee in 1949, he refused to testify, was indicted for contempt, and though acquitted, was suspended by Princeton. He left the United States, taking posts in São Paulo and at the Technion in Israel before settling in Britain, first at Bristol and then at Birkbeck College, London.

Work

Bohm's 1951 textbook Quantum Theory influenced Einstein's and his own rethinking of quantum mechanics; in 1952 he published the hidden-variable pilot-wave theory. With Yakir Aharonov he predicted the Aharonov–Bohm effect (1959), showing the physical significance of electromagnetic potentials. His later work, including Wholeness and the Implicate Order (1980), developed a holistic philosophy of an implicate and explicate order, and his long dialogues with Jiddu Krishnamurti explored thought and consciousness.
