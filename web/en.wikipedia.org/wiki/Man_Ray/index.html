Man Ray

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Le Violon d'Ingres (1924): a nude woman's back, f-holes superimposed to turn her body into a violin]

Man Ray (born Emmanuel Radnitzky; 27 August 1890 – 18 November 1976) was an American visual artist who spent most of his career in Paris, a leading figure of Dada and Surrealism across painting, film, objects and — most lastingly — photography, where his portraits, 'rayographs' (camera-less photograms) and solarised nudes reinvented the medium.

His celebrated works include the photographs Le Violon d'Ingres (1924), Noire et Blanche (1926) and Tears (c. 1932), the object Gift (1921, a flatiron studded with nails), the film Emak-Bakia (1926), and portraits of virtually every major artist in interwar Paris.

New York Dada to Paris

Born in Philadelphia and raised in Brooklyn, Radnitzky renamed himself Man Ray in 1911, met Marcel Duchamp in 1915 and with him formed New York Dada. Moving to Paris in 1921, he made his living as a portrait and fashion photographer while placing the rayograph — objects laid directly on photographic paper — at the centre of Surrealist practice; his studio assistant was the young Berenice Abbott, and his muse and collaborator Lee Miller rediscovered solarisation with him around 1929.

War and return

Man Ray spent the Hollywood years of the Second World War in California, painting and remaking lost objects, and returned to Paris in 1951. He is buried at Montparnasse Cemetery with the epitaph 'unconcerned, but not indifferent'. A vast posthumous reputation rests on how thoroughly he dissolved the boundaries between commercial craft, documentation and avant-garde art.
