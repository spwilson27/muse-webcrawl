Jelling style

The Jelling style (also spelled Jellinge style) is a phase of Viking art in Scandinavian art history, dated to roughly the 10th century, approximately 880–1000 AD. It takes its name from the royal burial complex at Jelling in Denmark, in particular a small silver cup recovered from the burial chamber of the North Mound associated with King Gorm the Old. The style is one of the sequence of Scandinavian animal ornament styles, preceded by the Oseberg and Borre styles and followed by the Mammen style.

Characteristics

The Jelling style is characterised by S-shaped animals in profile with spiral hips, elongated ribbon-like bodies, and tendril-like lappets and limbs that interlace in loose, open compositions. Motifs are typically arranged with a strong sense of symmetry and double contouring. Unlike the tight "gripping beast" of the preceding Borre style, Jelling animals appear more elegant and plant-like, with foliage elements beginning to blend into animal ornament.

Finds

Besides the Jelling cup, the style appears on runestones, brooches, harness mounts and other metalwork throughout Denmark, southern Norway and Sweden, and in Viking settlements in England and Ireland. The small cup itself, made from a single piece of silver with gilt decoration, carried a design of two S-shaped animals intertwined in the classic manner.

Sequence of styles

Scholars conventionally divide Viking animal art into the Oseberg, Borre, Jelling, Mammen, Ringerike and Urnes styles. The Jelling style overlaps chronologically with the late Borre style and gradually developed into the heavier, more ax-shaped animal forms of the Mammen style in the later 10th century.
