Julius Richard Petri (31 May 1852 – 20 December 1921) was a German microbiologist who is widely credited as the inventor of the Petri dish, a standard item of laboratory equipment used in cell culture and microbiology around the world.

Life and career

Petri was born in Barmen and studied medicine at the Kaiser Wilhelm Academy for military physicians in Berlin, graduating in 1876. He served as a military physician and was assigned from 1877 to 1879 to the Imperial Health Office in Berlin, where he worked as an assistant to Robert Koch, the founder of modern bacteriology.

The Petri dish

In Koch's laboratory, bacterial cultures were grown on flat slices of potato or on gelatin spread on flat glass plates covered with bell jars, which were prone to contamination. In 1887 Petri published a short paper, "A small modification of Koch's plating method", describing a shallow cylindrical glass dish with a slightly larger loose-fitting lid. The design — filled with agar medium — allowed cultures to be grown, observed and stacked while strongly reducing contamination.

Petri also developed other laboratory techniques, including methods for culturing bacteria from air and improvements to culture vessels. He later worked at the Göbersdorf sanatorium in Silesia, directed a museum of hygiene in Berlin, and published works on hygiene, tuberculosis and water filtration.

Legacy

Although the dish's invention has also been connected with similar vessels developed elsewhere, Petri's 1887 description became standard, and his name remains attached to one of the most ubiquitous objects in science. The agar plate technique he refined, using the medium developed by Fanny Hesse and Walther Hesse in Koch's lab, underpins routine microbiology to this day.
