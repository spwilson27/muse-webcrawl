Tu Youyou

Tu Youyou (Chinese: 屠呦呦; pinyin: Tú Yōuyōu; born 30 December 1930) is a Chinese pharmaceutical chemist and malariologist. She is best known for discovering artemisinin (also known as qinghaosu) and dihydroartemisinin, drugs derived from the sweet wormwood plant (Artemisia annua) that are used to treat malaria. Her discovery is credited with saving millions of lives, particularly in the developing world. In 2015 she was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, becoming the first Chinese laureate in that category and the first Chinese woman to receive a Nobel Prize in a scientific category.

Early life and education

Tu was born in Ningbo, Zhejiang province, in 1930. She attended Peking University Medical School, graduating in 1955 from its Department of Pharmaceutical Sciences. Unusually among leading scientists of her generation, she never studied abroad and held no doctorate, a fact that drew comment when she won the Nobel Prize.

Project 523 and the discovery of artemisinin

In 1967, during the Vietnam War, China launched a secret military program, Project 523, to find new antimalarial drugs. Tu was appointed head of a research group at the Institute of Chinese Materia Medica in 1969. Her team systematically screened traditional Chinese herbal remedies, testing more than 2,000 preparations.

A turning point came from re-reading an ancient text, A Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergencies by the fourth-century physician Ge Hong, which described soaking qinghao (sweet wormwood) in cold water rather than boiling it. Tu reasoned that high temperatures were destroying the active ingredient, and in 1971 her team obtained a highly effective low-temperature ether extract. The active compound, artemisinin, was isolated in 1972; Tu later developed the more potent derivative dihydroartemisinin.

Recognition

Tu received the Lasker–DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award in 2011. In 2015 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with William C. Campbell and Satoshi Ōmura, receiving half of the prize "for her discoveries concerning a novel therapy against malaria."
