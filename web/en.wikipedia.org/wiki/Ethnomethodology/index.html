Ethnomethodology is a discipline within sociology that studies the methods people use in everyday life to make sense of their world, produce and maintain a shared, orderly social reality, and make their actions accountable — recognisable and explicable — to others. Founded by Harold Garfinkel at UCLA in the 1950s and 1960s, it takes its name from the "methods" of the "folk" (ethno): the practical reasoning procedures through which social order is continuously accomplished rather than merely imposed by norms or institutions.

Key concepts

Garfinkel's Studies in Ethnomethodology (1967) set out central ideas. Indexicality is the context-dependence of expressions, whose sense depends on the occasion of their use. The documentary method of interpretation describes how people treat appearances as documents of an underlying pattern and elaborate each in the light of the other. Reflexivity means that descriptions of a setting are part of the setting they describe. In his famous breaching experiments, Garfinkel's students deliberately violated everyday expectations — bargaining in shops, acting as lodgers in their own homes — to reveal the normally invisible work by which ordinary order is maintained.

Conversation analysis

Ethnomethodology gave rise to conversation analysis, developed by Harvey Sacks with Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson, which examines the sequential organisation of talk — turn-taking, repair, openings and closings — from recordings of naturally occurring interaction. Ethnomethodological studies of work (of scientists, air-traffic controllers, software designers) have influenced fields from human–computer interaction to science and technology studies.

Criticism

Critics within sociology have charged ethnomethodology with ignoring power, inequality and large-scale social structure, and with an indifference to explanation beyond description. Garfinkel and his followers replied that macro-structures are themselves produced and sustained in members' local practices, so the study of those practices is foundational rather than trivial.
