Ibn Khaldun

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: An Ottoman-era manuscript page of the Muqaddimah in Arabic calligraphy]

Ibn Khaldun (27 May 1332 – 17 March 1406) was an Arab historian, philosopher and statesman from Tunis, author of the Muqaddimah (1377) — the 'Introduction' to his universal history that founded, in the judgement of many, the sciences of historiography, sociology and economics centuries before their European invention. A survivor of the Black Death (which killed his parents), he served — and fell from — the courts of Fez, Granada, Tlemcen and Cairo in turn, wrote his masterwork in Algerian seclusion, negotiated with Timur during the siege of Damascus (1401), and died as Cairo's chief Maliki judge. His cycle theory of dynasties — founded by desert solidarity (asabiyyah), corrupted by luxury, fallen within three or four generations — remains a living tool of historical analysis.

The Muqaddimah

Writing in five months at Qal'at Ibn Salama (1375–77) after abandoning court politics, Ibn Khaldun set out to explain why dynasties rise and fall: group feeling (asabiyyah) forged in hardship conquers cities; sedentary luxury dissolves it within about 120 years; senile dynasties fall to fresh asabiyyah. Around this core he built analyses of prices, taxation (revenue falls when rates rise too far — a curve rediscovered in the 20th century), urbanisation, the classification of sciences, and the criticism of historical sources — including demolitions of fantastic numbers in earlier chronicles. Arnold Toynbee called the Muqaddimah 'the greatest work of its kind that has ever yet been created by any mind in any time or place'.

A life among courts

Ibn Khaldun's career ran through the wreckage of the Maghreb's empires: secretary and chamberlain in Tunis, Fez and Granada (where he negotiated with Pedro the Cruel of Castile, and was expelled amid intrigues), tribal broker in Algeria, then from 1382 Cairo under the Mamluks — professor, judge (six times appointed, five times dismissed), pilgrim, and in 1401 the old man lowered by rope over Damascus' walls to parley with Timur, who questioned him closely on North Africa and let him return to Egypt. His tomb in Cairo is lost; his book, translated into Turkish, French and English, now anchors syllabuses worldwide.
