Joost van den Vondel (17 November 1587 – 5 February 1679) was a Dutch playwright and poet, generally regarded as the greatest writer of the Dutch Golden Age and the national poet of the Netherlands.

Life

Vondel was born in Cologne to Mennonite parents who had fled religious persecution in Antwerp; the family settled in Amsterdam, where he worked in the silk trade while educating himself in Latin and literature. He became a central figure in Amsterdam's literary life, associated with the circle of the Muiderkring. In 1641 he converted to Roman Catholicism, a step that shaped the religious drama of his later years. Financial losses late in life forced him to take a modest post at a municipal pawn bank, and he died in Amsterdam aged 91.

Works

Vondel wrote more than thirty plays, mostly tragedies on biblical and classical themes with choruses modelled on Greek drama. Gijsbrecht van Aemstel (1637), written for the opening of Amsterdam's new theatre, was performed annually on New Year's Day for centuries. His masterpiece Lucifer (1654), about the fall of the angels, was banned after two performances under pressure from the Reformed clergy; it is often cited as a possible influence on Milton's Paradise Lost. Other major works include Jeptha, Adam in Ballingschap and a verse translation of Virgil. His poetry ranges over satire, occasional verse and religious lyric, and a famous sonnet sequence on the death of his young son Constantijn.

Legacy

Amsterdam's largest park, the Vondelpark, is named after him, as are the Vondel Prize for literature and numerous streets and schools. His complete works remain a cornerstone of Dutch literary education.
