Marie Catherine Laveau (c. 1801 – 15 June 1881) was a Louisiana Creole practitioner of Voodoo, renowned in New Orleans as the "Voodoo Queen". A free woman of colour of mixed African, Native American, and French descent, she became the most famous and influential voodoo practitioner in nineteenth-century America. Her daughter, Marie Laveau II, continued her practices, and the two are often conflated in legend.

Life

Laveau was born in the French Quarter of New Orleans, the natural daughter of a free man of colour, Charles Laveaux, and Marguerite Darcantel. In 1819 she married Jacques Paris, a carpenter and fellow free person of colour from Saint-Domingue; after his death in the 1820s she entered a common-law marriage with Louis Christophe Duminy de Glapion, with whom she had several children. She worked as a hairdresser in the homes of wealthy white and Creole families, a position that gave her access to gossip and influence across the city's social strata, which she combined with her reputation for powerful charms, cures, and gris-gris.

Voodoo practice

Laveau is said to have led ceremonies at Congo Square and at Lake Pontchartrain, particularly on St. John's Eve (23 June). A devout Catholic as well as a voodoo priestess, she incorporated Catholic saints, prayers, and symbols into her practice, a syncretism characteristic of Louisiana Voodoo. Contemporary newspapers reported her influence over politicians, prisoners, and the condemned, whom she visited in jail. In old age she was succeeded by her daughter Marie Philomène Glapion, known as Marie Laveau II, whose more flamboyant career extended the legend.

Death and legacy

Marie Laveau died at home on 15 June 1881. Her tomb in St. Louis Cemetery No. 1 in New Orleans remains a place of pilgrimage; visitors traditionally marked the tomb with X's in hope of favours, a practice now discouraged as vandalism. She has become a central figure of New Orleans folklore and popular culture, appearing in songs, novels, and television series.
