Organ of Corti

The organ of Corti, or spiral organ, is the receptor organ for hearing and is located in the mammalian cochlea. This highly varied strip of epithelial cells allows for transduction of auditory signals into nerve impulses. The organ was first described in 1851 by the Italian anatomist Alfonso Giacomo Gaspare Corti (1822–1876).

Structure

The organ of Corti sits within the scala media of the cochlea, resting on the basilar membrane and covered by the gelatinous tectorial membrane. It contains two types of sensory hair cells arranged in rows along the cochlear spiral: a single row of about 3,500 inner hair cells, which are the principal sensory receptors, and three rows of about 12,000 outer hair cells, which act as a cochlear amplifier by changing length in response to stimulation (electromotility, driven by the protein prestin). Supporting structures include the tunnel of Corti formed by pillar cells, Deiters' cells, and the reticular lamina.

Function

Sound vibrations transmitted through the middle ear set up a travelling wave on the basilar membrane; because the membrane's stiffness varies along its length, each frequency produces maximal displacement at a characteristic place — high frequencies at the base, low frequencies at the apex (tonotopy). Displacement deflects the stereocilia of the hair cells against the tectorial membrane, opening mechanically gated ion channels. Potassium influx from the potassium-rich endolymph depolarizes the cells; inner hair cells release glutamate onto afferent fibres of the spiral ganglion, sending signals via the cochlear nerve to the brainstem.

Clinical significance

Damage to hair cells by noise, ototoxic drugs, or aging causes sensorineural hearing loss; in mammals these cells do not regenerate. Cochlear implants bypass the organ of Corti by electrically stimulating the auditory nerve directly.
