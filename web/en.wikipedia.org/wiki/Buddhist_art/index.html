Buddhist art

Buddhist art is the artistic production influenced by Buddhism, encompassing sculpture, painting, architecture and ritual objects created across Asia over more than two millennia. It originated in the Indian subcontinent in the centuries after the life of Siddhartha Gautama (c. 6th to 4th century BCE) and spread with the religion along the Silk Road to Central, East and Southeast Asia.

Aniconic phase

The earliest Buddhist art, seen at sites such as Bharhut and Sanchi (2nd–1st century BCE), was aniconic: the Buddha was not depicted in human form but represented by symbols — an empty throne, the Bodhi tree, footprints, the dharma wheel, or the stupa housing his relics.

The Buddha image

Anthropomorphic images of the Buddha appeared around the 1st century CE in two regions: Gandhara, in present-day Pakistan and Afghanistan, where Greco-Roman influence produced naturalistic, wavy-haired figures in heavy drapery; and Mathura in north India, which developed a more indigenous, sensuous style. Standardised iconography emerged: the ushnisha (cranial bump), long earlobes, the urna between the brows, and hand gestures (mudras) signifying teaching, meditation, protection and enlightenment.

Regional developments

Gupta-period India (4th–6th century) produced the serene classical Buddha type that influenced all Asia. As Mahayana and later Vajrayana Buddhism developed, art multiplied its subjects: bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara, wrathful protector deities, and elaborate mandalas, notably in Tibet and Nepal. In China, colossal cave temples at Yungang and Longmen gave way to refined Chan (Zen) ink aesthetics, transmitted to Japan, where sculpture, temple architecture and garden design reached distinctive forms. In Southeast Asia, Theravada countries built great monuments such as Borobudur in Java (Mahayana) and the temples of Pagan in Myanmar and Angkor in Cambodia.

Functions

Buddhist images serve as objects of devotion and merit-making, aids to meditation, teaching narratives of the Buddha's lives (jataka tales), and foci for pilgrimage. Consecrated statues are treated as living presences rather than mere representations.
