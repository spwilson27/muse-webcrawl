Certificate of deposit

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a time deposit sold by banks and credit unions. In return for depositing a fixed sum for an agreed term — commonly three months to five years — the depositor receives a fixed or variable rate of interest, typically higher than that of an ordinary savings account. Withdrawal before maturity usually incurs a penalty of several months' interest.

Characteristics

CDs are evidenced by a certificate or account record stating the principal, interest rate, and maturity date. In the United States, deposits at insured banks are covered by FDIC insurance and those at federal credit unions by NCUA insurance, generally up to $250,000 per depositor per institution. At maturity a CD may be withdrawn, renewed, or rolled over; many banks grant a short grace period before automatic renewal. Some CDs are callable by the issuing bank, and "brokered CDs" are distributed through brokerage firms and may be traded in a secondary market.

Variants

Variants include jumbo CDs (with large minimum deposits), bump-up CDs, which allow the holder to raise the rate once during the term, step-up CDs with scheduled rate increases, liquid CDs permitting limited penalty-free withdrawals, and add-on CDs that accept additional deposits. A common strategy, "CD laddering", staggers maturities so that part of the portfolio matures regularly, balancing yield against liquidity.

History

Negotiable certificates of deposit were introduced in the United States in 1961, when First National City Bank of New York (now Citibank) began issuing them to corporate depositors, creating an important money-market instrument. Retail CDs in their modern form spread widely during the high-interest-rate era of the late 1970s and early 1980s.
