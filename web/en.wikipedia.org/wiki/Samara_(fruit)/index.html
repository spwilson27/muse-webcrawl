Samara (fruit)

A samara is a winged achene, a type of dry fruit in which a flattened wing of fibrous, papery tissue develops from the ovary wall. The wing allows the seed to be carried by the wind away from the parent tree, a form of dispersal known as anemochory. Samaras are characteristic of trees such as maples, ashes, and elms, and the paired samaras of maples are commonly known as "helicopters", "whirlybirds", or "spinning jennies" because of the way they spin as they fall.

Formation and function

The samara is a simple dry fruit that does not split open at maturity (indehiscent). The wing is an outgrowth of the pericarp (fruit wall). As the fruit falls, air resistance on the wing causes it to autorotate, slowing its descent and increasing the distance the wind can carry it. The shape and angle of the wing determine the rate of descent and the stability of the spin.

Examples

Maples (Acer) produce paired samaras joined at the base, which separate as they fall. Ashes (Fraxinus) produce single samaras with a wing at one end. Elms (Ulmus) produce samaras in which the seed is centred within a circular or oval wing. The tree of heaven (Ailanthus altissima) and the hop tree also produce samaras. Among extinct plants, samara-like fruits appeared early in the fossil record, showing that wind dispersal of winged seeds is an ancient adaptation.

In culture

The spinning fall of maple samaras has inspired biomimetic designs, including single-winged micro air vehicles and autorotating seed-inspired drones developed for environmental sensing.
