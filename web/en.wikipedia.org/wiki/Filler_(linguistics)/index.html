Filler (linguistics)

In linguistics, a filler, filled pause, hesitation marker, or discourse marker is a sound or word that speakers use to signal that they are pausing to think but have not yet finished speaking. Common examples in English include uh, um, er, ah, like, okay, right, and you know. Fillers generally carry little or no propositional meaning, and removing them typically does not change the grammatical structure or literal content of an utterance.

Functions

Although often stigmatised as sloppy speech, fillers serve real communicative functions. They:

  • Hold the conversational floor while the speaker plans what to say next.
  • Signal the length of an expected delay — research by psycholinguists such as Herbert H. Clark and Jean Fox Tree suggests uh tends to precede short delays and um longer ones.
  • Soften statements, mark uncertainty, or structure discourse, as with well, so, and anyway.
  • Alert listeners that a correction or reformulation is coming.

Fillers across languages

Every language has conventional fillers. Examples include French euh, German ähm, Spanish este and pues, Italian ehm and cioè, Japanese eto (えーと) and ano (あのう), Mandarin nèige (那个) and jiùshì (就是), Korean eum (음), Russian eto (это) and nu (ну), and Welsh um. Learners of a second language often import fillers from their native tongue or overuse the target language's forms.

Perception and usage

Excessive use of fillers can reduce a speaker's perceived confidence and competence, which is why public-speaking training — and organisations such as Toastmasters, which counts "ah"s — often aims to reduce them. Nevertheless, moderate use is normal even among highly fluent speakers, appearing roughly every 10–15 seconds in spontaneous speech, and listeners process them unconsciously most of the time. Corpus studies find variation by age, gender, region, and register; the discourse use of like, for instance, expanded notably in late 20th-century English.

In speech technology

Automatic speech recognition and transcription systems must decide whether to preserve or strip fillers; retaining them can benefit applications such as clinical assessment (where filled pauses correlate with cognitive load and certain disorders) and dialogue systems, which may themselves generate fillers to sound natural.
