Resonator guitar

A resonator guitar or resophonic guitar is an acoustic guitar that produces sound by conducting string vibrations through the bridge to one or more spun metal cones (resonators) instead of to the sound board alone. Invented in the United States in the 1920s to make guitars loud enough to compete with horns and percussion in dance bands, resonator guitars — most famously the all-metal National instruments and the wooden-bodied Dobro — are prized for their bright, carrying tone and are closely associated with blues, bluegrass and Hawaiian music.

History

John Dopyera, a Slovak-American luthier, developed the first successful resonator designs for guitarist George Beauchamp, leading to the National "Tricone" of 1927 with three small cones, followed by cheaper single-cone models. Dopyera and his brothers then left to found Dobro, whose "spider bridge" design drives a single cone from above and produces a sweeter, sustaining tone favoured in bluegrass. Electrification in the 1930s made the resonator's volume advantage less necessary, but the instruments survived in folk genres.

Playing styles

Round-neck resonators are played in the conventional position with fretting and often a bottleneck slide, a signature sound of Delta blues players such as Son House. Square-neck models are played face-up on the lap with a steel bar, as in Hawaiian music and bluegrass dobro playing exemplified by Jerry Douglas. A metal-bodied resonator appears on the cover of Dire Straits' album Brothers in Arms.
