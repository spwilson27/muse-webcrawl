Secessio plebis

Secessio plebis ("secession of the plebeians") was a form of protest in the Roman Republic during the Conflict of the Orders, in which the plebeians — the common citizenry — would abandon the city en masse, leaving the patrician ruling class to fend for itself. Functioning like a general strike, a secession shut down shops and commerce and withdrew plebeian soldiers from the army, forcing the Senate to concede political reforms.

The secessions

Ancient sources record five secessions between 494 and 287 BCE:

  • 494 BCE — Burdened by debt, the plebs withdrew to the Mons Sacer (Sacred Mount). The secession ended with the creation of the office of tribune of the plebs, whose holders were sacrosanct and could veto magistrates' acts.
  • 449 BCE — Provoked by the tyranny of the decemvirs under Appius Claudius, the plebs seceded to the Aventine Hill; the decemvirate fell and the tribunate was restored, followed by the Valerio-Horatian laws.
  • 445 BCE — A threatened secession contributed to the passage of the lex Canuleia, permitting intermarriage between patricians and plebeians.
  • 342 BCE — During a military mutiny associated with debt grievances, the Genucian laws followed, addressing interest and access to the consulship.
  • 287 BCE — The final secession, to the Janiculum, produced the lex Hortensia, which made plebiscites binding on all citizens, a milestone that effectively ended the Conflict of the Orders.

Significance

Historians compare the secessio to the modern general strike or sit-down strike. Some modern scholars question the details of the early secessions as later annalistic inventions, though the institution of the tribunate and the broad arc of plebeian gains are accepted as historical.
