A fallacy is the use of invalid or otherwise faulty reasoning in the construction of an argument that may appear to be well-reasoned if unnoticed. The term was in use in the Western intellectual tradition by writers such as what was known as sophistry. Fallacies are commonly divided into "formal" and "informal". A formal fallacy is a flaw in the structure of a deductive argument that renders the argument invalid, while an informal fallacy originates in an error in reasoning other than an improper logical form. Arguments containing informal fallacies may be formally valid, but still fallacious.

History

The systematic study of fallacies began with Aristotle's On Sophistical Refutations, which catalogued thirteen fallacies in language and reasoning. Medieval logicians expanded the list, and the traditional catalogue of named fallacies — ad hominem, straw man, appeal to ignorance, false dilemma, and many others — grew through early modern and modern textbooks of rhetoric and critical thinking.

Formal fallacies

Formal fallacies are errors in the logical form of a deductive argument:

  • Affirming the consequent — "If A then B; B, therefore A."
  • Denying the antecedent — "If A then B; not A, therefore not B."
  • Affirming a disjunct — treating an inclusive "or" as exclusive.
  • Fallacy of four terms — a categorical syllogism using more than three terms.

Informal fallacies

Informal fallacies depend on content and context. Common groups include:

  • Fallacies of relevance — ad hominem (attacking the arguer), straw man (misrepresenting the opponent's position), appeal to authority, appeal to emotion, red herring.
  • Fallacies of presumption — begging the question, false dilemma, loaded question, hasty generalisation, slippery slope.
  • Fallacies of ambiguity — equivocation, amphiboly, composition and division.
  • Causal fallacies — post hoc ergo propter hoc, false cause, correlation treated as causation.

Use in argumentation theory

Modern approaches, such as pragma-dialectics developed by Frans van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst, treat fallacies as violations of the rules of a reasonable critical discussion rather than as a fixed list of errors. Critics note that many arguments traditionally labelled fallacious (such as appeals to authority or emotion) can be reasonable in context, so fallacy-spotting by label alone is discouraged.
