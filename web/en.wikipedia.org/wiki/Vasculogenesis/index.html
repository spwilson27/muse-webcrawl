Vasculogenesis

Vasculogenesis is the process of blood vessel formation, occurring by a de novo production of endothelial cells. It is the first stage of the formation of the circulatory system, and occurs mainly during embryonic development, though it can also take place in adult organisms through the contribution of circulating endothelial progenitor cells.

Vasculogenesis is distinguished from angiogenesis, in which new vessels sprout from pre-existing ones, and from arteriogenesis, the remodelling of collateral arteries.

Process

In the early embryo, mesodermal cells differentiate into hemangioblasts, which aggregate into clusters known as blood islands, first visible in the wall of the yolk sac. The outer cells of these islands become endothelial cells while the inner cells become primitive blood cells. The endothelial cells then connect into cords and tubes, forming a primitive vascular plexus that is subsequently remodelled into a hierarchical network of arteries, capillaries, and veins.

Regulation

Key signalling molecules include vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and its receptors VEGFR-1 and VEGFR-2, fibroblast growth factors, and the angiopoietins acting through the Tie2 receptor. Hypoxia-inducible factors help coordinate vessel formation with tissue oxygen demand.

Adult vasculogenesis

After birth, endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) derived from the bone marrow can be mobilised into the circulation and incorporated into sites of new vessel growth, for example after myocardial infarction or in wound healing. This postnatal vasculogenesis contributes to neovascularisation in both physiological repair and pathological states such as tumour growth.
