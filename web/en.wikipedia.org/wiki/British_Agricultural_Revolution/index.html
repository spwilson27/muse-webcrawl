British Agricultural Revolution

The British Agricultural Revolution was the unprecedented increase in agricultural production in Britain arising from increases in labour and land productivity between the mid-17th and late 19th centuries. Agricultural output grew faster than the population over the hundred-year period ending in 1770, and thereafter productivity remained among the highest in the world.

The revolution was the result of the interaction of many changes: the enclosure of common land, new crop rotations such as the Norfolk four-course system, selective breeding of livestock, improved farm implements such as Jethro Tull's seed drill, and better drainage and fertilisation. It supplied the food, labour and capital that underpinned the Industrial Revolution.

Major changes

Enclosure

Enclosure consolidated scattered strips in open fields into compact, hedged farms. Parliamentary enclosure, authorised by thousands of individual Acts — especially between 1750 and 1850 — enclosed roughly a fifth of England. Enclosure allowed farmers to experiment without communal agreement, but dispossessed many cottagers of common grazing rights.

Crop rotation

The Norfolk four-course rotation — wheat, turnips, barley, then clover or ryegrass — abolished the fallow year. Turnips fed livestock through winter, and clover fixed nitrogen in the soil. Charles "Turnip" Townshend popularised the system at Raynham Hall in Norfolk in the 1730s.

New implements

Jethro Tull's seed drill (1701) sowed seeds in neat rows at a controlled depth, and his horse-drawn hoe reduced weeding labour. Later came improved ploughs, threshing machines and drainage tiles.

Selective breeding

Robert Bakewell of Dishley Grange pioneered systematic selective breeding of sheep and cattle, producing the New Leicester sheep and improving Longhorn cattle. Breeding by "like begets like" roughly doubled the average weight of animals sold at Smithfield between 1710 and 1795.

Consequences

Between 1700 and 1850 the population of England roughly tripled while food output kept pace, and agricultural employment fell as a share of the workforce, releasing labour for factories. Historians debate how revolutionary the change was: some see a slow evolution from the 16th century, others a sharp acceleration after 1750. The term itself was popularised by Lord Ernle's English Farming Past and Present (1912).
