Reformed epistemology

Reformed epistemology is a school of thought in philosophical theology regarding the epistemology of religious belief, which holds that belief in God can be "properly basic" — rationally held without being inferred from evidence or argument. It is associated principally with the philosophers Alvin Plantinga, Nicholas Wolterstorff, and William Alston, and takes inspiration from the Reformed theologian John Calvin's notion of a sensus divinitatis (sense of the divine).

Alvin Plantinga developed the best-known account of Reformed epistemology. [Image placeholder]

Main claims

Reformed epistemologists challenge evidentialism and classical foundationalism, according to which religious belief is rational only if supported by sufficient propositional evidence. Plantinga argues that belief in God can be properly basic in the way that belief in other minds or the past is basic: not accepted on the basis of other propositions, yet warranted when produced by properly functioning cognitive faculties in an appropriate environment, according to a design plan successfully aimed at truth.

The sensus divinitatis and Aquinas/Calvin model

In Plantinga's "Aquinas/Calvin model", human beings possess a cognitive faculty — Calvin's sensus divinitatis — that in certain circumstances (guilt, beauty, danger, gratitude) produces belief in God; sin is said to damage this faculty, and faith is its restoration by the Holy Spirit. Warrant, on Plantinga's externalist account, does not require the believer to have access to arguments.

Criticisms

Critics raise the "Great Pumpkin objection" — that the model would license any community's basic beliefs — and question how competing religious claims of basic belief can be adjudicated, and whether the model begs the question by assuming the truth of theism in its account of warrant.
