Arianism is a Christological doctrine associated with Arius (c. 256–336), a presbyter in Alexandria, Egypt, which held that Jesus Christ, the Son of God, was created by God the Father and therefore was not co-eternal with him — in the slogan attributed to Arius, "there was a time when he was not". It was condemned as heresy by the First Council of Nicaea in 325, which issued the Nicene Creed in response.

The controversy

Arius taught that only the Father is truly eternal and unbegotten; the Son, though the first and greatest of creatures and the agent of creation, had a beginning. His bishop, Alexander of Alexandria, and Alexander's successor Athanasius opposed the teaching, insisting the Son was of the same substance (homoousios) as the Father. The dispute split the Eastern church and drew in emperors for much of the 4th century: councils alternately upheld and condemned Arian formulas, and Athanasius was exiled five times.

Later history

Although imperial orthodoxy settled against Arianism by 381, Arian Christianity survived for centuries among several Germanic peoples — Goths, Vandals, Burgundians and Lombards — converted in part through the mission of Ulfilas, who translated the Bible into Gothic. The Visigothic kingdom of Spain renounced Arianism under King Reccared in 589, and the Lombards gradually followed, ending Arianism as an organised church tradition.
