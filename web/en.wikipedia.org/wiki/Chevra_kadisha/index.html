Chevra kadisha

Members of a burial society performing ritual preparation (image placeholder)

A chevra kadisha (Aramaic: חברא קדישא, "holy society" or "holy fellowship") is a Jewish burial society, an organisation of men and women who ensure that the bodies of deceased Jews are prepared for burial according to Jewish law and tradition, and protected until interment.

Activities

The society's central task is the ritual of taharah: the body is washed, ritually purified with water, dressed in white linen shrouds (tachrichim), and placed in the coffin. Volunteers known as shomrim may watch over the body from death until burial, reciting psalms. The work is considered a supreme act of kindness (chesed shel emet, "true kindness") because the recipient cannot repay it.

History

Organised burial societies became a defining institution of Jewish communal life in early modern Europe; the famous Prague chevra kadisha was founded in 1564 under Rabbi Eliezer Ashkenazi. Societies often maintained cemeteries, supported widows and orphans, and commissioned distinctive silver and painted plaques. Today most Jewish communities, synagogues, and many funeral homes work with a local chevra kadisha.
