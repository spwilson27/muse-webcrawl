The Haida (Haida: X̱aayda) are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast of North America. Their traditional homeland is the archipelago of Haida Gwaii (formerly the Queen Charlotte Islands) off the coast of present-day British Columbia, and the southern part of Prince of Wales Island in Alaska, where they are known as Kaigani Haida. They are renowned for monumental totem poles, great cedar longhouses and ocean-going canoes, and for one of the richest art traditions of the Northwest Coast.

Society and culture

Haida society was organised into two exogamous moieties, Raven and Eagle, with rank, names, crests and property inherited matrilineally. Chiefs validated their status through the potlatch, a ceremonial distribution of goods. Haida artists carved house-frontal poles, mortuary poles and interior screens, and worked argillite, a black slate found only on Haida Gwaii, into intricate carvings for the trade that followed European contact.

History

Maritime fur traders reached Haida Gwaii from the 1770s, bringing wealth and, later, devastating epidemics. Smallpox in 1862 and other diseases reduced the population from perhaps 10,000 or more to a few hundred, and survivors consolidated at Masset and Skidegate. Missionaries and Canadian law suppressed the potlatch and the Haida language for generations.

Contemporary Haida Nation

The Council of the Haida Nation, founded in 1974, asserts Haida title to Haida Gwaii. In 1985 Haida opposition to logging on Lyell Island helped bring about the protection of Gwaii Haanas (South Moresby), now a national park reserve and Haida heritage site co-managed by Canada and the Haida Nation. In 2023 British Columbia and the Haida Nation signed the Gaayhllxid · Gíihlagalgang "Rising Tide" agreement recognising Haida Aboriginal title throughout Haida Gwaii, followed by provincial legislation in 2024. Haida language revitalisation, carving programs and the work of artists such as Bill Reid have driven a major cultural resurgence.
