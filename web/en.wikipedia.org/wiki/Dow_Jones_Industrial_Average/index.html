Dow Jones Industrial Average

[Image: a television screen showing the Dow Jones index value in green after a rise]

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA), also called 'the Dow', is a stock market index of 30 prominent US companies, created by Charles Dow and Edward Jones — first published in 1896 with twelve industrial stocks — and now maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices. Despite its narrow base it remains the most quoted single number for the US stock market in general media.

Construction and criticism

The Dow is price-weighted: a one-dollar move in any component moves the index equally, so high-priced shares dominate — a method preserved from the era of hand calculation and criticised as unrepresentative next to the market-capitalisation-weighted S&P 500. Its members — large firms in industrials, technology, finance, health care and consumer goods, long since not only 'industrials' — are chosen by a committee to represent the economy. Milestones in the index's climb (1,000 in 1972, 10,000 in 1999, 40,000 in 2024) mark eras of American business, and its worst days — 1929, Black Monday 1987 (−22.6%, still its largest one-day percentage fall) and March 2020 — mark crises. Index futures and ETFs allow investors to trade 'the Dow' itself.
