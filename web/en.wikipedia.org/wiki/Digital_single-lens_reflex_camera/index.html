A digital single-lens reflex camera (DSLR) is a digital camera that combines the optics and mechanisms of a single-lens reflex camera with a solid-state image sensor. In the reflex design, light travels through the lens, reflects from a mirror into an optical viewfinder via a pentaprism; when a photograph is taken, the mirror flips up, the shutter opens, and the image is projected onto the sensor. Interchangeable lenses and through-the-lens viewing are defining features.

History

Kodak produced the first commercially available DSLR, the DCS-100 (1991), a modified Nikon body with a separate storage unit; early DSLRs were expensive professional tools. The Nikon D1 (1999) brought the price to a level working photojournalists could afford, and Canon's EOS 300D/Digital Rebel (2003) first broke the US$1,000 barrier for consumers. DSLRs became the standard tools of professional and enthusiast photography in the 2000s, and from the Nikon D90 (2008) also shot high-definition video, influencing filmmaking.

Decline

From the 2010s, mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras — which drop the mirror box for electronic viewfinders — eroded DSLR advantages, offering smaller bodies, faster continuous shooting and advanced video. Canon and Nikon progressively wound down DSLR development in the early 2020s, and industry shipments of DSLRs fell to a small fraction of their peak, though millions remain in use.
