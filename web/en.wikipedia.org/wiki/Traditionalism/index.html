Traditionalism (politics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Traditionalism in politics is a doctrine emphasising the value of inherited institutions, customs, beliefs and social forms, and counselling caution toward rapid or rationally redesigned change. Within the broader family of Conservatism, traditionalists ground their position in religion, natural law or the accumulated experience of generations, and are sceptical of ideologies that promise to rebuild society from abstract principles.

The tradition runs from Edmund Burke's Reflections through the counter-revolutionary writers Joseph de Maistre and Juan Donoso Cortés to twentieth-century figures such as Russell Kirk, whose The Conservative Mind (1953) codified canons of conservative thought including belief in a transcendent order, affection for variety, the link between freedom and property, and prudence as the presiding political virtue.

Relation to other right-wing currents

Traditionalists differ from market liberals in subordinating economic efficiency to social and moral order, and from nationalist populists in their suspicion of mass politics and charismatic leadership; they differ from reactionaries, who seek actively to restore a lost order, in accepting gradual adaptation of what exists. In the United States, “fusionism” sought to combine traditionalist virtue with free-market economics and anti-communism within the post-war conservative coalition.
