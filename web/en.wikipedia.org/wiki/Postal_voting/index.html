Postal voting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Postal voting is voting in an election where ballot papers are distributed to voters by post (and usually returned by post), in contrast to in-person voting at a polling station. It includes absentee voting for those away or unable to attend, and all-mail elections in which every registered voter receives a ballot automatically (used for Swiss referendums, and by US states such as Colorado, Oregon, Washington and Utah, and widely expanded in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic).

Debates

Advantages claimed include higher turnout, convenience and time to study complex ballots; the Oregon experience and Swiss practice are the standard exhibits. Concerns involve the loss of the secret ballot's controlled environment (coercion, "family voting", vote harvesting by third parties collecting ballots), signature-verification disputes, slower counts and litigation, and postal reliability. Proven fraud rates in studies are very low but non-zero — a 2005 UK court judgment described postal-vote fraud in Birmingham in terms that forced reforms — and the topic's partisan polarisation in the US since 2020 has made administration choices fiercely contested.
