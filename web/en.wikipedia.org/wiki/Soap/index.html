Soap

Soap is a salt of a fatty acid, made by reacting fats or oils with a strong alkali — saponification: triglyceride + sodium hydroxide → glycerol + soap. Soap has cleaned skin, clothes and cities for at least four thousand years; Babylonian and Roman recipes are recorded.

Soap molecules are amphiphilic: a water-loving ionic head and a water-hating hydrocarbon tail. In water they surround grease as micelles, tails inward, and lift it away in the rinse. Hard water's calcium and magnesium ions precipitate soap as scum, which synthetic detergents (with sulfonate heads) avoid.

Making

Traditional soap boils tallow or plant oils (coconut, palm, olive — Castile soap) with lye; modern industry splits fats with steam and neutralises the fatty acids. Cold-process craft soap retains glycerol; commercial makers often remove it for cosmetics.

Chemistry notes

Soap solutions are mildly alkaline (pH ~9–10). 'Soap' in chemistry also includes metal salts of fatty acids used as greases (lithium soap) and driers in paints (cobalt, manganese soaps).
