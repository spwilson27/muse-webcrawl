Rheged was a Brittonic-speaking kingdom of the Hen Ogledd ("Old North") in post-Roman Britain, flourishing in the sixth century AD. Its territory is generally located in what is now Cumbria and Dumfries and Galloway in north-west England and south-west Scotland, though its precise extent is unknown and debated. Rheged is best known through the praise poetry of Taliesin addressed to its king, Urien, and through traditions associating it with the campaign against the Angles of Bernicia.

History

Little certain is known of Rheged's history, which must be reconstructed from Welsh poetry, genealogies and later chronicles. Its most famous king, Urien Rheged, is celebrated by the poet Taliesin as the victor of battles against the Angles, including the siege of Lindisfarne (Ynys Metcaud). Urien is said to have been assassinated at the instigation of his jealous ally Morcant Bulc during the campaign. His son, Owain mab Urien, also a figure of Taliesin's poems, was in later Welsh tradition made a knight of King Arthur's court and husband of the Lady of the Fountain.

At some point in the seventh or early eighth century, Rheged was absorbed into the expanding Anglian kingdom of Northumbria, seemingly by dynastic marriage and peaceful union rather than conquest: the Northumbrian royal monastery at Abercorn and records of the diocese of Carlisle hint at incorporation, and a Northumbrian prince is recorded with the Brittonic name element. The Cumbric language nevertheless survived in the region into the eleventh or twelfth century.

Location debate

Traditionally centred on Cumberland and the Solway plain, Rheged has also been argued—on the basis of the 2012–2017 excavations at Trusty's Hill in Galloway, where a royal citadel with Pictish symbols and high-status metalworking was found—to have included a major royal centre in the Rhins of Galloway. The Trusty's Hill site, destroyed by fire in the early seventh century, suggests Galloway formed part of the kingdom's core.

Legacy

Rheged gives its name to the Rheged discovery centre near Penrith, Cumbria. Its kings figure prominently in Welsh literature and in modern historical fiction about the age of Arthur and the wars of the Old North.
