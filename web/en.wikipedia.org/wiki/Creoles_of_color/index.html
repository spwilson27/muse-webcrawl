Creoles of color

Creoles of color formed a distinct free community in Louisiana

Creoles of color (French: Créoles de couleur) are an ethnic group of mixed European and African ancestry that developed in Louisiana during the French and Spanish colonial periods. They descend from the unions of French and Spanish colonists with enslaved and free people of African descent (and sometimes Native Americans), and formed a distinct class of free people of colour (gens de couleur libres) with their own culture, language, and social position between white colonists and the enslaved population.

History

Under French and Spanish rule, a three-tiered racial order emerged in Louisiana in which free people of color could own property, conduct business, and in some cases own slaves themselves. Many were artisans, merchants, and property holders concentrated in New Orleans and the Cane River region. After the Louisiana Purchase of 1803, the incoming Anglo-American binary racial system gradually eroded their intermediate status, a process accelerated after the Civil War and Reconstruction, when Jim Crow laws legally classified them with other African Americans.

Culture

Creoles of color were typically Francophone and Catholic, and developed distinctive traditions in music, cuisine, architecture, and Mardi Gras celebrations. Placage — formalised unions between white men and free women of color — was a recognised, if contested, feature of the colonial social order. Notable Creoles of color include the composer Edmond Dédé, the writer Victor Séjour, and civil rights activist Homer Plessy of Plessy v. Ferguson fame, who was an octoroon member of the Comité des Citoyens that challenged Louisiana's segregation law in 1892.
