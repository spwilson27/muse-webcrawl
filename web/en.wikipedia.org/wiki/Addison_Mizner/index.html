Addison Cairns Mizner (December 12, 1872 – February 8, 1933) was an American architect whose Mediterranean Revival and Spanish Revival style interpretations changed the character of southern Florida during the Florida land boom of the 1920s. Self-taught and flamboyant, Mizner is best known for popularizing a romantic, Spanish-inspired architecture in Palm Beach and for his ultimately failed attempt to build the city of Boca Raton around a grand resort.

Early life

Mizner was born in Benicia, California, the youngest of seven sons of Lansing Bond Mizner, a lawyer and diplomat who served as United States Minister to Central America. The younger Mizner traveled widely in Guatemala and elsewhere as a young man and briefly prospected for gold in Alaska and the Yukon during the Klondike Gold Rush. He had no formal architectural training, learning the craft through apprenticeships, travel, and extensive study of Spanish and Spanish Colonial buildings.

Florida career

Mizner arrived in Palm Beach in 1918, in poor health and seeking a warmer climate. His first major Florida commission, the Everglades Club (1918) for sewing machine heir Paris Singer, established his reputation: a picturesque complex of stucco walls, tile roofs, arcades, courtyards, and deliberately irregular detailing that looked centuries old despite being newly built. Mizner used distressed finishes, salvaged materials, and antiques — which he manufactured and sold through his own company, Mizner Industries — to give his buildings an aged, romantic character.

Wealthy clients commissioned Mizner palaces along Palm Beach's coast, and he designed the shopping arcade of Worth Avenue, including its distinctive "vias," narrow pedestrian alleys lined with shops and courtyards. Among his best-known works are El Mirasol for Edward T. Stotesbury and the buildings of the Everglades Club and the Bath & Tennis Club.

Boca Raton

In 1925, at the height of the Florida land boom, Mizner founded the Mizner Development Corporation to build a complete resort city at Boca Raton, planning a grand hotel (the Cloister Inn, now part of the Boca Raton resort), canals in the Venetian style, golf courses, and thousands of homes. The project sold tens of millions of dollars in lots amid frenzied speculation, but the collapse of the Florida land boom in 1926 and the devastating hurricane of that year ruined the enterprise; the corporation went into receivership in 1927, and Mizner was nearly bankrupted.

Later life and legacy

Mizner continued designing smaller projects in his final years and died in Palm Beach in 1933. His architecture — dismissed by modernist critics in the mid-20th century — was later reassessed as a foundational influence on Florida's Mediterranean Revival style. Many Mizner buildings are listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and Boca Raton, whose center he laid out, preserves numerous Mizner-designed structures.
