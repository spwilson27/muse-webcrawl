Wrasses are among the most diverse and colourful families of reef fishes.

The wrasses are a family, Labridae, of marine ray-finned fishes, many of them strikingly coloured, found in coastal waters of the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific Oceans. With roughly 600 species in more than 80 genera, the Labridae is one of the largest families of marine fishes. Most wrasses are small — under 20 cm — but the family also includes the humphead wrasse, which exceeds 2 metres and is among the largest reef fishes. Parrotfishes and the odacines are closely related and are often grouped within or beside the family.

Anatomy and feeding

Wrasses typically have elongate bodies, thick fleshy lips, and protrusible mouths with forward-pointing teeth; a second set of teeth in the throat (pharyngeal jaws) functions like a mortar and pestle to crush hard prey. Most are carnivores feeding on molluscs, crustaceans, sea urchins and other invertebrates picked from rock and reef. They swim primarily with their pectoral fins in a distinctive "rowing" motion. Several species have been observed using tools — carrying shellfish to a particular rock and smashing them against it to break them open — a behaviour documented in the blackeye thicklip wrasse and others.

Life cycle

Nearly all wrasses are protogynous hermaphrodites: individuals mature as females and the largest, most dominant may transform into brightly coloured "terminal phase" males, which often defend harems. Colour patterns change dramatically with sex and age, so much so that different phases of one species were historically described as separate species. Many bury themselves in sand at night or secrete a mucus cocoon to sleep, masking their scent from predators.

Cleaner wrasses and human use

The small bluestreak cleaner wrasse and its relatives are famous for cleaning symbiosis: they set up stations where larger fish queue to have parasites and dead skin picked off, and are rarely eaten by the predators they service. Wrasses are popular aquarium fishes, though many outgrow home tanks; several species are valued food fish in the Mediterranean and elsewhere. Cleaner wrasses are also farmed for use in salmon aquaculture, where they eat sea lice. The humphead wrasse, heavily fished for the live reef food trade, is listed as endangered.
