Slave Coast of West Africa

The Slave Coast is a historical name for the coastal region of West Africa along the Bight of Benin, roughly corresponding to the modern coastlines of Togo, Benin, and western Nigeria between the Volta River and the Lagos lagoon. European traders named stretches of this coast for their principal exports — the Gold Coast, the Ivory Coast, and here slaves — during the era of the Atlantic slave trade.

History

From the seventeenth to the nineteenth century, the Slave Coast was one of the most important sources of enslaved Africans for the Americas; an estimated two million people were embarked from its ports. The kingdom of Dahomey, the Yoruba Oyo Empire, and city-states and kingdoms such as Allada, Whydah (Ouidah), Porto-Novo, and Badagry supplied captives taken in warfare and raids to European trading posts. Ouidah became one of the busiest slave ports in Africa, served by Portuguese, French, British, and Dutch forts, and a major route led inland nine kilometres from the beach — now memorialized as the Route des Esclaves, ending at the "Door of No Return."

Trade and its end

Europeans bartered guns, textiles, cowrie shells, and metalware for captives held in barracoons before the Middle Passage to Brazil, the Caribbean, and North America. Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807 and pressured other nations to follow, but illegal trading continued for decades; the region's commerce gradually shifted to "legitimate" exports such as palm oil. The memory of the trade remains central to the heritage of Benin, Nigeria, and the African diaspora, including Afro-Brazilian returnee communities in the port towns.
