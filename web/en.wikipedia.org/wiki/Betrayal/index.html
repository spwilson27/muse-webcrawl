Betrayal

Betrayal is the breaking or violation of a presumptive contract, trust, or confidence that produces moral and psychological conflict within a relationship among individuals, between organisations, or between individuals and organisations. Someone who betrays others is commonly called a traitor or betrayer. Betrayal is also a commonly used plot device in fiction and is often associated with surprise revelations.

Definition and psychology

Betrayal requires that one party has expectations of loyalty, honesty, or support grounded in a relationship, and that the other party acts contrary to those expectations. Philosophers Judith Shklar and Peter Johnson have argued that betrayal is a distinctively relational wrong: the harm lies not only in the act itself but in the violation of trust placed in the betrayer. Psychologists study betrayal trauma, the distress that follows when a person or institution on whom one depends violates that trust, as in cases of infidelity, abuse, or institutional misconduct.

In history and culture

Famous betrayals recur throughout history and literature: Judas Iscariot's betrayal of Jesus for thirty pieces of silver; Brutus's role in the assassination of Julius Caesar; Benedict Arnold's defection during the American Revolutionary War. In Dante's Inferno, traitors occupy the ninth and lowest circle of Hell, frozen in ice — reflecting the medieval view of betrayal as the gravest of sins. Betrayal in the political and legal sphere overlaps with treason and espionage, while in everyday life it covers broken confidences, broken promises, and infidelity.
