Sabot (shoe)

A sabot is a clog from France or a neighbouring country such as Belgium or the Netherlands, typically carved from a single piece of wood and worn by peasants, farmers and workers. The French word sabot gives us the word sabotage: popular accounts link it to workers throwing their wooden shoes into machinery, though etymologists more often trace the term to the clatter of sabots and the notion of bungling work — to work "as if in sabots".

History and manufacture

Wooden footwear has been worn in Europe since the Middle Ages. Traditional sabots were hollowed out of willow, poplar, alder or beech by specialist makers called sabotiers, who often lived in forest workshops near their timber supply. A pair could be shaped in a few hours with auger, chisel and knife. Regional styles varied: Breton sabots were often painted or decorated, while Dutch klompen were left plain or lacquered.

Use

Sabots were cheap, warm and waterproof, protecting feet in mud, manure and cold factory floors. They were standard dress for agricultural labourers in France, Belgium and the Netherlands into the early twentieth century, and were worn by miners and factory workers; in Lancashire and other parts of industrial England the related wooden-soled clog served the same purpose. Today sabots survive as garden shoes, folk-dance costume and tourist souvenirs.
