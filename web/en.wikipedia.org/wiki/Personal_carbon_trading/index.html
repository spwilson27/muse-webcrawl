Personal carbon trading

Personal carbon trading is a set of proposed emissions trading schemes under which emissions credits are allocated to adult individuals on a (broadly) equal per capita basis, within national carbon budgets. Individuals surrender credits when buying fuel or electricity, and those who use less than their allocation can sell their surplus to those who need more. Personal carbon trading is an extension of downstream emissions trading to cover emissions from the domestic and personal transport sectors.

Proposals

Several specific schemes have been proposed. Tradable Energy Quotas (TEQs), developed by David Fleming from the mid-1990s, allocate units to all adults and require organizations to buy units at auction within a national budget that declines year by year. Personal Carbon Allowances (PCAs), proposed by Mayer Hillman, would allocate allowances equally to adults to cover household energy and personal travel. Other variants include Cap and Share, in which permits are allocated to citizens who sell them upstream to fuel suppliers.

Policy interest

In the United Kingdom, the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs conducted a pre-feasibility study in 2006–2008, concluding that personal carbon trading had potential but was ahead of its time, citing high implementation costs and public acceptability concerns. The idea has continued to attract academic and political interest as a way of engaging individuals directly with a national carbon budget and of distributing the costs of climate change mitigation equitably.

Criticism

Critics argue that personal carbon trading would be expensive to administer, intrusive, and unfair to people with high unavoidable emissions, such as rural residents or those in poorly insulated housing. Supporters respond that equal per-capita allocation is progressive, because wealthier individuals typically have larger carbon footprints.
