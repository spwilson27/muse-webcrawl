Endogenous viral element

An endogenous viral element (EVE) is a DNA sequence derived from a virus that is present within the germline genome of a non-viral organism. EVEs can be complete viral genomes, as in proviruses, or fragments of viral genomes generated during the process of viral integration. They arise when a viral DNA sequence becomes integrated into the chromosome of a germ cell that goes on to produce a viable organism, and the integrated sequence survives as a fixed allele within the host population.

Endogenous retroviruses

The best-known EVEs are endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), which derive from retroviruses that obligately integrate a DNA copy of their RNA genome into the host chromosome. About 8% of the human genome consists of ERV-derived sequences. Over time most ERVs accumulate mutations, deletions and rearrangements, becoming non-infectious genomic fossils, although some retain open reading frames and biological activity.

Non-retroviral EVEs

Since the 2010s, sensitive homology searches have revealed EVEs derived from many non-retroviral RNA and DNA virus families — including bornaviruses, filoviruses, parvoviruses, circoviruses and hepadnaviruses — in the genomes of animals, plants and fungi. RNA viruses lacking an integration step are thought to be integrated by host retrotransposon machinery or by chromosomal repair mechanisms acting on viral DNA intermediates.

Significance

EVEs provide a "fossil record" of ancient viruses, allowing palaeovirologists to date viral lineages and reconstruct long-term virus–host coevolution. Some EVE-derived genes have been co-opted (exapted) by hosts: the mammalian syncytin genes, essential for placenta formation, derive from retroviral envelope genes, and some arthropods use EVE sequences to generate antiviral immune RNAs.
