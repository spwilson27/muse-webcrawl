History of photography

The history of photography spans the chemical and mechanical processes for producing durable images, from the camera obscura of antiquity through the first permanent photographs of the 1820s to film, colour, and digital imaging.

First photographs

About 1826–27, Joseph Nicéphore Niépce produced the earliest surviving camera photograph, View from the Window at Le Gras, using a bitumen-coated pewter plate. His partner Louis Daguerre went on to develop the daguerreotype, announced to the public in 1839, the same year William Henry Fox Talbot presented his paper-negative calotype process, the ancestor of modern negative–positive photography.

19th-century developments

The wet collodion process (1851) improved sharpness and reproducibility; gelatin dry plates in the 1870s freed photographers from portable darkrooms. George Eastman's Kodak camera (1888) and roll film brought photography to amateurs. Colour processes culminated commercially in Autochrome (1907) and Kodachrome (1935).

20th century and digital era

35 mm rangefinder cameras, single-lens reflexes and photojournalism defined the mid-century, while Edwin Land's Polaroid system (1948) offered instant images. Digital sensors — beginning with Steven Sasson's 1975 prototype at Kodak — displaced film in the 2000s, and camera phones made photography ubiquitous, transforming visual culture and journalism.
