Southwestern Brittonic languages

The Southwestern Brittonic languages are the Brittonic Celtic languages descended from the variety of Common Brittonic spoken in southwestern Britain — the historical region of Dumnonia — which diverged from the variety that gave rise to Welsh. The group consists of Breton, spoken in Brittany, and Cornish, spoken in Cornwall. The two remained mutually intelligible in the early medieval period and are distinguished from Welsh and Cumbric principally by sound changes, most famously the assibilation of certain consonants and the development of /ø/ and other front rounded vowels.

Development

Common Brittonic was spoken across most of Britain south of the Firth of Forth at the end of Roman rule. After the Anglo-Saxon settlement of the 5th and 6th centuries split the Brittonic-speaking world, the southwestern variety developed distinctive features. Brittonic emigrants from the southwest carried the language to Armorica (Brittany) between roughly the 4th and 7th centuries, where it became Breton. Old Breton and Old Cornish remain extremely close in the earliest glosses; the languages diverged more clearly in their Middle periods as Cornish underwent further vowel changes.

Key features

The defining innovation of Southwestern Brittonic is the assibilation of Common Brittonic */t/ and */k/ in certain positions — for example, Brittonic *budd "triumph" gives Old Cornish budy and Breton bud but Welsh budd, while "new" appears as Cornish nowyth and Breton nevez versus Welsh newydd. Breton preserved front rounded vowels such as /ø/ (written eu). Later, Middle Cornish developed its own distinctive changes including pre-occlusion of /m/ and /n/ in some dialects.

Status

Cornish ceased to be a community vernacular in the 18th century — Dolly Pentreath (d. 1777) is often cited as the last fluent native speaker — but was revived in the 20th century and is now taught in schools and recognized under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages. Breton, though spoken by some 200,000 people, is classified by UNESCO as severely endangered, with most speakers elderly, though immersion schools (Diwan) sustain a younger generation of speakers.
