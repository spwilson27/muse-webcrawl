Ice wine

Ice wine (or icewine; German: Eiswein) is a type of dessert wine produced from grapes that have been frozen while still on the vine. Freezing concentrates the sugars and other dissolved solids in the grape, so that a small quantity of intensely sweet, concentrated wine can be pressed from the frozen fruit; the water remains behind as ice crystals in the press.

Grapes left to freeze naturally on the vine, harvested at temperatures of −8 °C or colder.

Production

True ice wine requires a hard freeze after the grapes have ripened, so grapes are left on the vine under netting for months and picked by hand, often at night, at temperatures typically −8 °C (18 °F) or below in Canada and Germany. The must is extremely concentrated, ferments slowly over months, and yields a wine with high residual sugar balanced by high acidity, usually 9–12% alcohol. Because yields are tiny — a single vine may produce only a glass — ice wine commands premium prices.

Regions

Germany, where Eiswein has been made since the late 18th century (traditionally from Riesling), is the historic home; Austria also produces it. Canada — especially Ontario's Niagara Peninsula, where winters are reliably cold — became the world's largest producer, using Vidal Blanc and Riesling under VQA rules requiring natural freezing; Inniskillin's 1989 Vidal icewine won the Grand Prix d'Honneur at Vinexpo in 1991, bringing international fame. Artificial freezing of picked grapes ("cryoextraction") produces "icebox wines", which in many jurisdictions may not be labelled ice wine.
