Arthur Holly Compton (1892–1962)

Arthur Holly Compton (September 10, 1892 – March 15, 1962) was an American physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1927 for his discovery of the Compton effect, which demonstrated the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation.

Compton effect

Working at Washington University in St. Louis in 1922–23, Compton observed that X-rays scattered from graphite were shifted to longer wavelengths, by an amount depending on the scattering angle. He explained the shift by treating the X-ray as a particle (photon) colliding elastically with an electron, transferring momentum to it — strong evidence for Einstein's light-quantum hypothesis. He shared the 1927 Nobel Prize with C. T. R. Wilson.

Later career

Compton moved to the University of Chicago in 1923, led worldwide surveys of cosmic rays in the 1930s that established their latitude dependence, and became a leading administrator of the Manhattan Project, directing the Metallurgical Laboratory that produced the first controlled nuclear chain reaction under Enrico Fermi in December 1942. After the war he served as chancellor of Washington University in St. Louis.
