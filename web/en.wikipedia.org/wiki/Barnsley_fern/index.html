The Barnsley fern is a fractal named after the British mathematician Michael Barnsley, who first described it in his book Fractals Everywhere (1988). It is a famous example of an iterated function system (IFS): four simple affine transformations, chosen at random with set probabilities, generate a point set that closely resembles the frond of a black spleenwort fern.

Construction

The fern is drawn by a "chaos game". Starting from a point at the origin, one of four transformations is applied repeatedly, with probabilities of 1%, 85%, 7% and 7%. The first transformation generates the stem; the second, used most of the time, produces successively smaller leaflets along the frond; the third and fourth draw the largest left- and right-hand leaflets. After many thousands of iterations, plotted points trace out the complete fern, self-similar at every scale: each leaflet is itself a miniature fern.

Formula

The four affine maps have the form f(x, y) = (ax + by + e, cx + dy + f). The stem transformation compresses points onto a vertical segment; the dominant transformation rotates and scales the whole figure slightly, placing the next leaflet; and the two remaining transformations mirror the figure to seed the lowest pair of leaflets. Changing the coefficients produces related plants — Barnsley and others generated whole families of fractal ferns, trees and leaves, and variants modelled on real species such as Asplenium adiantum-nigrum.

Significance

The Barnsley fern became a popular emblem of fractal geometry and of image compression research, demonstrating that intricate natural forms can be encoded by a handful of numbers. It is frequently used as a first programming exercise in computer graphics courses.
