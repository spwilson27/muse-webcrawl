Cantabri

The Cantabri were a pre-Roman Celtic people who inhabited the northern coast of Hispania, in the mountainous country corresponding roughly to the modern regions of Cantabria, eastern Asturias, northern Burgos and Palencia, and part of León. Their name survives in the modern autonomous community of Cantabria and in the Cantabrian Sea (the Roman Mare Cantabricum), the southern part of the Bay of Biscay.

Society

Classical authors describe the Cantabri as hardy highlanders organised in tribes and clans, living in fortified hilltop settlements (castros) and practising a pastoral economy supplemented by raiding their neighbours; Strabo famously remarked on their customs, including a form of matrilineal inheritance. Their principal settlements included Vellica and Aracillum. They spoke a Celtic language and worshipped gods later identified with Roman deities.

Cantabrian Wars

The Cantabri (with their Astures neighbours) were the last people of the Iberian Peninsula to resist Rome. The Cantabrian Wars (29–19 BC) required the personal presence of the emperor Augustus and the deployment of several legions. The campaigns were notoriously brutal: according to literary sources, captured Cantabri chose suicide over slavery, and mothers killed their children rather than surrender them. After their defeat the territory was incorporated into Hispania Tarraconensis, and Cantabrian auxiliaries later served in the Roman army.

The Cantabri are said to have fought with a distinctive round shield (caetra) and to have given their name to the military formation known as the circulus cantabricus, a cavalry manoeuvre described by Roman writers.
