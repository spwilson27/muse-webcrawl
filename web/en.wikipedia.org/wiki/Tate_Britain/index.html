Tate Britain, known from 1897 to 1932 as the National Gallery of British Art and from 1932 to 2000 as the Tate Gallery, is an art museum on Millbank in the City of Westminster, London. It is part of the Tate network of galleries in England, alongside Tate Modern, Tate Liverpool and Tate St Ives. It houses the national collection of British art from 1500 to the present day and is the world's largest collection of its kind.

History

The gallery was founded with a gift from sugar magnate Sir Henry Tate, who offered his collection of British art and funds for a building. It opened in 1897 on the site of the former Millbank Penitentiary. The building, designed by Sidney R. J. Smith, has been extended repeatedly, including the Clore Gallery of 1987, designed by James Stirling to house the Turner Bequest. When Tate Modern opened in 2000, the Millbank gallery was renamed Tate Britain and refocused on British art, while modern international works moved to the new site.

Collection

The collection spans Tudor portraiture through the Pre-Raphaelites, the landscapes of Constable and Turner — whose bequeathed works form its centrepiece — and on to contemporary artists. Major holdings include works by William Hogarth, Thomas Gainsborough, Joshua Reynolds, John Singer Sargent, Lucian Freud, Francis Bacon and David Hockney. The gallery hosts the annual Turner Prize exhibition in most years, alternating with other Tate venues.

Building and refurbishment

A major renovation completed in 2013, designed by Caruso St John, restored the grand entrance, rotunda and members' facilities, and reordered displays in a chronological circuit around the building. Displays are free to enter, as with other UK national museums.
