Building society

A building society is a financial institution owned by its members as a mutual organisation, offering banking and related financial services, especially savings accounts and mortgage lending. Building societies exist in the United Kingdom, Australia, and other countries. The term first arose in the 18th century in Great Britain from working men's cooperative savings groups: by pooling savings, members could finance the building of houses, and once every member had a house the society could dissolve. Modern societies are permanent institutions that accept deposits from the general public.

History

The first building society was Ketley's Building Society, founded in Birmingham, England, in 1775. Nineteenth-century "terminating" societies gave way to permanent societies, and by the early 20th century building societies had become the principal source of mortgage finance for British home buyers. The Building Societies Act 1986 expanded their powers but also opened the way to demutualisation: beginning with Abbey National in 1989, many large societies converted to banks and were listed on the stock market, and most were later absorbed by larger banking groups.

Mutual status

Unlike banks, building societies are owned by their savers and borrowers rather than by external shareholders; members are entitled to vote at annual general meetings on a one-member-one-vote basis. Societies that remained mutual, such as Nationwide — the largest, formed from many mergers — argue that mutuality allows them to offer better rates because no profits are paid out as dividends. UK societies are regulated by the Prudential Regulation Authority and the Financial Conduct Authority and are legally required to raise at least half of their funds from member deposits and to hold the bulk of their assets in residential mortgage lending.

Outside the UK

Australia developed a similar building-society sector in the 19th century; many societies converted to banks from the 1980s onward. In the United States, savings and loan associations performed a comparable role, while credit unions share the mutual principle.
