Cryogenics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, cryogenics is the production of, and behaviour of materials at, very low temperatures. There is no single agreed boundary, but the field is generally taken to concern temperatures below about −150 °C (123 K), at which common gases such as air liquefy. Common cryogenic fluids include liquid nitrogen (77 K), liquid oxygen (90 K), liquid hydrogen (20 K) and liquid helium (4.2 K).

History

The science developed in the late 19th century through the liquefaction of gases: oxygen and nitrogen were liquefied in the 1870s–80s by workers including Louis Paul Cailletet and Karol Olszewski; James Dewar liquefied hydrogen in 1898 and invented the vacuum flask that bears his name; and Heike Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium in 1908, opening the way to his discovery of superconductivity in 1911. Carl von Linde and others commercialised air separation for industrial gases.

Methods

Refrigeration to cryogenic temperatures is achieved with cycles based on the Joule–Thomson effect (as in the Linde–Hampson cycle), turbo-expanders, and, for the lowest temperatures, helium dilution refrigerators and adiabatic demagnetisation, which reach millikelvin and microkelvin ranges respectively.

Applications

  • Industrial gases and storage: liquefaction, transport and storage of gases, and LNG shipping.
  • Rocketry: liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen propellants.
  • Medicine and biology: cryosurgery, cryopreservation of blood, cells and tissues, and superconducting magnets for MRI scanners.
  • Research: superconducting magnets in particle accelerators, infrared astronomy detectors, and quantum computing hardware operated at millikelvin temperatures.
  • Food: rapid freezing with liquid nitrogen.

Cryogenics should not be confused with cryonics, the speculative preservation of deceased people at low temperature in the hope of future revival.
