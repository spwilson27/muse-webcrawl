Classical Gardens of Suzhou

The Classical Gardens of Suzhou are a group of scholar's gardens in the city of Suzhou, Jiangsu, China, inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. Four gardens — the Humble Administrator's Garden, the Lingering Garden, the Master of the Nets Garden, and the Mountain Villa with Embracing Beauty — were inscribed in 1997, and five more were added in 2000: the Couple's Retreat Garden, the Garden of Cultivation, the Great Wave Pavilion, the Lion Grove Garden, and the Retreat and Reflection Garden. UNESCO describes them as masterpieces of Chinese landscape garden design, in which art, nature, and architecture are integrated so as to create "beauty based on the harmony of man and nature".

History

Garden-building in Suzhou stretches back some 2,000 years, and flourished under the Ming and Qing dynasties, when wealthy officials, merchants, and scholars retired to the prosperous canal city and built private gardens as places of contemplation and cultivation. At their height there were more than 200 gardens within the city walls. The surviving gardens embody an aesthetic philosophy drawn from Confucian, Taoist, and Buddhist ideas: asymmetrical layouts, borrowed scenery, winding paths that reveal views gradually, artificial mountains of Taihu rock, ponds, pavilions, and plantings chosen for seasonal and symbolic effect.

The gardens

  • Humble Administrator's Garden (Zhuozheng Yuan), built in the early sixteenth century for the retired censor Wang Xianchen, is the largest (about 5.2 hectares) and regarded as one of the four most famous gardens of China.
  • Lingering Garden (Liuyuan), founded in 1593, is celebrated for its covered walkway, ornamental stones, and the elaborate Guanyun ("Cloud-Capped") rockery.
  • Master of the Nets Garden (Wangshi Yuan), the smallest of the group, is a model of compact design, with a residential quarter and garden arranged around a central pond.
  • Mountain Villa with Embracing Beauty (Huanxiu Shanzhuang) is famed for its rockery by the Qing master Ge Yuliang, considered one of the finest artificial mountains in China.
  • Lion Grove Garden (Shizilin), founded in 1342 by a Zen monk, is famous for its labyrinthine Taihu rock grottoes resembling lions.
  • Great Wave Pavilion (Canglang Ting), the oldest surviving Suzhou garden site (Song dynasty, 1044), was built for the poet Su Shunqin.

Preservation

Many gardens were damaged during the Taiping Rebellion and the wars of the twentieth century; they were restored by the People's Republic from the 1950s onward and opened to the public. The Suzhou gardens have profoundly influenced garden design across East Asia and are studied as the archetype of the Chinese scholar's garden.
