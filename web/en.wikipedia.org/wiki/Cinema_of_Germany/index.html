Cinema of Germany

The cinema of Germany has made major technical and artistic contributions to film history, from the expressionist classics of the Weimar era — The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927) — through the director-driven New German Cinema of the 1970s to internationally successful post-reunification films such as Run Lola Run (1998) and The Lives of Others (2006).

Weimar cinema

After the First World War, the UFA studio consolidated much of German production. Expressionist films used stylised sets and lighting to explore madness and authority; Lang and Murnau emigrated to Hollywood after 1933, carrying the style into film noir. Under the Nazi regime the industry was absorbed into the state propaganda apparatus, producing works such as Leni Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will (1935).

Divided Germany and New German Cinema

After 1945, East Germany's DEFA studio and West Germany's commercial industry developed separately. In 1962 the Oberhausen Manifesto declared "Papas Kino ist tot" ("Papa's cinema is dead") and launched the New German Cinema: directors including Alexander Kluge, Volker Schlöndorff, Margarethe von Trotta, Fassbinder, Herzog, and Wenders won international recognition in the 1970s.

Since reunification

Post-1990 hits include Good Bye, Lenin! (2003), Downfall (2004), The Lives of Others (2006, Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film), and Toni Erdmann (2016). The Berlinale, founded in 1951, remains one of the "Big Three" European film festivals.
