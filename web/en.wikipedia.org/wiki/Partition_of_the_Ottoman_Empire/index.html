Partition of the Ottoman Empire

The partition of the Ottoman Empire (30 October 1918 – 1 November 1922) was the geopolitical process by which the defeated Ottoman Empire was dismantled after the First World War, its territories in the Middle East divided between the victorious Allied powers and new national states. The process began with the Armistice of Mudros, proceeded through the occupation of Constantinople and the abortive Treaty of Sèvres (1920), and was reshaped by the Turkish War of Independence, ending with the abolition of the sultanate and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923).

Wartime agreements

During the war the Allies concluded secret arrangements for the empire's division, including the Constantinople Agreement (1915) promising Russia the Straits, the Sykes–Picot Agreement (1916) dividing the Arab provinces between Britain and France, the Balfour Declaration (1917) supporting a Jewish national home in Palestine, and promises to Sharif Hussein of Mecca in the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence — commitments whose tensions shaped the postwar Middle East.

Occupation and Sèvres

Allied forces occupied Constantinople from November 1918. The Treaty of Sèvres (August 1920) stripped the empire of its Arab provinces as League of Nations mandates (Palestine and Iraq to Britain; Syria and Lebanon to France), proposed an Armenian state, internationalised the Straits, and left a rump Turkey, but it was never ratified and was rejected by the Turkish national movement under Mustafa Kemal.

Lausanne and aftermath

Victory in the Turkish War of Independence forced renegotiation. The Treaty of Lausanne (July 1923) recognised the Republic of Turkey within roughly its modern borders, and the sultanate was abolished on 1 November 1922, the caliphate in 1924. The mandate system in Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, and Transjordan, and the unresolved status of Mosul and Alexandretta, left legacies that continue to influence the region.
