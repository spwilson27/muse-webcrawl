Climate finance

Climate finance is an industrial in-between space between finance and climate change. It refers to local, national, or transnational financing—drawn from public, private, and alternative sources of financing—that seeks to support mitigation and adaptation actions addressing climate change. The term is used in the context of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), whose financial mechanism channels funding to developing countries.

Definitions

The UNFCCC Standing Committee on Finance defines climate finance as finance that aims at reducing emissions, enhancing sinks of greenhouse gases, and reducing the vulnerability of, and maintaining and increasing the resilience of, human and ecological systems to negative climate change impacts. There is no single agreed definition, and estimates of flows vary accordingly.

Multilateral channels

Multilateral climate funds include the Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Global Environment Facility (GEF), the Climate Investment Funds, and the Adaptation Fund. These funds receive contributions from developed countries and disburse grants and concessional loans. Multilateral development banks, such as the World Bank, are also major intermediaries of climate finance.

The $100 billion goal

At the 2009 Copenhagen Summit, developed countries committed to jointly mobilize $100 billion per year by 2020 to address the needs of developing countries. The goal was formally met in 2022 according to OECD estimates, two years late. At COP29 in 2024, parties agreed a new collective quantified goal of at least $300 billion per year by 2035, with developed countries taking the lead.

Private finance

The bulk of tracked climate finance is private, dominated by corporate and household investment in solar energy, wind power, and energy-efficient transport. Public finance remains crucial for adaptation, which attracts little private capital because resilience projects often lack direct revenue streams.

Loss and damage

A dedicated fund for responding to loss and damage was agreed at COP27 in 2022 and operationalized at COP28 in 2023, marking the first multilateral fund intended to help countries cope with climate impacts that cannot be adapted to.
