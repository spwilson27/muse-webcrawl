Taro (Colocasia esculenta) is a root vegetable cultivated for its edible starchy corm. It is one of the earliest cultivated plants, with a history stretching back thousands of years in tropical Asia, and it spread across the Pacific with Austronesian voyagers—one of the "canoe plants" carried to Hawaiʻi and New Zealand. In Hawaiʻi, taro (kalo) is pounded into poi and holds deep cultural and spiritual significance.

Cultivation

Taro grows in flooded paddies (wetland taro) or in moist upland soils. The corms are harvested after 7–12 months. Major producers include Nigeria, China, Ghana, and Pacific island nations. In the Philippines, taro leaves are cooked in coconut milk as laing, a classic dish of Philippine cuisine.

Toxicity and preparation

Raw taro is toxic due to calcium oxalate raphides, which cause intense irritation; thorough cooking destroys the hazard. The leaves are also eaten after cooking, as in Hawaiian lūʻau and Filipino laing.

Culinary uses

Taro is used in savory dishes, desserts, and drinks worldwide: poi in Hawaiʻi, taro cakes and taro bubble tea (boba) in Taiwan, taro ice cream, chips, and the Caribbean dish callaloo. Its naturally purple-flecked flesh colors many desserts.
