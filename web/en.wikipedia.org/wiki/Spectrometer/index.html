Spectrometer

A spectrometer is a scientific instrument used to separate and measure the components of a physical phenomenon as a function of a variable such as wavelength, mass, or energy. The term is used in several fields:

  • In optics, an optical spectrometer separates light into its constituent wavelengths and measures their intensities, producing a spectrum.
  • In mass spectrometry, a mass spectrometer separates ions according to their mass-to-charge ratio.
  • In particle and nuclear physics, spectrometers measure properties such as the momentum or energy of particles, often using magnetic fields.

Optical spectrometers

An optical spectrometer typically consists of an entrance slit, a collimator that makes the incoming light parallel, a dispersive element — a prism, as in the instruments developed by Kirchhoff and Bunsen in the 19th century, or more commonly a diffraction grating — focusing optics, and a detector such as a CCD array. The instrument records intensity as a function of wavelength. Key performance characteristics include spectral range, spectral resolution, and stray-light rejection. Spectrophotometers additionally measure the absorption or transmission of a sample relative to a reference.

Optical spectrometers are used throughout science and industry: in astronomy to determine the composition, temperature, and motion of stars and galaxies; in chemistry for identifying substances from their spectral fingerprints; in environmental monitoring, food analysis, forensics, and the calibration of displays and lighting.

Mass spectrometers

A mass spectrometer ionises chemical species, accelerates them, and deflects them in electric or magnetic fields so that they can be sorted by mass-to-charge ratio and counted. Modern variants include time-of-flight, quadrupole, ion trap, and Fourier-transform instruments, with resolutions sufficient to determine exact molecular formulae. Mass spectrometry is indispensable in proteomics, drug development, isotope analysis, and space science.

History

The foundations of spectrometry were laid in the early 19th century when Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped the dark lines in the solar spectrum, and advanced decisively when Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen showed around 1860 that each chemical element produces a characteristic spectrum, enabling the discovery of new elements such as caesium and rubidium and launching astrophysical spectroscopy.
