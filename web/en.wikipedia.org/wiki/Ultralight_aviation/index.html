Ultralight aviation

Ultralight aviation is the flying of lightweight, 1- or 2-seat fixed-wing aircraft, powered hang gliders ("trikes"), powered parachutes and similar minimal machines. The category is defined differently in different countries, but all definitions limit weight, stall speed and fuel capacity so that the aircraft present minimal risk to third parties and can be regulated lightly. In much of Europe the equivalent term is microlight.

History

The modern ultralight movement began in the late 1970s, when hang-glider enthusiasts began adding small engines to foot-launched gliders. In the United States the FAA's Part 103 regulations of 1982 created a legal category for single-seat vehicles under 254 lb (115 kg) empty weight, requiring no licence or medical certificate. Manufacturers such as Quicksilver and Eipper produced simple tube-and-fabric kits that made powered flight cheap and accessible, though the early industry also suffered a poor safety record that led to improved training and two-seat exemptions for instruction.

Types

  • Weight-shift (trikes): a powered unit suspended beneath a Rogallo-type flexible wing, controlled by shifting weight via a control bar.
  • Three-axis microlights: conventional small aeroplanes with full aerodynamic controls, increasingly sophisticated and fast.
  • Powered parachutes and paramotors: a paraglider wing with a wheeled cart or a backpack engine.
  • Electric ultralights: a developing category using battery-electric propulsion.

Regulation

Definitions vary: the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale classifies microlights as landplanes with a maximum take-off mass of 300 kg (single seat) or 450 kg (two seat), with higher limits in some national rules. Pilots generally require a microlight licence, though requirements are far lighter than for private pilot licences. In the US, two-seat ultralights fall under the light-sport aircraft rules introduced in 2004.
