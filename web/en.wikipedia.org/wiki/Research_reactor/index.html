Research reactor

Research reactors are nuclear fission reactors that serve primarily as a neutron source rather than to generate electricity. They are used for neutron scattering research, materials testing, neutron activation analysis, the production of radioisotopes for medicine and industry, and training. They are smaller and simpler than power reactors, usually operate at low pressure and temperature, and number in the low hundreds worldwide, down from a peak of several hundred during the Cold War.

Types

Common designs include pool-type reactors, in which the core sits in a large open pool of water (such as Australia's OPAL reactor), TRIGA reactors designed for inherent safety and pulsing, heavy-water reactors such as Canada's NRU (closed 2018), and high-flux reactors such as the Institut Laue–Langevin reactor in Grenoble, which supplies exceptionally intense neutron beams for scattering instruments. Beam tubes, hot sources and cold sources tailor the neutron spectrum for experiments.

Applications

Research reactors produce the bulk of the world's supply of molybdenum-99, parent of the diagnostic isotope technetium-99m, along with isotopes for radiotherapy. Their neutrons probe the structure of materials — from protein crystals to engineering alloys — and irradiate samples for forensic and geological dating work.

Fuel and non-proliferation

Many older research reactors were fuelled with highly enriched uranium (HEU), attractive to weapons programmes. Since 1978 the Reduced Enrichment for Research and Test Reactors (RERTR) programme has driven conversion to low-enriched uranium fuels, and shutdown of surplus reactors continues as facilities age.
