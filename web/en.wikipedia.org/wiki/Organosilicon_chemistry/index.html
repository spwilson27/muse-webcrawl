Organosilicon chemistry

Organosilicon chemistry is the study of organometallic compounds containing carbon–silicon bonds. Silicon sits directly below carbon in group 14 of the periodic table, and organosilicon compounds share some similarities with carbon chemistry while differing in important respects: the Si–C bond is longer and more polarised than C–C, and silicon forms an exceptionally strong bond to oxygen, which drives much of the field's useful chemistry. Most organosilicon compounds are colourless, flammable, hydrophobic and stable to air.

Classes of compounds

Major classes include silanes (e.g. tetramethylsilane, the NMR reference standard), silanols, siloxanes, silicones (polymeric siloxanes such as polydimethylsiloxane), silyl ethers used as protecting groups in organic synthesis, and silenes and disilynes bearing silicon multiple bonds, stabilised only in the late 20th century. Hydrosilylation — the addition of Si–H bonds across alkenes, catalysed by platinum — is a major industrial reaction.

History and production

The first organosilicon compounds were prepared by Edward Frankland and Charles Friedel in the 1860s. Systematic study began with Frederic S. Kipping in the early 1900s, who coined the term "silicone" (mistakenly by analogy with ketones). Industry expanded during the Second World War with the direct process of Eugene Rochow and Richard Müller (1940), in which methyl chloride reacts with silicon metal over a copper catalyst to give methylchlorosilanes, the feedstocks for silicone polymers.

Applications

Silicones are used as sealants, adhesives, lubricants, medical implants, cookware, insulation and cosmetics because of their thermal stability, water repellency and low toxicity. Organosilicon reagents are mainstays of organic synthesis — for example, silyl protecting groups, the Peterson olefination, and allylsilanes in the Hosomi–Sakurai reaction. Unlike carbon, silicon does not support a rich chemistry of stable multiple bonds under ordinary conditions, and speculations about silicon-based life remain hypothetical.
