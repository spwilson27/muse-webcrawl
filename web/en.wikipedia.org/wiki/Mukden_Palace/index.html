Mukden Palace

Mukden Palace

Location: Shenyang, Liaoning, China

Built: 1625–1636 (expanded 1780–1783)

Area: about 60,000 m², over 300 rooms

UNESCO World Heritage Site: inscribed 2004 as an extension of the Forbidden City listing

The Mukden Palace (Chinese: 盛京宫殿; pinyin: Shèngjīng Gōngdiàn), also known as the Shenyang Imperial Palace, is the former imperial palace of the early Qing dynasty in Shenyang (then called Mukden), Liaoning, China. Built between 1625 and 1636 by Nurhaci, founder of the Qing, and his son Hong Taiji, it served as the seat of the Qing court until the capital moved to Beijing in 1644, and afterwards as a regional palace used by Qing emperors on their eastern tours.

Layout

The palace occupies about 60,000 square metres and contains more than 300 rooms arranged in eastern, central and western sections. The eastern section, the earliest, is dominated by the Dazheng Hall (Hall of Great Affairs), set on a terrace and flanked by the octagonal pavilions of the Ten Banner Pavilions, reflecting the Manchu Eight Banners military organisation. The central section includes the Chongzheng Hall, where Hong Taiji conducted state business, and the raised palace quarters behind it, built on a platform in Manchu style. The western section was added by the Qianlong Emperor in 1780–1783 to house libraries, including a copy of the Siku Quanshu.

History and museum

The architecture blends Han Chinese, Manchu and Tibetan elements. After the fall of the Qing, the palace became a museum in 1926 (the Shenyang Palace Museum), one of the first museums in modern China. In 2004 UNESCO inscribed it on the World Heritage List as part of the "Imperial Palaces of the Ming and Qing Dynasties in Beijing and Shenyang."
