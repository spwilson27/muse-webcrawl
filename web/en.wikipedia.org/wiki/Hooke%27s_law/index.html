Hooke's law

Hooke's law is a law of physics that states that the force (F) needed to extend or compress a spring by some distance (x) scales linearly with respect to that distance: F = kx, where k is a constant factor characteristic of the spring (its stiffness) and x is small compared to the total possible deformation. The law is named after 17th-century British physicist Robert Hooke, who first stated it in 1676 as a Latin anagram and published the solution in 1678: ut tensio, sic vis — "as the extension, so the force".

Formal definition

For a linear spring, the restoring force is proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium. In continuum mechanics, Hooke's law generalises to the linear relation between stress and strain tensors, with the elastic moduli of the material as constants of proportionality.

Applications and limits

Hooke's law underlies spring scales, seismometers, and the theory of elasticity. It is an approximation valid only within the elastic limit of a material; beyond the proportional limit, materials deform nonlinearly or permanently.
