Diana is a goddess in Roman and Hellenistic religion, primarily considered a patroness of the countryside and nature, hunters, and childbirth. She is equated with the Greek goddess Artemis, and absorbed much of Artemis's mythology, but had distinct origins as an Italic deity. With Luna and Hecate she was sometimes worshipped as a triple goddess (Diana triformis).

Worship

Diana's most ancient sanctuary was at Lake Nemi, in the Alban Hills, where she was worshipped as Diana Nemorensis, "Diana of the Grove". Her priest there, the Rex Nemorensis ("King of the Grove"), was a runaway slave who won the office by killing his predecessor in single combat — a rite famously discussed in Frazer's The Golden Bough. Her temple on Rome's Aventine Hill, said to have been founded by Servius Tullius, was a centre for the plebeians and the Latin League. Her festival, the Nemoralia (13–15 August), was a holiday for slaves, when hunting dogs were garlanded and women washed and dressed their hair.

Mythology

As Artemis's counterpart, Diana is a virgin huntress attended by nymphs. In Ovid's Metamorphoses she transforms the hunter Actaeon into a stag after he stumbles upon her bathing; his own hounds tear him apart. She also figures in the stories of Callisto, turned into a bear, and of Niobe, whose children Apollo and Diana slew.

Legacy

Diana remains a central figure in some modern Neopagan and Wiccan traditions, and her name and imagery recur in art, astronomy (the asteroid 78 Diana) and popular culture.
