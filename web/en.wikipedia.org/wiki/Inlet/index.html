Inlet

An inlet is a (usually long and narrow) indentation of a shoreline, such as a small arm, cove, bay, sound, fjord, lagoon or marsh, that leads to an enclosed larger body of water such as a lake, estuary, gulf or marginal sea. In sea coasts, the term "inlet" usually refers to the actual connection between a bay and the ocean and is often called an "entrance" or a recession in the shore.

Formation

Inlets form through several processes. Coastal inlets on barrier islands are cut by storms and maintained by tidal currents that scour the channel between the ocean and a back-barrier lagoon. Other inlets are drowned river valleys (rias), glacial troughs deepened below sea level (fjords), or channels eroded along fault lines and weaker rock strata. Because tidal flow concentrates through a narrow opening, inlets often have strong currents and shifting sandbars that make navigation hazardous.

Examples

Notable inlets include Cook Inlet in Alaska, a long arm of the Gulf of Alaska with some of the largest tidal ranges in the world; Burrard Inlet in British Columbia, around which the city of Vancouver developed; and the many tidal inlets along the Atlantic coast of the United States, such as Fire Island Inlet and Ocean City Inlet. Golden Gate, the strait connecting San Francisco Bay to the Pacific Ocean, functions as the bay's inlet.

Engineering

Many inlets are stabilised with jetties to maintain navigation channels, and some have been artificially created or enlarged by dredging. Inlet dynamics — the exchange of water, sediment and nutrients between coastal lagoons and the open sea — are an important subject in coastal engineering and estuarine ecology.
