Ossification

Ossification
SynonymsOsteogenesis, bone formation
TypesIntramembranous; endochondral
Key cellsOsteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts

Ossification (also called osteogenesis or bone formation) is the process by which new bone tissue is laid down during skeletal development and growth. In vertebrate embryos, bone is produced by two distinct mechanisms: intramembranous ossification, in which bone forms directly within condensed mesenchyme, and endochondral ossification, in which a hyaline cartilage model is gradually replaced by bone. Ossification continues after birth at the growth plates until skeletal maturity, and bone is remodelled throughout life.

Intramembranous ossification

In intramembranous ossification, mesenchymal cells differentiate directly into osteoblasts, which secrete an unmineralised matrix called osteoid that subsequently mineralises around collagen fibres. This process forms most of the flat bones of the skull, the mandible and much of the clavicle. The point where ossification begins is called a primary ossification centre.

Endochondral ossification

Endochondral ossification forms the long bones of the limbs, the vertebrae and the base of the skull. A cartilage template is first produced by chondrocytes; the cartilage calcifies, blood vessels invade, and osteoblasts deposit bone on the calcified scaffold. In long bones the primary ossification centre appears in the diaphysis (shaft) during fetal life, while secondary centres appear later in the epiphyses (ends). Between them lies the epiphyseal (growth) plate, where cartilage proliferation and replacement lengthen the bone until the plate closes in late adolescence.

Timeline in humans

The first bones to ossify in the human embryo are the clavicle and the bones of the jaw, beginning around the sixth to eighth week of development. By birth, the shafts of the long bones are largely ossified, while the ends remain cartilaginous. Many secondary centres appear during childhood, and the last growth plates—such as those of the clavicle—may not fuse until the mid-twenties.

Disorders

Defects of ossification underlie several skeletal dysplasias. In osteogenesis imperfecta, defective type I collagen weakens bone formation. Cleidocranial dysplasia impairs intramembranous ossification, leaving fontanelles open and clavicles underdeveloped, while achondroplasia disrupts endochondral growth at the growth plate. Bone may also form in soft tissues where it does not belong (heterotopic ossification), most dramatically in fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva.
