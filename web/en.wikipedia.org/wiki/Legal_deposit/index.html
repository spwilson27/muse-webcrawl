Legal deposit

Legal deposit is a legal requirement that a person or group submit copies of their publications to a designated repository, usually a national library. Legal deposit systems ensure that a country's published output — books, periodicals, newspapers, maps, music, and increasingly digital publications and websites — is collected, preserved, catalogued in the national bibliography, and made available to researchers, typically on the premises of the deposit libraries.

History

The earliest famous legal deposit law is the French Ordonnance de Montpellier of 1537, in which Francis I required a copy of every book printed in France to be deposited in the royal library — ostensibly to preserve learning, though deposit also served royal censorship. Similar schemes spread across Europe in the following centuries and were later tied to copyright: in Britain and the United States, deposit historically functioned as a condition or registration step of copyright protection. Modern systems generally treat deposit as a cultural-heritage obligation independent of copyright.

Examples by country

  • United Kingdom: Under the Legal Deposit Libraries Act 2003, publishers must deposit one copy of every publication with the British Library, and five other libraries (the Bodleian, Cambridge University Library, the National Libraries of Scotland and Wales, and Trinity College Dublin) may request copies. Since 2013 the scheme extends to electronic publications and UK websites.
  • United States: "Mandatory deposit" under the Copyright Act requires two copies of works published in the US to be deposited with the Library of Congress; deposit is distinct from copyright registration but linked to it.
  • France: The dépôt légal, administered by the Bibliothèque nationale de France, covers print, audiovisual works, software, and, since 2006, periodic harvesting of the French web.
  • Germany, Australia, Canada, Japan and others: National libraries (the German National Library, National Library of Australia, Library and Archives Canada, and the National Diet Library) operate deposit systems, generally requiring one or two copies from publishers.

Digital deposit

The shift to born-digital publishing has pushed many countries to extend deposit laws to e-books, e-journals, and the national web domain, raising practical questions about harvesting scale, access restrictions (deposited digital material is often readable only in library premises to protect publishers), and long-term preservation formats.
