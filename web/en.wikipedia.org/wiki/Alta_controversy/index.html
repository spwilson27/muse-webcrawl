The Alta controversy (Norwegian: Alta-saken) was a major political conflict in Norway between the late 1960s and early 1980s over the construction of a hydroelectric power station on the Alta-Kautokeino river system in Finnmark. Opposition united Sámi indigenous-rights activists — for whom the reservoir threatened reindeer-herding lands and the Sámi village of Máze — with the young Norwegian environmental movement. It became the largest civil-disobedience campaign in modern Norwegian history and a turning point in Norway's treatment of its Sámi minority.

Background

Plans to regulate the river for hydropower dated from the 1960s. An early scheme would have flooded Máze, a core Sámi settlement; parliamentary protest led to that village being spared in a 1973 revision, but a dam at Virdnejávri with a smaller reservoir remained. Opponents argued the project would damage one of Europe's finest salmon rivers and reindeer pastures for a modest amount of electricity, and that the decision process ignored Sámi rights.

Protests

The People's Movement against Development of the Alta-Kautokeino Watercourse (founded 1978) organised marches, an information campaign, and from 1979 a protest camp at Stilla, where activists chained themselves to construction machinery. In October 1979 Sámi activists staged a hunger strike outside the Storting in Oslo and briefly occupied the prime minister's office. The largest confrontation came in January 1981, when some 600 police — a substantial fraction of Norway's force — removed around a thousand demonstrators from the frozen construction site in the largest Norwegian police operation since the Second World War. Further actions, including a second hunger strike in 1981 and an attempted (symbolic) blowing-up of a bridge by opponents in 1982, failed to halt construction.

Outcome and legacy

The Supreme Court ruled in 1982 that the development was lawful, and the Alta Power Station entered service in 1987, with a dam of about 110 metres at Virdnejávri — smaller than first proposed, with Máze and most salmon fishing preserved.

Politically, however, the controversy transformed Sámi policy: the government appointed the Sámi Rights Committee, whose work led to the Sámi Act (1987) and the elected Sámi Parliament of Norway (opened 1989), and to a constitutional amendment recognising the Sámi. The case also professionalised Norwegian environmental campaigning and remains the reference point for later indigenous-rights disputes over energy projects, including the Fosen wind-farm conflict.
