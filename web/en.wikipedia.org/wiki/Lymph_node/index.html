A lymph node, or lymph gland, is a kidney-shaped organ of the lymphatic system and the adaptive immune system. A large number of lymph nodes are linked throughout the body by lymphatic vessels. They are major sites of T cells, B cells, and other immune cells, and act as filters for foreign particles and cancer cells, though they do not detoxify substances in the manner of the liver or kidneys.

Lymph nodes are important for the proper functioning of the immune system. They become inflamed or enlarged in various infections and diseases, which may range from throat infections to life-threatening cancers. The condition of lymph nodes is so significant that they are examined in cancer staging, which determines treatment and prognosis. Clinically, lymph nodes become inflamed through a process known as lymphadenitis, and swelling is known as lymphadenopathy.

Structure

Lymph nodes are kidney- or bean-shaped and range in size from a few millimetres to about 1–2 cm long. Each is surrounded by a fibrous capsule that extends inside as trabeculae. The substance of the node is divided into an outer cortex and an inner medulla, surrounded by sinuses through which lymph flows. Lymph enters the node through afferent lymphatic vessels on its convex side and leaves through a single efferent vessel at the hilum, a depression on the concave side.

Cortex and medulla

The cortex contains lymphoid follicles, many with germinal centres, populated mainly by B cells and follicular dendritic cells; the paracortex between follicles is rich in T cells and dendritic cells. The medulla contains medullary cords of plasma cells and B cells, and medullary sinuses lined with macrophages.

Function

Lymph nodes filter lymph as it passes through the sinuses: macrophages remove debris, bacteria, and foreign matter, while antigens are presented to lymphocytes. When antigen is detected, B cells activated in the follicles proliferate in germinal centres and differentiate into antibody-producing plasma cells; the node consequently swells during an immune response.

Distribution

Humans have approximately 500–600 lymph nodes distributed throughout the body, with clusters in the neck (cervical), armpits (axillary), chest (mediastinal), abdomen (mesenteric and para-aortic), and groin (inguinal). They drain specific regions of the body, and the pattern of drainage is clinically important in following the spread of infection and cancer.

Clinical significance

Enlarged lymph nodes may indicate infection, autoimmune disease, or malignancy. Lymph nodes are commonly biopsied or removed for diagnosis and staging of cancers, including lymphomas (cancers originating in lymphoid tissue) and metastases from solid tumours. Removal or radiation of nodes can disrupt lymph drainage and cause lymphoedema.
