Deliberative assembly

A deliberative assembly is a meeting of members of an organisation who use parliamentary procedure to make decisions. The term, used in Robert's Rules of Order, describes a body that meets to discuss and determine courses of action in the name of the entire group.

Characteristics

In Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised, a deliberative assembly is defined by features including: the group meets in a single room or area; business is conducted through motions debated and voted on; members have roughly equal rights to speak and vote; and decisions are taken by the majority, subject to protections for minorities such as quorum requirements.

Types

Examples include legislatures, local councils, conventions of delegates, meetings of boards of directors, and the annual or general meetings of societies and voluntary associations. Mass meetings and committees differ from deliberative assemblies in that their membership or authority is defined differently.

Procedure

Proceedings are governed by an adopted parliamentary authority — commonly Robert's Rules in North America — supplemented by the organisation's bylaws and standing rules. A presiding officer (chair) conducts the meeting, a secretary records minutes, and motions proceed through a standard order of business.
