Electric dipole

An electric dipole is a pair of equal and opposite electric charges separated by a small distance, or more generally any charge distribution whose net charge is zero but whose positive and negative charge centres do not coincide. It is characterised by its electric dipole moment, a vector quantity defined for two charges ±q separated by a displacement d as p = q·d, pointing from the negative to the positive charge.

Field of a dipole

At distances large compared with the separation of the charges, the electric potential of a dipole falls off as 1/r² and its field as 1/r³, more rapidly than the field of a single charge. The field lines emerge from the positive charge and terminate on the negative charge, forming the characteristic dipole pattern.

Dipoles in external fields

A dipole placed in a uniform electric field experiences no net force but a torque τ = p × E that tends to align it with the field; its potential energy is U = −p·E. In a non-uniform field it also experiences a net force. These behaviours underlie the response of dielectric materials, in which molecular dipoles align with an applied field to produce polarisation.

Molecular dipoles

Many molecules, such as water, have permanent dipole moments because of their asymmetric charge distribution; others acquire induced dipole moments in an external field. Dipole–dipole interactions, including hydrogen bonding, strongly influence the physical properties of matter.
