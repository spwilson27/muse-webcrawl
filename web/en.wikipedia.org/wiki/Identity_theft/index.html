Identity theft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Identity theft, also known as identity fraud, occurs when someone wrongfully obtains and uses another person's personal data — such as a name, date of birth, Social Security number, or bank account details — in a way that involves fraud or deception, typically for economic gain. Unlike a fingerprint, which cannot readily be changed, personal data can be misused repeatedly and may be difficult for victims to fully reclaim.

Types

  • Financial identity theft — using another's identity to open credit accounts, take out loans, or make purchases.
  • Criminal identity theft — giving another person's name and details to law enforcement when arrested, leaving the victim with a wrongful criminal record.
  • Medical identity theft — using another's identity to obtain medical care, drugs, or insurance payments.
  • Synthetic identity theft — combining real data (such as a genuine Social Security number) with fabricated details to create a new, fictitious identity.
  • Child identity theft — misusing a minor's identifiers, which may go undetected for years.

Techniques

Thieves obtain data through phishing emails, data breaches at companies and institutions, malware, mail theft, "dumpster diving" for discarded statements, skimming devices on card readers, social engineering, and purchasing breached data on illicit markets. Data breaches affecting hundreds of millions of records have made identity theft a mass-scale crime.

Prevalence and impact

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission collects identity theft reports through its Consumer Sentinel Network, which receives millions of reports annually; credit card fraud is consistently among the most reported categories. Victims may spend months resolving fraudulent accounts, correcting credit reports, and restoring their records.

Legal framework

In the United States, the Identity Theft and Assumption Deterrence Act of 1998 made identity theft a federal crime, and the Identity Theft Penalty Enhancement Act of 2004 created the aggravated offence of identity theft in connection with other felonies. The Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act gave consumers rights such as free credit reports and fraud alerts. Many other jurisdictions criminalize the misuse of identification information and impose data-protection duties on organizations under laws such as the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Prevention

Common protective measures include credit freezes, strong and unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, shredding sensitive documents, monitoring statements and credit reports, and caution with unsolicited requests for personal information.
