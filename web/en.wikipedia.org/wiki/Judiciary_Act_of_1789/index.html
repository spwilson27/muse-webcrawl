Judiciary Act of 1789

The Judiciary Act of 1789 (1 Stat. 73) was a landmark statute adopted by the first United States Congress that established the federal judiciary of the United States. Signed into law by President George Washington on September 24, 1789, it created the Supreme Court, the lower federal courts, and the office of Attorney General, implementing the brief Article III of the Constitution.

Provisions

The Act, drafted principally by Senator Oliver Ellsworth of Connecticut, provided for a Supreme Court of one Chief Justice and five Associate Justices; thirteen federal judicial districts, one for each state, each with a district court and a presidentially appointed judge; and three circuit courts, composed of Supreme Court justices "riding circuit" together with district judges. It created the offices of United States Attorney and United States Marshal for each district and established the office of Attorney General to represent the United States before the Supreme Court and advise the President.

Section 25 of the Act gave the Supreme Court jurisdiction to review final judgments of state high courts on questions of federal law — the provision upheld in Martin v. Hunter's Lessee (1816) and Cohens v. Virginia (1821) as essential to uniform interpretation of federal law. Section 13, purporting to give the Supreme Court original jurisdiction to issue writs of mandamus, became the point of conflict in Marbury v. Madison (1803), where Chief Justice John Marshall held that portion unconstitutional in the first exercise of judicial review over an Act of Congress.

Compromise and legacy

The Act was a compromise between Federalists, who wanted a strong federal judiciary, and Anti-Federalists, who feared it would swallow the state courts: most cases were left to state courts, and lower federal court jurisdiction was limited. Although much of its structure has been superseded by later statutes, the three-tier framework it created — district courts, circuit courts of appeals, and the Supreme Court — remains the skeleton of the United States federal judiciary.
