SNARE protein

SNARE proteins (an abbreviation derived from "SNAP REceptors", where SNAP stands for soluble NSF-attachment protein) are a large family of proteins — more than 60 in yeast and mammalian cells — whose primary role is to mediate the fusion of vesicles with their target membranes, as in the fusion of a neurotransmitter-filled synaptic vesicle with the presynaptic plasma membrane during exocytosis.

Mechanism

The best-studied SNAREs are those mediating neurotransmitter release: the vesicle protein synaptobrevin (VAMP), and the plasma-membrane proteins syntaxin and SNAP-25. Their characteristic SNARE motifs assemble into a stable four-helix bundle (the core complex), whose "zippering" from the membrane-distal to the membrane-proximal ends pulls the two bilayers together and supplies the energy that drives membrane fusion. After fusion, the ATPase NSF, together with SNAP adaptor proteins, disassembles the complex so the components can be reused.

Classification and specificity

SNAREs are structurally classified by the central residue of the SNARE motif into Q-SNAREs (glutamine) and R-SNAREs (arginine); functional complexes contain three Q motifs and one R motif. Different SNARE combinations serve different trafficking steps — endocytosis, endosomal and Golgi transport, and secretion — contributing to the specificity of intracellular transport, work recognised by the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to James Rothman, Randy Schekman and Thomas Südhof.

Toxins

Botulinum and tetanus neurotoxins are proteases that cleave neuronal SNAREs, blocking neurotransmitter release — botulinum toxin causing flaccid paralysis and tetanus toxin, which targets synaptobrevin in inhibitory interneurons, causing spastic paralysis.
