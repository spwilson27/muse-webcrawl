In neuroscience, the default mode network (DMN) is a large-scale brain network of interacting regions that is most active when a person is awake but not focused on the outside world — during daydreaming, mind-wandering, remembering the past, and imagining the future. It is typically deactivated during demanding external tasks, an observation first made in PET studies by Marcus Raichle and colleagues around 2001.

Anatomy and function

Core hubs include the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus, medial prefrontal cortex, and the angular gyri, with subsystems in the medial temporal lobe (memory) and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (social cognition). The network supports self-referential processing, autobiographical memory, theory of mind, and mental simulation. Resting-state fMRI reveals it as a coherent pattern of correlated spontaneous activity.

Clinical significance

  • Alzheimer's disease — DMN regions overlap strikingly with areas of amyloid deposition and early atrophy; connectivity declines with disease progression.
  • Depression — rumination is associated with abnormal dominance of the DMN and its coupling with the subgenual cingulate.
  • Schizophrenia and autism — altered DMN connectivity has been widely reported, though findings vary.
  • Psychedelics — psilocybin and LSD acutely disintegrate DMN integrity, correlating with subjective "ego dissolution".

Debate

Critics note that the DMN also activates during many tasks involving memory and social reasoning, so "task-negative" is an oversimplification. Its relationship to consciousness, and whether similar networks in other primates and rodents are homologous, remain active research questions.
