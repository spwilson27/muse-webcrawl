Dental papilla

In embryology, the dental papilla is a condensation of ectomesenchymal cells (derived from the neural crest) that lies beneath the enamel organ of the developing tooth germ. It first appears at the cap stage of tooth development, filling the concavity of the enamel organ, and is separated from it by a basement membrane across which the two tissues exchange inductive signals.

Development

Cells at the periphery of the dental papilla, adjacent to the inner enamel epithelium, are induced to differentiate into odontoblasts, the cells that secrete dentin (in the process of dentinogenesis). The central cells of the papilla form the dental pulp of the mature tooth, carrying its blood vessels and nerves, which enter through the apical foramen. The dental papilla also determines the shape of the tooth crown: experimental recombination studies show that the papilla instructs the overlying epithelium whether an incisor or a molar will form.

The dental papilla is one of three components of the tooth germ, together with the enamel organ and the dental follicle (dental sac). Mesenchymal stem cells resident in the papilla — including stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAP) of immature permanent teeth — are of interest in regenerative endodontics.
