Iamb (poetry)

An iamb (or iambus) is a metrical foot used in various types of poetry. Originally the term referred to one of the feet of classical quantitative metre, composed of a short syllable followed by a long syllable (as in Greek ἴαμβος, iambos). In modern accentual-syllabic verse, an iamb is a foot of two syllables: an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable — a pattern often rendered "da-DUM".

An iamb: one unstressed syllable followed by one stressed syllable.

Usage

Iambic metres are the most common verse form in English literature. Five iambs per line give iambic pentameter, the metre of Shakespeare's plays and sonnets and of Milton's Paradise Lost (in blank verse). Four iambs give iambic tetrameter; three give trimeter, as in much hymnody and ballad metre. Chaucer established iambic pentameter as a principal English metre, and it remained dominant from the Renaissance through the 19th century.

History and etymology

In ancient Greek poetry, iambic verse was associated with the poet Archilochus and with invective and lampoon — the genre called iambos. The word's origin is uncertain; Greek tradition linked it to Iambe, a figure in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter who cheered the goddess with jests. Greek and Latin drama used the iambic trimeter (six iambs counted in pairs) as the normal metre of spoken dialogue.

Related feet

The iamb's reverse is the trochee (stressed–unstressed). Other disyllabic feet are the spondee (stressed–stressed) and the pyrrhic (unstressed–unstressed). In classical quantitative terms, corresponding feet include the trochee (long–short) and spondee (long–long).
