The 2002 Winter Olympics bid scandal was a corruption scandal that erupted in late 1998 over the campaign by Salt Lake City, Utah, to host the 2002 Winter Olympics. Members of the Salt Lake Organizing Committee's bid team were found to have given cash payments, scholarships, medical care, gifts, lavish trips and other inducements worth roughly $1 million to International Olympic Committee members and their families in exchange for support in the 1995 host-city vote, which Salt Lake City won decisively.

Exposure and investigation

The scandal broke when a letter documenting a tuition payment to the daughter of an IOC member surfaced in a local television investigation, and IOC executive Marc Hodler publicly alleged that a significant minority of IOC members routinely sold their votes. Investigations by the IOC, the US Olympic Committee, the FBI and the US Department of Justice followed. Bid leaders Tom Welch and Dave Johnson resigned in January 1999 and were later indicted on federal charges including racketeering and fraud; they were ultimately acquitted in 2003 after key counts were dismissed.

IOC consequences

Ten IOC members were expelled or resigned — the largest purge in Olympic history — and 24 more were sanctioned. Under pressure, IOC president Juan Antonio Samaranch survived a confidence vote, and the 1999 IOC 2000 reform commission enacted sweeping changes: bans on member visits to bid cities, a $200 cap on gifts, term and age limits, athlete membership, and the creation of an ethics commission.

Aftermath

Businessman Mitt Romney was brought in to lead the reorganised Salt Lake Organizing Committee, cut its budget and restored sponsor confidence. The 2002 Winter Olympics themselves were widely judged a success. The scandal nevertheless permanently changed Olympic bidding, making overt courting of IOC members a liability rather than a necessity.
