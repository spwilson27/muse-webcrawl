Henry Fielding (22 April 1707 – 8 October 1754) was an English novelist, irony writer, and dramatist known for earthy humour and satire. His comic novel Tom Jones is still widely appreciated. He and Samuel Richardson are considered founders of the traditional English novel. He also holds a place in the history of law enforcement, having used his authority as a magistrate to found the Bow Street Runners, London's first professional police force.

Early life and theatre

Fielding was born at Sharpham Park in Somerset, the son of Lieutenant-General Edmund Fielding. He was educated at Eton and briefly studied law at the University of Leiden. Returning to London, he turned to writing for the stage, producing more than two dozen plays in the 1730s — comedies, burlesques such as Tom Thumb, and the ballad opera The Grub-Street Opera — many of them sharply satirical of Robert Walpole's government. Walpole's revenge came with the Theatrical Licensing Act of 1737, which imposed censorship on the stage and effectively ended Fielding's dramatic career. He then read for the bar, qualifying as a barrister in 1740.

Novels

Fielding began writing prose fiction in response to Samuel Richardson's Pamela (1740), which he first parodied anonymously in Shamela (1741). Joseph Andrews (1742), begun as a parody of Richardson, grew into an original comic epic "in prose" narrating the adventures of Pamela's brother. His masterpiece, The History of Tom Jones, a Foundling (1749), is celebrated for its architectonic plot, its panoramic picture of mid-18th-century English society, and its intrusive, essayistic narrator, who addresses the reader directly in prefatory chapters to each of the eighteen books. Amelia (1751), his last and most sombre novel, drew on his experiences of poverty, debt, and the criminal courts. He also wrote the satirical Jonathan Wild (1743) and edited political journals including The Champion and The Covent-Garden Journal.

Magistrate and police

In 1748 Fielding was appointed justice of the peace for Westminster, and in 1749 for Middlesex, operating from his house in Bow Street, Covent Garden. He attacked the corruption of the "trading justices", published pamphlets on robbery and poverty, and organised a small band of professional thief-takers — the Bow Street Runners — who became the forerunners of London's Metropolitan Police. After his death in 1754, his half-brother John Fielding, the blind magistrate, continued the work.

Broken in health by gout, asthma, and overwork, Fielding sailed for Portugal in 1754 in search of a better climate and died in Lisbon shortly after arrival. His Journal of a Voyage to Lisbon, published posthumously in 1755, is a wry account of the journey.
