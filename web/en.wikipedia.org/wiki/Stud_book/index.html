Stud book

A stud book (also called a breed registry) is an official list of animals within a specific breed whose parents are known. Animals are usually registered by their breeders when they are still young, and the term is often used for thoroughbred horses, purebred dogs, and registered livestock such as cattle and sheep.

A stud book may be closed or open. A closed stud book accepts only offspring of animals already registered in it, requiring that all ancestors be traced to the foundation stock of the registry. An open stud book allows registration of animals with only one registered parent, or admits animals that meet breed standards after inspection, permitting new bloodlines to enter over time.

History

The earliest modern stud books were compiled for Thoroughbred horses in England. The General Stud Book, first published in 1791 by James Weatherby, recorded the pedigrees of racehorses and remains the foundation of Thoroughbred registration. Similar registries soon followed for other horse breeds, dogs (such as the Kennel Club Stud Book, begun in 1874), and farm animals.

Functions

Stud books serve to:

  • Preserve documented pedigrees and breed purity over generations.
  • Verify parentage, increasingly through DNA testing.
  • Support selective breeding for desired traits.
  • Manage genetic diversity and inbreeding in small or endangered populations, including zoo breeding programmes such as those coordinated by the European Endangered Species Programme.
