Spintronics (a portmanteau of spin transport electronics), also known as spin electronics, is the study and exploitation of the intrinsic spin of the electron and its associated magnetic moment, in addition to its fundamental electronic charge, in solid-state devices. Conventional electronics largely ignores spin; spintronics seeks to use it to store, carry and process information.

History

The field emerged from experiments on spin-dependent electron transport in the 1980s. In 1988 Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg independently discovered giant magnetoresistance (GMR): the electrical resistance of a stack of alternating ferromagnetic and non-magnetic layers changes dramatically with the relative magnetisation of the layers. GMR spin valves were rapidly adopted as read heads in hard disk drives, enabling large increases in storage density, and Fert and Grünberg received the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics. The related effect of tunnel magnetoresistance underlies magnetic random-access memory (MRAM).

Concepts and devices

Central topics include spin injection and detection, spin relaxation and coherence times, the spin Hall effect, spin-transfer torque (using a spin current to switch a magnet without an external field), and spin-orbitronics. Proposed devices include the Datta–Das spin field-effect transistor (proposed 1990), spin LEDs, and spin-based quantum computing elements in which electron spin states act as qubits. Challenges include efficiently injecting spin polarisation from a ferromagnet into a semiconductor and maintaining coherence at room temperature.
