Group decision-making (also known as collaborative decision-making or collective decision-making) is a situation faced when individuals collectively make a choice from the alternatives before them. The decision is no longer attributable to any single individual, but is the product of the group's deliberation and social dynamics. It is studied in social psychology, organisational behaviour, political science and economics.

Advantages and disadvantages

Groups can pool more information and a wider range of perspectives than individuals, generate more alternatives, and produce decisions with greater acceptance among those who must implement them. Against this, group decisions are slower, can be dominated by high-status or talkative members, diffuse responsibility, and are vulnerable to well-documented pathologies:

  • Groupthink (Irving Janis) — cohesive groups suppress dissent and critical evaluation in the interest of unanimity, leading to defective decisions such as those behind the Bay of Pigs invasion.
  • Group polarisation — discussion tends to shift the group toward a more extreme version of its initial leanings, whether riskier ("risky shift") or more cautious.
  • Social loafing and diffusion of responsibility reduce individual effort and accountability.
  • The Abilene paradox describes groups agreeing to a course of action that no member actually wants, each mistakenly believing the others favour it.

Decision rules and techniques

Groups decide under different rules: unanimity and consensus, majority vote, plurality, or delegation to a leader or expert. Structured techniques developed to counter group pathologies include the Delphi method (anonymous iterative expert judgement), the nominal group technique (silent idea generation before discussion), devil's advocacy, and dialectical inquiry. Social choice theory, beginning with Arrow's impossibility theorem, studies the formal problems of aggregating individual preferences into a group choice.
