Fly rod

A fly rod is a fishing rod designed for fly fishing, used to cast a weighted fly line and an almost weightless artificial fly. Fly rods are long, thin and flexible, typically 7 to 11 feet (2.1–3.4 m) for single-handed rods, and are rated by the weight of line they are designed to cast, from ultralight 0-weight rods for small streams to 14-weight rods for large saltwater game fish. Unlike spinning or baitcasting rods, a fly rod has its reel mounted below the handle, large line guides (including a wide stripping guide), and a grip usually of cork.

Construction

Historic fly rods were built from split bamboo (Tonkin cane), prized for its smooth, progressive flex; modern rods are overwhelmingly made of carbon fibre (graphite) composites, with fibreglass retaining a niche for short, delicate rods. A rod's "action" describes where it bends: fast-action rods flex mainly near the tip, slow-action rods bend deeper into the butt. Two-handed Spey and switch rods, 11 to 16 feet long, are used on large rivers for salmon and steelhead, and the Japanese tenkara tradition uses a long rod with a fixed length of line and no reel.

Use

In casting, the rod acts as a spring and lever: loading (bending) during acceleration and unloading at the stop to launch the line loop. Rod length aids line control ("mending") on the water and leverage when playing fish, which are typically landed with the reel's drag or by stripping line by hand.

History

Fly rods evolved from the long wooden rods of early modern angling, through nineteenth-century split-bamboo craftsmanship (makers such as Hiram Leonard, E. F. Payne and H. L. Leonard's successors, and Hardy of England) to the fibreglass of the 1950s and the graphite revolution begun in the 1970s by manufacturers such as Fenwick, Orvis and Sage.
