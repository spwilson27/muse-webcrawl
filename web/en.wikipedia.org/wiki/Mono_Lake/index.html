Mono Lake (/ˈmoʊnoʊ/ MOH-noh) is a saline soda lake in Mono County, California, formed at least 760,000 years ago as a terminal lake in an endorheic basin. The lake's lack of an outlet causes high levels of salts to accumulate, making its water alkaline (about two and a half times as salty as the ocean) and supporting a unique ecosystem of brine shrimp and alkali flies, which in turn feed millions of migratory birds.

Geology

Mono Lake lies in the Mono Basin at the eastern edge of the Sierra Nevada. Its distinctive limestone tufa towers formed underwater where calcium-rich spring water mixed with the lake's carbonate-rich water; falling lake levels during the twentieth century exposed the towers above the surface. The lake's two main islands, Negit and Paoha, are volcanic in origin, and the Mono–Inyo Craters chain south of the lake records eruptions as recent as the 14th century.

Ecology

Despite its extreme chemistry — a pH near 10 — Mono Lake is highly productive. Trillions of brine shrimp (Artemia monica, found nowhere else) and alkali flies sustain grebes, phalaropes, and California gulls; the lake is one of the most important nesting sites for California gulls in the world. In 2010, researchers reported the bacterium GFAJ-1 from Mono Lake sediments, initially claimed to substitute arsenic for phosphorus in its biomolecules — a claim that was later widely disputed and refuted.

Water diversions and conservation

Beginning in 1941, the City of Los Angeles diverted water from four of the lake's five main inflow streams into the Los Angeles Aqueduct. The lake level fell by about 45 feet (14 m), salinity roughly doubled, and Negit Island became a peninsula, exposing gull nests to coyotes. The Mono Lake Committee, founded in 1978, campaigned to save the lake; in 1983 the California Supreme Court held in National Audubon Society v. Superior Court that the public trust doctrine applied to the diversions, and in 1994 the State Water Resources Control Board ordered Los Angeles to raise the lake to a management level of 6,392 feet above sea level.
