Aboriginal title

Aboriginal title is a common law doctrine that the land rights of indigenous peoples to customary tenure persist after the assumption of sovereignty under settler colonialism. The requirements of proof for the recognition of aboriginal title, and the content of such title, vary significantly by jurisdiction, but the principle itself is recognised in countries including Australia, Canada, India, New Zealand and the United States.

Origins

The doctrine traces its roots to the jurisprudence of the British Empire concerning the legal consequences of the acquisition of territory inhabited by indigenous peoples. Chief Justice John Marshall's decisions in the United States Supreme Court in the early nineteenth century, notably Johnson v. M'Intosh (1823), held that discovery gave the discovering sovereign ultimate title to the land, subject to the indigenous right of occupancy, which could only be extinguished by the sovereign.

By jurisdiction

In Canada, aboriginal title was recognised in Calder v British Columbia (1973) and given content in Delgamuukw v British Columbia (1997), which held that title confers the right to exclusive use and occupation of the land for a variety of purposes. In Australia, the High Court's decision in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992) rejected the doctrine of terra nullius and recognised native title where a continuing connection with the land could be shown.

Extinguishment

Generally, aboriginal title can only be extinguished by the sovereign through a clear and plain legislative intention, and in some jurisdictions only with the consent of the title holders or through surrender to the Crown.
