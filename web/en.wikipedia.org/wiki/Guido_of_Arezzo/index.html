Guido of Arezzo (Guido Aretinus; c. 991 – after 1033) was an Italian music theorist and Benedictine monk of the medieval era. He is regarded as the inventor — or at least the great systematiser — of modern staff notation, which replaced the ambiguous neumatic notation of plainchant and made it possible to learn melodies without an oral teacher.

Life

Guido was a monk at the abbey of Pomposa, near Ferrara, where his new teaching methods reportedly provoked hostility from fellow monks. Around 1025 he moved to Arezzo under the patronage of Bishop Theodald (Tedaldo). Summoned to Rome around 1028, he demonstrated his antiphonary and method to Pope John XIX, who was enthusiastic. His principal treatise, the Micrologus (c. 1026), became the most widely read music treatise of the Middle Ages after Boethius. His other works include the Prologus in antiphonarium, the Regulae rhythmicae, and a famous letter to his friend Michael of Pomposa explaining his methods.

Innovations

Guido refined the four-line staff, using lines and spaces to fix pitches precisely, with coloured lines for F (red) and C (yellow) — origins of the modern clefs. He is also credited with solmisation: assigning the syllables ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la to the steps of the hexachord, taken from the initial syllables of successive lines of the hymn Ut queant laxis in honour of John the Baptist. The system survives today as do-re-mi solfège. The "Guidonian hand", a mnemonic mapping notes onto the joints of the hand, is attributed to his tradition. His aim was practical: he claimed singers using his method could learn in months what previously took ten years.
