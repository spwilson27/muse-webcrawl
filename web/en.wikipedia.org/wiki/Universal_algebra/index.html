Universal algebra

Universal algebra (sometimes called general algebra) is the field of mathematics that studies algebraic structures in general, rather than specific examples such as groups, rings, or vector spaces. Instead of focusing on one kind of structure, universal algebra examines what groups, rings, lattices, and similar objects have in common: a set equipped with a collection of operations, studied through homomorphisms, subalgebras, congruences, and the identities they satisfy.

Lattices are among the structures studied in universal algebra

Basic concepts

The subject begins with the notion of a signature (or type): a set of operation symbols, each with an arity — the number of arguments it takes. An algebra of a given signature is a set together with an interpretation of each operation symbol as an actual operation on the set. Terms are formal expressions built from variables and operation symbols, and an algebra satisfies an identity when every interpretation of the variables makes the two sides of the identity equal.

Varieties and Birkhoff's theorem

A class of algebras defined by a set of identities is called a variety. In 1935 Garrett Birkhoff proved his celebrated HSP theorem: a class of algebras of the same signature is a variety if and only if it is closed under Homomorphic images, Subalgebras, and direct Products. Birkhoff also proved that every algebra has a presentation by generators and relations through free algebras, laying the foundations of the modern field.

History

The subject took shape in the 1930s with Birkhoff's papers, building on earlier work by Alfred North Whitehead, whose 1898 Treatise on Universal Algebra gave the field its name (though his concerns were different). Alfred Tarski and his school developed related ideas in model theory. Later developments include the theory of clones, congruence lattices, commutator theory for congruence-modular varieties, and tame congruence theory, as well as applications in theoretical computer science, where universal-algebraic ideas underlie algebraic specification of data types and term rewriting.
