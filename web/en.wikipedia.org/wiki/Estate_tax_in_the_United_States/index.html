Estate tax in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The estate tax in the United States is a federal tax on the transfer of the taxable estate of a deceased person, levied on the estate itself rather than on the beneficiaries (compare an inheritance tax). A large exemption — an "applicable exclusion amount" — shelters most estates: in recent years the basic exclusion has exceeded $13 million per person (it was $13.61 million in 2024), and only a small fraction of estates owe any tax. Amounts above the exemption are taxed at graduated rates reaching a top rate of 40 percent.

History

The modern federal estate tax dates to the Revenue Act of 1916, motivated in part by revenue needs on the eve of American entry into the First World War and by progressive-era concern over inherited wealth. The Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001 raised the exemption and reduced rates, leading to a one-year repeal in 2010 before the tax was reinstated; the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 temporarily doubled the basic exclusion, with a scheduled sunset after 2025 that later legislation addressed. The tax is closely integrated with the federal gift tax through a unified credit.

Computation

The taxable estate is the gross estate — all property in which the decedent had an interest, including jointly held and certain transferred property — minus allowable deductions, notably for debts, administration expenses, charitable bequests, and property passing to a surviving spouse (the unlimited marital deduction). The executor files Form 706 with the Internal Revenue Service, and an unused exemption may be transferred ("ported") to a surviving spouse. Interests in family businesses and farms may qualify for special valuation or deferral. Several states impose their own estate or inheritance taxes with lower thresholds.

Debate

Opponents, who often call it the "death tax", argue it burdens family farms and businesses and taxes assets twice; supporters respond that it reaches only the largest estates, encourages charitable giving, and mitigates dynastic wealth concentration.
