Summary judgment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Summary judgment is a judgment entered by a court for one party against another without a full trial, available where there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. A party may move, typically after discovery, arguing that the evidence (pleadings, depositions, documents, affidavits) could not support a reasonable fact-finder's verdict for the opponent.

Role and criticism

In US federal practice the 1986 trilogy (Celotex, on shifting production burdens; Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, applying the trial standard of proof to the summary record; Matsushita, on implausible claims) made summary judgment a central disposition mechanism — the endpoint of most federal civil litigation that survives pleading, in a system where trials have nearly vanished. England's summary judgment (CPR Part 24: no real prospect of success) and kindred summary procedures elsewhere serve the same economy with less doctrinal machinery. Critiques run structural: judges decide contested fact-laden questions (credibility, intent) on paper, often employment and civil-rights cases; defendants with resources weaponise the motion's cost; and the Seventh Amendment jury right is honoured by defining the judge's role as merely asking whether a jury could reasonably find — a formula whose application is the whole game.
