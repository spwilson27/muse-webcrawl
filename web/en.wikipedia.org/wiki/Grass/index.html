Grass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Grasses are monocotyledonous flowering plants of the family Poaceae — about 12,000 species — characterised by hollow, jointed stems, narrow leaves with parallel veins, and wind-pollinated flowers in spikelets. They dominate the grassland biomes and are, by a wide margin, humanity's most important plant family.

Importance

Grasses supply the world's staple cereals — wheat, rice, maize, barley, oats, sorghum, millet — and sugarcane, bamboo (the largest grasses), pasture and lawns, and thatch. Grasslands cover roughly a third of Earth's land, supporting great herds of grazing animals and storing large amounts of carbon in their soils. Grazing and fire maintain grasslands against encroaching trees.

Adaptations

Grasses grow from the base of the leaf (intercalary meristems), so grazing or mowing removes only tips; many tropical grasses use C4 photosynthesis for hot, dry conditions. Silica bodies in leaves wear down grazers' teeth — an arms race visible in the high-crowned teeth of horses.
