Regulated verse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Regulated verse (Chinese: 近體詩, jìntǐshī, "modern-style poetry") is a form of Chinese poetry that developed during the Tang dynasty (618–907). It is defined by strict rules governing line length, tonal patterns, parallelism and rhyme, and is distinguished from the freer "old-style" verse (gǔtǐshī) that preceded it.

Forms

Regulated verse comes in three principal forms, each with lines of either five or seven characters: the eight-line lǜshī (律詩), the four-line juéjù (絕句, quatrain), and the extended páilǜ (排律), which lengthens the lǜshī form to ten or more lines. In a lǜshī, the middle two couplets must be strictly parallel in grammar and imagery, a technique known as duìzhàng.

Rules

The form requires that tones alternate according to fixed patterns based on the level (píng) and oblique (zè) tone classes of Middle Chinese. Only one rhyme is used throughout the poem, drawn from the level-tone rhyme groups, with rhyming lines falling on the even-numbered lines. Great Tang masters of the form include Du Fu, widely regarded as its supreme exponent, as well as Li Bai, Wang Wei and Bai Juyi in some of his works. Regulated verse was also adopted for the imperial examination system and influenced poetic traditions in Japan, Korea and Vietnam.
