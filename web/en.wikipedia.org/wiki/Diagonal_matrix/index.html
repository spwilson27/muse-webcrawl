Diagonal matrix

In linear algebra, a diagonal matrix is a matrix in which the entries outside the main diagonal are all zero. The diagonal entries themselves may be zero or non-zero. A square matrix D is diagonal if Dij = 0 whenever i ≠ j. The term usually refers to square matrices, though rectangular matrices with the same property are sometimes included.

Notation and examples

A diagonal matrix with entries d1, …, dn is written diag(d1, …, dn). Special cases include the identity matrix (all diagonal entries 1), the zero matrix (all entries 0) and scalar matrices (all diagonal entries equal). A 1×1 matrix is trivially diagonal.

Properties

Diagonal matrices are easy to compute with: they commute with each other under multiplication, their product and sum correspond to entrywise operations on the diagonals, their determinant is the product of the diagonal entries, and they are invertible exactly when no diagonal entry is zero (the inverse is diag(1/di)). Powers are equally simple, which is why diagonalisation — expressing a matrix as PDP−1 with D diagonal — is so valuable: the diagonal entries of D are the eigenvalues of the original matrix. Every normal matrix is unitarily diagonalisable, and symmetric matrices are diagonalisable by orthogonal matrices (the spectral theorem). Diagonal matrices are simultaneously the simplest examples of both triangular matrices and symmetric matrices.
