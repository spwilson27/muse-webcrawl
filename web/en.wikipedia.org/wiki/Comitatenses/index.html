The comitatenses were the mobile field armies of the late Roman Empire, as distinguished from the limitanei, the static troops garrisoning the frontiers (limites). The name derives from comitatus, the imperial retinue or court, since the field armies originally accompanied the emperor on campaign.

Origins

The system developed from reforms begun under Gallienus and Diocletian and was formalised by Constantine I in the early 4th century. Constantine expanded a central mobile reserve that could be deployed to threatened sectors, rather than relying exclusively on troops fixed along the borders. The field armies were commanded by magistri militum and comites rei militaris.

Organisation

Comitatenses were better paid and held higher status than limitanei; units of the imperial escort armies, the comitatus praesentales, ranked highest of all. Legionary units in the field armies were smaller than their Principate predecessors — perhaps around 1,000 men — supplemented by auxiliary cohorts, cavalry vexillationes, and elite auxilia palatina. Regional field armies were stationed in Gaul, Illyricum, Thrace, and the East.

Role and decline

The field armies formed a strategic reserve intended to defeat invaders who had penetrated the frontier, and they frequently intervened in civil wars between rival emperors. Heavy losses in civil conflicts and disasters such as the Battle of Adrianople (378) progressively weakened them, and in the West the system disintegrated during the 5th century.
