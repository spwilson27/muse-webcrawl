Manchester Ship Canal

The Manchester Ship Canal is a 36-mile (58 km) inland waterway in North West England linking Manchester to the Irish Sea at Eastham on the Mersey estuary. Opened on 1 January 1894 (officially opened by Queen Victoria on 21 May 1894), it made Manchester — then the heart of the world's cotton industry — a seaport, freeing its merchants from Liverpool dock dues and railway charges. At the time of construction it was the largest navigation canal in the world.

Construction

The canal was promoted by Manchester manufacturer Daniel Adamson and built under contractor Thomas Walker, employing at peak some 17,000 navvies. Its construction involved the Barton Swing Aqueduct, which still carries the Bridgewater Canal over the ship canal, and a series of locks raising ships 60 feet (18 m) from the Mersey to Manchester. The project cost over £15 million and bankrupted its contractor, requiring the City of Manchester to bail out the company.

Heyday and decline

Traffic peaked in the 1950s at around 18 million tonnes a year; Manchester ranked among Britain's busiest ports, and the canal corridor attracted industries including oil refining at Stanlow. Containerisation and larger ships shifted trade to coastal ports, and Manchester Docks closed in 1982; the docklands were redeveloped as Salford Quays, now home to MediaCityUK. The canal remains in commercial use for freight, and proposals under the "Atlantic Gateway" concept aim to revive traffic.
