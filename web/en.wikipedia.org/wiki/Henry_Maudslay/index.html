Henry Maudslay (pronounced "Maudsley"; 22 August 1771 – 14 February 1831) was an English machine tool innovator, tool and die maker, and inventor. He is considered a founding father of machine tool technology. His inventions were an important foundation for the Industrial Revolution, and his workshop trained an influential generation of engineers including Joseph Whitworth, Joseph Clement, James Nasmyth and Richard Roberts.

Early life

Maudslay was born in Woolwich, the son of a wheelwright employed at the Royal Arsenal. At twelve he was put to work filling cartridges at the Arsenal; by eighteen his skill as a smith brought him to the attention of the inventor Joseph Bramah, whose London workshop he joined in 1789. Maudslay became Bramah's right-hand man, devising the machine tools that made Bramah's famous unpickable lock commercially producible.

Screw-cutting lathe

After a dispute over wages, Maudslay set up his own business in 1797. Around 1800 he built his celebrated screw-cutting lathe, which used a lead screw and change gears to cut threads of different pitches accurately and repeatably. By making it practical to standardise screw threads, the lathe freed mechanics from cutting every nut and bolt by hand as a matched pair, and is regarded as one of the key inventions of the machine age. His bench micrometer, accurate to a ten-thousandth of an inch and nicknamed "the Lord Chancellor" because disputes were settled by its verdict, became the workshop standard for precision measurement.

Portsmouth block mills

With Marc Isambard Brunel and Samuel Bentham, Maudslay designed and built the Portsmouth Block Mills for the Royal Navy — 43 machines, driven by a steam engine, that produced ships' pulley blocks. Ten men with the machines did the work of 110 skilled craftsmen, turning out up to 130,000 blocks a year, each fully interchangeable. Completed by 1808, it was the first example of purpose-built mass-production machinery in Britain.

Maudslay, Sons and Field

His Lambeth firm, later Maudslay, Sons and Field, became a leading builder of marine steam engines, supplying engines for, among others, HMS Lightning and early cross-Channel packets. Maudslay died in 1831 after catching a chill on a return voyage from France and was buried at Woolwich.

Legacy

Maudslay's true monument was his pupils: the "Maudslay school" of engineers carried his standards of accuracy and design across nineteenth-century engineering, making him arguably the most influential machine-tool maker of his era.
