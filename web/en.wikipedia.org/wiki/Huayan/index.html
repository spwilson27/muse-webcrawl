Huayan


Statue of Vairocana Buddha, central figure of Huayan Buddhism.

Huayan (Chinese: 華嚴; pinyin: Huáyán; Japanese: Kegon; Korean: Hwaeom), or Flower Garland school of Buddhism, is a tradition of Mahayana Buddhist philosophy that flourished in China during the Tang dynasty. The school is based primarily on the Avataṃsaka Sūtra (Flower Garland Sutra).

The school's doctrines include the Fourfold Dharma Realm (dharmadhātu): the realm of phenomena, the realm of principle, the realm of the non-obstruction of principle and phenomena, and the realm of the non-obstruction of phenomena with phenomena. Its central idea is the "interpenetration" of all phenomena (dharmas), each containing and reflecting all others — famously illustrated by the image of Indra's net, a vast web of jewels each reflecting all the rest.

History

The school was systematized by a lineage of five patriarchs: Dushun (557–640), Zhiyan (602–668), Fazang (643–712), Chengguan (738–839), and Zongmi (780–841). Fazang, patronized by Empress Wu Zetian, is credited with articulating the school's core philosophy, including his essay "On the Golden Lion".

Influence

Huayan thought deeply influenced other East Asian Buddhist traditions, including Chan (Zen) and Pure Land, and its classification of teachings (panjiao) shaped how Chinese Buddhists organized the Buddhist canon.
