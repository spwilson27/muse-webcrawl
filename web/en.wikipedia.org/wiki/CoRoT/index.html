CoRoT (French: Convection, Rotation et Transits planétaires; English: Convection, Rotation and planetary Transits) was a space telescope mission operated by the French space agency CNES in conjunction with the European Space Agency (ESA) and other international partners. Launched on 27 December 2006 from the Baikonur Cosmodrome aboard a Soyuz rocket, CoRoT was the first space mission dedicated to the search for exoplanets using the transit method, and it also performed asteroseismology — the study of stellar oscillations — to probe the internal structure of stars.

Objectives

The mission had two main scientific goals. First, to search for exoplanets — particularly small, rocky planets in short-period orbits — by continuously monitoring the brightness of tens of thousands of stars for the tiny periodic dips caused by a planet passing in front of its star. Second, to record oscillations in starlight with extreme precision, allowing astronomers to determine the mass, age, and internal composition of stars through asteroseismology.

Discoveries

CoRoT discovered more than thirty confirmed exoplanets during its lifetime. Its most famous discovery, announced in February 2009, was CoRoT-7b, the first rocky, Earth-sized exoplanet found transiting its star — a dense "super-Earth" orbiting so close to its star that its dayside is hot enough to melt rock. Other notable finds include CoRoT-9b, a temperate gas giant on a 95-day orbit similar to Mercury's, and CoRoT-20b, an unusually dense giant planet.

Mission end

CoRoT was designed for a mission of two and a half years but operated for nearly six. In November 2012 an onboard computer failure ended data collection; attempts to recover the spacecraft were unsuccessful, and the mission was formally concluded in 2013, with the satellite passivated and left to deorbit. CoRoT paved the way for the much larger NASA Kepler mission and ESA's later PLATO mission.
