Self-adjoint operator

In mathematics, a self-adjoint operator on a complex vector space V with inner product ⟨·,·⟩ is a linear operator A on V that is equal to its own adjoint: A = A*. The adjoint may also be denoted by A†. In finite dimensions the self-adjoint operators are exactly the Hermitian matrices.

Bounded operators

For a bounded operator defined on all of a Hilbert space, self-adjointness means that ⟨Ax, y⟩ = ⟨x, Ay⟩ for all vectors x and y. The spectrum of a bounded self-adjoint operator is real, and such operators admit a spectral decomposition generalising the diagonalisation of Hermitian matrices.

Unbounded operators

Many operators of physics — position, momentum and Hamiltonian operators in quantum mechanics — are unbounded and defined only on a dense subspace. For these, the domain of the operator and its adjoint matter: an operator is self-adjoint when its domain equals that of its adjoint and the two agree there; a weaker condition, symmetric (or Hermitian) operator, requires only that ⟨Ax, y⟩ = ⟨x, Ay⟩ on the operator's domain. By the Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem, a symmetric operator defined everywhere on a Hilbert space is necessarily bounded.

Spectral theorem

The spectral theorem states that every self-adjoint operator can be represented as multiplication by a real-valued function on a suitable measure space, or equivalently decomposed through a projection-valued measure. This result underlies the mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics, in which observable quantities correspond to self-adjoint operators, guaranteeing real measurement values.

Extensions

A symmetric operator may possess self-adjoint extensions; von Neumann's theory classifies them using deficiency indices. Whether an extension exists and is unique has direct physical significance, for example for Hamiltonians on half-lines.
