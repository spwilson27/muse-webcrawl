The monarchy of Canada is the form of government embodied by the Canadian sovereign and head of state. It is one of the key components of Canadian sovereignty and sits at the core of Canada's constitutional federal structure and Westminster-style parliamentary democracy. The current monarch is Charles III, who has reigned since 8 September 2022.

Role and functions

The monarch is represented in Canada by the governor general at the federal level and by lieutenant governors in each province; the sovereign's powers — the Royal Prerogative — are exercised on the advice of ministers responsible to the elected House of Commons. The Crown is a corporation sole in right of Canada and in right of each province, holding title to Crown land (about 89% of Canada's land area) and serving as the legal embodiment of the state: laws are enacted in the monarch's name, and members of the armed forces, police, and public servants swear allegiance to the Crown.

History

Canada's monarchy descends from the French and British crowns that claimed and ruled northern North America from the 15th century. Confederation in 1867 created the Dominion of Canada under Queen Victoria; the Statute of Westminster 1931 and the Canada Act 1982 completed Canada's legislative independence, while the Royal Style and Titles Act gives the monarch the distinct title "King of Canada," reflecting that the Canadian Crown is legally separate from the British Crown though held by the same person. The monarchy's relationship with Indigenous peoples — through the Royal Proclamation of 1763 and the treaties — remains constitutionally significant.

Public debate

Supporters argue the monarchy provides non-partisan continuity and anchors Canadian distinctiveness from the United States; republican critics contend a foreign-resident hereditary monarch is anachronistic. Amending the office of the King is among the most difficult constitutional changes in Canada, requiring unanimity of Parliament and all provincial legislatures.
