Biocentrism

Biocentric ethics extends moral consideration to all living organisms.

Biocentrism is an ethical point of view that extends inherent value to all living things. In environmental ethics, biocentrism holds that all living organisms have intrinsic worth independent of their usefulness to human beings, and it is contrasted with anthropocentrism, which centers value on humans, and with ecocentrism, which values ecosystems as wholes.

Outline

Biocentric ethics is associated with thinkers such as Albert Schweitzer, whose philosophy of "reverence for life" held that the good consists in maintaining and furthering life and the evil in destroying or hindering it, and with Paul W. Taylor, whose 1986 work Respect for Nature developed a systematic biocentric theory. Taylor proposed principles — including self-defense, proportionality, minimum wrong, and restitutive justice — for resolving conflicts between human and non-human interests.

Varieties

Some biocentric theories are egalitarian, treating every organism's good as equally important, while others accept hierarchies among living things. Biocentrism underlies much of the deep ecology movement and arguments for the legal rights of animals, plants, and even individual species in conservation policy.

Criticism

Critics argue that strict biocentric egalitarianism is impractical, since human life unavoidably depends on consuming other organisms, and that it struggles to arbitrate conflicts — for example, between an endangered plant and the herbivore that eats it. Ecocentrists such as Aldo Leopold have argued that value should attach primarily to the "biotic community" rather than to individuals.
