Dougong
Chinese斗拱 (dǒugǒng)
ComponentsDou (block) and gong (bow-shaped arm)
Used inTraditional Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese timber architecture

Dougong (Chinese: 斗拱; pinyin: dǒugǒng) is a structural element of interlocking wooden brackets, one of the most important elements in traditional Chinese architecture. It consists of a series of blocks (dou) and bow-shaped arms (gong) fitted together without glue or fasteners, transferring the weight of an overhanging roof from its tiles down to the columns of a building.

History

Dougong first appeared in Chinese buildings of the later Zhou dynasty and became increasingly elaborate through the Han, Tang and Song dynasties, when its forms were standardised in the building manual Yingzao Fashi (1103). During the Ming and Qing dynasties the brackets became smaller, more numerous and increasingly decorative rather than purely structural.

Function

By interlocking multiple brackets, dougong joins the column to the crossbeam and projects outward in tiers to support deep roof eaves. The flexible joints also help a timber building withstand earthquakes, distributing and absorbing seismic energy. The size and complexity of a building's bracket sets historically signalled its rank and the status of its owner; elaborate dougong was restricted to important official and religious buildings.

Related traditions

Variants of the system spread throughout East Asia: it is known as tokyō in Japanese architecture, gongpo in Korean architecture, and đấu củng in Vietnamese architecture. Famous surviving examples include the bracket sets of the Forbidden City in Beijing and the Foguang Temple on Mount Wutai, one of China's oldest wooden buildings.
