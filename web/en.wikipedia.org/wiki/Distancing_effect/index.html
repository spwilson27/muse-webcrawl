Distancing effect

The distancing effect, also known as the alienation effect (German: Verfremdungseffekt or V-Effekt), is a concept in Bertolt Brecht's epic theatre that prevents the audience from losing itself passively in the character created by the actor, and leads the audience to be a conscious observer and critic of the action.

Distancing effect
German: Verfremdungseffekt
Associated with: Bertolt Brecht, epic theatre

Techniques

Brechtian devices include actors speaking stage directions or addressing the audience directly, visible lighting rigs and stage machinery, placards and projections announcing events in advance, songs that interrupt rather than heighten the action, episodic structure, and "gestus" — stylised gestures revealing social relations. Plays such as Mother Courage and Her Children, The Good Person of Szechwan and The Caucasian Chalk Circle exemplify the method.

Purpose

Against Aristotelian catharsis and identification, Brecht — drawing on Marxist ideas — wanted spectators to judge the social conditions shown as historical and changeable, rather than inevitable. The term derives from Russian formalist Viktor Shklovsky's concept of defamiliarisation (ostranenie), which Brecht adapted.

Influence

The effect influenced modernist theatre, political cinema (including Godard), and postdramatic performance.
