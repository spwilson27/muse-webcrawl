Erosion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Erosion is the action of surface processes — flowing water, ice, wind, waves and gravity — that removes soil, rock and dissolved material from one location and transports it elsewhere. It differs from weathering, which breaks rock down in place; together they sculpt landscapes, wearing down mountain ranges and carving features such as the Grand Canyon.

Agents

Water is the dominant agent: rain splash, sheet wash, rivers cutting valleys and gorges, and coasts eroded by waves. Glacial erosion plucks and abrades bedrock, carving U-shaped valleys and fjords. Wind erosion deflates deserts and built the loess deposits of China. Mass wasting — landslides and creep — moves material downslope under gravity, while living organisms (roots, burrowers) contribute bioturbation.

Human dimension

Accelerated soil erosion from deforestation and poor farming has stripped farmland throughout history and was central to the 1930s Dust Bowl; it remains a major threat to food security. Countermeasures include terracing, cover crops, contour ploughing, reforestation and coastal defences. Over geological time, erosion balances tectonic uplift in a dynamic equilibrium that shapes the face of the Earth.
