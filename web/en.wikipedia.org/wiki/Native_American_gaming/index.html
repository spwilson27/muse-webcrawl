Native American gaming comprises the casinos, bingo halls and other gambling operations run by federally recognised Native American tribes on tribal land in the United States. It is among the largest segments of the American gambling industry.

Legal origins

Tribes began opening high-stakes bingo halls in the 1970s, asserting that their sovereignty exempted them from state gambling limits. The Supreme Court upheld that position in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians (1987), and Congress responded with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, which created three classes of gaming and required tribal-state compacts for casino-style (Class III) gaming.

Scale and impact

More than 240 tribes operate over 500 gaming facilities across some 29 states, generating annual revenues exceeding forty billion dollars. Major resorts such as Foxwoods and Mohegan Sun in Connecticut rank among the largest casinos in the world. Revenues fund tribal governments, healthcare, education and infrastructure, though benefits vary greatly by location and tribe.

Debate

The industry has been debated over questions of sovereignty, the distribution of per-capita payments, off-reservation casino proposals, and compacts' revenue-sharing terms with states.
