The Copley Medal is a scientific award given by the Royal Society, London, for "outstanding achievements in research in any branch of science". Created in 1709 by the will of Sir Godfrey Copley, for which he bequeathed £100 to the Royal Society to be used to encourage experimental investigation, it is the oldest scientific award still presented today and is seen as a precursor to the Nobel Prize.

History

The Copley Medal was created following a donation of £100 by Sir Godfrey Copley (1653–1709), an elected member of the Royal Society in 1691. Initially the prize was manufacturer-funded and not awarded every year. The first medal, awarded in 1731, was given to Stephen Gray for his experiments with electricity. In the early years the award alternated between a silver medal and a cash prize; from 1836 the medal was struck in gold and accompanied by a grant.

Recipients

Recipients have included Benjamin Franklin (1753), Captain James Cook (1776), William Herschel (1781), Michael Faraday (1832 and 1838), Charles Darwin (1864), James Clerk Maxwell (1870), J. J. Thomson (1914), Albert Einstein (1925), Dorothy Hodgkin (1976) and Stephen Hawking (2006). Because of the seniority of many awardees, the medal is often regarded as a lifetime achievement award. Multiple winners include John Theophilus Desaguliers and Michael Faraday.
