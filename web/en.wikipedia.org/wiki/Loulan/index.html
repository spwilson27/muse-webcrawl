Loulan

Loulan (Chinese: 樓蘭; pinyin: Lóulán), also called Kroraina in the local Prakrit documents, was an ancient kingdom and oasis city on the northeastern edge of the Tarim Basin in present-day Xinjiang, China, near the lake Lop Nur. A key stop on the northern route of the Silk Road, Loulan flourished from about the 2nd century BCE until it was abandoned around the 4th–5th centuries CE, apparently as its water supply failed. Its rediscovery by explorers around 1900 yielded famous mummies, wooden documents and the ruins of a Chinese garrison town.

History

Loulan first appears in Chinese accounts of the Han dynasty. In 108 BCE Han forces compelled its king to submit; the Chinese later renamed the kingdom Shanshan (鄯善) after executing its king in 77 BCE, but the city of Loulan remained an important Chinese military and postal station guarding the route west. Documents recovered from the site, written in Chinese and in the Kharoshthi script used for Gandhari Prakrit, record the administration of the kingdom of Kroraina in the 3rd century CE, when the local population spoke a Tocharian language. The settlement was abandoned as the rivers feeding it shifted and Lop Nur migrated, and the region became desert.

Rediscovery and archaeology

The Swedish explorer Sven Hedin reached the ruins in 1900, and his workmen recovered ancient letters, including one written on paper. Aurel Stein excavated Loulan in 1906 and 1914, uncovering a Chinese garrison town with a stupa, houses, silk fragments, coins, and hundreds of wooden documents known as the Loulan papers, which illuminated Silk Road administration and daily life. Chinese expeditions from 1979 onward found cemeteries containing remarkably preserved mummies, including the so-called "Beauty of Loulan," a woman who lived around 1800 BCE — far earlier than the historic kingdom.

The Beauty of Loulan

The mummified remains of a woman found in 1980 near the Tiebanhe cemetery, dated to roughly 3,800 years ago, became famous in China and abroad. With fine features, a woven cloak, and grave goods including wheat and a winnowing basket, she attests to very early settlement along the routes that later formed the Silk Road. The mummies of the Tarim Basin remain the subject of scholarly study and political sensitivity.
