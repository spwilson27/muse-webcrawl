The A-level (Advanced Level) is a subject-based school-leaving qualification, formally the General Certificate of Education Advanced Level, offered in England, Wales and Northern Ireland and by British-curriculum schools worldwide. Introduced in 1951, it is the main qualification taken by students aged 16 to 18 in sixth forms and further-education colleges, and the principal route to university admission in the United Kingdom.

Structure

Students typically study three or four subjects over two years, chosen to suit intended degree courses — for example, medicine applicants commonly take chemistry, biology and mathematics. Grades run from A* (introduced in 2010) down to E, with U denoting failure. Universities make offers expressed in A-level grades or UCAS tariff points. A-levels are awarded by examination boards including AQA, Edexcel, OCR and WJEC/Eduqas, regulated by Ofqual.

Reforms

A-levels were long divided into AS (first year) and A2 (second year) components with coursework elements. Reforms rolled out in England from 2015 "decoupled" the AS-level into a standalone qualification and made A-levels linear, assessed mainly by final examinations. Scotland uses a separate system (Highers and Advanced Highers), and alternatives in England include BTECs, T Levels and the International Baccalaureate.
