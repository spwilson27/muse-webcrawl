Leg press

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Leg press
A 45° incline leg press

The leg press is a strength training exercise performed on a machine in which the individual pushes a weight or resistance away from them using their legs. It is a common compound exercise for developing the muscles of the lower body, principally the quadriceps femoris of the thigh, the hamstrings, and the gluteus maximus of the buttocks. The term also denotes the machine used to perform the exercise.

Types

There are three main types of leg press machine:

  • Incline (45-degree) leg press — the lifter sits reclined at an angle and pushes a sled loaded with weight plates upward along rails at about 45°; the most common type in commercial gyms, allowing very heavy loads.
  • Vertical leg press — the lifter lies on their back and pushes the plate directly upward; an older design found in some powerlifting gyms.
  • Horizontal (seated) leg press — the lifter sits upright and pushes a footplate connected to a weight stack by a cable; common in machine circuits and rehabilitation settings.

Technique and variations

The lifter sits with the feet placed on the footplate roughly hip- to shoulder-width apart, lowers the sled by flexing the knees and hips until the thighs approach the torso, then extends the legs to push the weight back up. Foot position changes the emphasis: a higher placement stresses the glutes and hamstrings more, while a lower placement stresses the quadriceps. A narrower stance emphasises the outer quadriceps and a wider stance the inner thigh (adductors). Safety stops limit the descent of the sled, and handles beside the seat release the locking catches.

Because the back is supported and the path of movement is fixed, the leg press requires less balance and stabilisation than the barbell squat, and lifters can usually press substantially more weight than they can squat. Coaches nevertheless caution against locking the knees forcefully at full extension and against allowing the lower back to round (posterior pelvic tilt) at the bottom of a deep repetition, which can load the lumbar spine.

Records

Heavily loaded sled presses have been used for exhibition lifts; loads above 1,000 kg (2,200 lb) have been pressed for partial repetitions by strongmen and bodybuilders. Such feats are typically performed over a reduced range of motion and are not standardised as competitive records.
