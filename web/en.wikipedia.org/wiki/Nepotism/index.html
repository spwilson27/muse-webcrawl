Nepotism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nepotism is the practice among those with power or influence of favouring relatives, spouses or friends — especially by giving them jobs or other advantages — regardless of merit. The word derives from the Latin nepos (nephew), a nod to medieval and Renaissance popes who elevated their "nephews" (often sons) to cardinalates and benefices, institutionalised as the office of cardinal-nephew until Innocent XII curtailed it in 1692.

Rules and realities

Modern public law treats nepotism as a species of conflict of interest: anti-nepotism statutes bar officials from appointing relatives (a US federal statute of 1967, passed after President Kennedy appointed his brother Robert as Attorney General, restricts such appointments across the branches — its application to White House staff was litigated in 2017), and civil-service examination systems were designed historically to defeat it. Political dynasties (the Kennedy, Bush, Gandhi, Bhutto, Aquino and Abe families, hereditary Diet seats in Japan) show nepotism's electoral variant: voters themselves ratify the brand. Private-sector nepotism is lawful and common in family firms, defended as trust and continuity, criticised as ascriptive closure. The line anthropologists draw is between societies where kin obligation is a moral duty (making anti-nepotism rules culturally costly) and the Weberian ideal of office as impersonal trust — most systems live uneasily between.
