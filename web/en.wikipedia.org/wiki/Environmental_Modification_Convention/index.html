Environmental Modification Convention

The Convention on the Prohibition of Military or Any Other Hostile Use of Environmental Modification Techniques, commonly called the Environmental Modification Convention or ENMOD, is an international treaty prohibiting the military or other hostile use of environmental modification techniques having widespread, long-lasting or severe effects. It opened for signature in Geneva on 18 May 1977 and entered into force on 5 October 1978.

Background

Concern about weather warfare grew during the Vietnam War, when the United States conducted Operation Popeye, a cloud-seeding programme intended to extend the monsoon season and soften road surfaces used by North Vietnamese forces. Disclosure of the programme in the early 1970s led the US Senate to call for an international agreement, and the United States and the Soviet Union jointly proposed a treaty to the Conference of the Committee on Disarmament in 1975.

Provisions

Article I prohibits parties from engaging in "military or any other hostile use of environmental modification techniques having widespread, long-lasting or severe effects as the means of destruction, damage or injury to any other State Party." Understandings attached to the treaty define widespread as encompassing an area of several hundred square kilometres, long-lasting as lasting for months or approximately a season, and severe as involving serious or significant disruption or harm. "Environmental modification techniques" include deliberate manipulation of natural processes — the dynamics, composition or structure of the Earth (including its biota, lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere) or of outer space. Article III expressly preserves peaceful uses of such techniques.

Parties

The convention has been ratified by dozens of states, including the United States, Russia (as successor to the Soviet Union), the United Kingdom, and many others, though some major military powers have signed without ratifying or remain outside the treaty.
