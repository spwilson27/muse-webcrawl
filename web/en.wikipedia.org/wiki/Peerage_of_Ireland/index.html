Peerage of Ireland

A coronet of rank in the Peerage of Ireland.

The Peerage of Ireland consists of those titles created by the English and later British monarchs in their capacity as Lord or King of Ireland, before the Acts of Union 1800 united Ireland with Great Britain. Irish peers held seats in the Irish House of Lords until its abolition in 1801. It is one of the five peerages of the British Isles, alongside those of England, Scotland, Great Britain, and the United Kingdom.

Ranks

The peerage comprises five ranks, in descending order: duke, marquess, earl, viscount, and baron. The premier peerage title is the Dukedom of Leinster, created in 1766. Many Irish peerages were granted to people who also held English or Scottish titles, and some, such as the Earl of Inchiquin, descend from native Irish dynasties.

History

The earliest Irish peerages date from the medieval Lordship of Ireland; the Earldom of Kildare, created in 1316, is among the oldest surviving. Peerages continued to be created after the Kingdom of Ireland was proclaimed in 1542. Following the Union, 28 Irish representative peers sat in the Westminster House of Lords; elections ceased after 1922, and the last representative peer died in 1961. New creations in the Peerage of Ireland effectively ended in the nineteenth century, though the titles continue to be inherited.

Privileges

Irish peers who did not also hold a peerage of England, Scotland, Great Britain, or the United Kingdom were historically entitled to stand for election to the House of Commons, a privilege used, for example, by Lord Palmerston, an Irish viscount who served as British prime minister. Under the House of Lords Act 1999, most hereditary peers lost their automatic seats in the Lords, but Irish titles persist as hereditary dignities.
