Standard solar model

The standard solar model (SSM) is a mathematical treatment of the Sun as a spherical ball of ionised gas (plasma) whose structure is determined by the equations of stellar structure, calibrated so that the model, evolved for the Sun's age of 4.57 billion years, reproduces the observed luminosity, radius and surface composition of the present Sun. It is the reference model of stellar astrophysics and the baseline against which other stars are understood.

Assumptions and structure

The model assumes the Sun began as a homogeneous sphere of mostly hydrogen and helium and evolves under hydrostatic equilibrium, mass continuity, energy transport (radiative diffusion in the interior, convection in the outer envelope) and energy generation by nuclear fusion — chiefly the proton–proton chain in the core, where the temperature reaches about 15.7 million kelvin. Free parameters, principally the initial helium abundance and the mixing-length parameter for convection, are adjusted to fit the present-day observations.

Tests: helioseismology and neutrinos

The model predicts the Sun's internal sound-speed profile, which helioseismology confirmed to remarkable precision (to within fractions of a percent) from the 1990s onward, validating the model's structure. It also predicts the flux of solar neutrinos from core fusion. For decades detectors such as Ray Davis's Homestake experiment measured only about one-third of the predicted electron-neutrino flux — the "solar neutrino problem" — which was resolved not by changing the solar model but by the discovery of neutrino oscillation, confirmed by the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in 2001–02. More recent tension between revised surface abundances and helioseismic data (the "solar abundance problem") remains an active research topic.
