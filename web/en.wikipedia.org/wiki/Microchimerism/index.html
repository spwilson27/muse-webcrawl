Microchimerism is the presence of a small number of cells in an individual that originate from a genetically distinct individual. The phenomenon is most commonly a consequence of pregnancy: cells traffic bidirectionally across the placenta, so that fetal cells can persist in the mother (fetal microchimerism) and maternal cells in the child (maternal microchimerism) for decades after birth. Microchimerism can also arise from blood transfusion, organ transplantation, or exchange between twins in utero.

History

The survival of fetal cells in the mother was demonstrated in the 1990s, notably by Diana Bianchi and colleagues, who found male fetal progenitor cells in women decades after they had given birth to sons. Such cells have since been detected in a wide range of maternal tissues, including blood, skin, liver, thyroid and brain, and fetal cells have been shown in some studies to home to sites of injury and participate in tissue repair.

Health effects

Research into microchimerism has produced a complex picture. Persisting fetal cells have been associated both with protection against some diseases, including certain cancers, and with an increased risk of autoimmune disorders such as systemic sclerosis, which disproportionately affect women after their childbearing years. Maternal microchimerism in offspring has likewise been linked to both tolerance of maternal antigens and autoimmune conditions such as juvenile dermatomyositis and type 1 diabetes. The mechanisms — whether microchimeric cells are protective, pathogenic or neutral bystanders — remain an active field of study.
