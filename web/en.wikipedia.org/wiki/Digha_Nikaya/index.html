Digha Nikaya

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Digha Nikaya (Pali: dīghanikāya, "Collection of Long Discourses") is a Buddhist scripture, the first of the five nikayas, or collections, in the Sutta Pitaka of the Pali Canon, the scriptures of Theravada Buddhism. It contains 34 long suttas (discourses), grouped into three divisions, and includes some of the most famous discourses attributed to the Buddha, such as the Mahaparinibbana Sutta, the Sigalovada Sutta, and the Samaññaphala Sutta.

Structure

The 34 suttas are arranged in three vaggas (divisions) of roughly equal length:

  • Silakkhandha-vagga ("Division Concerning Morality"), 13 suttas, covering ethical training and the fruits of the contemplative life. It opens with the Brahmajala Sutta, the "net of views", and includes the Samaññaphala Sutta and the Kevatta Sutta.
  • Mahā-vagga ("Great Division"), 10 suttas, many with titles beginning Mahā ("great"), including the Mahapadana Sutta on past Buddhas, the Mahanidana Sutta on dependent origination, the Mahaparinibbana Sutta recounting the Buddha's last days and death, and the Mahasatipatthana Sutta on the foundations of mindfulness.
  • Pāthika-vagga, 11 suttas, named from its first discourse, the Pathika Sutta, including the Sigalovada Sutta — the Buddha's advice on lay ethics, sometimes called the "Vinaya of the householder" — and the Aggañña Sutta, a discourse on origins and society.

Significance

Because its discourses are long and comparatively self-contained, the Digha Nikaya is often the first nikaya recommended to new readers of the Pali Canon. Scholars regard some of its suttas, such as the Mahaparinibbana, as composite texts assembled over time. Parallel versions of most discourses survive in the Sanskrit and Chinese Dirgha Agama, allowing comparative study of early Buddhist literature across schools.

Translations

The collection was translated into English by T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids as Dialogues of the Buddha (1899–1921) for the Pali Text Society, and more recently by Maurice Walshe as The Long Discourses of the Buddha (1987).
