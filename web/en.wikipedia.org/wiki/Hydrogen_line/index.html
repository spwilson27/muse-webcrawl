Hydrogen line

Hydrogen line (21 cm line)
Frequency: 1420.40575 MHz
Wavelength: 21.106 cm
Transition: Hyperfine spin-flip in neutral hydrogen
Predicted: H. C. van de Hulst (1944); detected 1951

The hydrogen line, 21-centimetre line, or H I line is a spectral line created by the hyperfine transition of neutral hydrogen atoms: when the electron's spin flips relative to the proton's spin, the atom emits or absorbs electromagnetic radiation at a frequency of 1420.40575 MHz, corresponding to a wavelength of 21.106 cm in vacuum.

Significance in astronomy

Predicted by Hendrik van de Hulst in 1944 and first detected in 1951 by Harold Ewen and Edward Purcell at Harvard (with simultaneous Dutch and Australian confirmation), the line revolutionised radio astronomy. Because 21 cm radiation penetrates interstellar dust, it maps neutral hydrogen throughout the Milky Way, revealing spiral structure and galaxy rotation curves — key evidence for dark matter — and traces the cosmic dawn and reionisation via redshifted signals.

Other uses

The transition's frequency, together with its wavelength, was used as a fundamental unit of time and length on the Pioneer plaques and Voyager Golden Record, on the assumption any advanced civilisation would know it.
