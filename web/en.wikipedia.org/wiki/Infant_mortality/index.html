Infant mortality is the death of a child under one year of age. The infant mortality rate (IMR) — the number of infant deaths for every 1,000 live births — is an important indicator of the overall physical health of a community and is used by demographers and public-health agencies to compare health outcomes across places and over time. Deaths in the first 28 days of life are counted as neonatal mortality, a subset of the IMR.

Causes

Globally, the leading causes of infant death are complications of preterm birth, birth asphyxia and trauma, pneumonia, diarrhoeal diseases, malaria, congenital anomalies, and sepsis and other infections. Undernutrition underlies a large share of infant and child deaths. Many of these causes are preventable with interventions such as skilled birth attendance, breastfeeding, immunisation, clean water, oral rehydration therapy, insecticide-treated bed nets, and access to antibiotics and neonatal care.

Trends

World infant mortality has fallen dramatically: from roughly 65 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1990 to below 30 by the early 2020s, reflecting rising incomes, female education, sanitation, and medical care. Rates remain highest in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia and lowest in high-income countries such as Japan and the Nordic states (around 2–3 per 1,000). Historically, in many pre-industrial societies, IMRs of 200 or more per 1,000 were common. The IMR differs from the under-five mortality rate, which includes ages one to four.
