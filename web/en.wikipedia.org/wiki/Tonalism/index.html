Tonalism

Years activec. 1880s–1915
CountryUnited States
Major figuresGeorge Inness, James McNeill Whistler, Dwight William Tryon
InfluencesBarbizon school, Aestheticism

Tonalism was an artistic style that emerged in the 1880s when American artists began to paint landscape forms with an overall tone of coloured atmosphere or mist. Between 1880 and 1915, dark, neutral hues such as gray, brown or blue dominated works by artists associated with the movement. Late 19th-century art critics typically applied the term to works influenced by the Barbizon school and by the late paintings of George Inness, especially his visionary, spiritually charged landscapes of the 1890s.

Characteristics

Tonalist paintings are modest in scale and palette, favouring twilight, dawn and moonlit scenes in which forms are softened and local detail suppressed in favour of mood. The movement is linked with the Aesthetic movement's ideal of art for art's sake; Whistler's "nocturnes" epitomised the musical analogy of harmonies and arrangements of tone. Tonalism was a largely American reaction against the bright light and objectivity of Impressionism, and has been compared to the introspective quality of Symbolism, though most Tonalists avoided overt literary subject matter.

Artists and legacy

Beyond Inness and Whistler, exponents include Dwight William Tryon, Birge Harrison, Thomas Dewing and the Californian painters of the Eucalyptus school. Tonalism waned with the rise of modernism after the 1913 Armory Show but has attracted renewed scholarly and market interest, and is sometimes identified as an ancestor of later American minimal landscape traditions.
