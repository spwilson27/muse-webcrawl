Funding bias

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Funding bias arises when a study's sponsor has an interest in a particular result.

Funding bias, also known as sponsorship bias, funding outcome bias, funding publication bias, or the funding effect, refers to the tendency of a scientific study to support the interests of the study's financial sponsor. The phenomenon is recognised across medicine, nutrition science, tobacco research, and other fields where results have commercial consequences.

Mechanisms

Deliberate misconduct is not required. Funding can bias a body of research through:

  • Question selection: sponsors fund studies likely to flatter their products and neglect unflattering questions.
  • Design choices: weak comparators, low doses of rival products, short durations, or surrogate endpoints.
  • Selective reporting and publication: favourable results are published promptly; unfavourable ones are delayed, reframed, or shelved (see publication bias).
  • Framing: conclusions emphasising benefit over harm even on identical data.

Evidence

Systematic reviews consistently find the association. A 2017 Cochrane review (Lundh et al.) found that industry-sponsored drug and device studies more often reported favourable efficacy results and conclusions than non-industry studies, despite similar risk of bias on standard measures. Earlier work found similar effects in tobacco-funded research on second-hand smoke, in pharmaceutical trials, and in nutrition research funded by the sugar and soft-drink industries — including the sugar industry's funding in the 1960s of reviews that downplayed sugar's role in heart disease, documented in a 2016 JAMA Internal Medicine historical analysis.

Countermeasures

Proposed and adopted remedies include mandatory trial registration, disclosure of conflicts of interest in journals and conferences, independent statistical analysis, preregistration of analysis plans, prospective meta-analysis, and public or pooled funding insulated from individual sponsors. Critics note that disclosure alone may not change outcomes and can even license bias ("moral licensing").
