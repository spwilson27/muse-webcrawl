Comparative genomic hybridization

[Image: Metaphase CGH]

Comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) is a molecular cytogenetic method for analysing copy number variations (CNVs) relative to ploidy level in the DNA of a test sample compared to a reference sample, without obtaining a cell culture. The aim of this technique is to quickly and efficiently compare two genomic DNA samples arising from two sources, which are most often closely related, because of the suspicion that they contain differences in gains or losses of either whole chromosomes or subchromosomal regions.

Method

In the original metaphase CGH, DNA from the test and reference samples is labelled with different fluorochromes (typically green and red), mixed with unlabelled repetitive-sequence blocking DNA, and hybridised to normal metaphase chromosomes. The ratio of fluorescence intensities along each chromosome reflects the relative copy number of sequences in the test genome: gains appear as excess of the test colour and deletions as excess of the reference colour. The technique was introduced in 1992 by Kallioniemi and colleagues, largely for the analysis of tumour genomes.

Array CGH

Array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH, also called chromosomal microarray analysis) replaces metaphase chromosomes with thousands to millions of defined DNA probes — oligonucleotides or bacterial artificial chromosomes — arrayed on a slide. This greatly increases resolution, allowing detection of imbalances of a few kilobases. Array CGH became a first-line clinical test for developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism and congenital anomalies, and is widely used in prenatal diagnosis and cancer profiling. It cannot detect balanced rearrangements such as translocations or inversions, nor polyploidy in some implementations, and is increasingly complemented or replaced by genome sequencing.
