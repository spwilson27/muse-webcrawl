Andrei Bely (Russian: Андре́й Бе́лый), the pen name of Boris Nikolaevich Bugayev (1880–1934), was a Russian novelist, poet, and critic, and with Alexander Blok a leading figure of the Symbolist movement. His novel Petersburg — first published in book form in 1916 and revised in 1922 — is regarded as a masterpiece of modernist fiction, praised by Vladimir Nabokov as one of the four greatest novels of the twentieth century, comparable to Joyce's Ulysses.

Life

Bely was the son of Nikolai Bugayev, a distinguished mathematician and dean at Moscow University; the tension between his father's rationalism and his mother's artistic temperament pervades his autobiographical writings. He studied natural sciences and then philology at Moscow University, and became a disciple of Rudolf Steiner's Anthroposophy, spending years at the Goetheanum in Dornach, Switzerland. His intense friendship with Blok broke down partly over Bely's love for Blok's wife, Lyubov — a celebrated Symbolist love triangle. After the Revolution he taught, travelled, and wrote memoirs that remain key sources for the period.

Works and style

Bely's prose is famous for its rhythmic, incantatory cadence — he wrote theoretical studies of rhythm in verse and prose — its shifting narrative voices, colour symbolism, and grotesque humour. The Silver Dove (1910) and Petersburg explore revolutionary ferment, terrorism, and the clash of East and West in Russia; Kotik Letaev (1918) renders early childhood consciousness in experimental prose. His critical essays helped define Symbolist aesthetics, and his memoir trilogy — On the Border of Two Centuries, The Beginning of the Century, and Between Two Revolutions — chronicles the era in vivid detail.
