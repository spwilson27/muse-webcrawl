Narration

Narration is the use of a written or spoken commentary to convey a story to an audience. A narration may be presented by a character in the work — a first-person narrator — or by an external voice, the "third-person narrator", who is not a participant in the story's events. The person or voice performing the narration is called the narrator, and the systematic study of narration and narrative forms is narratology.

Narration encompasses every choice by which a story is mediated: who speaks, who perceives, the order in which events are told, how much detail is given, and the distance — temporal, emotional, and moral — between narrator, characters, and audience. In film and television, narration frequently takes the form of voice-over; in theatre it may be delivered by a chorus or stage narrator.

Narration mediates a story between teller and audience, in literature, film, and oral tradition.

Point of view

First person

A first-person narrator recounts events as "I", participating in or witnessing the action. The mode gives immediacy and intimacy but restricts knowledge to what the narrator could know, and creates the possibility of unreliability, as in the naïve or self-deceiving narrators of Huckleberry Finn or The Remains of the Day.

Second person

Second-person narration addresses the protagonist as "you", casting the reader as a character. It is rare in long fiction but used memorably in works such as Jay McInerney's Bright Lights, Big City and in interactive fiction and games.

Third person

Third-person narrators stand outside the story. They may be omniscient, with access to all characters' thoughts and to events anywhere, or limited, following a single character's perspective closely; Gérard Genette's theory of focalization refines these distinctions by asking who perceives, separately from who speaks.

Reliability

An unreliable narrator is one whose account the audience has reason to distrust — because of ignorance, bias, madness, or deceit. Wayne C. Booth coined the term in The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961), defining reliability by the distance between the narrator's norms and those of the implied author. Unreliable narration is a staple of works such as The Catcher in the Rye, Fight Club, and We Need to Talk About Kevin.

Structure and technique

Narrators may tell events chronologically or reorder them through flashback (analepsis) and flashforward (prolepsis). A frame narrative presents a story within a story, as in The Canterbury Tales or Heart of Darkness. Stream of consciousness narration attempts to render the continuous flow of a character's thought. Gérard Genette's categories of tense, mood, and voice in Narrative Discourse (1972) remain the standard vocabulary for analysing narration.

Narration in other media

In film, voice-over narration ranges from hard-boiled detective commentary to the retrospective narration of Sunset Boulevard; documentaries rely on expository narration, sometimes criticised as the "voice of God". Sports broadcasting, audio description for blind audiences, audiobooks, podcasts, and video games all employ narrators as mediators between content and audience.
