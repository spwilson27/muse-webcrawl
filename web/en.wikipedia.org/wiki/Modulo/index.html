Modulo

In computing, the modulo operation (sometimes called modulus, or in some textbooks remainder) returns the remainder of the division of one number by another, called the modulus of the operation. Given two positive numbers, a and n, a modulo n (often abbreviated as a mod n) is the remainder of the Euclidean division of a by n, where a is the dividend and n is the divisor.

Definition

For a positive integer n, two integers a and b are said to be congruent modulo n, written a ≡ b (mod n), if their difference a − b is an integer multiple of n. The number n is called the modulus of the congruence. This notion was introduced by Carl Friedrich Gauss in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801).

In computing

Most programming languages provide a modulo operator, commonly %, mod or Mod. Implementations differ in how they handle negative operands: some follow truncated division (the result takes the sign of the dividend), while others follow floored division, giving a result with the sign of the divisor — a distinction that matters in practice, for example when implementing hash functions or cyclic buffers.

Applications

Modular arithmetic underlies much of number theory and cryptography, including the RSA and Diffie–Hellman algorithms, checksum and check-digit schemes such as ISBN and IBAN validation, hash tables, pseudorandom number generators, and clock arithmetic. In mathematics more broadly, the phrase "modulo" also appears in expressions like "unique modulo isomorphism", meaning unique up to the stated equivalence.
