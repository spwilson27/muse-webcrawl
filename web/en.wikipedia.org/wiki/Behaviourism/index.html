Behaviourism

Behaviourism (American English: behaviorism) is a systematic approach to understanding the behaviour of humans and other animals. It assumes that behaviour is either a reflex evoked by the pairing of stimulus factors in the environment, or a consequence of that individual's history — including reinforcement and punishment — together with the individual's current motivational state and controlling stimuli. Although behaviourists generally accept the role of heredity, their primary focus is on environmental factors. Behaviourism dominated academic psychology from the 1920s to the 1950s and profoundly influenced education, animal training and therapy.

History

The school was formally launched by John B. Watson's 1913 paper "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It", which rejected introspection and the study of consciousness in favour of observable behaviour. It built on Ivan Pavlov's work on conditioned reflexes in dogs and Edward Thorndike's law of effect. Watson's "Little Albert" experiment (1920) controversially demonstrated conditioned fear in an infant. From the 1930s, B. F. Skinner developed radical behaviourism and operant conditioning, using the "Skinner box" to study schedules of reinforcement, and applied the framework to language in Verbal Behavior (1957).

Criticism and legacy

Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Verbal Behavior is often cited as a turning point, arguing that language acquisition could not be explained by conditioning alone. The rise of cognitive psychology in the 1960s displaced strict behaviourism from dominance, but its methods survive in applied behaviour analysis (widely used in autism intervention), behaviour therapy, token economies and the experimental analysis of behaviour. Edward Tolman's work on cognitive maps in rats showed that even within the tradition, purely stimulus–response accounts had limits.
