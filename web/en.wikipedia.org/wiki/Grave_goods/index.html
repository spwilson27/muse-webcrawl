Grave goods

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Grave goods, in archaeology and anthropology, are items buried with a body in a grave or cremation urn. They include personal possessions, supplies to smooth the deceased's journey into an afterlife, and offerings to gods or ancestors. The practice of burying goods with the dead is one of the oldest and most widespread funerary customs known, and the study of grave goods is a principal source of information about past societies, their belief systems, craft technologies, trade networks, and social hierarchies.

History

The earliest secure evidence for deliberate burial with goods comes from the Upper Palaeolithic. At Sungir in Russia, burials dated to roughly 34,000 years ago contained thousands of mammoth-ivory beads, spears, and ornaments that would have taken enormous labour to produce, suggesting both ritual significance and social differentiation. Neanderthal burials, such as those at Shanidar Cave in Iraq, have also been interpreted as containing offerings, though such claims remain debated.

With the advent of agriculture and settled societies, grave goods became more elaborate. In ancient Egypt, tombs were stocked with furniture, food, jewellery, and ushabti figurines intended to serve the deceased in the afterlife; the tomb of Tutankhamun, discovered in 1922, contained more than 5,000 objects. In China, Shang dynasty royal tombs held bronze ritual vessels, jade, and sacrificed retainers, while the First Emperor Qin Shi Huang was guarded by the Terracotta Army of some 8,000 life-sized soldiers. The ship burials of early medieval Europe, such as Sutton Hoo in England and Oseberg in Norway, preserved entire vessels together with weapons, tools, textiles, and household items.

Types of grave goods

Grave goods are conventionally divided into several functional categories:

  • Personal possessions — clothing, jewellery, weapons, and tools used by the deceased in life.
  • Provisions — food and drink, or vessels to hold them, for the journey to or sustenance in the afterlife.
  • Ritual objects — amulets, figurines, masks, and items with apotropaic or magical functions.
  • Status symbols — regalia, precious metals, and exotic imports displaying rank and wealth.
  • Offerings — gifts to deities, ancestors, or the dead person from mourners.

Interpretation

Archaeologists caution that grave goods do not straightforwardly reflect the wealth or status of the deceased: they are selected by the living, and may express the mourners' claims, obligations, or ideas about the afterlife as much as the dead person's identity. The presence, absence, and arrangement of goods have been used to infer social ranking (as in the processual archaeology of Lewis Binford and Arthur Saxe), gender roles, craft specialisation, and long-distance exchange. Changes in grave-good assemblages over time can also track religious conversion; the decline of furnished burial in early medieval Europe, for example, accompanied the spread of Christianity.

Legal and ethical issues

The excavation and retention of grave goods raise ethical questions, particularly where burials belong to Indigenous peoples. Legislation such as the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) in the United States requires the return of funerary objects to descendant communities. The commercial trade in looted grave goods is a major driver of archaeological site destruction worldwide.
