Customary law

Customary law is a body of law derived from long-established customs and practices of a community, recognised as binding by its members, rather than from legislation or judicial precedent. Customary law was the foundation of most legal systems before codification and continues to operate alongside state law in many countries, especially in Africa, the Pacific, and in indigenous legal systems.

Characteristics

For a practice to count as customary law it must generally be ancient, continuous, peaceably enjoyed, reasonable, and certain, and it must be followed from a sense of legal obligation (in international law, opinio juris). Customary rules commonly govern marriage, inheritance, land tenure, and dispute resolution within a community, and are often unwritten, preserved through oral tradition and the decisions of elders or chiefs.

In national legal systems

Many constitutions expressly recognise custom: South Africa's constitution requires courts to apply customary law where applicable, subject to the Bill of Rights. In England, local customs could historically be pleaded as exceptions to the common law if they met strict tests of antiquity and certainty. Conflicts between customary norms and statutory human-rights guarantees — especially on gender equality in inheritance — are a recurring theme in modern case law.

International law

Customary international law is one of the principal sources of international law, listed in Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice. It arises from the general practice of states accepted as law, and includes fundamental norms such as the prohibitions of genocide and slavery, some of which are peremptory (jus cogens).
