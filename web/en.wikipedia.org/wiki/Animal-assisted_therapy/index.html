Animal-assisted therapy

Other namesPet therapy
FieldPsychotherapy, rehabilitation
Common animalsDogs, horses, cats

Animal-assisted therapy (AAT) is a goal-oriented intervention in which an animal meeting specific criteria is an integral part of a treatment process, directed by a health or human-service professional. It is distinct from informal animal-assisted activities such as volunteer pet visits.

History

Animals were used therapeutically as early as the eighteenth century at the York Retreat in England. Boris Levinson, who coined the term "pet therapy" in the 1960s after observing his dog's effect on a withdrawn child patient, is often credited with formalising the field. Equine-assisted therapy and hippotherapy developed in parallel in the twentieth century.

Applications

AAT is used with children with autism, patients with dementia, people with post-traumatic stress disorder, and in physical rehabilitation. Reported benefits include reduced anxiety and blood pressure, improved social interaction, and increased motivation in therapy. Dogs are the most common therapy animals, followed by horses.

Evidence

Research suggests positive effects on mood and anxiety, though studies vary in quality and the field continues to debate standardisation, animal welfare, and the strength of evidence.
