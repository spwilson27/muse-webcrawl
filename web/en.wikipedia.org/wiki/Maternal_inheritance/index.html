Maternal inheritance

Maternal inheritance is a form of uniparental inheritance in which a trait or genetic material is transmitted only through the mother. The best-known example is the inheritance of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA): because the egg cell contributes nearly all of the cytoplasm and organelles to the zygote, while the sperm contributes almost none, offspring inherit their mitochondria — and the DNA inside them — essentially exclusively from their mother.

Mechanism

During fertilization, sperm mitochondria may enter the egg but are typically targeted for destruction (a process involving ubiquitination and autophagy), and in any case are vastly outnumbered by the egg's mitochondria. As a result, mtDNA mutations are passed from a mother to all of her children, but only her daughters pass them on to the next generation.

Mitochondrial diseases

Mutations in mtDNA cause a range of disorders, including Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), MELAS, MERRF, and Leigh syndrome. These diseases show maternal inheritance patterns and often display heteroplasmy — a mixture of normal and mutant mtDNA within cells — which makes their severity and inheritance difficult to predict. Mitochondrial replacement therapy ("three-parent IVF") has been developed to prevent transmission of serious mtDNA disease.

Other examples

In plants, chloroplast DNA is maternally inherited in most flowering plants, though paternal or biparental inheritance occurs in some species. Uniparental inheritance of organelles is thought to have evolved to limit conflicts between genomes and the spread of selfish cytoplasmic elements.

Evolutionary genetics

Because mtDNA is inherited as a single non-recombining unit through females, it is widely used to trace maternal lineages, leading to concepts such as "Mitochondrial Eve," the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all living humans.
