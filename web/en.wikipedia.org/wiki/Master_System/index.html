The Master System is an 8-bit third-generation home video game console manufactured by Sega. It was originally a remodeled export version of the Sega Mark III, the third iteration of the SG-1000 series of consoles, released in Japan in 1985. The Master System launched in North America in 1986, followed by Europe in 1987, and Brazil in 1989 through distributor Tec Toy.

History

The Mark III featured a more powerful video display processor than its rivals and backward compatibility with SG-1000 software. For the Western launch the console was redesigned with a futuristic black-and-red casing, bundled with a light gun and combination cartridges, and renamed the Master System. Despite technically superior hardware in some respects — a larger colour palette than the Nintendo Entertainment System — the console was hampered by Nintendo's restrictive third-party licensing practices, which kept most major developers from publishing on Sega's platform.

Market performance

The Master System was commercially unsuccessful against the NES in North America and Japan, selling far fewer units. However, it achieved considerably greater success in Europe, where it outsold or rivalled the NES in several markets, and in Brazil, where Tec Toy continued manufacturing variants for decades, making the console a long-running phenomenon there. Games such as Alex Kidd in Miracle World, Phantasy Star, Wonder Boy, and ports of arcade hits sustained the platform into the early 1990s alongside its successor, the 16-bit Mega Drive/Genesis.

Technical specifications

The system uses a Zilog Z80A CPU at 3.58 MHz, 8 KB of RAM, and 16 KB of video RAM, displaying 64 colours on screen from a palette of 64... (palette of 64 colours with up to 32 simultaneously) at a resolution of 256 × 192 pixels. Software was distributed on ROM cartridges and on the credit-card-sized Sega Card format. Later models, including the Master System II (1990), built Alex Kidd in Miracle World or Sonic the Hedgehog into the console memory.

Legacy

Master System games have been re-released through services such as Nintendo's Virtual Console and various Sega compilations. The console's enduring Brazilian afterlife — with new hardware variants sold into the 2010s — is considered unique in gaming history.
