Bubblegum music

Bubblegum music (also known as bubblegum pop or simply bubblegum) is a genre of pop music originating in the United States in the late 1960s. It is characterised by catchy, simple melodies, repetitive hooks, upbeat lyrics often using food and candy metaphors for love, and slick, producer-driven production aimed at pre-teens and teenagers.

History

The genre was largely created by producers and songwriters rather than by performing bands. Jerry Kasenetz and Jeffry Katz of Super K Productions are widely credited with inventing the sound, scoring hits with studio groups such as the Ohio Express ("Yummy Yummy Yummy", 1968) and the 1910 Fruitgum Company ("Simon Says", 1968). Other landmark bubblegum records include "Sugar, Sugar" (1969) by the Archies — a cartoon group whose hit was one of the best-selling singles of its year — and records by the Banana Splits, the Cuff Links and Tommy Roe ("Dizzy", 1969).

The Monkees, assembled for television, occupied a borderline position between bubblegum and mainstream pop-rock. In Britain, writers such as Nicky Chinn and Mike Chapman adapted the formula with acts like the Sweet, who later moved toward glam rock.

Characteristics and influence

Bubblegum songs typically feature simple chord progressions, handclaps, singalong choruses and danceable rhythms, with sessions played by professional studio musicians. Although dismissed by critics at the time as disposable, the genre's economy and melodic directness influenced punk rock (the Ramones were avowed fans), power pop, new wave and modern teen pop and J-pop/K-pop production models.
