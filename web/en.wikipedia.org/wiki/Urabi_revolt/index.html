The Urabi revolt (Arabic: الثورة العرابية), also known as the Urabi revolution, was a nationalist uprising in Egypt from 1879 to 1882. Led by Colonel Ahmed Urabi, it sought to depose the Khedive Tawfiq Pasha and end British and French influence over the country, which had deepened through control of Egypt's finances following the debt crisis of the 1870s. It was the first manifestation of modern Egyptian nationalism and ended with a British invasion and the beginning of the British occupation of Egypt.

Background

Egypt under Khedive Isma'il accumulated massive debts to European creditors, leading in 1876 to Anglo-French "Dual Control" of Egyptian finances and the imposition of foreign ministers in the cabinet. Native Egyptian officers resented the dominance of Turco-Circassian officers in the army and cuts to soldiers' pay. In February 1879, unpaid officers rioted; in April, Isma'il dismissed the European-dominated ministry, prompting Britain and France to pressure the Ottoman Sultan to depose him in favour of his son Tawfiq.

Course of the revolt

Urabi, an officer of peasant (fellah) origin, emerged as leader of a movement demanding a constitutional government, a larger army, and equality of promotion. In September 1881, Urabi marched with troops to Abdeen Palace and confronted Tawfiq with demands for a parliament and the dismissal of the government — a defining moment summarized in his declaration that Egyptians were "not slaves" and would no longer be inherited. Urabi became Minister of War in early 1882, and a nationalist government under Mahmoud Sami el-Baroudi took office.

British and French warships gathered at Alexandria, and in June 1882 riots in the city killed dozens of Europeans and Egyptians. On 11 July 1882 the British fleet bombarded Alexandria. A British expeditionary force under Sir Garnet Wolseley landed, and on 13 September 1882 decisively defeated Urabi's army at the Battle of Tel el-Kebir. Cairo was occupied, the revolt collapsed, and Urabi was tried and exiled to Ceylon (Sri Lanka), where he remained until 1901.

Consequences

Although Britain repeatedly declared its occupation temporary, British forces remained in Egypt until 1956, and the "veiled protectorate" of the Consul-General, Lord Cromer, dominated Egyptian politics for decades. The revolt is commemorated in Egypt as a foundational episode of national resistance; Urabi's stand at Abdeen Palace remains a celebrated image in Egyptian national memory.
