Furosemide

Furosemide, sold under the brand name Lasix among others, is a loop diuretic medication used to treat fluid build-up (oedema) due to heart failure, liver scarring, or kidney disease, and to treat high blood pressure. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is one of the most widely prescribed diuretics in the world.

Mechanism of action

Furosemide inhibits the Na+-K+-2Cl− cotransporter (NKCC2) in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle in the kidney. By blocking reabsorption of sodium, potassium, and chloride at this site — where roughly a quarter of the filtered sodium load is normally reclaimed — it produces a potent, rapid diuresis. It reaches its site of action by secretion into the tubular lumen via organic anion transporters, which is why higher doses are needed in renal impairment.

Medical uses

Furosemide is first-line therapy for acute decompensated heart failure with volume overload, where intravenous administration relieves pulmonary oedema within minutes to hours. It is also used for oedema in cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome, as an adjunct in hypertension, and in hypercalcaemia. In veterinary medicine it is widely used in dogs, cats, and horses; in racehorses it is administered to reduce exercise-induced pulmonary haemorrhage, a use that remains controversial, and it is prohibited in human sport by WADA as a potential masking agent.

Adverse effects

Common adverse effects include hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hypomagnesaemia, volume depletion, and metabolic alkalosis. High intravenous doses or rapid infusion can cause ototoxicity, sometimes irreversible, particularly in renal failure or with other ototoxic drugs. As a sulfonamide derivative, it may cross-react in patients with sulfa allergy, though evidence for this is limited.

History

Furosemide was developed by Hoechst and introduced in the 1960s (approved in the United States in 1966), quickly displacing the more toxic mercurial diuretics and becoming a mainstay of cardiovascular medicine.
