Obscene Publications Act 1959

The Obscene Publications Act 1959 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that reformed the law of obscenity in England and Wales. It significantly strengthened the protection of literature against suppression while retaining penalties for material deemed obscene, and it remains the principal statute in this area.

Provisions

Section 1 retains the historic test from R v Hicklin (1868): an article is obscene if its effect, taken as a whole, tends to "deprave and corrupt" persons likely to read, see, or hear it. Section 2 makes it an offence to publish an obscene article, whether for gain or not, or to have such an article for publication for gain.

The Act's major innovation was section 4, the "public good" defence: a conviction cannot stand if publication is justified as being for the public good in the interests of science, literature, art, or learning. For the first time, the literary merit of a work — attested by expert witnesses — could be weighed by the jury.

The Lady Chatterley trial

The Act is inextricably associated with the 1960 prosecution of Penguin Books for publishing the unexpurgated text of D. H. Lawrence's novel Lady Chatterley's Lover, one of the first prosecutions under the new law. The trial at the Old Bailey became a landmark cultural event: dozens of literary and academic witnesses defended the novel's merit, and the prosecuting counsel's question — whether it was a book "you would wish your wife or your servants to read" — was widely mocked. The jury acquitted Penguin, and the resulting paperback sold millions of copies.

The Act was supplemented by the Obscene Publications Act 1964, which targeted those who stocked obscene material for sale. Later prosecutions, such as the Oz schoolkids issue trial (1971) and the Inside Linda Lovelace case (1976), showed prosecutions of serious literature becoming increasingly rare, and enforcement concentrated on pornography.
