Insurrection of 10 August 1792

Date10 August 1792
LocationTuileries Palace, Paris, France
ResultRepublican victory; monarchy suspended; Louis XVI imprisoned
BelligerentsParis Commune and fédérés vs. French monarchy
CommandersInsurgent sections vs. Marquis de Mandat (Swiss Guards)

The insurrection of 10 August 1792 was a defining event of the French Revolution, when armed Parisians and provincial fédérés stormed the Tuileries Palace in Paris, massacred the Swiss Guards defending it, and forced the Legislative Assembly to suspend King Louis XVI and imprison the royal family. The uprising effectively ended the French monarchy and paved the way for the proclamation of the First French Republic on 21 September 1792.

Background

France had declared war on Austria in April 1792, and the war went badly from the start. The Brunswick Manifesto, issued by the commander of the allied Prussian and Austrian armies in late July, threatened Paris with destruction if the royal family were harmed; rather than intimidating the capital, it inflamed revolutionary opinion and convinced many Parisians that the king was colluding with the enemy. The sections of Paris organized themselves for action, and fédérés — volunteer National Guardsmen from the provinces, including the famous contingent from Marseille whose marching song became the Marseillaise — poured into the city.

The storming of the Tuileries

On the night of 9–10 August, a new revolutionary Paris Commune seized control of the Hôtel de Ville. In the morning, a vast crowd marched on the Tuileries. The king, warned of the attack, took refuge with his family in the Legislative Assembly. The palace was defended chiefly by some 900 Swiss Guards, the National Guards having largely defected. Fighting broke out — who fired first remains disputed — and the Swiss initially drove the attackers back before Louis, hoping to stop the bloodshed, ordered them to cease fire and withdraw. Cut off and leaderless, hundreds of Swiss were massacred by the crowd, both in the palace and later in prison.

Aftermath

The Assembly suspended the king and summoned a National Convention, elected by universal male suffrage, to decide France's future. Louis XVI and his family were imprisoned in the Temple. The insurrection radicalized the Revolution: within weeks came the September Massacres, the abolition of the monarchy, the proclamation of the Republic, and the road to the king's trial and execution in January 1793.
