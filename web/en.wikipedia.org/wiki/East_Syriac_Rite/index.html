East Syriac Rite

The East Syriac Rite, also called the Edessan Rite, Assyrian Rite, or Persian Rite, is a Christian liturgical rite that uses the East Syriac dialect of the Aramaic language. It originated in Edessa, Mesopotamia, and was historically used in the Church of the East in Persia, from which it spread to Central Asia, China, and India (the Saint Thomas Christians). It is distinguished from the West Syriac Rite used by the Syriac Orthodox and related churches.

Usage

The rite is used today by the Assyrian Church of the East and the Ancient Church of the East (based in Iraq), and by the Chaldean Catholic Church and the Syro-Malabar Church (in India), the latter two in communion with Rome. The principal Eucharistic liturgy is the Liturgy of Addai and Mari, one of the oldest anaphoras in continuous use, traditionally attributed to apostles of Edessa; other anaphoras, of Theodore of Mopsuestia and Nestorius, are used on certain feasts.

Features

The East Syriac liturgy is noted for its numerous hymns (onithas) ascribed to Ephrem the Syrian and Narsai, its cycle of liturgical seasons (including the seasons of Annunciation, Epiphany, Lent, Apostles, Summer, Elijah, Moses, and Dedication), and distinctive practices such as the use of leavened bread preserved through the tradition of the "holy leaven" (malka). The Liturgy of Addai and Mari attracted scholarly attention because its received form lacks an explicit institution narrative; in 2001 the Vatican recognised its validity, facilitating ecumenical relations with the Assyrian Church of the East.
