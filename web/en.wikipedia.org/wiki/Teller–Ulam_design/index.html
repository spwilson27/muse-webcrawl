Teller–Ulam design

TypeThermonuclear weapon design
DesignersStanisław Ulam, Edward Teller
Date1951
First testIvy Mike, 1 November 1952

The Teller–Ulam design is the technical concept behind most modern thermonuclear weapons (hydrogen bombs). It is a two-stage design in which a fission bomb (the primary) generates intense X-radiation that compresses and ignites a separate fusion fuel assembly (the secondary), a process called radiation implosion. The design is named for physicists Stanisław Ulam and Edward Teller, who developed it at Los Alamos in early 1951.

Basic concept

The primary and secondary are enclosed in a heavy radiation case (hohlraum). When the primary detonates, X-rays fill the case and ablate the surface of the secondary's outer pusher/tamper, driving it inward and compressing the fusion fuel — typically lithium deuteride — to extreme densities. A fissile "spark plug" at the centre of the secondary then ignites, heating the compressed fuel from within so that fusion reactions proceed. The energy released can be scaled by adding stages, and the tamper may itself be made of uranium-238, which fissions in the intense neutron flux and often contributes half or more of the total yield.

History

After the first Soviet atomic test in 1949, the United States pressed ahead with the "Super" — a hydrogen bomb concept that calculations by Ulam showed would not work in its original (classical Super) form. Ulam proposed staging and compression by radiation; Teller refined the idea into radiation implosion. The concept was proven first by the Greenhouse George shot (May 1951), an experimental device, and then at full scale by Ivy Mike on 1 November 1952, which yielded 10.4 megatons and vaporized the island of Elugelab. The first deliverable weapon using the design followed in the Castle Bravo test of 1954.

Proliferation

All five declared nuclear-weapon states independently developed equivalent staged designs; details remain among the most closely guarded military secrets, and open literature discusses the concept only in general terms derived from declassified sources and the controversial 1979 Progressive magazine case in the United States.
