Damocles

Damocles is a character in a Greek anecdote, most often associated with Dionysius I of Syracuse, that illustrates the peril that accompanies great power and ostensible good fortune. In the story, Damocles, a courtier, flatters the tyrant Dionysius by praising his wealth and power as the height of happiness. Dionysius offers to trade places for a day so that Damocles may experience it himself.

At the banquet that evening, Damocles enjoys the luxuries of the court — but looks up to see a sharp sword hanging directly above his head, suspended by a single hair of a horse's tail. He immediately begs to be released, having understood that with great fortune comes great and constant danger. The phrase "the sword of Damocles" has since become a byword for an ever-present, looming peril, especially one hanging over those in power.

Sources

The anecdote appears in Cicero's Tusculan Disputations (5.61) and is also mentioned by Horace, Persius and other Roman authors. Its historicity is doubtful; Damocles is not attested outside the moralizing tale.

In culture

The sword of Damocles has been referenced by figures from Shakespeare to U.S. President John F. Kennedy, who used it in a 1961 address to the United Nations to describe the threat of nuclear war hanging over every man, woman and child. The image recurs in literature, political cartoons and popular culture as a symbol of precarious power and impending disaster.
