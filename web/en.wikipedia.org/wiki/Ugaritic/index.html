Ugaritic is an extinct Northwest Semitic language known through the Ugaritic texts discovered by French archaeologists in 1929 at the ancient city of Ugarit (Ras Shamra) in northern Syria. It is regarded as one of the most important discoveries for the study of ancient Semitic languages and religions, and its literature provides a crucial bridge between Mesopotamian traditions and the later Hebrew Bible.

Writing system

Ugaritic was written with a unique cuneiform alphabet of 30 characters, pressed into clay tablets. Unlike the syllabic cuneiform used in Mesopotamia, the Ugaritic script was alphabetic (an abjad), with three signs for the glottal stop combined with the vowels a, i, and u. The order of its letters closely resembles that of the later Phoenician and Hebrew alphabets, making it a key witness to the early history of alphabetic writing.

Literature

The Ugaritic corpus includes administrative, legal, and diplomatic texts alongside major mythological compositions. The most famous is the Baal Cycle, a set of six tablets narrating the struggles of the storm god Baal with Yam (Sea) and Mot (Death). Other major texts include the Legend of Keret and the Tale of Aqhat. The chief deities of the Ugaritic pantheon were El, the aged father of the gods; his consort Athirat (Asherah); Baal; and Anat. These texts illuminate the religious background against which biblical literature developed, sharing poetic forms, divine titles, and imagery.
