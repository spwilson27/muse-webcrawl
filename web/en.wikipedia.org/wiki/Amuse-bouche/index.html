An amuse-bouche (French: [a.myz.buʃ]; plural amuse-bouches or amuses-bouche) is a single, bite-sized hors d'œuvre served free of charge before a meal in a restaurant. The term is French for "mouth amuser". Unlike appetisers, amuse-bouches are not ordered from a menu; they are selected by the chef and served to all diners at the table to prepare the palate and offer a glimpse of the chef's style.

Description

The practice arose with the nouvelle cuisine movement in France and became widespread in fine dining during the 1980s. An amuse-bouche is typically small and intensely flavoured — a few mouthfuls at most — and may be served in a spoon, a small glass, or on a miniature plate. It is often accompanied by a suitable wine. Because it is unsolicited, it also serves as a vehicle for the kitchen to showcase ingredients or techniques not represented elsewhere on the menu.

The related term amuse-gueule is an older, slightly more rustic synonym; gueule refers to the mouth or muzzle of an animal and is considered less refined in polite usage, which is why amuse-bouche became the standard term in restaurants.
