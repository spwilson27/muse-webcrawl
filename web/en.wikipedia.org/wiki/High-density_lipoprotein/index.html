High-density lipoprotein

High-density lipoprotein (HDL) is one of the five major groups of lipoproteins, particles composed of lipids and proteins that transport fats through the blood. HDL particles are the smallest and densest of the lipoproteins because they contain the highest proportion of protein. HDL is popularly known as "good cholesterol" because it removes excess cholesterol from tissues and arteries and carries it back to the liver, a process called reverse cholesterol transport.

Structure and metabolism

HDL particles are assembled in the liver and intestine around the protein apolipoprotein A-I. Discoidal nascent HDL picks up free cholesterol from peripheral tissues via the transporter ABCA1; the enzyme LCAT esterifies the cholesterol, maturing the particle into a spherical form. HDL delivers cholesterol to the liver directly or transfers it to other lipoproteins via the CETP protein.

Health significance

Epidemiological studies associate higher HDL cholesterol levels with a lower risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease. Low HDL is a component of the metabolic syndrome. However, clinical trials of drugs that raise HDL — such as CETP inhibitors and niacin — generally failed to reduce cardiovascular events, leading researchers to question whether HDL level itself is causal or mainly a marker, and to focus instead on HDL function and particle quality.

Measurement

HDL cholesterol is measured as part of a standard lipid panel. Guidelines commonly consider levels below 40 mg/dL (men) or 50 mg/dL (women) as low, and 60 mg/dL or above as protective.
