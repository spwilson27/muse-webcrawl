Didier Patrick Queloz (born 23 February 1966) is a Swiss astronomer and a professor at the University of Geneva and the University of Cambridge. As Michel Mayor's doctoral student, he built the data analysis for the ELODIE spectrograph and in 1994–95 detected the periodic radial-velocity signal of 51 Pegasi — identifying it as a planet, 51 Pegasi b, the first Exoplanet around a Sun-like star. The discovery earned him a share of the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics at age 53, among the youngest physics laureates.

Queloz later led work in transit photometry (the NGTS facility in Chile) and Earth-twin searches, chaired Cambridge's exoplanet research, and directed the Leverhulme Centre for Life in the Universe — reflecting the field's shift from finding planets to characterising them and searching for biosignatures.

The discovery

Assigned to test a new spectrograph, Queloz wrote analysis software so sensitive that he initially suspected an instrumental error when 51 Pegasi's velocity swung by ~55 m/s every 4.23 days. Checked and rechecked through 1995, the signal held: a planet of at least half Jupiter's mass orbiting closer than Mercury — a configuration that forced theorists to invent planetary migration.
