The coccyx (plural coccyges), commonly called the tailbone, is the final segment of the vertebral column in all apes, including humans, and in other mammals without tails. It consists of three to five fused vestigial vertebrae below the sacrum and is regarded as the remnant of a vestigial tail — though it is far from useless.

Structure and function

The coccyx is a small triangular bone. It articulates with the sacrum at the sacrococcygeal joint and provides attachment for the gluteus maximus, the coccygeus muscle, the anococcygeal ligament and muscles of the pelvic floor (levator ani). Together with the two ischial tuberosities, it forms a tripod that bears weight when a person sits leaning back.

Clinical significance

Pain in the coccyx, called coccydynia, commonly follows a fall onto the buttocks or prolonged sitting, and occasionally childbirth. Fracture or dislocation can occur in trauma. The name derives from the Greek kokkyx, "cuckoo", because the bone was thought to resemble a cuckoo's beak.
