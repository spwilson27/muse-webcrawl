Probate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Probate is the judicial process by which a will is proved valid (or invalid) in a court of law and accepted as the true last testament of a deceased person. More broadly, the term denotes the legal process of administering a deceased person's estate: resolving claims, paying debts and taxes, and distributing property to heirs and beneficiaries. In England and Wales the court issues a grant of probate to named executors; where there is no valid will, letters of administration are granted instead.

Process

Typically, the executor named in the will applies to the probate court. The court verifies the will's authenticity, inventories and appraises the estate's assets, notifies creditors, supervises payment of debts and taxes, and finally authorises distribution according to the will — or, in intestacy, according to statutory rules of succession.

Intestacy

When a person dies without a valid will, the probate court appoints an administrator, and the estate is divided among the surviving spouse, children and other relatives in an order fixed by local law.

Avoiding probate

Because probate can be slow and public, estate planners often use joint ownership, beneficiary designations, payable-on-death accounts and living trusts so that assets pass outside probate. Many jurisdictions offer simplified procedures for small estates.

Historical origin

The word derives from the Latin probatum, "that which has been proved". In England, jurisdiction over wills long belonged to ecclesiastical courts before passing to the Court of Probate in 1857.
