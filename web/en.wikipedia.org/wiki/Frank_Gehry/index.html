Frank Gehry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Guggenheim Bilbao: titanium scales catching the Nervión river light, the museum that renamed an effect]

Frank Owen Gehry (born Frank Owen Goldberg; 28 February 1929) is a Canadian-American architect and designer, among the most celebrated and influential architects of the contemporary era. His buildings — sculptural, titanium-clad, seemingly caught mid-motion — include the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (1997), the Walt Disney Concert Hall in Los Angeles (2003), the Louis Vuitton Foundation in Paris (2014), the Dancing House in Prague (1996) and his own Gehry Residence in Santa Monica (1978), the chain-link and corrugated-metal house that announced him.

Winner of the Pritzker Prize (1989), the National Medal of Arts and the Presidential Medal of Freedom (2016), Toronto-born and Los Angeles-raised, Gehry studied at USC and Harvard and founded his practice in 1962, working first in commercial architecture and cardboard furniture (his Easy Edges Wiggle chair, 1972) before the Residence made his name.

The Bilbao effect

Bilbao's Guggenheim — built with CATIA aerospace software to realise its impossible curves — regenerated a declining industrial city so completely that 'the Bilbao effect' entered urban policy worldwide: mayors ever since have sought the icon-building miracle. Gehry's own view of the copies is ambivalent; his office's later work (the Disney Hall's resolved acoustics, the Fondation's glass sails, 8 Spruce Street in New York) balances sculptural freedom with urban argument.

Reception

To admirers he is the artist who freed architecture from the box; to detractors (and to the Simpsons episode that had him crumple a letter into a building) a formalist of expensive gestures. His nineties and hundreds of built works settle the argument differently: no living architect has changed more city skylines or more clients' fortunes.
