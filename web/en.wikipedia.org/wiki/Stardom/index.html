Stardom

Stardom is the state of being a "star" — a person of great fame and public recognition, typically a performer in film, theatre, music, or sport. The word may also refer collectively to stars as a class ("the world of stardom"). This article surveys the concept; it is distinct from Stardom the Japanese professional wrestling promotion (World Wonder Ring Stardom) and from various films and albums of the same name.

History of the concept

While celebrated performers existed in earlier centuries — the actors of the eighteenth-century London stage and operatic castrati and divas drew crowds and fortunes — stardom as a mass-media phenomenon is usually dated to the early twentieth-century film industry. The Hollywood studio system named, promoted, and typecast "movie stars", beginning with figures such as Mary Pickford and Charlie Chaplin, whose faces became internationally recognizable through cinema and fan magazines. Television, popular music, and later social media each produced new kinds of stars and new pathways to fame.

Star studies

Within film and cultural studies, "star studies" — founded by works such as Richard Dyer's Stars (1979) — analyses the star as a constructed image combining film roles, publicity, promotion, and criticism, and examines how stars embody social values and ideologies. Sociologists and economists of "superstars" have studied the winner-take-all economics (notably Sherwin Rosen's 1981 analysis) by which small differences in talent or exposure produce enormous differences in reward.

Effects and criticism

Stardom brings wealth and influence but is also associated with intense public scrutiny, loss of privacy, and pressures that can damage mental health. Critics of celebrity culture argue that fame is increasingly detached from achievement — the condition captured in Daniel Boorstin's description of the celebrity as "a person who is known for his well-knownness".
