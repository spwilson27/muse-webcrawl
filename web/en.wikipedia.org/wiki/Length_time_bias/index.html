Length time bias

Length time bias (also called length bias) is a form of selection bias that arises in screening studies for disease. Because screening tends to detect slowly progressing cases more readily than rapidly progressing ones, screen-detected cases appear to have better outcomes even if the screening itself provides no benefit.

Mechanism

Any screening test applied at a single point in time samples prevalent cases. Conditions with a long preclinical (detectable but asymptomatic) phase are far more likely to be caught by such a snapshot than conditions that move quickly from onset to symptoms or death. The detected pool is therefore enriched with indolent disease, biasing survival comparisons in favour of screening.

Example

In cancer screening, a tumour that grows slowly may be detectable for years before causing symptoms, whereas an aggressive tumour may arise and spread between screening rounds. Comparing survival of screen-detected patients with that of clinically detected patients then mixes this sampling effect with any real treatment benefit, overstating the apparent value of the test.
