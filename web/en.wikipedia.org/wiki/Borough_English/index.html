Borough English

Borough English is a customary form of inheritance in parts of England whereby real property descended not to the eldest son, as under primogeniture, but to the youngest son — a practice technically termed ultimogeniture. The custom prevailed in many boroughs and manors, particularly in Kent, Sussex, Surrey and Middlesex, and in the City of London for freemen's lands.

[ Image: Medieval English manor court roll recording a customary descent to the youngest son ]

Origin and rationale

The origins of the custom are obscure. A traditional explanation held that the youngest son, being the least able to provide for himself and likely still to be at home caring for aged parents, was favoured; another theory connects the custom with pre-Conquest or "folkland" tenures. In boroughs the rule often applied also to burgage tenements, and if there were no sons the property might pass to the youngest daughter, or equally among daughters.

Decline and abolition

As a local custom, Borough English survived only where it could be proved as an immemorial usage of a particular manor or borough. It was progressively displaced by voluntary conveyancing and finally abolished, along with gavelkind and other customary descents, by the property legislation of 1922–1925 (the Law of Property Act 1922 taking effect on 1 January 1926), which imposed a uniform system of succession.
