Lucius Cassius Dio Cocceianus (c. 155 – c. 235), known in English as Cassius Dio, was a Roman historian and senator of Greek origin who wrote in Greek. He published 80 volumes of the history of ancient Rome, beginning with the arrival of Aeneas in Italy. The volumes documented the subsequent founding of Rome (753 BC), the formation of the Republic (509 BC), and the creation of the Empire (27 BC) up to 229 AD, during the reign of Severus Alexander. Written in Attic Greek, Dio's work is one of the most important surviving narrative sources for Roman history, particularly for the late Republic and the first two centuries of the Empire.

Life

Dio was born and raised at Nicaea in Bithynia, northwestern Anatolia. He was the son of Cassius Apronianus, a Roman senator who served as governor of Dalmatia and Cilicia. Dio arrived in Rome around 180 AD and pursued a successful senatorial career: he was praetor, served as governor of Africa and of Dalmatia, and held the consulship twice — first as suffect consul around 220, and then as ordinary consul in 229 alongside the emperor Severus Alexander. His strict discipline as governor of Pannonia Superior made him unpopular with the Praetorian Guard, and he retired to his native Bithynia, where he died around 235.

Roman History

Dio's Roman History comprised 80 books, written over roughly 22 years. He spent a decade collecting material for the period up to the death of Septimius Severus, and twelve years composing the work. Only books 36–60 survive in substantially complete form, covering 68 BC to AD 47; the remaining books survive in fragments and in the epitome made by the Byzantine monk John Xiphilinus in the 11th century, together with excerpts preserved in Byzantine collections.

For the period from 100 BC to the death of Marcus Aurelius, Dio is often the sole or principal surviving narrative source. His account reflects a senator's perspective: distrustful of emperors who diminished the Senate, admiring of firm government, and attentive to omens and portents. Modern historians value his detail while treating his speeches — composed by Dio himself in the manner of Thucydides — as rhetorical reconstructions rather than transcripts.
