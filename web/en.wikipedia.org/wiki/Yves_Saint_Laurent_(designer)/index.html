Yves Henri Donat Mathieu-Saint-Laurent (1 August 1936 – 1 June 2008), known professionally as Yves Saint Laurent, was a French fashion designer who founded the luxury fashion house Yves Saint Laurent in 1961. He is regarded as one of the greatest designers of the 20th century, credited with popularising ready-to-wear, the women's tuxedo suit ("Le Smoking"), and drawing inspiration from art and non-Western cultures into haute couture.

Early life and Dior

Born in Oran, Algeria, to a French family, Saint Laurent moved to Paris as a teenager. After winning a design competition, he was introduced to Christian Dior and hired as his assistant in 1955. On Dior's sudden death in 1957, the 21-year-old Saint Laurent became head designer of the house; his 1958 "Trapeze" collection saved the company from financial ruin and made him an international star.

Founding his house

Conscripted into the French army in 1960, Saint Laurent suffered a breakdown and was dismissed by Dior. With his partner Pierre Bergé, he founded his own house in 1961, funded by American backer J. Mack Robinson. His 1960s collections were revolutionary: the Mondrian dress (1965), inspired by Piet Mondrian's paintings; the first tuxedo for women, "Le Smoking" (1966); safari jackets; and see-through blouses. In 1966 he launched Saint Laurent Rive Gauche, a landmark ready-to-wear line that democratised designer fashion.

Later career and legacy

Saint Laurent continued to define Paris fashion through the 1970s — the era of his bestselling Opium perfume (1977) — while struggling with addiction and depression. He was the first living designer honoured with a retrospective at the Metropolitan Museum of Art (1983). He retired from haute couture in 2002, and the house's ready-to-wear continues under the Saint Laurent name. He died of brain cancer in Paris in 2008; his and Bergé's art collection was sold in a record-breaking 2009 auction.
