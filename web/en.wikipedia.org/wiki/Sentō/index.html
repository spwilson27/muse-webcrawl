Sentō

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sentō (銭湯) is a type of Japanese communal bathhouse where customers pay for entrance. Traditionally these bathhouses are quite plain and utilitarian, with a tall barrier separating the sexes within one large room, a minimum of lined-up faucets on both sides, and a single large bath for the mixed-seating (sex-segregated) attendees. Since the second half of the 20th century, these communal places have been dwindling in number as more and more Japanese residences have baths. Some Japanese find social importance in going to public baths, out of the theory that physical closeness brings emotional closeness, known in Japanese as "skinship" (sukinshippu, スキンシップ).

Layout

A typical sentō has an entrance area with lockers for shoes, a changing room (datsuiba) with coin lockers and often a bandai counter where the attendant sits, and the bathing area itself. Bathers wash thoroughly at rows of taps and showers before entering the large, deep, hot bath; entering the water unwashed is considered a serious breach of etiquette. Many sentō feature a painted mural — traditionally of Mount Fuji — on the wall of the bathing room, electric baths (denki-buro), jacuzzis, and saunas.

History

Public bathhouses appeared in Japan in the Edo period; the first sentō in Edo (Tokyo) opened in 1591. Their numbers peaked in the late 1960s, when Tokyo alone had over 2,600, before declining steeply as home bathing became universal. The word derives from the price once charged: one sen coin for a bath (tō/yu, hot water).

Sentō and onsen

Unlike an onsen, which uses naturally hot spring water, a sentō heats ordinary tap water. Sentō with special features, multiple baths, and leisure facilities are marketed as "super sentō".
