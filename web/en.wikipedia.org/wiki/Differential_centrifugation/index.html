Differential centrifugation

Differential centrifugation (also known as differential pelleting) is a common procedure in biochemistry and cell biology, which is used to separate cellular components by their mass and sedimentation coefficient using a centrifuge.

Principles

In a suspension of particles, gravity causes larger and denser particles to sediment faster. A centrifuge increases the effective force, allowing separation of components that would otherwise take very long to settle. In the typical procedure, a tissue sample is first homogenised to break the cell membranes and mix up the contents. The homogenate is then subjected to repeated centrifugations, each time removing the pellet and increasing the centrifugal force applied to the supernatant.

Typical fractionation sequence

At low speed (around 1,000 × g), nuclei and unbroken cells pellet. At medium speed (around 10,000 × g), mitochondria, lysosomes and peroxisomes sediment. At high speed in an ultracentrifuge (100,000 × g or more), microsomes — fragments of endoplasmic reticulum and plasma membrane — pellet, leaving the cytosol in the final supernatant; even higher speeds and longer times can pellet ribosomes.

Applications

Differential centrifugation is a first step in isolating organelles for biochemical study. The technique was central to the work of Albert Claude, Christian de Duve and George Palade, who shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning the structural and functional organisation of the cell.
