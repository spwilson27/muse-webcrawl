The Slavery Abolition Act 1833 (3 & 4 Will. 4. c. 73) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom which provided for the gradual abolition of slavery in most parts of the British Empire. It was passed by Earl Grey's government and expanded the jurisdiction of the Slave Trade Act 1807, which had banned the Atlantic slave trade but not slavery itself. The Act freed more than 800,000 enslaved Africans in the Caribbean, South Africa, and Canada.

Background

By the 1820s, a mass abolition movement — combining parliamentary campaigners such as William Wilberforce and Thomas Fowell Buxton with petition campaigns, sugar boycotts, and, crucially, resistance by enslaved people themselves, including the Baptist War in Jamaica (1831–32) — had made the continuation of slavery politically untenable. The Reform Act 1832 weakened the West India interest in Parliament, opening the way for abolition legislation in 1833.

Provisions

The Act provided for the emancipation of enslaved people in most British colonies, but with two major concessions to slave owners. First, freed people over the age of six were redesignated as "apprentices" and required to work for their former masters for up to six years (reduced in practice; apprenticeship ended everywhere by 1 August 1838). Second, slave owners received £20 million in compensation — roughly 40% of the government's annual budget — raised as a loan that British taxpayers did not finish repaying until 2015. The Act did not apply to territories held by the East India Company, Ceylon, or Saint Helena; slavery there was addressed by the Indian Slavery Act 1843.

Legacy

1 August 1834, when the Act came into force, is commemorated as Emancipation Day in several Caribbean countries and in Canada. The compensation records, documenting awards to slave owners, have become an important historical resource, notably through the University College London Legacies of British Slavery project. The Act itself was repealed by the Statute Law (Repeals) Act 1998 as spent.
