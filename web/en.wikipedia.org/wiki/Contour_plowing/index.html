Contour plowing

Contour plowing (also contour farming or contour ploughing) is the farming practice of ploughing and planting across a slope following its elevation contour lines. The furrows and crop rows created act as a series of small dams that slow the runoff of rainwater, giving water time to infiltrate the soil and preventing it from washing topsoil downslope. It is one of the simplest and most widely used soil-conservation practices.

Effectiveness

Ploughing up and down a slope creates channels that accelerate erosion; ploughing on the contour can reduce soil loss by roughly half on moderate slopes, with the greatest benefit on slopes of about 2–10%. Effectiveness diminishes on very steep land and during extreme rainfall, when contour ridges can be breached and concentrated overflow can cause severe gully erosion along the break. For such situations contour plowing is combined with other practices such as terracing, strip cropping, grassed waterways and conservation buffers.

History

Although ancient Phoenicians and other early farmers practised forms of contour cultivation, the technique was promoted systematically in the United States during the 1930s. After the Dust Bowl, the Soil Conservation Service (now the Natural Resources Conservation Service) taught contour plowing, along with strip farming and cover crops, to farmers across the Great Plains and the Midwest; the curving patterns it produces in fields became an iconic landscape feature visible from the air. Thomas Jefferson had already experimented with contour ploughing on his Virginia plantation in the early 19th century after learning of its erosion benefits.

Related practice

A closely related idea is the keyline design system developed in Australia by P. A. Yeomans, which ploughs slightly off the contour to redistribute water from wet valleys towards dry ridges.
