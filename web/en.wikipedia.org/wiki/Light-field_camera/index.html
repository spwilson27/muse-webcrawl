Light-field camera

A light-field camera, also called a plenoptic camera, is a camera that captures information about the light field emanating from a scene — not only the intensity of light at each point, as a conventional camera records, but also the direction in which the light rays travel. Because both position and direction are recorded, a light-field photograph can be computationally refocused after it has been taken, and its viewpoint can be shifted slightly.

Design

The most common design places an array of microlenses between the main lens and the image sensor. Each microlens produces a small image of the main lens's aperture on the pixels behind it, so each pixel records light arriving from a particular direction. Software then reconstructs images focused at different depths by combining rays appropriately. The trade-off is spatial resolution: the sensor's pixels are divided among directional samples, so the final refocused image has far fewer pixels than the raw sensor count.

History

The concept descends from integral photography, proposed by Gabriel Lippmann in 1908. Modern research prototypes were developed in the 1990s and 2000s, notably at Stanford University by Ren Ng and colleagues. The company Lytro, founded by Ng, launched a consumer light-field camera in 2012 and a more advanced model, the Illum, in 2014; Lytro ceased operations in 2018. Other companies, such as Raytrix, have produced plenoptic cameras for industrial and scientific use, and light-field techniques are used in microscopy (light-field microscopy) to capture volumetric data in a single exposure.

Applications

Post-capture refocusing, depth estimation, extended depth of field, and viewpoint shifting are the characteristic applications. Light-field capture is also relevant to virtual and augmented reality, where dense directional information supports realistic rendering of real scenes.
