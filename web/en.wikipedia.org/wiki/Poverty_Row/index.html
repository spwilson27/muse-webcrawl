Poverty Row

Poverty Row studios around Hollywood's Gower Street produced films on minimal budgets

Poverty Row was a term used in Hollywood from the 1920s through the 1950s for the small, low-budget film studios that produced B movies: cheap westerns, serials, crime pictures, comedies, and horror films shot in a few days on rented sets with little-known actors. The name referred both to the studios' modest circumstances and, for several of them, to their actual location around Gower Street in Hollywood, a shabby district remote from the great studio lots.

The studios

The best-remembered Poverty Row companies were Monogram Pictures and Producers Releasing Corporation (PRC), along with Republic Pictures — the most capable and prosperous of the group — and a shifting population of smaller outfits such as Grand National, Tiffany, and Mascot. Many survived by renting stages by the day, reusing standing sets and stock footage, and working with ageing stars or newcomers whose salaries were low. Directors such as Edgar G. Ulmer, who made the noir classic Detour (1945) for PRC, occasionally transcended the constraints to produce works now regarded as masterpieces.

Production and distribution

Poverty Row films were typically sold to independent theatres and regional exchanges on a "states' rights" basis, playing as the lower half of double bills. Budgets could be as low as a few tens of thousands of dollars, schedules as short as a week, and running times often under an hour. Genres were formulaic and prolific: series westerns with singing cowboys, Charlie Chan and Bowery Boys entries at Monogram, jungle pictures, and exploitation horror.

Decline and legacy

The B-movie market collapsed in the 1950s under competition from television and the dissolution of the studio system's block booking after the 1948 Paramount Decrees. Monogram transformed itself into Allied Artists; Republic turned to television production; PRC had already been absorbed. The Poverty Row output, long dismissed, has been reappraised by critics for its economy, speed, and occasional brilliance, and its methods anticipated later independent and direct-to-video production.
