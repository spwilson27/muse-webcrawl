Rabies is a viral disease that causes encephalitis in humans and other mammals. It was historically referred to as hydrophobia ("fear of water") because its symptoms include panic when presented with liquids to drink. Early symptoms can include fever and abnormal sensations at the site of exposure, followed by violent movements, uncontrolled excitement, fear of water, an inability to move parts of the body, confusion, and loss of consciousness. Once symptoms appear, the result is virtually always death. The time between contracting the disease and the start of symptoms is usually one to three months, but can vary from less than one week to more than one year.

Cause and transmission

Rabies is caused by lyssaviruses, including the rabies virus and Australian bat lyssavirus. It is spread when an infected animal bites or scratches a human or other animal; saliva from an infected animal can also transmit rabies if it comes into contact with the eyes, mouth, or an open wound. Dogs are responsible for up to 99% of human cases worldwide. In the Americas, bats are the most common source of human infections.

After exposure, the virus travels along peripheral nerves toward the central nervous system, a journey that accounts for the long incubation period. Once it reaches the brain it causes inflammation, and it then spreads to the salivary glands, enabling further transmission.

Prevention and treatment

Rabies is vaccine-preventable. Post-exposure prophylaxis — thorough wound washing, a series of rabies vaccine doses, and, where indicated, rabies immunoglobulin — is highly effective if given before symptoms develop. Controlling rabies in dogs through mass vaccination campaigns is the principal strategy for eliminating human deaths. Louis Pasteur and Émile Roux developed the first rabies vaccine in 1885, famously used to treat the boy Joseph Meister.

Once clinical symptoms appear, treatment is essentially ineffective, with only a handful of documented survivors, some treated under the experimental Milwaukee protocol. Rabies causes about 59,000 deaths per year, mainly in Africa and Asia.

History

Rabies has been described since at least 2000 BCE; the Mesopotamian Code of Eshnunna imposed fines on owners of rabid dogs. The disease's name may derive from the Latin rabies, "madness".
