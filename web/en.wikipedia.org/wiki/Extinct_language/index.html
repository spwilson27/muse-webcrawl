An extinct language is a language that no longer has any speakers, especially where it leaves no living descendants. A language becomes extinct through language death, the process by which a community of speakers gradually shifts to another language until the original tongue is no longer used. By contrast, a dead language such as Latin may continue to be studied and used for liturgical or scholarly purposes while having no native speakers; "extinct" is sometimes reserved for languages about which nothing, or almost nothing, survives.

Causes

Languages die when their speakers are absorbed into larger linguistic communities through conquest, colonisation, economic pressure, urbanisation or the prestige of a dominant language in education and media. The process usually runs through generations of bilingualism: children learn the dominant language as their primary tongue, and the ancestral language retreats to ever narrower domains until the last fluent speakers die. Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, a large share are endangered; linguists estimate a language falls silent about every few weeks.

Examples

Well-documented extinct languages include Sumerian, Akkadian, Gothic, Etruscan and the Dalmatian language, whose last speaker, Tuone Udaina, died in 1898. Many languages of the Americas and Australia vanished following European colonisation, some recorded only in wordlists. Ubykh, a Northwest Caucasian language famed for its huge consonant inventory, died with Tevfik Esenç in 1992.

Revival

Extinction is not always permanent. Language revitalization has returned Hebrew to everyday native use — the outstanding success case — and has produced new speakers of Cornish and Manx, both once considered extinct. Such revivals depend on written records and on community commitment.
