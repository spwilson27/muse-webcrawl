La Reforma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

La Reforma (Spanish for "The Reform") was a period of liberal reform in Mexico between roughly 1855 and 1876, during which the Liberal Party, led by figures including Benito Juárez, Miguel Lerdo de Tejada and Melchor Ocampo, sought to modernise Mexico by ending the legal privileges (fueros) of the Catholic Church and the army, separating church and state, and creating a society of individual citizens and private property.

Reform Laws

The reforms were enacted in a series of measures known collectively as the Reform Laws (Leyes de Reforma). The Juárez Law (1855) abolished separate military and ecclesiastical courts; the Lerdo Law (1856) required corporations, including the Church and indigenous communities, to sell land not directly used for their purposes; the Iglesias Law limited parish fees. The liberal Constitution of 1857 incorporated these principles, provoking conservative resistance that escalated into the Reform War (1858–1861), won by the liberals under Juárez.

Later reforms and conflict

During and after the war further laws nationalised Church property, established civil marriage and registries, and guaranteed freedom of worship. The reforms formed part of the backdrop to the French intervention and the Second Mexican Empire (1862–1867). Many reform principles were carried into the Constitution of 1917, and the church–state settlement remained a central theme of Mexican politics into the twentieth century, including the Cristero War of the 1920s.
