Weightlessness

Weightlessness is the complete or near-complete absence of the sensation of weight, i.e., zero apparent weight. It is experienced when there are no contact forces acting on a body, as in free fall. The environment in which this occurs is often called microgravity, although strictly the gravitational field in low Earth orbit is about 90% as strong as at the surface; objects in orbit are weightless because they are continuously falling around the Earth.

Physics

Weight is normally felt through the normal force exerted by a surface. In free fall, gravity acts essentially uniformly on a body and its surroundings, so no internal stresses produce the sensation of weight. Einstein's equivalence principle states that a freely falling frame is locally indistinguishable from one far from any gravitational field. In practice, tidal (gravity-gradient) forces, atmospheric drag and internal vibrations leave tiny residual accelerations — hence "microgravity", typically around a millionth of Earth's gravity aboard the International Space Station.

Ways of experiencing weightlessness

On Earth, brief periods of weightlessness can be produced by drop towers, by aircraft flying parabolic arcs (such as NASA's KC-135 "Vomit Comet" and commercial zero-gravity flights), and by suborbital rockets. Sustained weightlessness occurs in orbital flight and on interplanetary trajectories.

Effects on the human body

Prolonged weightlessness causes space adaptation syndrome, redistribution of body fluids, cardiovascular deconditioning, loss of bone density (about 1–2% per month in load-bearing bones) and muscle atrophy. Astronauts counter these with exercise regimens, and most effects reverse after return to Earth, though vision changes associated with raised intracranial pressure (spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome) remain under study.
