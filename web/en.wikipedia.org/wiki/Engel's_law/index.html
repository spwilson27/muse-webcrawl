Engel's law

Engel's law is an economic observation stating that, as a family's income increases, the percentage of income spent on food decreases, even though the absolute amount spent on food generally rises. It was formulated by the German statistician Ernst Engel (1821–1896), who published it in 1857 based on studies of household budgets in Saxony and Belgium.

Explanation

Food is a necessity with limited scope for expanded consumption: once basic nutritional needs are met, additional income is directed toward other goods and services — clothing, housing, education, recreation and luxuries. The proportion of expenditure devoted to food, called the Engel coefficient, is therefore used as an indicator of a household's or a nation's standard of living: the lower the coefficient, the wealthier the population.

Applications

Engel's law underlies the long-run decline of agriculture's share of employment and output as economies develop, and it informs welfare measurement and poverty lines. The United Nations uses Engel coefficients in classifying living standards, and the law has been confirmed across many countries and periods, although the food share also depends on prices, culture and household composition.
