Cyrus Cylinder

Cyrus Cylinder
Material: baked clay
Date: c. 539–530 BCE
Created: Babylon, after Cyrus the Great's conquest
Current location: British Museum, London
[Image: The Cyrus Cylinder, a barrel-shaped clay inscription in Akkadian cuneiform]

The Cyrus Cylinder is an ancient clay cylinder, now broken into fragments, inscribed with a declaration in Akkadian cuneiform script in the name of Cyrus the Great, founder of the Achaemenid Empire. It was issued after the Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE and deposited in the foundations of the Esagila temple.

Content

The text denounces the last Babylonian king, Nabonidus, as an impious incompetent, and presents Cyrus as chosen by the god Marduk to restore order. It describes Cyrus's peaceful entry into Babylon, his restoration of temples and cults, the return of divine statues and displaced peoples to their sanctuaries, and repairs to Babylon's walls. The document belongs to a long Mesopotamian tradition of royal foundation inscriptions and parallels the biblical accounts of Cyrus allowing exiled peoples, including the Jews, to return home.

Discovery

The cylinder was excavated at Babylon in 1879 by Hormuzd Rassam for the British Museum and identified by Henry Rawlinson. A further fragment identified in 2010 at Yale enabled more of the text to be read. It has been displayed in Tehran and elsewhere on loan.

Modern interpretation

Since the 1970s the cylinder has often been popularly described as an early "charter of human rights," a reading promoted under the last Shah of Iran and reflected in a replica displayed at the United Nations. Most scholars regard that description as anachronistic: the text is a conventional royal inscription in Mesopotamian style, though its policies of religious tolerance and repatriation were notable statecraft. It remains an important source for Cyrus's reign and for Persian imperial ideology.
