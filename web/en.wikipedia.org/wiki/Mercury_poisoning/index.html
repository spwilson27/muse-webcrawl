Mercury poisoning is a type of metal poisoning due to exposure to mercury or its compounds. Mercury is a heavy metal that is toxic to the nervous system, kidneys, lungs and immune system. The symptoms depend strongly on the chemical form: organic methylmercury chiefly damages the brain and developing nervous system; inhaled elemental mercury vapour injures the lungs and central nervous system; inorganic mercury salts mainly damage the kidneys and gastrointestinal tract.

Causes and exposure

The most common route of exposure in the general population is eating fish and shellfish contaminated with methylmercury, which accumulates up aquatic food chains; large predatory fish such as shark, swordfish, king mackerel and tilefish carry the highest levels. Occupational exposure historically occurred in chloralkali plants, gold mining using mercury amalgamation (still a major source in artisanal mining), and the felt-hat industry, where mercuric nitrate caused the tremor and psychiatric symptoms known as erethism — the origin of the phrase "mad as a hatter". Broken thermometers and fluorescent lamps are minor household sources.

Signs and symptoms

Chronic methylmercury poisoning causes paraesthesia (tingling) of the hands and feet, constriction of the visual fields, hearing loss, slurred speech and ataxia; severe cases progress to coma and death. Prenatal exposure is particularly damaging, producing developmental delay and cerebral palsy-like symptoms even when the mother is little affected. Acute elemental vapour exposure causes cough, breathlessness and a chemical pneumonitis, while chronic vapour exposure produces tremor, irritability and memory problems (erethism).

Notable incidents

The most famous episode is Minamata disease, identified in 1956 in Minamata, Japan, where methylmercury discharged by the Chisso chemical plant contaminated the bay's seafood, ultimately killing or disabling thousands. A similar outbreak occurred at Niigata (1965). In Iraq in 1971–72, seed grain treated with a methylmercury fungicide was baked into bread, hospitalising over 6,000 people and killing at least 459.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention relies on dietary advice (especially for pregnant women), workplace controls and global regulation, including the Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013), which phases down mercury use and emissions. Treatment is mainly removal from exposure; chelation therapy with agents such as DMSA or DMPS is used selectively, since evidence of benefit is limited once neurological damage is established.
