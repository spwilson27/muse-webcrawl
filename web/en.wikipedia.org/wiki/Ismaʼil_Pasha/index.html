Isma'il Pasha

Isma'il Pasha (Arabic: إسماعيل باشا Ismā'īl Bāshā; 12 January 1830 – 2 March 1895), known as Ismail the Magnificent, was the Khedive of Egypt and conqueror of Sudan from 1863 to 1879, when he was removed at the behest of Great Britain and France. Sharing the ambitious outlook of his grandfather, Muhammad Ali Pasha, he greatly modernized Egypt and Sudan during his reign, investing heavily in industrial and economic development, urbanisation, and the expansion of the country's boundaries in Africa.

His philosophy can be glimpsed in a statement he made in 1879: "My country is no longer in Africa; we are now part of Europe. It is therefore natural for us to abandon our former ways and to adopt a new system adapted to our social conditions." However, his grand projects — above all the celebrations surrounding the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 — were financed by ruinous foreign borrowing. By 1876 Egypt was effectively bankrupt, and Isma'il's loss of control over his country's finances led directly to his deposition and, soon after, to the British occupation of Egypt in 1882.

Isma'il Pasha, Khedive of Egypt and Sudan from 1863 to 1879.

Early life and accession

Born in Cairo, Isma'il was the second son of Ibrahim Pasha and the grandson of Muhammad Ali, the founder of modern Egypt. He was educated in Paris, where he attended the École d'état-major, and returned to Egypt after his father's early death in 1848. During the reign of his uncle Sa'id Pasha he undertook diplomatic missions, including to the Pope, to Napoleon III of France, and to the Ottoman Sultan. On Sa'id's death in 1863, Isma'il succeeded as Wāli (governor) of Egypt without opposition.

In 1866, after lavish gifts and payments in Constantinople, he obtained a firman from Sultan Abdülaziz changing the rule of succession in Egypt from agnatic seniority to direct male primogeniture, securing the throne for his own sons. In 1867 he obtained the hereditary title of Khedive in place of Wāli, and in 1873 a further firman granted him near-complete autonomy in internal administration, including the right to conclude treaties and maintain an army.

Modernization and reform

Isma'il pursued an aggressive programme of modernization. Under his rule Egypt's railway network expanded dramatically, telegraph lines and postal services were built, ports at Alexandria and Suez were developed, and hundreds of bridges, irrigation canals, and sugar refineries were constructed. He encouraged cotton cultivation, which boomed during the American Civil War when Egyptian cotton replaced blockaded American exports, and founded sugar and textile industries in Upper Egypt. Cairo was rebuilt with wide Parisian-style boulevards, squares, gardens, and the opulent new district of Ismailia; the Cairo Opera House was erected in 1869, and Giuseppe Verdi's opera Aida — commissioned for the new house — premiered in Cairo in 1871.

He reformed the administration and the legal system, convening an assembly of delegates in 1866 that evolved into a proto-parliament, and in 1875 established the Mixed Courts, which tried cases involving foreigners under modern codes. Education expanded through new schools, the national library (Dar al-Kutub) was founded in 1870, and the Egyptian Museum's collections were developed under Auguste Mariette. He also formally abolished the corvée system of forced labour in connection with the Suez Canal works, though forced labour persisted elsewhere.

The Suez Canal

Although the canal was a project of his uncle Sa'id's reign, it was completed under Isma'il and opened on 17 November 1869 with lavish ceremonies attended by Empress Eugénie of France, Emperor Franz Joseph of Austria, and other dignitaries. The celebrations, lasting weeks, were among the most extravagant of the century and added heavily to Egypt's debts. The canal transformed Egypt's strategic importance, making control of the country a central concern of British policy toward India.

Expansion in Africa

Isma'il sought to build an Egyptian empire in northeastern Africa. He annexed Darfur in 1874, extended control down the Red Sea coast, and sponsored expeditions into the Equatorial Lakes region, employing European officers such as Samuel Baker and Charles George Gordon as governors of Equatoria. His invasion of Ethiopia ended in disaster at the battles of Gundet (1875) and Gura (1876), where Egyptian armies equipped with modern weapons were defeated by the forces of Emperor Yohannes IV. Attempts to control the Somali coast and the port of Zeila brought him the firman of 1875 granting him those territories from the Sultan.

Debt and deposition

Isma'il inherited a debt of about £7 million; by 1876 it had grown to roughly £100 million, raised from European financiers at punishing effective interest rates. In 1875, desperate for cash, he sold Egypt's 44 percent shareholding in the Suez Canal Company to the British government of Benjamin Disraeli for about £4 million — a transaction of enduring strategic consequence. In 1876 payments were suspended, and the creditors imposed the Caisse de la Dette Publique, an international commission controlling Egyptian revenues. In 1878 Isma'il was forced to accept a ministry containing British and French ministers (the "European ministry").

In April 1879 Isma'il dismissed the European ministry and attempted to rally nationalist support. Britain and France responded by pressing the Ottoman Sultan to depose him; on 26 June 1879 Abdülhamid II deposed Isma'il in favour of his son Tewfik. Isma'il left Egypt aboard his yacht for Naples, and eventually settled at Emirgan on the Bosphorus in Istanbul, where he died in 1895. The chain of events he set in motion — foreign financial control, the 'Urabi revolt, and the British invasion of 1882 — ended Egypt's effective independence for decades.
