Isochron dating

Isochron dating is a common technique of radiometric dating and is applied to date certain events, such as crystallization, metamorphism, shock events, and differentiation of precursor melts, in the history of rocks. Isochron dating can be used to date marine sediments and has been applied to dating iron meteorites and other very old materials by cosmochemists.

Method

The technique compares the amounts of a naturally occurring radioactive isotope and its decay products, using known decay rates, in several samples from the same rock body. To perform isochron dating, the concentrations of the parent isotope, the daughter isotope, and a non-radiogenic isotope of the daughter element are measured in multiple minerals or whole-rock samples that formed at the same time from a common pool of matter.

On a graph of the daughter/reference isotope ratio against the parent/reference isotope ratio, samples plot along a straight line (the isochron) if the system has remained closed. The slope of the line equals eλt − 1, from which the age t is calculated; the intercept gives the initial daughter isotope ratio — so, unlike simpler dating methods, no assumption about the initial concentration of the daughter nuclide is required.

Assumptions

For a valid age, all samples must have been isotopically homogenized at the time of formation, and the system must have remained closed to parent and daughter elements since then. If the isochron is disturbed, the points scatter (a line fitted to scattered data is sometimes called an "errorchron").

Applications

The rubidium–strontium (Rb–Sr) isochron method is the classic application; others include Sm–Nd, Lu–Hf, Re–Os, and Pb–Pb isochrons, the latter famously used to determine the age of the Earth (about 4.55 billion years) from meteorites.
