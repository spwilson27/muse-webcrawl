Ravenstail weaving


A Ravenstail robe with geometric twined designs.

Ravenstail weaving (Tlingit: Yeil Koowú) is an Indigenous textile tradition of the Northwest Coast of North America, practised historically by the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, and neighbouring peoples. It is an ancient twined weaving technique used to produce ceremonial robes and regalia featuring bold geometric designs.

Technique

Ravenstail garments are woven by hand without a loom, using a two-strand twining technique. The warp was traditionally made from mountain goat wool, sometimes combined with shredded cedar bark, producing a dense fabric suited to geometric patterning. Designs were built from rectangles, zigzags, and stepped forms in natural whites, blacks, and yellows, with later blue colouring.

History and revival

By the early 20th century, knowledge of Ravenstail weaving had nearly been lost, displaced in part by the related Chilkat weaving tradition with its curvilinear formline designs. Beginning in the 1980s and 1990s, weaver Cheryl Samuel and Indigenous artists such as Teri Rofkar led a revival, reconstructing the technique from museum-held historic robes.
