Antithrombin

Antithrombin (AT) is a small glycoprotein produced by the liver that inactivates several enzymes of the coagulation system. It is a serpin (serine protease inhibitor) encoded by the SERPINC1 gene, and is historically known as antithrombin III. By irreversibly inhibiting thrombin (factor IIa) and other activated clotting factors — especially factors Xa, IXa, XIa and XIIa — antithrombin is the body's principal natural anticoagulant.

Mechanism

Antithrombin binds its target proteases through a reactive centre loop that acts as a bait; cleavage traps the enzyme in an inactive complex. This activity is accelerated roughly a thousand-fold by heparin and related glycosaminoglycans, which bind antithrombin and change its conformation — the basis of the anticoagulant action of heparin drugs.

Clinical significance

Inherited antithrombin deficiency, usually autosomal dominant, is a thrombophilia that predisposes to venous thromboembolism, often at a young age or in unusual sites. Acquired deficiency occurs in liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, disseminated intravascular coagulation and during heparin therapy. Plasma-derived and recombinant antithrombin concentrates are used to treat deficiency in selected situations, such as surgery or childbirth in severely deficient patients, and in heparin resistance during cardiac surgery.
