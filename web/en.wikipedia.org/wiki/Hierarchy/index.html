Hierarchy

[Image: An organisational chart diagram showing a branching hierarchy of boxes connected from a single top node down through several levels]

A hierarchy (from Greek hierarkhia, "rule of a high priest") is an arrangement of items — people, groups, concepts or objects — in which each item is ranked above, below, or at the same level as others, forming a structure of superordinate and subordinate elements.

Origins and uses

The term was first applied to the ordered ranks of angels in Christian theology and then to the graded orders of the clergy. It is now used widely: for chains of command in states, armies and companies; for social stratification; for classification schemes such as biological taxonomy; and in computer science for tree-like data structures and file systems.

Formal properties

In a strict hierarchy, each element (except the top) has exactly one direct superior, producing a tree or pyramid structure. Hierarchies may be represented visually by organisational charts or dendrograms. Real systems often depart from the pure model, for instance in matrix organisations where staff report to two managers.

Criticism and alternatives

Hierarchy is debated in political and organisational theory. Defenders argue it enables coordination, accountability and expertise to be exercised at scale; critics associate it with domination and rigidity and have proposed alternatives such as heterarchy, networks, and flat or holacratic organisations. In social theory, hierarchical ordering by class, caste, race or gender is a central subject of study.
