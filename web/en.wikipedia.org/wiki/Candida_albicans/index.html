Candida albicans is an opportunistic pathogenic yeast and a common member of the human gut flora. It is the most common cause of candidiasis, a group of fungal infections ranging from superficial conditions such as oral thrush and vaginal yeast infections to potentially life-threatening systemic disease in immunocompromised patients. It can be detected in the gastrointestinal tract and mouth in 40–60% of healthy adults.

Morphology and switching

C. albicans is dimorphic: it can grow as oval budding yeast cells or as filamentous forms — pseudohyphae and true hyphae — and the switch between forms is closely tied to virulence. Hyphal growth helps the fungus invade tissues and escape immune cells. The organism also undergoes phenotypic ("white-opaque") switching, which affects mating and interaction with the host.

Pathogenesis

Under normal conditions, C. albicans is kept in check by the immune system and competing bacteria. Disruption of the normal flora by antibiotics, immunosuppression (as in HIV/AIDS or chemotherapy), diabetes, or indwelling medical devices can allow overgrowth. The fungus forms biofilms on catheters and prosthetics, which resist antifungal drugs and immune defences. Secreted aspartyl proteases, adhesins such as Als3, and the peptide toxin candidalysin contribute to tissue damage.

Treatment

Superficial infections are commonly treated with topical azole antifungals or nystatin; systemic infections are treated with fluconazole, echinocandins, or amphotericin B, though drug resistance is an increasing concern.

Research model

C. albicans is a major model organism for fungal pathogenesis. Its genome, sequenced in 2004, is diploid and roughly 16 megabases across eight chromosomes; unusually, it translates the CUG codon as serine rather than leucine.
