Non-fiction

Non-fiction (or nonfiction) is any document or media content that attempts, in good faith, to convey information about the real world rather than an imagined one. It stands in contrast to fiction, which presents invented stories, though the boundary can blur in practice. Non-fiction encompasses an enormous range: history, biography, science writing, journalism, essays, memoirs, textbooks, reference works and documentary film.

Varieties

Narrative non-fiction tells factual stories using literary techniques; creative nonfiction, a term popularised from the 1960s onward and associated with the New Journalism of writers such as Truman Capote (In Cold Blood, 1966) and Joan Didion, applies the devices of fiction — scene, dialogue, point of view — to factual material. Other major forms include the essay, the memoir and autobiography, the biography, the diary and journal, the travelogue, and expository and reference genres.

Distinctions and controversies

The claim of factuality is central to non-fiction's contract with the reader, and fabrications in works marketed as non-fiction have provoked notable scandals — such as the exposure of invented episodes in James Frey's memoir A Million Little Pieces (2003). At the same time, genres such as historical fiction, docudrama and "faction" intentionally mix fact and invention, and some scholars argue that all non-fiction involves selection and shaping that borrow from narrative art. Libraries organise non-fiction with systems such as the Dewey Decimal Classification, whose main classes are overwhelmingly devoted to it.
