Ad eundem degree

An ad eundem degree, from the Latin ad eundem gradum ("to the same rank"), is an academic degree awarded by one university to an alumnus of another, recognising the recipient's existing degree at an equivalent level without further examination. The practice developed as a courtesy between older universities, particularly Oxford and Cambridge, and the Ivy League universities in the United States.

[Image: University graduation ceremony]

Practice

Historically, a graduate of one ancient university who became a member of another — for example, a Cambridge graduate joining Oxford — could be "incorporated" and admitted ad eundem gradum to the corresponding degree, allowing them full membership rights. Oxford, Cambridge and the University of Dublin (Trinity College) long recognised each other's degrees reciprocally in this way (their graduates being known as the "steward" arrangement between the three). In the United States, some universities historically conferred ad eundem master's degrees on faculty who held degrees elsewhere, notably of their own professors, so that they could sit in the faculty senate.

Distinction from honorary degrees

An ad eundem degree differs from an honorary degree: it acknowledges a degree already earned by coursework and examination at another institution, rather than honouring achievement without study.
