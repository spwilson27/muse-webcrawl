The Arts Council of Great Britain was a non-departmental public body dedicated to the promotion of the performing, visual, and literary arts throughout Great Britain. Incorporated by royal charter in 1946, it was the principal channel of state subsidy for the arts in Britain until 1994, when it was divided into the separate arts councils for England, Scotland, and Wales.

Origins

The council grew out of the wartime Council for the Encouragement of Music and the Arts (CEMA), founded in 1940 to sustain cultural life and morale during the Second World War by touring concerts, theatre, and exhibitions to factories, shelters, and garrison towns. At war's end the decision was taken to put this support on a permanent footing. The economist John Maynard Keynes, who had chaired CEMA, became the Arts Council's first chairman and negotiated its charter and Treasury funding, though he died a few months before the charter was granted in August 1946. Keynes articulated the philosophy of public arts funding at "arm's length" from government—the state would provide the money, but artistic decisions would be made independently—a principle that became a hallmark of British cultural policy.

Work and organisation

The council received an annual grant-in-aid from the Treasury (later via the responsible government department) and distributed it to client organisations: national companies such as the Royal Opera, the Royal Shakespeare Company, and the National Theatre; regional theatres, orchestras, and touring companies; and art galleries and literature organisations. In England much distribution was delegated to twelve regional arts associations (later regional arts boards). Scottish and Welsh committees enjoyed increasing autonomy and became the Scottish Arts Council and the Arts Council of Wales in all but name before the formal split.

Debates

Throughout its life the council faced recurring arguments: over the balance between London and the regions, between "excellence" and wider access (the community arts movement of the 1970s pressed the latter), and over the political independence of subsidised artists, notably under the Thatcher governments, when funding was squeezed and business sponsorship promoted as an alternative. Its charter objectives—to develop and improve the knowledge, understanding, and practice of the arts and to increase their accessibility to the public at large—remained broadly unchanged.

Division and legacy

On 1 April 1994 the council was abolished and replaced by the Arts Council of England, the Scottish Arts Council, and the Arts Council of Wales. The arrival of the National Lottery in the same year transformed the scale of arts funding, which the successor bodies administered. The arm's-length model pioneered in 1946 was widely copied internationally and remains central to British cultural governance through Arts Council England and its counterparts.
