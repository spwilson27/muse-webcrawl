Endymion (poem)

AuthorJohn Keats
LanguageEnglish
GenreNarrative poem
Publication date1818
FormFour books, heroic couplets

Endymion is a poem by John Keats first published in 1818 by Taylor and Hessey of Fleet Street in London. It begins with the famous line "A thing of beauty is a joy for ever". Across four books of roughly 1,000 lines each, written in heroic couplets, Keats retells the Greek myth of Endymion, the shepherd loved by the moon goddess Selene (called Cynthia in the poem).

Synopsis

The poem follows Endymion's visionary quest for an ideal beloved whom he has glimpsed in a dream. He journeys through the underworld and beneath the sea, encountering figures such as Glaucus and Alpheus, before discovering that the goddess Cynthia and the earthly Indian maiden he has fallen for are one and the same, at which point he is made immortal.

Composition and reception

Keats composed Endymion between April and November 1817, regarding it as a test of his powers and "a feverish attempt, rather than a deed accomplished". Contemporary reviews in Blackwood's Magazine and the Quarterly Review attacked the poem harshly as part of their assault on the so-called "Cockney School" of poetry; Percy Bysshe Shelley defended Keats in his elegy Adonais. Modern critics view the poem as a flawed but rich apprentice work whose lush imagery and myth-making anticipate Keats's mature odes of 1819.
