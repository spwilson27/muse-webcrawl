The French ship Méduse was a 40-gun Pallas-class frigate of the French Navy, launched in 1810. She took part in the Napoleonic Wars, serving in a squadron in the Indian Ocean and participating in the Mauritius campaign of 1809–1811. After the Bourbon Restoration she was used as a troopship.

Méduse is best known for her wreck in 1816 on the Bank of Arguin off the coast of present-day Mauritania. The disaster, caused by the incompetence of her captain, Hugues Duroy de Chaumareys, led around 147 people to be set adrift on a hastily constructed raft. Only 15 survived after 13 days at sea, enduring starvation, dehydration and cannibalism. The scandal became a cause célèbre in post-Napoleonic France and was immortalised in Théodore Géricault's monumental painting The Raft of the Medusa (1818–1819).

Career

Méduse was built at Paimboeuf to plans by Jacques-Noël Sané. Commissioned in September 1810 under Captain Joseph François Raoul, she formed part of a frigate squadron sent to the Indian Ocean alongside Nymphe and the heavy frigate Renommée. In May 1811 the squadron fought the inconclusive action against a British force off Madagascar, and Méduse eventually returned to France after the fall of Mauritius.

During the Hundred Days in 1815 she was armed en flûte and made cruises to Martinique and Saint-Domingue.

The wreck of 1816

In June 1816 Méduse sailed from Rochefort as the flagship of a small flotilla carrying administrators, soldiers and settlers to re-establish the French colony at Saint-Louis, Senegal. Her captain, Chaumareys, was an émigré royalist who had barely sailed for twenty years and owed his command to political patronage. Ignoring the advice of his officers and relying on an inexperienced volunteer navigator, he allowed the ship to drift far from the prescribed course. On 2 July 1816 Méduse ran aground on the Bank of Arguin, some 50 kilometres from the African coast.

Attempts to refloat the frigate failed. The ship's boats could carry only part of the roughly 400 people aboard, so a raft about 20 metres long was built from the ship's timbers and loaded with some 147 passengers and crew. The raft was to be towed ashore, but the towing boats cut it loose. The survivors endured thirteen days of storms, mutiny, murder and cannibalism before the brig Argus found them on 17 July; only fifteen were still alive, and five of those died shortly afterwards.

Aftermath and legacy

The affair caused a sensation in France. The survivor Henri Savigny and Alexandre Corréard published an account that exposed the incompetence of the Restoration government. Chaumareys was court-martialled and sentenced to three years' imprisonment, a punishment many considered lenient. The tragedy inspired Géricault's The Raft of the Medusa, exhibited at the Paris Salon of 1819, which became a landmark of French Romantic painting and an enduring symbol of shipwreck and human suffering.
