Hair cell

Hair cells are the sensory receptors of the auditory system and of the vestibular system in the ears of vertebrates, and of the lateral line system in fishes and some amphibians. They are mechanoreceptors: through mechanotransduction, hair cells detect movement in their environment — sound vibrations in the cochlea, and head movement and acceleration in the vestibular organs.

Structure

Each hair cell bears an apical bundle of stiff projections called stereocilia, arranged in rows of increasing height and linked by fine filaments known as tip links, along with (in most vertebrate hair cells) a single true cilium, the kinocilium. Deflection of the bundle toward the tallest row stretches the tip links, opening mechanically gated ion channels and depolarising the cell; deflection in the opposite direction closes the channels and hyperpolarises it.

Types

In the mammalian cochlea there are two types: inner hair cells, a single row of about 3,500 cells that are the true sensory receptors sending signals to the brain via the auditory nerve, and outer hair cells, three rows totalling roughly 12,000 cells that act as mechanical amplifiers, changing length in response to voltage (electromotility, mediated by the protein prestin) to sharpen frequency tuning.

Damage and regeneration

Hair cells are vulnerable to noise trauma, ageing and ototoxic drugs. In mammals, lost cochlear hair cells are not naturally replaced, so the damage is a major cause of permanent sensorineural hearing loss; in birds and many other non-mammalian vertebrates, hair cells can regenerate.
