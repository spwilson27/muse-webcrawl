Odesa

Odesa (Ukrainian: Одеса; Russian: Одесса, Odessa) is Ukraine's third-largest city and its principal Black Sea port, on the north-western shore of the Black Sea. Founded by decree of Catherine the Great in 1794 on the site of the Tatar-Ottoman settlement of Khadjibey — conquered in the war in which Grigory Potemkin directed operations — it grew explosively as a free port for grain into one of the Russian Empire's greatest and most cosmopolitan cities, famed for its Italianate architecture, its humour, and its Jewish, Greek, Italian and multi-ethnic culture. Its historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 2023, simultaneously placed on the danger list during the war).

History

Under its first governor, the Duc de Richelieu (a French émigré who later became prime minister of France), and the free-port regime (1819–59), Odesa boomed: by the late nineteenth century it was the empire's second port and fourth city. Pushkin wrote part of Eugene Onegin here in exile (1823–24). The 1905 revolution brought the mutiny of the battleship Potemkin and a massacre on the harbour steps — immortalised, with artistic licence, in Eisenstein's film. In the Second World War the city endured a 73-day siege and Romanian occupation, during which most of its large Jewish population was murdered. After 1991 Odesa remained Ukraine's great southern port; since 2022 its port — vital to world grain exports under the Black Sea Grain Initiative — and its old town have repeatedly come under missile and drone attack.
