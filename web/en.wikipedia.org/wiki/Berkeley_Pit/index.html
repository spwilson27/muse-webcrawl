Berkeley Pit

The Berkeley Pit, a former copper mine now filled with highly acidic, metal-laden water.

The Berkeley Pit is a former open-pit copper mine in the western United States, located in Butte, Montana. A mile long by half a mile wide and roughly 1,780 feet (540 m) deep, the pit has filled with water that is heavily laden with dissolved metals and is strongly acidic — one of the largest and most notorious Superfund sites in the United States.

History

The pit was opened in 1955 by the Anaconda Copper Mining Company, shifting Butte's copper industry from underground mining to open-pit extraction on "the Richest Hill on Earth". Whole neighbourhoods of Butte were demolished or relocated as the pit expanded. Operated later by ARCO (Atlantic Richfield), the mine produced about 320 million tons of ore before closing in 1982; when mining ended, the pumps that had kept groundwater out of the workings were switched off, and the pit began to fill.

Environmental problem

As water flooded the pit it reacted with exposed sulfide minerals, forming a reservoir of acidic water — with a pH around 2.5 — containing high concentrations of copper, zinc, cadmium, arsenic and other metals. By the 1990s the pit held tens of billions of gallons of toxic water and had become part of the largest Superfund site complex in the nation. In 1995 hundreds of snow geese died after landing on the pit, and in 2016 a storm drove some 60,000 geese onto the water, thousands of which perished despite hazing efforts. Technologies developed in the pit's extreme water, including metal-eating microorganisms studied by local researchers, have attracted scientific interest.

Cleanup and viewing

A water treatment plant at the pit began operating in 2019, treating water from the pit and discharging it to Silver Bow Creek in the first reduction of the pit's water level in decades. A public viewing stand offers visitors a look over the pit, which remains a stark monument to both Butte's mining wealth and its environmental cost.
