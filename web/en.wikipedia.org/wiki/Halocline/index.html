A halocline is a vertical layer within a body of water across which salinity changes rapidly with depth. It is a subtype of the broader density gradient known as a pycnocline, and a close relative of the thermocline, in which temperature changes rapidly instead.

Occurrence

Haloclines are common where fresh water overlies denser salt water: in estuaries, fjords, and flooded coastal caves (cenotes) of Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, where divers can see a shimmering, mist-like boundary between the fresh upper layer and the saline water below. In the open ocean, a permanent halocline exists at depth in high-latitude regions, while in the Arctic the halocline — fed by river inflow and Pacific water — insulates sea ice from the relatively warm Atlantic water beneath it, playing a key role in the region's climate.

Properties

Because salinity strongly affects density, haloclines can act as effective barriers to vertical mixing, influencing nutrient supply, oxygen distribution, and sound propagation in the sea. In anchialine caves, distinct microbial communities inhabit the zones above, within, and below the halocline.
