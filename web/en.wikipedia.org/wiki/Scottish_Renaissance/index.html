Scottish Renaissance

The Scottish Renaissance (Scottish Gaelic: Ath-beòthachadh na h-Alba) was a mainly literary movement of the early to mid-20th century that sought to revitalise Scottish culture, language and identity. Inspired by modernism and by the national revivals of Ireland and other European nations, its leading figures aimed to reconnect Scotland with its medieval literary heritage — particularly the makars such as Dunbar and Henryson — while engaging fully with contemporary European thought.

Key figures

The movement's central figure was the poet Hugh MacDiarmid (Christopher Murray Grieve), whose Scots-language masterpiece A Drunk Man Looks at the Thistle (1926) demonstrated that a synthetic form of Scots ("Lallans") could carry ambitious modern poetry. Other associated writers included Lewis Grassic Gibbon, author of the trilogy A Scots Quair (1932–34); the novelist and poet Naomi Mitchison; Eric Linklater; Edwin Muir, who controversially argued that Scots could no longer sustain full literary expression; the Gaelic poet Sorley MacLean, who transformed Gaelic poetry; and the Orcadian writer George Mackay Brown.

Themes and influence

The Renaissance rejected the sentimental "Kailyard" school of Scottish fiction and tartan nostalgia, instead confronting industrialisation, class, land and language loss. It fed into broader Scottish nationalism and influenced the direction of Scottish literature for the rest of the century, laying groundwork for the later flowering of urban Scots writing.
