Anabaptism

Believer's baptism, practised by Anabaptists since 1525

Anabaptism (from Neo-Latin anabaptista, "rebaptiser", via Greek) is a Christian movement that traces its origins to the Radical Reformation of the 16th century. Anabaptists hold that baptism is valid only when the candidate confesses their faith — "believer's baptism" — and therefore reject infant baptism; the label "rebaptisers" was imposed by opponents, since Anabaptists themselves denied that infant baptism was baptism at all. Historically persecuted by both Catholics and magisterial Protestants, Anabaptists survive today chiefly as the Mennonites, Amish, and Hutterites, together numbering in the millions worldwide.

Origins

The movement is conventionally dated to 21 January 1525 in Zürich, when Conrad Grebel baptised Georg Blaurock, who then baptised others, in defiance of Huldrych Zwingli's city council — the founding act of the Swiss Brethren. Anabaptist ideas spread rapidly through Switzerland, Austria, Moravia, and the Low Countries, carried by figures such as Felix Manz (executed by drowning in Zürich in 1527), Michael Sattler, and Balthasar Hubmaier. The Schleitheim Confession of 1527, drafted under Sattler's leadership, set out seven articles characterising mainstream Anabaptism: believer's baptism, church discipline (the Ban), a memorial Lord's Supper, separation from the world, congregational choice of pastors, nonresistance and refusal of the sword, and refusal of oaths.

Persecution and Münster

Anabaptism was outlawed across the Holy Roman Empire, and several thousand Anabaptists were executed — burned, drowned, or beheaded — by Catholic and Protestant authorities alike; their witness was collected in the Martyrs Mirror (1660), still a devotional classic among plain Anabaptists. The movement's reputation was badly damaged by the Münster Rebellion (1534–1535), in which millenarian Anabaptists under Jan Matthys and John of Leiden seized the city of Münster, instituted polygamy and a theocracy, and were crushed by siege, after which surviving mainstream Anabaptists under Menno Simons consolidated a resolutely pacifist movement — the origin of the Mennonite name.

Beliefs and modern communities

Anabaptist theology emphasises discipleship (Nachfolge Christi), the church as a voluntary, disciplined community distinct from the state, nonviolence and enemy-love, simple living, and mutual aid. Hutterites practise full community of goods in colonies; the Amish, descending from Jakob Ammann's 1693 schism, maintain separation through dress, technology limits, and the Ordnung; Mennonites span a spectrum from Old Order conservatism to fully acculturated urban congregations. Anabaptist numbers are growing fastest in Africa, Asia, and Latin America through mission movements; Mennonite World Conference counts over two million baptised members globally. Anabaptist ethics — mediated by scholars such as John Howard Yoder — have influenced wider Christian pacifism and post-Christendom theology.
