Roger William Corman (5 April 1926 – 9 May 2024) was an American film director, producer, and actor, nicknamed the "King of the B's" and the "Pope of Pop Cinema". Across a career spanning some 500 productions, he perfected a system of fast, extremely low-budget filmmaking — often shooting features in days for American International Pictures and later his own companies — while training many of the most important filmmakers and actors of the New Hollywood era.

Career

After studying engineering at Stanford and a brief stint in the industry, Corman began directing in 1955 (Five Guns West). For AIP he churned out westerns, gangster films, and creature features such as It Conquered the World (1956) and The Wasp Woman (1959). His reputation rose with the stylish Edgar Allan Poe cycle for AIP — House of Usher (1960), The Pit and the Pendulum (1961), The Raven (1963), The Masque of the Red Death (1964) — starring Vincent Price, alongside the original Little Shop of Horrors (1960), famously shot in about two days, and the biker film The Wild Angels (1966).

New World Pictures and later work

In 1970 he founded New World Pictures, producing hits such as Death Race 2000 (1975) and distributing art-house films by Bergman, Fellini, Kurosawa, and Truffaut in the United States. He sold New World in 1983 and continued with Concorde Pictures and later companies, producing hundreds of genre films (including the unreleased 1994 Fantastic Four). He received an Academy Honorary Award in 2009.

The "Corman school"

Corman's low-budget productions served as a film school for Francis Ford Coppola, Martin Scorsese, Jonathan Demme, James Cameron, Ron Howard, Joe Dante, John Sayles, Peter Bogdanovich, and actors including Jack Nicholson, Robert De Niro, and Peter Fonda — a record of mentorship unmatched in American cinema.
