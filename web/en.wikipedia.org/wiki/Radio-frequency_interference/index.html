Radio-frequency interference

A spectrum analyser trace showing unwanted radio-frequency emissions.

Radio-frequency interference (RFI) is the disturbance generated by an external source that affects an electrical circuit by electromagnetic induction, electrostatic coupling, or conduction. It is a form of electromagnetic interference (EMI) that specifically affects signals in the radio frequency spectrum.

Causes

Sources of RFI include natural phenomena such as lightning and solar radiation, and man-made sources such as spark gaps, electric motors, switching power supplies, fluorescent lamps, and radio transmitters operating on adjacent or harmonic frequencies. Poorly shielded cables can both emit and receive interference.

Effects

RFI can degrade the performance of radio receivers, producing static, distortion, cross-talk, or complete loss of signal. In digital systems it may cause bit errors and reduced data throughput. Sensitive equipment such as radio telescopes and medical devices is particularly vulnerable.

Mitigation

Common mitigation techniques include shielding, filtering, ferrite chokes, proper grounding, twisted-pair cabling, and frequency coordination. Regulations issued by bodies such as the FCC in the United States and ETSI in Europe limit permissible emissions from electronic devices. See also electromagnetic interference and radio spectrum.
