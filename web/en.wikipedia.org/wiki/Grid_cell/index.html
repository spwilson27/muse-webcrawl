Grid cell

A grid cell is a type of neuron found in the entorhinal cortex of the brain that fires when an animal is at any of a set of locations arranged in a regular hexagonal (triangular) lattice covering its environment. Grid cells were discovered in rats in 2005 by the Norwegian neuroscientists Edvard Moser and May-Britt Moser and their colleagues.

Function

Together with place cells in the hippocampus, head-direction cells, border cells and speed cells, grid cells form part of the brain's navigation system — an internal "GPS" that represents position and supports path integration, the ability to track location by integrating self-motion. Each grid cell has a characteristic spacing, orientation and phase; groups of cells with similar spacing form modules that together can encode an animal's position with high precision. Grid cells have been found in many mammals, including evidence from human patients with implanted electrodes. The discovery earned the Mosers, together with John O'Keefe (discoverer of place cells), the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
