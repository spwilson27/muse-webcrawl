Wergild

Wergild (also spelled weregild, wergeld or weregeld) was a monetary compensation in Germanic customary law payable by a person who killed or injured another, or by their kindred, to the family of the victim. The word derives from Old English wer ("man") and gield ("payment, tribute"). It is frequently cited in histories of early medieval law because it preserved peace by substituting a negotiated financial settlement for the violent obligations of the blood feud.

In practice

The laws of the Franks (the Lex Salica), Lombards, Anglo-Saxons, Frisians and Scandinavians all contain detailed tariffs. A freeman's wergild in Salic law was 200 solidi; killing a woman of child-bearing age, a royal retainer or a cleric attracted higher multiples. The amount was usually shared among the victim's paternal and maternal kin according to fixed rules, and defaulting on payment could expose the killer and his relatives to lawful vengeance. Injuries were separately priced — an eye, a hand or a toe each had its own tariff — a practice often called bót.

Historical development

Wergild survived into the central Middle Ages in parts of Europe and left traces in later compensation schemes. In England it disappeared as homicide came under royal jurisdiction in the 12th century, but the notion of a tariffed "price" for a life persisted in literary memory — appearing in Beowulf, where Grendel's refusal to pay wergild marks him as outside human society.
