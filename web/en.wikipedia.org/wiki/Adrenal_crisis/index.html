Adrenal crisis

An adrenal crisis (also called addisonian crisis or acute adrenal insufficiency) is a life-threatening medical emergency caused by a severe deficiency of cortisol, usually occurring in a person with adrenal insufficiency who is subjected to physiological stress such as infection, trauma or surgery, or who abruptly stops glucocorticoid replacement. Without prompt treatment with intravenous hydrocortisone and fluids, it can progress to shock and death.

Presentation and causes

Features include profound weakness, nausea and vomiting, abdominal and back pain, fever, dehydration, low blood pressure and reduced consciousness; blood tests may show hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia and hypoglycaemia. The commonest setting is known primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease), but crisis may be the first presentation of previously undiagnosed disease, for example after bilateral adrenal haemorrhage (Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome in meningococcal sepsis) or pituitary apoplexy. A frequent precipitant in patients on long-term steroids is abrupt withdrawal or failure to increase the dose during intercurrent illness ("sick day rules").

Management

Treatment must not be delayed for diagnostic testing: blood is drawn for cortisol and ACTH, then high-dose hydrocortisone (typically a 100 mg intravenous bolus followed by 200 mg over 24 hours) and rapid isotonic saline are given. Patients with known adrenal insufficiency are advised to carry a steroid emergency card and an emergency injection kit and to double or triple their oral dose during illness.
