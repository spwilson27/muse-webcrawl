Behavior-altering parasite

Behavior-altering parasites are parasites that change the behavior of their hosts in ways that enhance the parasite's transmission or survival — an example of the "extended phenotype" described by Richard Dawkins. Such manipulation has evolved independently in many parasite lineages, including protozoa, fungi, worms, and parasitoid wasps, and can be strikingly specific, altering host activity, boldness, habitat choice, or responses to predators.

Examples

Toxoplasma gondii

The protozoan Toxoplasma gondii must reach a cat, its definitive host, to reproduce sexually. Rodents infected with Toxoplasma lose their innate aversion to cat odors — and may even be attracted to them — making them more likely to be eaten by cats. The parasite forms cysts in the brain and appears to influence dopamine signaling. Because a large fraction of humans carry latent infections, researchers have investigated (controversially) possible subtle effects on human behavior and mental health.

Other classic cases

The lancet liver fluke Dicrocoelium dendriticum causes infected ants to clamp onto grass tips at dusk, where grazing livestock may eat them. Hairworms drive infected crickets to leap into water, where the adult worm emerges to reproduce. The fungus Ophiocordyceps unilateralis compels infected ants to bite vegetation in a "death grip" elevated above the forest floor, from which the fungus fruits. Parasitoid wasps such as Hymenoepimecis argyraphaga induce spiders to build specialized webs that protect the wasp's cocoon, and rabies virus increases aggression and biting in infected mammals, aiding its transmission through saliva.

Mechanisms

Parasites manipulate hosts through secretion of neuroactive compounds, alteration of neurotransmitter and hormone levels, direct invasion of neural tissue, and manipulation of the host immune system, whose signaling molecules also affect the brain. Distinguishing true adaptive manipulation from the side effects of sickness remains a central challenge in the field.
