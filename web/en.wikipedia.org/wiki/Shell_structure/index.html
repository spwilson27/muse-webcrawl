Shell structure

A shell structure is a structure composed of a thin, curved plate — a shell — whose thickness is very small compared with its other dimensions. Because curvature gives the surface geometric stiffness, shells carry loads mainly through in-plane (membrane) stresses rather than bending, allowing remarkably thin constructions to span large distances, especially in reinforced concrete and steel.

Types and geometry

Shells are classified by their surface geometry: single-curvature forms such as cylinders and cones (barrel vaults); double-curvature synclastic forms such as domes and spherical caps; anticlastic forms such as hyperbolic paraboloids ("hypars") and conoids; and free-form shells defined computationally. Other categories include folded plates, gridshells (a lattice formed into a shell shape, often timber or steel), and stressed-skin and membrane structures.

History and notable examples

Thin vaulting has ancient roots, but the modern reinforced-concrete shell era began in the 1920s with thin barrel roofs for factories and airship hangars. Masters of the form include Eduardo Torroja, Pier Luigi Nervi, Félix Candela (whose hyperbolic paraboloid restaurants and chapels in Mexico used shells only a few centimetres thick), Heinz Isler, and Jørn Utzon and Ove Arup's Sydney Opera House, whose precast ribbed "shells" crown the harbour. The analysis of shells — membrane theory, bending boundary effects, and buckling, the governing failure mode — became a major branch of structural mechanics, now handled chiefly by finite-element methods.
