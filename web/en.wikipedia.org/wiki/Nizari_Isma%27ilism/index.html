Nizari Isma'ilism is the largest branch of Isma'ili Shia Islam, comprising the overwhelming majority of Isma'ili Muslims today. Nizaris follow a living, hereditary imam descended from Nizar ibn al-Mustansir, the elder son of the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir Billah.

Origins

When al-Mustansir died in 1094, a succession dispute split the Isma'ili movement: supporters of his elder son Nizar became the Nizaris, while supporters of the younger son al-Musta'li formed the Musta'li branch. Under Hasan-i Sabbah, the Nizaris established a network of mountain fortresses in Persia and Syria, centred on Alamut Castle, and became known in Western legend as the "Assassins" because of their targeted killings of political opponents.

History after Alamut

The Mongol invasion destroyed Alamut in 1256, and the Nizari imamate continued in concealment for centuries. In the 19th century the imams, based in Persia and then India, received the title Aga Khan. Aga Khan III was a prominent international figure; his grandson Aga Khan IV became the 49th imam in 1957 and led the community for nearly seven decades.

Beliefs and institutions

Nizaris emphasise the esoteric (batin) interpretation of scripture guided by the imam of the time. The Aga Khan Development Network funds education, health, and cultural institutions across Asia and Africa, and Nizari communities (jamat) now live in more than 25 countries, with large populations in South Asia, East Africa, Central Asia and the West.
