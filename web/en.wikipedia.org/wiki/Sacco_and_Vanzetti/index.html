Sacco and Vanzetti

Nicola Sacco (1891–1927) and Bartolomeo Vanzetti (1888–1927) were Italian immigrant anarchists convicted of murdering a paymaster and a security guard during an armed robbery in South Braintree, Massachusetts, on 15 April 1920. Their trial, held amid the anti-radical hysteria of the first Red Scare, became a worldwide cause célèbre, and the fairness of their conviction has been debated ever since. Both men were executed in the electric chair at Charlestown State Prison on 23 August 1927.

Background

Sacco, a shoemaker, and Vanzetti, a fishmonger, were followers of the anarchist Luigi Galleani. They were arrested on 5 May 1920 carrying firearms and anarchist literature. Neither had a criminal record of violence. The prosecution's case rested on eyewitness identifications and ballistic testimony linking a bullet to Sacco's pistol; the defence produced alibi witnesses, many of them Italian immigrants whose testimony the jury may have discounted.

Trial and appeals

Judge Webster Thayer presided and was accused of bias — he reportedly boasted of his handling of "those anarchist bastards". Six years of appeals and motions for a new trial, including a confession by another man, Celestino Madeiros, failed. Protests erupted in cities around the world, and prominent figures including Albert Einstein, H. G. Wells and Edna St. Vincent Millay — whose poem "Justice Denied in Massachusetts" captured the mood — pleaded for clemency.

Legacy

In 1977 Massachusetts governor Michael Dukakis issued a proclamation declaring that the trial had been permeated by prejudice and that any disgrace attaching to the men's names should be removed. The case remains a touchstone in debates over capital punishment, immigration and political justice, commemorated in songs by Woody Guthrie, Joan Baez's "Here's to You", and the 1971 film Sacco e Vanzetti.
