Rural Electrification Act

The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 was a United States federal law enacted as part of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. It provided federal loans for the installation of electrical distribution systems to serve isolated rural areas of the United States.

Background

In the mid-1930s barely one farm in ten in the United States had electric power: private utilities considered rural lines unprofitable because of the long distances between customers. Roosevelt first created the Rural Electrification Administration (REA) by executive order in 1935; the Act of 20 May 1936, sponsored by Representative John E. Rankin and Senator George W. Norris, put the programme on a statutory footing.

Provisions and effects

The Act authorised the REA to make low-interest, long-term loans, with preference given to non-profit and cooperative organisations rather than investor-owned utilities. Hundreds of local electric cooperatives were formed by farmers themselves to build lines and distribute power. The results were dramatic: by 1953 more than 90 percent of U.S. farms were electrified, transforming rural life with lighting, refrigeration, milking machines, and pumped water.

Legacy

The REA's successor functions continue within the USDA's Rural Utilities Service, which has extended the same cooperative model to rural telephone service and, in recent decades, broadband. More than 800 electric cooperatives founded under the programme still serve millions of members across the United States.
