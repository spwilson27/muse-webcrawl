Elizabethan theatre

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Elizabethan theatre refers to the tradition of English theatre during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I (1558–1603) and, more broadly, to the flourishing of English Renaissance drama through the Jacobean and Caroline periods until the closing of the theatres in 1642. It produced some of the most celebrated drama in world literature, including the works of William Shakespeare, Christopher Marlowe, Ben Jonson, and Thomas Middleton.

The playhouses

Before permanent theatres existed, companies of actors toured, performing in inn yards and great halls. In 1576 James Burbage built The Theatre in Shoreditch, the first successful purpose-built playhouse in London. It was followed by the Curtain, the Rose (1587), the Swan (1595), and the Globe (1599), built by Shakespeare's company from the timbers of The Theatre on the south bank of the Thames. These open-air amphitheatres held up to 3,000 spectators: "groundlings" stood in the yard for a penny, while wealthier patrons sat in galleries. Private indoor theatres such as Blackfriars charged more and catered to fashionable audiences.

Companies and performers

Acting was organised into companies under noble patronage — the Lord Chamberlain's Men (later the King's Men), the Admiral's Men, and others — structured as joint-stock ventures in which leading actors were shareholders. All female roles were played by boy apprentices. Star performers such as Edward Alleyn and the clown Richard Tarlton became celebrities. Plays were performed in repertory, in the afternoon, with minimal scenery, elaborate costumes, and continuous action on a thrust stage beneath a painted canopy known as the "heavens".

The drama

Playwrights drew on classical models, English chronicles, and Italian tales. Marlowe's mighty line of blank verse in Tamburlaine and Doctor Faustus transformed English tragedy; Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy popularised revenge drama; Shakespeare's histories, comedies, and tragedies defined the era; and Jonson's comedies of humours satirised London life. Censorship by the Master of the Revels, recurrent plague closures, and Puritan hostility were constant pressures, culminating in Parliament's closure of all theatres in 1642.

Legacy

The period's plays survive through printed quartos and the Shakespeare First Folio of 1623. Modern reconstructions, notably Shakespeare's Globe in London (opened 1997), have revived interest in original staging practices.
