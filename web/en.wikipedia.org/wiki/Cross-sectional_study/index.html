In medical research, social science, and biology, a cross-sectional study (also known as a cross-sectional analysis, transverse study, or prevalence study) is a type of observational study that analyzes data from a population, or a representative subset, at a specific point in time — that is, cross-sectional data.

Cross-sectional studies are used to measure the prevalence of health outcomes, understand determinants of health, and describe features of a population. Unlike case-control studies, they can be used to describe odds ratios and absolute and relative risks from prevalences. They differ from longitudinal studies, which follow the same subjects over time.

Characteristics

Cross-sectional studies are usually relatively inexpensive and allow researchers to collect a great deal of information quickly. Data are collected at a defined time, and once collected, researchers examine the relationships between variables of interest. A sample of the population is examined to find the proportion of subjects with the disease or exposure of interest at that moment, which yields a prevalence estimate rather than an incidence estimate.

Repeated cross-sectional studies take fresh samples at successive times and can track changes in population-level prevalence, although they do not follow the same individuals.

Uses

Routinely collected survey data such as national health and nutrition examination surveys (for example, the US NHANES program) are major sources for cross-sectional analyses. In economics, cross-sectional regression analyzes the relationships among variables for many units — individuals, firms, countries — at one point in time. In psychology and education research, cross-sectional designs compare different age groups to study development, a design sometimes criticized when cohort effects are plausible explanations.

Advantages

  • They can measure prevalence for all factors under investigation simultaneously.
  • They are quick and comparatively cheap, with no need for follow-up.
  • Multiple outcomes and exposures can be studied in one survey.
  • They are useful for generating hypotheses and for public-health planning.

Disadvantages

  • Because exposure and outcome are measured simultaneously, the temporal sequence cannot usually be established, so causal inference is weak (susceptible to reverse causation).
  • They are prone to prevalence–incidence (Neyman) bias: conditions that kill or resolve quickly are underrepresented.
  • Rare diseases and short-duration conditions are poorly captured without very large samples.

Examples

Classic cross-sectional work includes the British Doctors Study's early analyses, seroprevalence surveys conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic to estimate cumulative infection rates, and cross-sectional census and demographic surveys used worldwide to estimate the burden of malnutrition, anemia, and chronic disease risk factors.
