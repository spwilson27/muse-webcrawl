Chobham armour

Chobham armour is the informal name of a composite armour developed in the 1960s at the Military Vehicles and Engineering Establishment, the British tank research centre at Chobham Common in Surrey, England. First known as Burlington, it consists of ceramic tiles set in a resin matrix between steel plates, and gives greatly improved protection against high-explosive anti-tank (HEAT) warheads and kinetic energy penetrators compared with steel armour of the same weight.

Development

Research at Chobham in the 1960s sought protection against shaped-charge warheads, which defeat steel by focusing an explosive jet. The ceramic-faced arrays tested there eroded and disrupted the jet. The armour was first applied to the British Challenger 1 and later Challenger 2 tanks. A version was licence-produced in the United States for the M1 Abrams, and variants equip tanks such as the German Leopard 2.

Mechanism

When a shaped-charge jet strikes the ceramic layer, the ceramic fractures and the resulting debris and interface movement erode and deflect the jet, spreading its energy. Against long-rod penetrators, the hard ceramic dulls and breaks the rod while the backing absorbs the remainder. Details of the exact composition remain classified; later generations (such as the British Dorchester level) incorporate additional materials such as depleted uranium or tungsten alloys in some applications.

Applications

Chobham-type composite armour is a defining feature of third-generation main battle tanks. Its success displaced spaced and homogeneous steel armour on front-line vehicles, though add-on explosive reactive armour is often fitted over it for further protection.
