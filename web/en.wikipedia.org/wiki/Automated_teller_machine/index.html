An automated teller machine (ATM) is an electronic telecommunications device that enables customers of financial institutions to perform financial transactions — principally cash withdrawal, deposits, funds transfers and account information queries — at any time and without direct interaction with bank staff.

History

The first ATM was installed by Barclays Bank in Enfield, north London, on 27 June 1967; actor Reg Varney was the first customer. The machine was devised by John Shepherd-Barron's team at De La Rue and dispensed £10 notes in exchange for special paper vouchers. James Goodfellow's 1966 patent introduced the combination of a plastic card with an encoded personal identification number (PIN). In the United States, Chemical Bank installed a Docutel machine in Rockville Centre, New York, in 1969 with the advertising slogan "Our bank will open at 9 and never close again!".

Operation

Customers authenticate with a chip or magnetic-stripe card and PIN. The machine communicates with the bank's host processor over networks such as Visa and Mastercard's interbank systems; cash is stored in cassettes within a safe and dispensed note by note, with sensors detecting double notes and counterfeit currency. Modern machines also accept envelope-free deposits, dispense stamps or tickets, and support cardless access by phone.

Security

ATM crime includes card skimming, PIN capture by hidden cameras, cash trapping and physical attacks such as ram-raiding and explosive attacks on the safe. Countermeasures include EMV chips, anti-skimming bezels, ink-staining of notes and transaction monitoring.
