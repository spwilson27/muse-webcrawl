Louis Camille Maillard

Louis Camille Maillard (4 February 1878 – 12 May 1936) was a French physician and chemist. He is best known for describing, in 1912, the chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars that gives browned food its distinctive flavour, now known worldwide as the Maillard reaction. He also made important early contributions to the study of kidney disorders, including the "Maillard coefficient" of uremic severity.

Early life and education

Maillard was born in Pont-à-Mousson in Lorraine. He studied medicine and science at the University of Nancy, where he proved an exceptionally precocious student, publishing scientific work while still in his teens. He obtained his doctorate in medicine in 1903 and later a doctorate in science.

Career

Maillard worked in Nancy and then in Paris, where he became a professor in the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Paris. During the First World War he served as a military physician. His research spanned physiological chemistry, kidney function and metabolism. In 1913 he published a major work on uremia in which he proposed a prognostic coefficient relating the concentration of urea in the blood to the severity of kidney failure — an early landmark in nephrology.

The Maillard reaction

In 1912 Maillard published a paper reporting that when amino acids and sugars are heated together they react to form brown pigments (melanoidins) and flavour compounds. He had been investigating how the body synthesises proteins, but the reaction he described turned out to be of immense importance to food science: it accounts for the browning and much of the flavour of seared meat, baked bread crusts, roasted coffee and many other cooked foods. The reaction is a form of non-enzymatic browning and remains an active field of research in both food chemistry and medicine, where its products (advanced glycation end-products) are implicated in aging and disease.

Death and honours

Maillard died suddenly in Paris in 1936 while serving as a judge in a scholarship competition. He was elected a member of the French Academy of Medicine and received numerous honours. The importance of his 1912 discovery was only fully recognised decades after his death, and the Maillard reaction is now among the most widely known named reactions in chemistry.
