Trafalgar Square is a public square in the City of Westminster, central London, built around the area formerly known as Charing Cross. Its name commemorates the Battle of Trafalgar, the British naval victory in the Napoleonic Wars over France and Spain that took place on 21 October 1805 off the coast of Cape Trafalgar. The square has been a significant landmark since the 13th century, when the area housed the King's Mews.

History

The site was the location of the royal mews from the reign of Edward I. In the 1820s, the Prince Regent commissioned architect John Nash to redevelop the area, but the scheme was only fully realised in the 1840s under the designs of Charles Barry. The square was officially opened in 1844. At its centre stands Nelson's Column, built between 1840 and 1843 to commemorate Admiral Horatio Nelson, who died at the Battle of Trafalgar.

Monuments and statues

Nelson's Column is guarded by four monumental bronze lions, sculpted by Sir Edwin Landseer and installed in 1867. The square contains four plinths: three hold statues of generals and admirals, while the fourth plinth, originally intended for an equestrian statue of William IV, remained empty for over 150 years and since 1999 has hosted a rolling programme of temporary contemporary artworks known as the Fourth Plinth commissions.

Fountains

Two large fountains, designed as part of Barry's original layout, occupy the square. They were elaborated in the 1930s with replacement centrepieces designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens, including dolphins, mermaids, mermen and tritons sculpted by William McMillan and Charles Wheeler.

Uses

Trafalgar Square has long been a focal point for political demonstrations, protests and public celebrations, as well as London's traditional New Year's Eve gatherings and Christmas celebrations, centred on a Christmas tree donated annually by Norway since 1947 in gratitude for British support during the Second World War. The National Gallery stands on the square's north side.
