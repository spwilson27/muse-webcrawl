Operation Goodwood was a British offensive during the Second World War, fought from 18 to 20 July 1944 as part of the Battle for Caen in Normandy. The operation was carried out by the British Second Army's VIII Corps, with three armoured divisions, east of the city, and was preceded by the heaviest aerial bombardment yet used in support of ground forces.

Plan

General Bernard Montgomery intended the attack to wear down German strength around Caen, secure the eastern part of the city and the Bourguébus Ridge, and keep German panzer divisions pinned in the east while the American First Army prepared its breakout in the west (Operation Cobra). Whether Goodwood was also meant to attempt a full armoured breakout towards Falaise has been debated by historians ever since.

The attack

On the morning of 18 July, more than 2,000 Allied bombers dropped over 7,000 tons of bombs on German positions east of Caen. The 11th, 7th and Guards Armoured divisions then advanced across the open corn plain. Initial progress was rapid, but the attack lost momentum against a defence in depth: surviving German units, anti-tank guns on the Bourguébus Ridge, and counter-attacks by the 1st SS and 21st Panzer divisions stopped the armour short of its objectives. A thunderstorm on 20 July turned the battlefield to mud and the operation was halted. To the west, the simultaneous Canadian Operation Atlantic completed the liberation of most of Caen south of the Orne.

Outcome

The British advanced about 11 kilometres and gained ground, but lost over 400 tanks (many later repaired) and some 5,500 casualties; German losses included roughly 2,500 men and about 100 tanks. Critics at the time, including in the American press and at SHAEF, saw Goodwood as a costly failure, but it achieved its attritional purpose: the German armoured reserve remained concentrated around Caen, far from the sector where Operation Cobra broke through a week later.
