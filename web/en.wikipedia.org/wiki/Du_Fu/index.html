Du Fu (Chinese: 杜甫; pinyin: Dù Fǔ; 712–770) was a Chinese poet and politician during the Tang dynasty. Together with his elder contemporary Li Bai, he is frequently called the greatest of the Chinese poets. His ambition was to serve his country as a successful civil servant, but he proved unable to make the necessary accommodations. His life, like the whole country, was devastated by the An Lushan Rebellion of 755, and his last 15 years were a time of almost constant unrest.

Life

Du Fu was born near Luoyang into a family of scholar-officials; his grandfather Du Shenyan was a noted court poet. He failed the imperial examinations in 735 and spent years travelling in Shandong and Hebei. In 751 he gained an audience with the emperor and eventually received a minor post, but the outbreak of the An Lushan Rebellion forced him to flee; he was captured by rebels in Chang'an in 757 before escaping to rejoin the exiled court. He later served under the strict governor Yan Wu in Chengdu, Sichuan, where he lived in a modest thatched cottage (now the Du Fu Thatched Cottage museum). His final years were spent drifting down the Yangtze with his family, dying in 770.

Poetry

Around 1,500 of Du Fu's poems survive. He mastered every form, particularly regulated verse (lüshi), whose strict tonal and parallel structures he combined with dense historical and personal feeling. His poems chronicling war, famine, taxation, and official corruption earned him the title "Poet-Historian" (詩史); his moral seriousness and compassion earned him "Poet-Sage" (詩聖). Famous works include "Spring View" (春望), written during the rebellion, which opens "The nation is broken, but mountains and rivers remain."
