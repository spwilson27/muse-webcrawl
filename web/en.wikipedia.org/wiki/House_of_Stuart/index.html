The House of Stuart, originally spelled Stewart, was a royal house of Scotland, later also ruling England and Ireland. Founded by Robert II of Scotland, who came to the throne in 1371, the dynasty descends from the hereditary High Stewards of Scotland — hence the name. The Stewarts ruled Scotland for over three centuries, and from 1603, when James VI of Scotland inherited the English crown as James I, they ruled the three kingdoms in personal union.

Scottish origins

The family originated in Brittany and settled in Scotland under David I in the twelfth century. Walter fitz Alan became the first hereditary High Steward around 1150. His descendant Walter Stewart married Marjorie Bruce, daughter of Robert the Bruce; their son became Robert II in 1371. Successive Stewart kings — James I through James V — consolidated royal power against the great magnates. Mary, Queen of Scots (r. 1542–1567), the most famous Stuart, was forced to abdicate and was eventually executed in England in 1587; her son James VI succeeded Elizabeth I in England in 1603.

Stuart Britain

The seventeenth century saw repeated conflict between the Stuart kings and Parliament. Charles I's quarrels led to the English Civil War, his trial, and his execution in 1649, followed by the Commonwealth under Oliver Cromwell. The monarchy was restored in 1660 under Charles II, but James II's Catholicism provoked the Glorious Revolution of 1688, which replaced him with his daughter Mary II and her husband William III. The last Stuart monarch, Queen Anne (r. 1702–1714), presided over the Acts of Union 1707 that created the Kingdom of Great Britain.

Jacobitism and aftermath

The exiled line — James II's son James Francis Edward Stuart ("the Old Pretender") and grandson Charles Edward ("Bonnie Prince Charlie") — inspired Jacobite risings in 1715 and 1745, both defeated, the latter at Culloden (1746). The direct line ended with the death of Henry Benedict Stuart, Cardinal York, in 1807. The Jacobite succession thereafter passed through the houses of Savoy, Modena, and Bavaria; the current heir by that reckoning is Franz, Duke of Bavaria.
