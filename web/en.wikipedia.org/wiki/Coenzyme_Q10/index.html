Coenzyme Q10

IUPAC name2,3-dimethoxy-5-methyl-6-decaprenyl-1,4-benzoquinone
FormulaC59H90O4
Other namesUbiquinone, ubidecarenone, CoQ10
RoleElectron carrier in the mitochondrial respiratory chain

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), also known as ubiquinone, is a naturally occurring benzoquinone found in the membranes of most aerobic organisms, from bacteria to mammals. It is a fat-soluble substance resembling a vitamin and is present in most eukaryotic cells, primarily in the mitochondria, where it is a component of the electron transport chain and participates in aerobic cellular respiration, generating energy in the form of ATP.

Function

CoQ10 accepts electrons from complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) and complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) and transfers them to complex III, shuttling between its oxidised (ubiquinone) and reduced (ubiquinol) forms. Because it is lipid-soluble and mobile within the inner mitochondrial membrane, it collects reducing equivalents from several sources, including fatty-acid oxidation. It also functions as an antioxidant in its reduced form. Humans synthesise CoQ10 from tyrosine and mevalonate, and the "10" in its name refers to the ten isoprenoid units in its tail in the human form.

Supplementation

CoQ10 is widely sold as a dietary supplement. It has been studied in conditions including heart failure, statin-associated muscle symptoms and migraine, with some trials reporting modest benefits, though overall evidence is mixed and it is not approved as a treatment for any disease in most jurisdictions. Rare genetic defects of CoQ10 synthesis cause primary CoQ10 deficiency, which does respond to supplementation.
