Expo 58, also known as the Brussels Universal and International Exhibition, was a world exposition held at the Heysel Park in Brussels, Belgium, from 17 April to 19 October 1958. It was the first major world's fair after the Second World War, and its theme, "A World View — A New Humanism", reflected a postwar optimism about science, technology and peaceful progress. Some 48 nations participated and 41.5 million visitors attended.

Site and symbol

The fair's enduring symbol is the Atomium, a 102-metre structure designed by engineer André Waterkeyn representing an iron crystal magnified 165 billion times: nine interconnected stainless-steel spheres linked by escalators, the highest containing a restaurant with panoramic views of Brussels. Intended as a temporary structure, it proved so popular that it was never dismantled and remains one of Belgium's best-known landmarks.

Highlights

The United States pavilion, a vast circular structure designed by Edward Durell Stone, and the Soviet pavilion faced each other across the central avenue, staging one of the great exhibition rivalries of the Cold War; the USSR displayed replicas of its Sputnik satellites, launched the previous year. The Philips Pavilion, designed by Le Corbusier and Iannis Xenakis, presented the Poème électronique, an early multimedia spectacle combining film, coloured light and music by Edgard Varèse. The fair also drew attention — and later criticism — for its Congolese village, in which Congolese people were exhibited in a simulated "native" setting, an exhibit now remembered as one of the last "human zoos" in Europe.

Legacy

Expo 58 left Belgium with infrastructure improvements including new roads around the Heysel, and the Atomium continues to anchor the site, near the King Baudouin Stadium. The fair is remembered as the moment Brussels presented itself as the emerging capital of a uniting Europe — the Treaty of Rome had been signed the previous year — and as a landmark of mid-century modernist design.
