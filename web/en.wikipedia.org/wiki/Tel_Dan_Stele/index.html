Tel Dan Stele

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Material: Basalt
Writing: Old Aramaic
Created: c. 840 BCE
Discovered: 1993–1994, Tel Dan, Israel
Present location: Israel Museum, Jerusalem

The Tel Dan Stele is a fragmentary Aramean royal inscription in Old Aramaic, discovered in 1993 and 1994 during excavations at Tel Dan (biblical Dan) in northern Israel. The stele was erected by an Aramean king—often identified as Hazael of Aram-Damascus—boasting of victories over the king of Israel and the king of the "House of David" (bytdwd).

Significance

The phrase "House of David" in the inscription is widely regarded as the earliest extra-biblical reference to King David and his dynasty, providing important evidence in debates over the historicity of the biblical United Monarchy. A minority of scholars have proposed alternative readings, but the mainstream interpretation reads bytdwd as "House of David".

Content

The surviving text describes conflicts between Aram and the kingdoms of Israel and Judah, including the deaths of kings likely identified as Jehoram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah in the 9th century BCE. The stele is dated to roughly the second half of the 9th century BCE.
