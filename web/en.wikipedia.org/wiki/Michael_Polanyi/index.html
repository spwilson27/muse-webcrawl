Michael Polanyi
Born: March 11, 1891, Budapest, Austria-Hungary
Died: February 22, 1976 (aged 84), Northampton, England
Fields: Physical chemistry, philosophy of science, economics
Known for: Tacit knowledge; "We can know more than we can tell"

Michael Polanyi (11 March 1891 – 22 February 1976) was a Hungarian-British polymath who made major contributions to physical chemistry, economics, and the philosophy of science. He is best known for his theory of tacit knowledge, summarised in his dictum "we can know more than we can tell," and for his defence of a free, self-organising scientific community against central planning.

Scientific career

Trained as a physician and then a physical chemist in Budapest, Polanyi emigrated to Berlin in 1920, working at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Fiber Chemistry, where he pioneered the interpretation of X-ray fibre diffraction (with Hermann Mark) and developed theories of crystal plasticity and adsorption. Fleeing Nazi Germany in 1933, he became professor of physical chemistry at the University of Manchester. His son John Polanyi later won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1986) for work on reaction dynamics that built in part on Michael's theory of the transition state (developed independently with Henry Eyring).

Philosophy

From the 1940s Polanyi turned increasingly to philosophy and social thought. In works such as Science, Faith and Society (1946), The Logic of Liberty (1951), and his magnum opus Personal Knowledge (1958), he argued that all knowing rests on tacit, personally committed judgment — skills, perceptions, and traditions that cannot be fully formalised in explicit rules. His unfinished late work The Tacit Dimension (1966) developed the structure of tacit knowing through the distinction between subsidiary and focal awareness. In economics he described science itself as a "republic" coordinated by spontaneous order, an idea elaborated in his essay "The Republic of Science" (1962), influencing debates on science policy and freedom of inquiry.

Legacy

Polanyi's ideas influenced Thomas Kuhn's account of paradigms, management theory's concept of tacit knowledge, and later discussions of expertise and artificial intelligence, where the difficulty of encoding tacit skill is sometimes called "Polanyi's paradox."
