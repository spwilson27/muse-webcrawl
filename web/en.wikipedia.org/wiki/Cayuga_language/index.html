Cayuga (Cayuga: Gayogo̱hó꞉nǫʼ, "People of the Great Swamp") is a Northern Iroquoian language of the Cayuga people, one of the nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy. It was traditionally spoken around Cayuga Lake in what is now New York State. Today the largest community of speakers is at the Six Nations of the Grand River reserve in Ontario, Canada, with additional learners in New York and Oklahoma.

Status

Cayuga is severely endangered: the number of fluent first-language speakers is small, commonly estimated at fewer than a few hundred and largely elderly. Intensive revitalisation efforts are under way, including immersion programmes, adult second-language classes, and community language nests at Six Nations, where a distinct "Lower Cayuga" dialect tradition is centred at the Cayuga Longhouse. Documentation work, including dictionaries and recorded corpora, has been carried out by linguists and community scholars.

Phonology and grammar

Cayuga is a polysynthetic language: verbs are morphologically complex, incorporating pronominal prefixes that index subject and object, aspect suffixes, and incorporated nouns. The language famously lacks labial consonants such as /m/ and /p/. Its sound inventory includes oral and nasal vowels; vowel length and accent are contrastive, and word accent is determined by a complex interaction of syllable structure and vowel quality that has made Cayuga a classic case study in phonological theory.
