Andalusi classical music

Andalusi classical music (Arabic: al-mūsīqā al-andalusiyya), also called Andalusian music or al-āla in Morocco, is the art-music tradition of the Maghreb that traces its origins to the court music of al-Andalus, Muslim Iberia. It survives today in distinct regional schools in Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya, preserved by oral transmission over many centuries.

Origins

Tradition credits the ninth-century musician Ziryab, who emigrated from Baghdad to Córdoba, with founding the Andalusi school, systematizing song cycles and adding a fifth string to the oud. As Christian kingdoms conquered al-Andalus — culminating in the fall of Granada in 1492 and the expulsion of the Moriscos in 1609 — Muslim and Jewish refugees carried the repertoire to North African cities such as Fez, Tlemcen, Algiers, Constantine, Tunis, and Tripoli.

The nūba

The core form is the nūba, a suite of vocal and instrumental movements in a single melodic mode (ṭabʿ) unfolding through a fixed sequence of rhythmic cycles, traditionally lasting hours. Each regional tradition preserves its own set of nūbāt: Moroccan al-āla counts eleven complete suites, Algerian schools distinguish san'a (Algiers), gharnati (Tlemcen), and malouf (Constantine), and Tunisian malouf has its own canon. Texts are sung in classical Arabic and Andalusi dialect, drawing on the muwashshah and zajal poetic forms.

Instruments and preservation

Typical ensembles feature the oud, the rabab, violins held upright on the knee, the qanun, the tar and other frame drums, and the nay. Because the music was transmitted orally, modern conservatories and associations in North Africa have worked to notate and record the repertoire; festivals in Fez and elsewhere sustain the tradition today.
