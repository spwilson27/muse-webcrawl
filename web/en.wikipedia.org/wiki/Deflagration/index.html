Deflagration

Deflagration (Latin: de + flagrare, "to burn down") is subsonic combustion in which a flame front propagates through a gas or explosive by thermal conduction: hot combustion products heat the adjacent layer of unburned material above its ignition temperature, and the process repeats. Deflagration is distinguished from detonation, in which a shock wave travelling at supersonic speed compresses and ignites the material.

Characteristics

Flame speeds in deflagration range from well below 1 m/s in open burning to hundreds of metres per second in confined fuel–air mixtures, but always below the speed of sound in the unreacted material. The pressure rise is relatively gradual compared with a detonation, although confinement can allow pressure to build until a vessel or structure fails — the mechanism of many dust explosions and gas explosions in buildings. Most propellants, including gunpowder and smokeless powder in firearms, deflagrate rather than detonate; this controlled burning generates the expanding gases that propel a projectile. Fireworks likewise rely on deflagrating pyrotechnic compositions.

Deflagration to detonation transition

Under some conditions — strong confinement, long run-up distances in pipes, obstacles that generate turbulence — a deflagration can accelerate and undergo the deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT), a serious hazard in fuel handling, mining, and vapour cloud accidents. Explosives engineering and process safety design therefore pay close attention to venting, inerting, and limiting flame travel distances so that deflagrations cannot develop into detonations.
