Young's interference experiment

Young's interference experiment, also called Young's double-slit experiment, was performed by the English polymath Thomas Young in 1801 and published in his Bakerian Lecture of that year and his Course of Lectures (1807). By passing light through two closely spaced slits and projecting the result on a screen, Young observed alternating bright and dark fringes — a pattern explicable only if light from the two slits interferes, like overlapping water waves. The experiment was pivotal early evidence for the wave theory of light against Newton's corpuscular theory.

The experiment

Sunlight was admitted through a small hole and divided by a card edge (in Young's original form) or by two slits, so that the two beams were mutually coherent. Where the path lengths from the slits differ by whole wavelengths, the waves reinforce (bright fringe); where they differ by half wavelengths, they cancel (dark fringe). From the fringe spacing Young estimated the wavelength of light of different colours — the first such measurements.

Legacy

Fresnel's mathematical wave theory (1815–1818) built on Young's interference principle; the far-field pattern of the double slit is a case of Fraunhofer diffraction. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the double-slit experiment was repeated with electrons and other quantum particles — famously described by Richard Feynman as containing "the only mystery" of quantum mechanics — and single-particle versions demonstrate interference of each particle with itself.
