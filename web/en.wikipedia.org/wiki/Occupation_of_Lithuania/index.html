Occupation of Lithuania

The occupation of Lithuania refers to the successive occupations of the independent Republic of Lithuania by the Soviet Union (1940–1941 and 1944–1990) and by Nazi Germany (1941–1944). Together with Latvia and Estonia, Lithuania is one of the three Baltic states that were seized under the secret protocols of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and annexed by the USSR in 1940.

Soviet occupation of 1940

Following an ultimatum in June 1940, the Red Army occupied Lithuania, a staged election produced a "people's parliament", and in August 1940 the country was incorporated into the Soviet Union as the Lithuanian SSR. The first Soviet year brought nationalization, arrests, and, in June 1941, mass deportations of more than 17,000 people to Siberia.

Nazi occupation, 1941–1944

When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, Lithuanians initially greeted the Germans as liberators, but Nazi rule incorporated the country into the Reichskommissariat Ostland. The occupation was catastrophic for Lithuanian Jewry: about 195,000 of Lithuania's roughly 210,000 Jews were murdered in the Holocaust, many in mass shootings such as those at Ponary, often with the participation of local auxiliaries — one of the highest proportional losses in Europe.

Second Soviet occupation, 1944–1990

The return of the Red Army in 1944 restored Soviet rule for nearly five decades. Armed resistance by the "Forest Brothers" continued into the early 1950s. Collectivization, industrialization, immigration of Russian speakers, and further deportations — in total more than 130,000 people were deported from Lithuania — reshaped the country, while Lithuanian language and culture survived considerable Russification pressure.

Restoration of independence

Under glasnost, the Sąjūdis movement mobilized mass support for sovereignty. On 11 March 1990 Lithuania became the first Soviet republic to declare the restoration of its independence. The USSR responded with an economic blockade and, in January 1991, a military crackdown in Vilnius in which 14 civilians were killed defending the television tower. After the failed August 1991 coup in Moscow, Soviet recognition followed, and the last Russian troops withdrew in 1993. Many Western states had never recognized the annexation de jure, treating the restored republic as the continuation of the pre-war state.
