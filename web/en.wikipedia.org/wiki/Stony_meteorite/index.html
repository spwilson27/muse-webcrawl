Stony meteorite

Stony meteorites are meteorites composed largely of silicate minerals. They are by far the most numerous kind of meteorite: about 94% of all meteorites observed to fall are stones. Because they weather quickly and resemble terrestrial rocks, however, they are under-represented among chance finds compared with iron meteorites.

Classes

Stony meteorites divide into two great families. Chondrites are the most primitive objects in the Solar System: aggregates of chondrules (millimetre-sized once-molten droplets), refractory inclusions and fine matrix whose parent asteroids never melted. They are subdivided into ordinary, carbonaceous and enstatite chondrites and take their chemistry close to that of the Sun. Achondrites lack chondrules because their parent bodies melted and differentiated; they include basalts from Mars, the Moon and the asteroid Vesta (the HED meteorites), as well as more primitive achondrites such as acapulcoites and ureilites.

Characteristics

Most stony meteorites carry a thin dark fusion crust formed as the surface melted during atmospheric entry. Fresh interiors are typically grey; ordinary chondrites show flecks of nickel–iron metal and are weakly attracted to a magnet. Radiometric dating of chondrites gives an age of about 4.56 billion years for the oldest components, the calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions.

Scientific value

Because chondrites preserve a near-solar composition and record processes in the protoplanetary disc, they are central to cosmochemistry and to understanding planetary formation. Achondrites in turn sample the crusts and mantles of other worlds: several hundred meteorites are recognised pieces of Mars and of the Moon, delivered to Earth without any spacecraft.
