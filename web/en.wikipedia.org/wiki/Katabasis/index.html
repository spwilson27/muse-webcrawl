Katabasis

Katabasis

A katabasis (from Ancient Greek κατάβασις, katábasis, "descent") is a descent of some type, such as moving downhill, the sinking of the winds, or a military retreat. In poetry and mythology the term is used for a hero's journey down into the underworld—the land of the dead—and return to the world of the living, typically to seek knowledge, rescue a loved one, or complete a task.

In classical literature

The oldest surviving examples include the Sumerian and Akkadian descent of Inanna/Ishtar and Gilgamesh's journey in the Epic of Gilgamesh. In Greek and Roman literature famous katabases include:

  • Odysseus, who consults the shades in Book 11 (the Nekyia) of the Odyssey;
  • Orpheus, who descends to reclaim Eurydice and loses her by looking back;
  • Heracles, whose twelfth labour was to capture Cerberus, and who also rescued Theseus;
  • Theseus and Pirithous, who attempted to abduct Persephone and were trapped;
  • Aeneas, who descends with the Sibyl in Book 6 of Virgil's Aeneid to meet his father Anchises and learn Rome's destiny—itself the model for Dante's Inferno.

Analysis

Scholars of mythology, including Joseph Campbell in his monomyth framework, treat katabasis as a stage of the hero's journey symbolising death and rebirth, initiation, or confrontation with the unconscious. The successful return (anabasis) transforms the hero. The motif recurs across cultures—in Egyptian funerary texts, Japanese myth (Izanagi in Yomi), Maya Hero Twins in Xibalba, and in modern literature and film.

Military and other senses

In classical military writing, katabasis can mean a retreat or a march down from high ground to the sea, famously the return march of the Ten Thousand described in Xenophon's Anabasis. In rhetoric it denotes a gradual descent in style; in meteorology, katabatic winds flow downhill under gravity.
