The prime minister of Canada (French: premier ministre du Canada) is the head of government of Canada and the country's chief executive. The prime minister is appointed by the governor general — the monarch's representative — and by constitutional convention must be the person best able to command the confidence of the elected House of Commons, almost invariably the leader of the largest party there. The office is not established by the text of the Constitution, which mentions it only obliquely, yet the prime minister is the dominant figure in Canadian politics: chairing the Cabinet, directing the executive, and advising the Crown on appointments, dissolution, and the summoning of Parliament. The incumbent since 14 March 2025 is Mark Carney, the 24th prime minister, who succeeded Justin Trudeau as leader of the Liberal Party and then led the Liberals to victory in the 2025 federal election.

Role and powers

The prime minister selects Cabinet ministers, allocates portfolios, and may dismiss ministers at will; determines government policy and legislative priorities; advises the governor general on the appointment of senators, judges (including of the Supreme Court), provincial lieutenant governors, and senior officials; and represents Canada internationally as head of government — in the G7, the Commonwealth, and NORAD and NATO councils. The prime minister's power over the machinery of government, party discipline, and appointments is extensive by comparative standards — Lord Hailsham's phrase "elective dictatorship" has often been applied to the Canadian variant — checked principally by federalism, the courts under the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, minority parliaments, and the confidence convention itself.

History

The office dates from Confederation in 1867, when Sir John A. Macdonald, architect of the union of the British North American colonies, became the first prime minister. Thirty individuals (counting repeat terms separately) have held the office. Long-serving premiers have defined eras: Macdonald (1867–73, 1878–91), Wilfrid Laurier (1896–1911), William Lyon Mackenzie King — Canada's longest-serving prime minister, in office for over 21 years across the interwar and Second World War periods — and Pierre Trudeau (1968–79, 1980–84), who patriated the Constitution in 1982. Kim Campbell (1993) is the only woman to have held the office. Stephen Harper (2006–15) led the modern Conservative Party's first governments; his successor Justin Trudeau (2015–2025) served nearly a decade before resigning amid caucus pressure, handing over to Carney, a former governor of both the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England.

Residence and office

The official residence is 24 Sussex Drive in Ottawa, occupied by prime ministers since 1951 but vacated in 2015 because of its deteriorated condition; Justin Trudeau and Mark Carney have lived at Rideau Cottage on the grounds of Rideau Hall. The prime minister's working office is in the Office of the Prime Minister and Privy Council building (formerly Langevin Block) opposite Parliament Hill, supported by the Prime Minister's Office (PMO) and the Privy Council Office.
