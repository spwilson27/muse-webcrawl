Hepatoscopy

Hepatoscopy (also called hepatomancy) is the practice of divination by inspecting the liver of a sacrificed animal. It is a form of extispicy — reading omens in animal entrails — and was one of the most prestigious and technically developed branches of divination in the ancient Near East and the Mediterranean world.

Mesopotamia

The practice reached its fullest development in Babylonia and Assyria, where specialist diviners, the bārû, examined the liver of a sacrificed sheep, treating it as a tablet on which the gods wrote their verdict on a question put by the king or a client. Features of the organ — the gall bladder, lobes, markings and anomalies — were interpreted according to a vast compendium of omens, the bārûtu series. Clay models of livers, inscribed with omen texts, survive from the second millennium BC; one famous example comes from Babylon and is now in the British Museum.

Etruscans and Rome

The Etruscans practised a related art, the haruspicina, whose practitioners became the Roman haruspices. The bronze Liver of Piacenza (c. 100 BC) is a life-size model of a sheep's liver divided into regions inscribed with the names of Etruscan gods, mapping the organ onto the sky. Hepatoscopy and related extispicy survived in Roman state ritual for centuries before declining with the Christianisation of the empire in the fourth century AD.

Basis

The liver was a natural focus for divination: it is the largest internal organ, rich in blood — the seat of life in ancient thought — and varies visibly between animals. The practice is one of the earliest examples of systematic anatomical observation, albeit in a religious framework.
