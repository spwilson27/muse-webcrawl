Sum of the parts valuation

Sum of the parts valuation (SOTP) is a method of valuing a company by valuing each of its business segments or subsidiaries separately and adding the individual values together to arrive at the total enterprise value. The method is especially common for conglomerates and holding companies whose divisions operate in different industries, where a single set of valuation multiples would be misleading.

Method

The analyst first identifies the company's distinct business units. Each unit is valued on a standalone basis using the technique most appropriate to its industry — commonly a multiple of earnings (EV/EBITDA, P/E) drawn from comparable listed companies, or a discounted cash flow analysis. Listed stakes in other companies are typically valued at market price, sometimes with a holding-company discount. The segment values are summed, corporate overhead, net debt, pension deficits, minority interests, and other corporate items are then added or deducted, and the result is compared with the company's market capitalisation to judge whether the stock trades at a conglomerate discount or premium.

Uses

SOTP analysis is a standard tool of activist investors and takeover research: if the sum of the parts substantially exceeds the market value, they may argue for break-ups, spin-offs, or asset sales to unlock value. It is also used to value companies with a mix of consolidated operations and significant quoted investments, such as Berkshire Hathaway or diversified Asian conglomerates, and to analyse the value of a business ahead of a de-merger.

Limitations

The method depends heavily on segment-level disclosures, which may be coarse; synergies and shared costs between divisions are hard to allocate; and the resulting total often requires a discount for holding-company costs, tax leakage on hypothetical disposals, and the corporate centre — the source of the well-known "conglomerate discount".
