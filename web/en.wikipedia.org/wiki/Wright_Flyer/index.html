The Wright Flyer (also known as the Kitty Hawk Flyer or Flyer I) was the first successful heavier-than-air powered aircraft. Designed and built by the Wright brothers, it made the first sustained, controlled flights by a powered aeroplane on 17 December 1903 at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina.

Design

The Flyer was a biplane built of spruce and ash covered with muslin fabric, with a wingspan of 12.3 metres. Its 12-horsepower petrol engine, built by the brothers' mechanic Charlie Taylor, drove two pusher propellers through chain drives. Crucially, the aircraft could be controlled in all three axes: pitch by a forward elevator, yaw by a rear rudder, and roll by wing-warping, in which the pilot, lying prone on the lower wing, twisted the wingtips by moving a hip cradle. The brothers had developed this three-axis control through their 1902 glider experiments.

The first flights

After winning a coin toss, Wilbur made an unsuccessful attempt on 14 December. On 17 December 1903, with Orville at the controls, the Flyer made its first powered flight, lasting 12 seconds and covering 36.5 metres (120 feet). Four flights were made that day; the fourth, piloted by Wilbur, lasted 59 seconds and covered 259.7 metres (852 feet). A gust of wind overturned and damaged the machine after the final flight, and it never flew again.

Preservation

The Flyer was exhibited at the Science Museum in London from 1928 until 1948, during a long dispute between Orville Wright and the Smithsonian Institution over credit for the first flight. It was then transferred to the Smithsonian, where it now hangs in the National Air and Space Museum in Washington, D.C.
