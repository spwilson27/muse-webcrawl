Seal (East Asia)

In the cultures of East Asia, a seal is a general name for a printing stamp used in place of, or alongside, a signature. Seals originated in China and spread to Japan, Korea, Vietnam and neighbouring regions. They are used by individuals and organisations on documents, works of art and correspondence, and remain legally significant in several countries.

History

Chinese seals (印, yìn) have been used since at least the Shang and Zhou dynasties, initially in bronze. The First Emperor of Qin standardised the imperial seal; the Heirloom Seal of the Realm, carved from jade, became a symbol of legitimate rule. Private seals proliferated during the Han dynasty and later became an art form among scholar-artists, who carved their own seals in stone with seal script characters.

Use

Seal impressions are made with a red paste of cinnabar and oil, applied to the seal face and pressed onto paper. In Japan, personal seals (hanko) are registered with local government and used for official transactions; similar systems exist in South Korea (dojang) and Taiwan. In calligraphy and painting, seals of the artist, collectors and connoisseurs are stamped on the work itself.

Seal carving

The carving of seals (篆刻) is recognised as a traditional art; designs may show the characters in relief (red characters on white ground) or intaglio (white characters on red ground).
