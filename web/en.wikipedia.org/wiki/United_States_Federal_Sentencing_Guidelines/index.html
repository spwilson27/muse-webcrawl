United States Federal Sentencing Guidelines

The United States Federal Sentencing Guidelines are policies issued by the United States Sentencing Commission that set out a uniform sentencing policy for individuals and organisations convicted of felonies and serious misdemeanours in the federal courts of the United States. Created by the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 and effective from 1 November 1987, the Guidelines were designed to reduce unwarranted sentencing disparities among similarly situated defendants. Initially mandatory, they have been advisory since the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Booker (2005), although federal judges must still calculate the applicable guideline range and consider it at sentencing.

Structure

A defendant's guideline sentence is determined principally by two factors:

  • The offense level (1 to 43), computed from a base level for the crime of conviction and adjusted for "specific offense characteristics", the defendant's role, obstruction, and acceptance of responsibility.
  • The criminal history category (I to VI), based mainly on the length and recency of prior sentences.

The Guidelines also address fines, probation, supervised release, restitution and organisational defendants. A key feature is relevant conduct: sentencing is based substantially on the defendant's actual conduct, including conduct underlying dismissed or even acquitted counts — a practice upheld in United States v. Watts (1997) and still controversial.

History

The Guidelines emerged from bipartisan dissatisfaction with indeterminate sentencing and wide judicial disparity. The Sentencing Reform Act created the Sentencing Commission as an independent agency in the judicial branch; its first chair was Judge William W. Wilkins Jr., and Judge Stephen Breyer was an influential early commissioner. The Supreme Court upheld the Commission's constitutionality in Mistral v. United States (1989).

In United States v. Booker (2005), decided the term after Blakely v. Washington, the Court held that the mandatory Guidelines violated the Sixth Amendment right to jury trial insofar as they required judges to impose higher sentences based on facts not found by a jury; the remedial opinion excised the mandatory provisions, rendering the Guidelines advisory. Subsequent cases — Rita, Gall and Kimbrough (all 2007) — confirmed appellate review for reasonableness and district courts' discretion to vary based on policy disagreements, for example over the 100-to-1 crack/powder cocaine disparity, which Congress reduced to 18-to-1 in the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010.

Criticism

The Guidelines have been criticised for severity (especially for drug offences driven by congressionally mandated minimums), for transferring discretion from judges to prosecutors, and for complexity. Defenders credit them with measurable reductions in regional and demographic sentencing disparities.
