The Haudenosaunee (/ˌhoʊdɪnoʊˈʃoʊniː/; "People of the Longhouse"), also known as the Iroquois Confederacy or the Six Nations, are a league of Native American nations in the Northeastern Woodlands. The confederacy consists originally of five nations — the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca — unified under the Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'ko:wa), with the Tuscarora joining in the early 18th century as the sixth nation.

History

According to tradition, the confederacy was founded by the Great Peacemaker (Deganawida), aided by Hiawatha and the clan mother Jigonhsasee, who persuaded the five warring nations to bury their weapons beneath the Tree of Peace at Onondaga and govern through a Grand Council of fifty sachems, nominated by clan mothers in a matrilineal system. The date of the union is debated, with proposals ranging from 1142 to the late 16th century.

The Haudenosaunee became a dominant power in the Northeast through diplomacy and the Beaver Wars of the 17th century, playing French, Dutch, and English interests against each other under the Covenant Chain. The confederacy split during the American Revolutionary War — most nations sided with Britain, the Oneida and Tuscarora with the Americans — and Sullivan's 1779 expedition devastated Iroquoia, after which much of the population resettled in Canada or on reduced reservations in New York State.

Governance and culture

The Grand Council still meets at Onondaga, the confederacy's capital fire. Haudenosaunee society is traditionally organised in matrilineal clans (Turtle, Wolf, Bear, and others) across the nations; longhouse ceremonies of the Handsome Lake tradition (Gaiwiio) remain central to community life, and the confederacy issues its own passports and fields a celebrated national lacrosse team in the sport its people created (Deyhontsigwa'ehs, "they bump hips"). Scholars debate the extent of the Great Law's influence on the United States Constitution, an influence acknowledged by a 1988 U.S. Senate resolution.
