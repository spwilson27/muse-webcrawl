Copyright registration

Copyright registration is the process of formally recording a copyright claim with a public authority. Under the Berne Convention, copyright arises automatically when an original work is fixed, so registration is not required for protection in most countries; however, some jurisdictions — most notably the United States — attach significant legal advantages to registering a work with the national copyright office.

United States

In the United States, registration is administered by the United States Copyright Office. Although voluntary, registration (or a refusal of registration) is generally a prerequisite for filing an infringement suit over a U.S. work, as confirmed by the Supreme Court in Fourth Estate Public Benefit Corp. v. Wall-Street.com (2019). Timely registration — before infringement or within three months of publication — makes the registrant eligible for statutory damages and attorney's fees, which are often decisive in litigation. Applicants deposit copies of the work, which historically enriched the collections of the Library of Congress.

Other countries

Most Berne Convention countries have no registration requirement. Some, such as Canada, China and Spain, maintain voluntary registries whose certificates serve as prima facie evidence of ownership. Historically, formalities such as registration, notice and renewal were mandatory in the U.S.; the renewal requirement left many older works in the public domain when owners failed to renew.
