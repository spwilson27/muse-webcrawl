Tribunal (Catholic Church)

In the Catholic Church, a tribunal is a court of canon law that exercises the judicial power of a diocesan bishop or of the Holy See. Tribunals are governed principally by Book VII of the 1983 Code of Canon Law and are staffed by officials including the judicial vicar, judges, the defender of the bond, the promoter of justice, advocates, and notaries.

Structure

Each diocese has a tribunal of first instance, ordinarily headed by the judicial vicar (officialis). Appeals go to a tribunal of second instance — often a metropolitan archdiocesan tribunal or another tribunal designated by the Holy See. At the universal level, the Roman Rota is the ordinary appellate tribunal of the Apostolic See, and the Supreme Tribunal of the Apostolic Signatura serves as the Church's highest administrative and judicial oversight body.

Cases

Most diocesan tribunal work concerns petitions for declarations of nullity of marriage ("annulments"), in which petitioners argue that a marriage was invalid from the beginning on grounds such as lack of due discretion, simulation, or an impediment. Tribunals also hear penal cases, contentious cases about rights and property, and cases concerning the rights of the faithful. In 2015 Pope Francis, through the motu proprio Mitis Iudex Dominus Iesus, streamlined the nullity process, abolishing the automatic double sentence and introducing a shorter process before the bishop in manifest cases.
