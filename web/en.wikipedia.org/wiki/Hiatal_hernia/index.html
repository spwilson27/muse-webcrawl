Hiatal hernia

Hiatal hernia
SpecialtyGastroenterology, general surgery
TypesSliding (type I); paraesophageal (types II–IV)
SymptomsOften none; heartburn, reflux, chest pain, difficulty swallowing
Risk factorsAge, obesity, raised intra-abdominal pressure

A hiatal hernia (also hiatus hernia) is a condition in which part of the stomach protrudes upward through the oesophageal hiatus of the diaphragm into the chest. Hiatal hernias are very common, especially with increasing age, and many cause no symptoms at all. When symptomatic, they most often produce gastro-oesophageal reflux — heartburn, regurgitation and sometimes difficulty swallowing — because the hernia disrupts the anti-reflux barrier at the gastro-oesophageal junction.

Types

The commonest form (about 95%) is the sliding hernia (type I), in which the gastro-oesophageal junction and the gastric cardia slide up into the chest. In a paraesophageal hernia (type II) the junction stays in place but part of the stomach fundus herniates beside the oesophagus. Type III combines the two, and type IV involves herniation of other organs, such as colon or spleen, into the chest. Paraesophageal hernias less often cause reflux but risk gastric volvulus, obstruction and strangulation.

Diagnosis

Hernias are frequently discovered incidentally on imaging done for other reasons. Diagnosis is by barium swallow X-ray, upper endoscopy, or CT scan; oesophageal manometry and pH studies may be used when surgery is planned or reflux is being evaluated.

Treatment

Asymptomatic sliding hernias need no treatment. Symptomatic reflux is managed with weight loss, dietary measures, elevation of the head of the bed, and acid-suppressing drugs, especially proton pump inhibitors. Surgery — laparoscopic repair of the hiatus, often combined with a fundoplication — is considered for large or paraesophageal hernias, complications such as volvulus or bleeding (including iron-deficiency anaemia from Cameron erosions), or reflux refractory to medical therapy.
