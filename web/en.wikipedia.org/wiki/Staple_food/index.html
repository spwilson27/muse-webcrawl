Staple food

A staple food, or simply a staple, is a food that is eaten often and in such quantities that it constitutes a dominant portion of a standard diet for an individual or a population group, supplying a large fraction of energy needs and generally a significant proportion of the intake of other nutrients as well. Most people live on a diet based on just a small number of food staples, which vary by place, climate and culture.

Major staples

Staple foods are typically cheap, widely available, storable and calorie-dense. They include cereals such as rice, wheat, maize (corn), millet and sorghum; root and tuber crops such as potatoes, cassava, yams, taro and sweet potatoes; and, in some regions, pulses and plantains. Of the more than 50,000 edible plant species in the world, only a few hundred contribute significantly to food supplies, and just three cereals — rice, wheat and maize — provide roughly 60% of the world's food energy intake.

Nutrition

Staples are rich in starch but often low in certain essential amino acids, vitamins and minerals — cereal proteins, for example, tend to be low in lysine, while legumes are low in methionine — so traditional diets typically combine staples with complementary foods such as beans, vegetables, dairy or meat. Heavy dependence on a single staple carries risks: diets dominated by polished rice were historically associated with beriberi (vitamin B1 deficiency), and maize-dependent diets with pellagra (niacin deficiency) where the grain is not nixtamalised.

History and production

The Neolithic Revolution centred on the domestication of staple crops — wheat and barley in the Fertile Crescent, rice in China, maize in Mesoamerica, potatoes in the Andes — and surpluses of storable staples underpinned the rise of cities and states. Today, global staple production is dominated by a handful of high-yield crops grown with modern inputs, a concentration that makes food security sensitive to climate shocks, plant disease and price volatility in those commodities.
