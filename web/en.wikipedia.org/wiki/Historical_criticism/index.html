Historical criticism

Also known asHigher criticism, historical-critical method
FieldBiblical studies, historiography, literary studies
Origins17th–19th century Europe

Historical criticism, also known as the historical-critical method or higher criticism, is a branch of criticism that investigates the origins of ancient texts in order to understand "the world behind the text" and to discover the original or primary meaning of the text in its own historical context. It is the primary method of study for scholars in the field of biblical criticism, but it is also applied to other religious and secular texts.

Historical criticism began in the 17th century, reached its apex in the 19th century, and was a dominant approach through the mid-20th century. It begins with the assumption that a text's meaning is recoverable, at least in part, by reconstructing the circumstances of its composition.

Methods

The historical-critical method is not a single technique but an umbrella for several complementary approaches:

  • Source criticism seeks to identify the sources an author drew upon, such as the hypothesized sources of the Pentateuch in the documentary hypothesis.
  • Form criticism classifies texts by genre and attempts to trace each unit to its pre-literary oral stage and its setting in life (Sitz im Leben).
  • Redaction criticism examines how editors assembled and modified source material to serve their own theological aims.
  • Tradition criticism studies how traditions developed and changed as they were transmitted.
  • Textual criticism (or lower criticism) works to reconstruct the most accurate wording of the text from its surviving manuscripts.

History

Early practitioners included Baruch Spinoza, whose Tractatus Theologico-Politicus (1670) questioned Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch, and Richard Simon, a French Catholic priest who applied critical methods to the biblical text. In the 19th century, German scholarship — figures such as Julius Wellhausen, whose documentary hypothesis reshaped Pentateuchal studies — set the research agenda. The method provoked controversy with religious authorities; the Roman Catholic Church initially condemned it during the Modernist crisis before accepting much of it in the 20th century, notably in the 1943 encyclical Divino afflante Spiritu.

Criticism and alternatives

From the later 20th century, historical criticism was supplemented and sometimes challenged by literary approaches — narrative criticism, canonical criticism, and rhetorical criticism — that focus on the final form of the text rather than its prehistory, as well as by postcolonial and feminist readings. Critics have charged the historical-critical method with speculative reconstructions of sources and with privileging origins over meaning.
