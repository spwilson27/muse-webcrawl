Praxeology

Praxeology (from Greek praxis, "action") is the study of human action — in its best-known form, the deductive methodology of economics developed by the Austrian School economist Ludwig von Mises. Mises held that economic theory begins from a self-evident axiom — humans act purposefully to achieve chosen ends — and deduces its conclusions logically, rather than testing hypotheses against empirical data as in the natural sciences. He set out the system most fully in Human Action (1949).

Method

For Mises, praxeology is a priori: because the action axiom is undeniable (denying it is itself an action), the theorems deduced from it — concerning value, exchange, prices, money, interest and the business cycle — are necessarily true of any world in which people act, in the same way that geometry follows from its axioms. Praxeology therefore rejects econometrics, mathematical modelling of the mainstream kind and the idea that economic propositions can be falsified by statistics; history can illustrate but never test theory.

Criticism

Mainstream philosophers of science and economists have criticised praxeology for exactly this insulation from evidence: critics such as Terence Hutchison and Mark Blaug argue that if deductions rest on auxiliary assumptions about the real world, those assumptions are empirical and corrigible. Defenders, notably Murray Rothbard and later the Mises Institute, respond that praxeology is the logic of action itself and that its critics confuse the study of ends-directed behaviour with physics. The term is also used more broadly, outside economics, for the general study of human conduct and efficient action (as in the work of the Polish philosopher Tadeusz Kotarbiński).
