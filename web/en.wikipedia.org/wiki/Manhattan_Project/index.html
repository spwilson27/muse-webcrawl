Manhattan Project
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

The Manhattan Project was a research and development programme undertaken during World War II by the United States, with the United Kingdom and Canada, that produced the first nuclear weapons. Led by the US Army's General Leslie Groves, with J. Robert Oppenheimer directing the Los Alamos laboratory, it employed more than 100,000 people at secret sites including Oak Ridge, Hanford and Los Alamos.

Prompted by fears that Nazi Germany would build a bomb first (and by the Einstein–Szilárd letter of 1939), the project developed two weapon designs: a uranium gun-type bomb ('Little Boy') and a plutonium implosion bomb ('Fat Man'), the latter tested at Trinity on 16 July 1945. In August 1945 the bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, killing well over 100,000 people, mostly civilians, and hastening the end of the war — an outcome debated ever since. After the war, civilian control passed to the Atomic Energy Commission, and the project alumni shaped postwar physics.

Science and industry

Key technical feats included industrial-scale uranium enrichment (electromagnetic, gaseous-diffusion and thermal methods at Oak Ridge) and plutonium production in Hanford's reactors — solving Fermi's Chicago Pile physics at gigantic scale, in secrecy, in under three years.
