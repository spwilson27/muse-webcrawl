Nuclear programme of India

The nuclear programme of India encompasses the development of nuclear power generation, research reactors and nuclear weapons capability. India has a largely indigenous programme, shaped by its limited uranium reserves and abundant thorium deposits, and by its position outside the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT).

Homi Bhabha and the three-stage programme

The physicist Homi J. Bhabha founded the Atomic Energy Commission in 1948 and the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research, and in the 1950s formulated India's three-stage nuclear power programme:

  1. Pressurised heavy water reactors (PHWRs) burning natural uranium, producing plutonium as a by-product.
  2. Fast breeder reactors using that plutonium to breed further fuel and convert thorium into fissile uranium-233.
  3. Advanced reactors based on the thorium–uranium-233 cycle, exploiting India's large thorium reserves.

India's first research reactor, Apsara, went critical in 1956, the first in Asia outside the Soviet Union. The 500 MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor at Kalpakkam represents stage two.

Weapons and tests

India conducted a nuclear explosion in 1974, described as a "peaceful nuclear explosion" (Smiling Buddha), and weaponised tests in May 1998 (Pokhran-II), after which it declared a policy of credible minimum deterrence and no first use. India is estimated to possess a growing stockpile of warheads delivered by land-based and air-launched missiles and by nuclear-powered submarines of the Arihant class.

Civil nuclear cooperation

Following the 2005 India–United States civil nuclear initiative and a 2008 waiver from the Nuclear Suppliers Group, India concluded fuel and reactor agreements with several countries and placed its civilian reactors under IAEA safeguards, while keeping its military programme outside them.
