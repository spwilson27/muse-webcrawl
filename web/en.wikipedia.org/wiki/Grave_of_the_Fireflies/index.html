Grave of the Fireflies (Japanese: 火垂るの墓, Hotaru no Haka) is a 1988 Japanese animated war drama film written and directed by Isao Takahata, and produced by Studio Ghibli. It is based on Akiyuki Nosaka's 1967 semi-autobiographical short story of the same name. Set in Kobe in the final months of the Second World War, it follows two siblings, Seita and Setsuko, struggling to survive after their mother is killed in an air raid.

Plot

In March 1945, an American firebombing raid on Kobe kills Seita and Setsuko's mother; their father serves in the Imperial Japanese Navy and cannot be reached. The children move in with a distant aunt, but relations sour as food grows scarce, and they leave to live alone in an abandoned air-raid shelter by a pond, catching fireflies for light and foraging or stealing food. As malnutrition and illness take hold, Setsuko weakens and dies; Seita cremates her, places her ashes in a tin of Sakuma drops — the fruit candies she loved — and, after Japan's surrender and the news of his father's death, dies of starvation at Sannomiya Station. The film is framed by Seita's narration as a ghost, reunited with Setsuko among the fireflies.

Production and release

Takahata, who had himself survived the firebombing of Okayama as a boy, insisted on realistic portrayal of the period's hardships and on animating the children's behaviour from observation. The film was released on a double bill with Hayao Miyazaki's My Neighbor Totoro, a pairing of starkly contrasting tones; it underperformed theatrically relative to expectations but gained its reputation through television broadcasts, home video and critical reassessment. A live-action television film followed in 2005, and a live-action feature adaptation in 2008.

Reception and legacy

Grave of the Fireflies is widely regarded as one of the greatest animated films ever made and one of the most powerful anti-war films in any medium. Roger Ebert included it in his "Great Movies" series, calling it "an emotional experience so powerful that it forces a rethinking of animation". Critics have debated whether it is best read as an anti-war statement — Takahata denied intending a simple pacifist message — or as a tragedy of pride and social breakdown, since Seita's refusal to return to his aunt seals the children's fate.
