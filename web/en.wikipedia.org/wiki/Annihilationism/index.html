Annihilationism

In Christian theology, annihilationism (also known as extinctionism or destructionism) is the belief that after the Last Judgment, all damned humans and fallen angels (including Satan) will be totally destroyed, so as to not exist, or their consciousness will be extinguished, rather than suffer everlasting torment in hell.

Overview

Annihilationism is often paired with conditional immortality: the view that the human soul is not inherently immortal, and that immortality is a gift granted only to the saved. Proponents appeal to biblical passages describing the wicked "perishing", being "destroyed", and suffering the "second death" as evidence that hell ends in non-existence rather than eternal conscious suffering.

History

Though a minority view through most of church history, annihilationist ideas were defended in the nineteenth century by figures such as Edward White, and in the twentieth century gained wider attention through advocates including John Stott, who described himself as tentatively holding the view, and the theologian Edward Fudge, author of The Fire That Consumes (1982). Denominations such as Seventh-day Adventists and Jehovah's Witnesses teach forms of annihilationism.

Criticism

Critics maintain that scriptural language about "eternal punishment" (Matthew 25:46) parallels the "eternal life" of the righteous, implying conscious duration, and that annihilationism undermines the seriousness of divine judgment.
