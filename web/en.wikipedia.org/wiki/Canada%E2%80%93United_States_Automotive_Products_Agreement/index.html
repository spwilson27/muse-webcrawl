Canada–United States Automotive Products Agreement

The Canada–United States Automotive Products Agreement, commonly known as the Auto Pact, was a trade agreement between Canada and the United States signed in January 1965 and implemented in 1966. It removed tariffs on cars, trucks, buses and original-equipment auto parts between the two countries, creating a single North American market for the "Big Three" American automakers while guaranteeing minimum production levels in Canada.

Terms

In return for duty-free treatment, manufacturers committed to maintain Canadian production in proportion to Canadian sales and to increase Canadian value added. The pact rationalized the industry: instead of short runs of many models for the small Canadian market, Canadian plants produced fewer models for the whole continent, greatly increasing productivity and employment in Ontario's auto sector.

End

The Auto Pact was superseded in effect by the Canada–U.S. Free Trade Agreement (1989) and NAFTA (1994). After a WTO ruling that its remaining benefits violated trade rules, the pact was formally terminated in 2001. It is widely regarded as one of the most successful sectoral trade agreements and a foundation of the integrated North American auto industry.
