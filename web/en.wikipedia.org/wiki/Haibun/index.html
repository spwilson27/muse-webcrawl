Haibun (Japanese: 俳文, "haikai writings") is a prosimetric literary form originating in Japan, combining prose and haiku. It is often used for travel writing and diaries, in which spare, heightened prose passages are punctuated by one or more haiku that resonate with, rather than merely summarise, the prose around them.

Bashō's haibun record his pilgrimages on foot through northern Japan.

History

The form was perfected by Matsuo Bashō in the late seventeenth century. His masterpiece, Oku no Hosomichi (Narrow Road to the Deep North, composed after a 1689 journey and published in 1702), interweaves lyrical prose descriptions of famous places with haiku written on the road. Other Edo-period masters, including Yosa Buson and Kobayashi Issa, wrote haibun, and the form was revived in twentieth-century Japan.

Characteristics

Classical haibun prose is concise, imagistic and often written in the first person, moving between scene, reflection and allusion. The haiku typically stands apart from the prose: rather than repeating it, it shifts the angle of vision, creating the resonant gap prized in haikai aesthetics. Subjects range from journeys and nature to poverty, friendship and death.

Haibun in English

From the late twentieth century, English-language poets adopted haibun widely, especially in haiku journals and small-press collections. Modern English haibun often permits greater narrative looseness and multiple haiku or tanka sequences, while retaining the core dialogue between prose and poem.
