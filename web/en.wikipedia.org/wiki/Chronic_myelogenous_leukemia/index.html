Chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), also known as chronic myeloid leukemia, is a cancer of the white blood cells. It is a form of leukemia characterised by the increased and unregulated growth of myeloid cells in the bone marrow and the accumulation of these cells in the blood. CML is a clonal bone marrow stem cell disorder in which proliferation of mature granulocytes and their precursors occurs, driven by the BCR-ABL fusion gene created by the Philadelphia chromosome.

Signs and symptoms

Many patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis, which is often made from a routine blood count showing leukocytosis. When symptoms occur they include fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, low-grade fever, and fullness in the left upper abdomen from splenomegaly.

Cause

CML is caused by a reciprocal translocation, t(9;22)(q34;q11), between chromosomes 9 and 22. The shortened chromosome 22 is the Philadelphia chromosome. The translocation fuses the ABL1 gene from chromosome 9 with the BCR gene on chromosome 22, producing a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives uncontrolled cell division. Radiation exposure is a risk factor; benzene is a weaker one.

Phases

Untreated CML progresses through three phases: a chronic phase (often lasting years, with mild or no symptoms), an accelerated phase, and a blast crisis, in which the disease behaves like an acute leukemia and is rapidly fatal without treatment.

Treatment

The advent of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) transformed CML from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition. Imatinib (Gleevec), introduced in 2001, was the first targeted cancer therapy of its kind; second- and third-generation TKIs include dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib and ponatinib. Allogeneic stem cell transplantation remains an option for resistant disease. Many patients achieve deep molecular responses permitting treatment-free remission trials.

Epidemiology

CML accounts for roughly 15% of adult leukemias, with an incidence of about 1–2 cases per 100,000 people per year. It affects slightly more men than women and is primarily a disease of older adults, though it can occur at any age.
