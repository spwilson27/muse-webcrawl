Grebes are aquatic diving birds in the order Podicipediformes and family Podicipedidae. They are widely distributed across freshwater habitats on every continent except Antarctica, with some species also visiting marine coasts in winter. Grebes range in size from the least grebe, at about 23 cm long, to the great grebe of South America, which may exceed 70 cm.

Description

Grebes have soft, dense plumage, a sharp pointed bill in most species, and legs set far back on the body — an adaptation for swimming that makes them awkward on land. Unlike ducks and coots, they have lobed toes rather than fully webbed feet. They are excellent divers, propelling themselves underwater with their feet, and feed mainly on fish, crustaceans, molluscs and aquatic insects. Several species, such as the great crested grebe, develop ornate head plumes in the breeding season.

Behaviour and breeding

Grebes are famous for their elaborate courtship displays, including synchronised "dances" in which partners rush across the water surface together. They build floating nests of plant material anchored to emergent vegetation. The downy chicks are precocial and are frequently carried on their parents' backs. Grebes habitually eat their own feathers and feed feathers to their young, apparently to aid digestion and protect the stomach from sharp fish bones.

Evolution and taxonomy

Molecular studies indicate that grebes are most closely related to flamingos, with which they form the clade Mirandornithes — a surprising result, given the birds' very different appearance and ecology. The fossil record of grebes extends back to the Late Oligocene or Early Miocene. Several species have become extinct in recent centuries, including the Alaotra grebe and the Atitlán grebe, and a number of surviving species are threatened by habitat loss and introduced predators.
