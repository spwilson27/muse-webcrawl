Bournville is a model village on the south side of Birmingham, England, best known for its chocolate factory, the Cadbury family's former business. It was founded by the Quaker philanthropists Richard and George Cadbury, who moved their cocoa and chocolate factory from central Birmingham to the greenfield site in 1879, choosing a location by the Bourn stream with good canal and rail access. The name combined "Bourn" with the French word ville ("town"), partly because French chocolate enjoyed a high reputation.

The model village

From 1893 George Cadbury bought land around the factory to build a garden suburb of well-designed Arts and Crafts-style cottages with gardens, intended to provide healthy housing at affordable rents. In 1900 he established the Bournville Village Trust to administer and develop the estate independently of the company. Houses were widely spaced along tree-lined roads with generous open spaces, a village green, schools, a church and community facilities; in keeping with the Cadbury family's Quaker temperance convictions, the village famously had no public houses. Alcohol sales remained restricted in the area for well over a century, until a licence was first granted in the 2010s.

Influence and present day

Bournville became a landmark of British social housing and an inspiration for the garden city movement associated with Ebenezer Howard, as well as for later model settlements such as Port Sunlight and New Earswick. The estate — now around 1,000 acres with thousands of homes — continues to be managed by the Bournville Village Trust. The Cadbury factory remains in operation under Mondelez International, and the visitor attraction Cadbury World opened on the site in 1990.
