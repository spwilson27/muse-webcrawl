In Greek mythology, Aeolus (Greek: Αἴολος, "quick-moving, nimble") is a name shared by several figures, most famously the divine keeper of the winds. Ancient authors sometimes conflated these figures, and later tradition merged them into a single god of the winds.

Aeolus, keeper of the winds

In Homer's Odyssey, Aeolus, son of Hippotes, is the mortal ruler of the floating island of Aeolia, appointed by Zeus as steward of the winds. He receives Odysseus hospitably and gives him a bag containing all the adverse winds, leaving only the favourable west wind free to blow the ship home. Within sight of Ithaca, Odysseus's crew open the bag while he sleeps, and the released winds drive the ship back to Aeolia, where Aeolus refuses further help to a man he believes cursed by the gods.

In Virgil's Aeneid, Aeolus keeps the winds imprisoned in a mountain cave and, at Juno's request, releases a storm against Aeneas's fleet.

Other figures named Aeolus

Another Aeolus was a son of Hellen, ancestor of the Aeolian Greeks, and brother of Dorus and Xuthus. A third was a son of Poseidon and Arne, twin of Boeotus, associated with the Aeolian Islands and the city of Tyrrhenia in some traditions.

Legacy

Aeolus gives his name to aeolian processes (wind-driven geological processes), the Aeolian harp, and the Aeolian Islands off Sicily, where his island home was located by later writers.
