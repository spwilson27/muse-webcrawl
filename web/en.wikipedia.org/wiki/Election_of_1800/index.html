The 1800 United States presidential election was the fourth quadrennial presidential election. It was held from October 31 to December 3, 1800. In what is sometimes called the "Revolution of 1800", the Democratic-Republican Party candidate, Vice President Thomas Jefferson, defeated the incumbent Federalist President John Adams. The election was a political realignment that ushered in a generation of Democratic-Republican leadership and the decline of the Federalist Party.

The tie

Under the original Constitution, electors cast two undifferentiated votes for president. Every Democratic-Republican elector voted for both Jefferson and his intended running mate, Aaron Burr, producing a 73–73 tie between them and throwing the election to the lame-duck, Federalist-controlled House of Representatives. Burr refused to step aside, and the House deadlocked through dozens of ballots in February 1801. On the 36th ballot, after Federalist James Bayard of Delaware and others abstained, Jefferson was elected president and Burr became vice president.

Causes and issues

The campaign was among the most bitter in American history. The Democratic-Republicans attacked the Alien and Sedition Acts, the direct tax on houses and land, and Adams's military buildup during the Quasi-War with France. Federalists portrayed Jefferson as a dangerous radical and atheist. Adams's peace settlement with France, which ended the Quasi-War, split his party; the faction led by Alexander Hamilton worked against his re-election. Hamilton's (reluctant) support for Jefferson over Burr in the House crisis proved decisive.

Consequences

The crisis exposed the flaw in the electoral system and led directly to the Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, which required electors to cast distinct votes for president and vice president. The election also established the precedent of a peaceful transfer of power between rival parties in the United States.
