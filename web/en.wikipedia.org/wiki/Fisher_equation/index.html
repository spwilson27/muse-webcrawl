Fisher equation

In economics, the Fisher equation shows the relationship between the nominal interest rate, the real interest rate, and the inflation rate. Named after Irving Fisher, it states that the nominal interest rate equals the sum of the real interest rate and the (expected) inflation rate:


Irving Fisher, who developed the theory of interest and the distinction between nominal and real rates.
i ≈ r + πe

The exact relation, when rates are expressed as decimals, is:

1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + π)  ⇒  i = r + π + rπ,

where i is the nominal interest rate, r the real interest rate, and π the inflation rate. When inflation is low, the cross-term rπ is negligible and the approximation i ≈ r + πe is close; at high inflation the exact formula matters.

Derivation and interpretation

A lender who lends one unit of money today receives (1 + i) units next year. If prices rise at rate π, each of those units buys 1/(1 + π) as much, so the real gross return is (1 + i)/(1 + π) = 1 + r. Because the return must be contracted before inflation is known, markets use expected inflation πe, giving an ex ante real rate; realised (ex post) real rates differ when inflation surprises.

Uses

  • Real returns: converting nominal yields (e.g. on bonds) into inflation-adjusted returns.
  • Break-even inflation: the gap between yields on nominal government bonds and inflation-indexed bonds (such as TIPS) is read as the market's expected inflation via the Fisher relation.
  • The Fisher effect: the hypothesis that, in the long run, nominal interest rates move one-for-one with expected inflation, leaving the real rate determined by real factors.
  • Monetary policy: central banks reason in terms of real rates — e.g. a Taylor rule translates inflation gaps into nominal rate settings.

History

Fisher developed the distinction in Appreciation and Interest (1896) and fully in The Theory of Interest (1930). The "Fisher effect" became a cornerstone of monetarist and later mainstream macroeconomics; it should not be confused with Fisher's separate equation of exchange, MV = PQ.
