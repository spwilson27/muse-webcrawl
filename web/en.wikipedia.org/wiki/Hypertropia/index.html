Hypertropia

Hypertropia is a condition of misalignment of the eyes (strabismus), in which the visual axis of one eye is higher than the fellow fixating eye. The downward deviation of the other eye is called hypotropia; a hypertropia of one eye is therefore equivalent to a hypotropia of the other.

Causes

Hypertropia most commonly results from weakness or paralysis of the muscles that depress the eye — the superior oblique (in fourth cranial nerve palsy, the most frequent cause) and the inferior rectus — or from restriction of the elevators. Other causes include superior oblique palsy acquired from head trauma, skew deviation from brainstem lesions, dissociated vertical deviation, Brown syndrome, thyroid eye disease and orbital fractures.

Signs and symptoms

Depending on severity and age of onset, hypertropia can cause double vision (diplopia), abnormal head posture — typically a head tilt away from the affected side in superior oblique palsy — suppression and, in children, amblyopia of the deviating eye. The Parks–Bielschowsky three-step test is used to identify which muscle is paretic.

Treatment

Management includes correcting refractive errors, prism glasses to align the images, treatment of underlying causes, and strabismus surgery on the extraocular muscles when the deviation is stable and symptomatic.
