Kroncong

Kroncong (pronounced [krɔɲtʃɔŋ]; also spelled keroncong) is the name of a ukulele-like instrument and an Indonesian musical style that originated with Portuguese-influenced communities in the archipelago. The music is characterised by a strummed string ensemble, an interlocking rhythmic pattern, and sentimental sung melodies.


A kroncong ensemble. The small guitar-like instrument gives the genre its name.

History

Kroncong developed in the 16th and 17th centuries from the music of Portuguese sailors, soldiers and enslaved people brought to Batavia (Jakarta), notably in the Portuguese-speaking community of Tugu. Its name is said to imitate the "chrong-chrong" sound of the strummed instrument. By the early 20th century kroncong had spread across the Dutch East Indies and become popular entertainment music, recorded extensively on 78 rpm discs.

Instrumentation and style

A modern kroncong ensemble typically includes two small guitars (the cak, steel-strung, and the cuk, nylon-strung), flute or violin, cello played pizzicato, double bass and guitar, with a singer. The classic kroncong asli form uses a 28-bar structure. During the Japanese occupation and the Indonesian revolution, songs such as "Bengawan Solo" (by Gesang) became enduring standards, and kroncong acquired associations with national sentiment.

Modern developments

Later styles include langgam jawa, which combines kroncong with Javanese gamelan scales, and pop kroncong fusions. Solo in Central Java is regarded as the heartland of the genre, which remains celebrated despite declining mainstream popularity.
