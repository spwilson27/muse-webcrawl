Videotape is a magnetic tape recording medium used for storing video and usually sound in addition. Descended from magnetic audiotape, videotape stores information along the length of a thin, magnetizable coating on a strip of plastic film, wound on reels or enclosed in cassettes. From the 1950s to the 2000s it was the dominant medium for television production and distribution and for home video, before being displaced by optical discs, hard drives, and solid-state storage.

History

Early experiments in recording television signals on magnetic tape took place in the early 1950s, but the tape speeds required were impractical until engineers adopted helical scan and transverse scanning, in which rotating heads sweep rapidly across slowly moving tape. Ampex's VRX-1000 of 1956, using 2-inch Quadruplex tape, was the first practical and commercially successful videotape recorder, revolutionizing broadcasting by freeing television from live-only transmission and kinescope film copies. Charles Ginsburg's team at Ampex led the development. Tape widths shrank over time — 1-inch, then 3/4-inch U-matic (1971) — and cassette systems brought recording to consumers.

Technology

Video signals have far greater bandwidth than audio, so videotape systems record them using rotating head drums (helical scan for most formats) and techniques such as frequency modulation of the luminance signal. Audio and control tracks run longitudinally along the tape edge. Tape consists of a polyester base with a binder holding magnetic particles; deterioration of the binder ("sticky-shed syndrome"), demagnetization, and physical damage threaten archival tapes, driving digitization efforts.

Decline

Manufacture of VCRs effectively ended in 2016, and broadcasters moved to file-based workflows. Videotape survives mainly in archives, where preserving its large surviving record of late-20th-century television is a pressing conservation challenge.
