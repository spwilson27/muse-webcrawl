The Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 (Pub. L. 63–212, 38 Stat. 730) is a part of United States antitrust law, enacted to clarify and strengthen the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890. Whereas the Sherman Act broadly prohibited conspiracies in restraint of trade and monopolization, the Clayton Act enumerated specific prohibited practices — price discrimination, exclusive dealing and tying arrangements, anticompetitive mergers, and interlocking directorates — where their effect "may be substantially to lessen competition" or tend to create a monopoly. It also exempted labor unions and agricultural organizations from antitrust liability and limited the use of injunctions in labor disputes.

Provisions

  • Section 2 — prohibits price discrimination between different purchasers where it may harm competition (later amended by the Robinson–Patman Act).
  • Section 3 — prohibits tying and exclusive dealing contracts that lessen competition.
  • Section 7 — prohibits mergers and acquisitions whose effect may substantially lessen competition (strengthened by the Celler–Kefauver Act of 1950 to cover asset acquisitions).
  • Section 8 — prohibits interlocking directorates among competing corporations.
  • Section 4 — gives private parties injured by violations the right to sue for treble (triple) damages.
  • Section 6 — declares that "the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce," exempting unions from being treated as illegal combinations.

Background and enforcement

The Act was a centerpiece of President Woodrow Wilson's "New Freedom" program and passed alongside the Federal Trade Commission Act, which created the FTC to police unfair methods of competition. The Clayton Act is enforced by the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice's Antitrust Division, as well as by private plaintiffs.

Legacy

Section 7 became the principal statutory basis for modern merger review in the United States. The labor provisions fell short of union hopes — Samuel Gompers called the Act labor's "Magna Carta," but courts continued to issue labor injunctions until the Norris–La Guardia Act of 1932. The Clayton Act remains, with the Sherman Act, a foundation of U.S. competition law.
