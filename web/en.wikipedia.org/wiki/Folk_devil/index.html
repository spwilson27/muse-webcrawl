Folk devil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A folk devil is a person or group of people portrayed in the media and popular culture as deviant outsiders who are blamed for crimes or other social problems. The term is closely associated with the theory of moral panics, in which disproportionate public concern about a perceived threat is whipped up by moral entrepreneurs, politicians and the press.

Origin of the term

The concept was developed by the sociologist Stanley Cohen in his 1972 book Folk Devils and Moral Panics, a study of the public reaction to disturbances between the Mods and Rockers youth subcultures in English seaside towns in the 1960s. Cohen argued that societies appear to be subject, every now and then, to periods of moral panic, during which a condition, episode, person or group emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests. Its nature is presented in a stylised and stereotypical fashion by the mass media; the moral barricades are manned by editors, bishops, politicians and other right-thinking people.

Characteristics

Folk devils are typically stripped of all favourable characteristics and presented as wholly evil. They are held responsible for problems far beyond anything they have actually done, and their supposed menace serves to unite a community, justify new laws or increased policing, and sell newspapers. Historical and modern examples commonly cited include accused witches in early modern Europe, immigrants, drug users, juvenile delinquents, football hooligans, video-game players, and various youth subcultures such as punks and goths.

Process

Cohen outlined a sequence of stages: a warning sign or event; inventory and exaggeration by the media; sensitisation of the public; an increased police and court response; and a resulting deviancy amplification spiral, in which heavy-handed control measures themselves produce more of the behaviour they target. Once the panic subsides, the folk devil may be forgotten or, occasionally, rehabilitated.

Criticism

Later sociologists have debated how far moral panic theory implies that public concerns are always baseless; some argue the framework can be used to dismiss genuine harms. Others have extended the concept to social media, where folk devils can be created and destroyed within days.
