Sine

In mathematics, the sine is a trigonometric function of an angle. The sine of an acute angle in a right triangle is defined as the ratio of the length of the side opposite the angle to the length of the hypotenuse. More generally, the sine function is defined for all real numbers via the unit circle and extended to complex numbers, making it one of the fundamental functions of analysis. It is usually abbreviated sin.

Definitions

For an angle θ measured in radians, sin θ is the y-coordinate of the point where the terminal side of the angle meets the unit circle. The function is periodic with period 2π, odd (sin(−θ) = −sin θ), and ranges between −1 and 1. Its graph is a smooth wave crossing the origin, reaching a maximum at π/2 and a minimum at 3π/2. Familiar values include sin 0 = 0, sin(π/6) = 1/2, sin(π/4) = √2/2, sin(π/3) = √3/2 and sin(π/2) = 1.

The sine can also be defined by the power series sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − …, convergent for all real (and complex) x. It is the imaginary part of the complex exponential: eix = cos x + i sin x (Euler's formula), and together with the cosine it satisfies the Pythagorean identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. The derivative of sin x is cos x, and its integral is −cos x plus a constant.

History

The function descends from the chord tables of Greek astronomy and the half-chord tables of Indian mathematics (the jyā), transmitted through Arabic astronomy — whence the Latin sinus ("bay" or "fold"), arising from a translation chain through the Sanskrit jīva and Arabic jayb. Sine tables were essential to navigation, surveying and astronomy for centuries before electronic computation.

Applications

Sines describe simple harmonic motion and waves: sound, light, alternating current and tides are all modelled sinusoidally, and Fourier analysis decomposes arbitrary periodic signals into sums of sines and cosines. The law of sines relates the sides and angles of any triangle and is a basic tool of trigonometry, geodesy and engineering.
