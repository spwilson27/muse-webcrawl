Normal space

[Image: Separation of disjoint closed sets by neighbourhoods]

In topology and related branches of mathematics, a normal space is a topological space X that satisfies axiom T4: given any two disjoint closed sets E and F of X, there exist neighbourhoods U of E and V of F that are also disjoint. Such a space is called perfectly normal if it additionally is a Gδ space in the appropriate sense — more precisely, if every closed set is a countable intersection of open sets.

Definitions

A normal space that also satisfies the T1 axiom (points are closed) is a T4 space. Normal spaces are important because of Urysohn's lemma, which states that two disjoint closed sets in a normal space can be separated by a continuous function taking values in [0, 1], and the Tietze extension theorem, which extends continuous real-valued functions from closed subsets to the whole space.

Examples

Every metric space is perfectly normal, as are all compact Hausdorff spaces and all paracompact Hausdorff spaces. The Sorgenfrey line is an example of a normal space that is not metrizable, while the Sorgenfrey plane shows that products of normal spaces need not be normal. The long line and CW complexes provide further examples.

Properties

Closed subspaces of normal spaces are normal, but arbitrary subspaces need not be. A space is normal if and only if every open cover by two sets admits a shrinking, equivalently if disjoint closed sets can be separated by a partition of unity-style construction.
