David I (c. 1084 – 24 May 1153) was a 12th-century ruler of Scotland, reigning from 1124 until his death in 1153. The youngest son of Malcolm III and Saint Margaret, he spent much of his early life in England at the court of Henry I, an experience that profoundly shaped his later kingship. He is widely regarded as one of Scotland's most significant monarchs, credited with transforming the kingdom through administrative, legal, religious and economic reforms often collectively termed the Davidian Revolution.

Early life

David was born around 1084, the sixth and youngest son of Malcolm III. After his parents' deaths in 1093, Scotland descended into succession disputes, and David was taken to England, where he was raised at the Anglo-Norman court. In 1113 he married Maud, Countess of Huntingdon, widow of Simon de Senlis, acquiring through her the vast Honour of Huntingdon in England. Under his brother Alexander I, David ruled southern Scotland as Prince of the Cumbrians.

Reign

On Alexander's death in 1124, David succeeded to the throne, though his accession was contested by Máel Coluim mac Alaxandair, a nephew. With English military support David secured his kingship. During the English civil war known as the Anarchy, David intervened in support of his niece, the Empress Matilda, invading northern England; his army was defeated by the English at the Battle of the Standard in 1138, though he retained control of much of Northumbria and Cumberland in the ensuing settlements.

Davidian Revolution

David's reign saw the introduction of feudal landholding on the Anglo-Norman model, the encouragement of Norman and Flemish settlers, the foundation of royal burghs such as Edinburgh, Perth, Stirling and Inverness, and the issue of Scotland's first native coinage. He reformed the Scottish church, founding or refounding monasteries including Holyrood, Kelso, Jedburgh and Melrose abbeys, and established new dioceses, notably Glasgow. His administrative innovations, including sheriffs and justiciars, laid foundations for later Scottish government.

Death and legacy

David died at Carlisle on 24 May 1153 and was succeeded by his grandson Malcolm IV. He was later recognised as a saint by popular acclaim, though never formally canonised. Historians have praised his statecraft while noting that his reforms also began the gradual feudalisation and anglicisation of Scottish society.
