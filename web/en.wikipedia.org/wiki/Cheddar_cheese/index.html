Cheddar cheese

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A wedge of orange cheddar cheese on a wooden board]

Cheddar cheese is a relatively hard, off-white (sometimes orange) cheese originating in the English village of Cheddar in Somerset. It is the most popular cheese in the United Kingdom and one of the most produced and eaten cheeses in the world, made on every continent. Cheddar ranges from mild and creamy when young to sharp, crumbly and crystalline when matured for a year or more ("vintage" or "extra mature").

Production and cheddaring

Cheddar is named for its distinctive making process, "cheddaring": after the curd is cut, heated and drained, it is milled into blocks which are stacked and turned so they knit together and expel more whey, developing the dense, smooth texture. The curd is then salted, pressed into moulds and aged. Annatto, a natural colouring from the achiote tree, gives many cheddars their orange hue, a tradition dating to when colour suggested rich, high-fat milk.

History

Cheese has been made around Cheddar — famous also for its gorge and caves, where the cool, humid air suited ageing — since at least the 12th century. Joseph Harding, the "father of Cheddar cheese", systematised and taught its manufacture in the 19th century, spreading the method to Scotland, North America and Australasia. Factory production in the United States and elsewhere turned cheddar into a global commodity cheese.

Varieties

Protected West Country Farmhouse Cheddar is made by traditional methods on Somerset farms. Elsewhere, cheddar styles include clothbound farmhouse cheddars, mass-market blocks, smoked and flavoured versions, and coloured variants such as Red Leicester's cousin cheeses. Cheddar melts well and anchors dishes from macaroni and cheese to the cheese toastie and ploughman's lunch.
