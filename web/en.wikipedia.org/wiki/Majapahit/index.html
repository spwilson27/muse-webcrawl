Majapahit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The split gate (candi bentar) of Wringin Lawang at Trowulan, Majapahit's capital]

Majapahit was a Javanese Hindu-Buddhist thalassocratic empire centred in East Java (1293 – c. 1527), the last and greatest of the Indonesian Hindu kingdoms. Founded by Raden Wijaya, who turned a Mongol invasion force against his enemies and then against the Mongols themselves, it reached its height under Queen Tribhuwana and her prime minister Gajah Mada (r. 1331–1364) — who swore the Palapa Oath not to taste spices until the whole archipelago was united — and King Hayam Wuruk. From its capital near modern Trowulan, Majapahit's influence (by conquest, tribute and trade) extended across much of present Indonesia and Malaysia; its memory remains central to Indonesian national identity.

Rise and golden age

After the Mongol expedition of 1293 withdrew, Wijaya (as Kertarajasa) founded Majapahit amid the ruins of Singhasari. Gajah Mada's campaigns brought Bali (1343), Sunda (the tragedy of Bubat, 1357, where a royal wedding turned to slaughter), and the ports of Sumatra, Borneo and the eastern islands into tributary orbit. The court poem Nagarakertagama (1365) describes a splendid capital of brick walls, temples and markets; Chinese, Arab and European sources confirm Java's rice, spices and shipping wealth.

Decline

A succession war (the Paregreg civil war, 1404–1406) weakened the empire as Islam spread through the coastal trading states — Malacca, Demak and the north-coast ports — whose merchant wealth escaped Majapahit's control. The capital fell to Demak's forces, traditionally in 1478 (a Javanese chronogram) or by the 1520s; Hindu-Buddhist court culture survived in Bali, where it endures today. The Garuda, the flag colours red and white, and the national motto Bhinneka Tunggal Ika ('Unity in Diversity', from a Majapahit poem) all descend from Majapahit.
