Kamehameha II

Kamehameha II, born Liholiho (c. 1797 – 14 July 1824), was the second king of the Kingdom of Hawaii, reigning from 1819 until his death in 1824. The eldest son of Kamehameha I, he is remembered above all for abolishing the traditional Hawaiian religion and the kapu system in 1819, shortly before the arrival of the first American Protestant missionaries.

Reign

Liholiho succeeded his father in May 1819. Under the influence of his father's widow Kaʻahumanu — who became kuhina nui (co-ruler) — and his mother Keōpūolani, the highest-ranking aliʻi of her time, he publicly broke the ʻai kapu (the eating taboos separating men and women and regulating food) at a feast in November 1819, ordering the heiau (temples) destroyed and the images burned. The traditional priesthood rebelled under his cousin Kekuaokalani, but royal forces defeated them at the Battle of Kuamoʻo in December 1819.

The dismantling of the old religion, which left a spiritual vacuum filled soon after by Christianity, is known in Hawaiian history as the ʻAi Noa ("free eating").

Death in London

In 1823 Kamehameha II and his favourite wife, Queen Kamāmalu, sailed to London aboard the whaler L'Aigle to negotiate with the British Crown and seek protection for his kingdom. While awaiting an audience with George IV, both contracted measles, to which Hawaiians had no immunity. Kamāmalu died on 8 July 1824 and the king followed on 14 July. Their bodies were returned to Hawaii by HMS Blonde under Captain Lord Byron, arriving in 1825 to national mourning; they were eventually interred at the Royal Mausoleum at Mauna ʻAla.

Legacy

Though sometimes portrayed as weak-willed, Kamehameha II presided over the most consequential religious transformation in Hawaiian history. His younger brother succeeded him as Kamehameha III.
