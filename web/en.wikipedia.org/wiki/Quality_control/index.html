Quality control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quality control (QC) is a process by which entities review the quality of all factors involved in production. It emphasises testing of products to uncover defects and reporting the results to management, which decides whether to allow or deny release. QC is contrasted with quality assurance, which focuses on improving and stabilising the processes themselves so defects do not occur.

Approaches

Common QC methods include inspection (100% inspection of every unit, or sampling), statistical process control using control charts pioneered by Walter Shewhart, acceptance sampling plans of the kind formalised by Dodge and Romig, and product testing to failure. Modern frameworks such as Six Sigma, total quality management and the ISO 9000 family of standards integrate QC into wider quality management systems.

History

Formal inspection grew with mass production in the early 20th century. Statistical methods were developed at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the 1920s–30s, applied widely during the Second World War, and carried to Japan after the war by W. Edwards Deming and Joseph Juran, where they became central to the Japanese manufacturing revival.
