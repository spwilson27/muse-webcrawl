North Atlantic oscillation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) is a weather phenomenon across the North Atlantic Ocean of fluctuations in the difference of atmospheric pressure at sea level between the Icelandic Low and the Azores High. Through fluctuations in the strength of these pressure centers, it controls the strength and direction of westerly winds and storm tracks across the North Atlantic, strongly influencing the climate of Europe and eastern North America.

Phases

In its positive phase, a stronger-than-average Azores High and deeper Icelandic Low increase the pressure gradient, strengthening westerlies. This brings mild, wet, stormy winters to northern Europe and the eastern United States, while southern Europe and the Mediterranean tend to be dry. In the negative phase, weakened pressure centers reduce the gradient; cold air outbreaks plunge into eastern North America and Europe, and storm tracks shift south into the Mediterranean.

Index

The NAO index is commonly defined as the normalized difference in sea-level pressure between stations in Iceland (e.g., Reykjavík or Stykkishólmur) and the Azores, Lisbon, or Gibraltar. The pattern is most prominent in winter. Sir Gilbert Walker first described the oscillation in the 1920s.

Effects

NAO variations affect winter temperatures, precipitation, sea ice in the Labrador and Greenland seas, ocean circulation, and even ecosystems and fisheries. Much of Europe's winter climate variability, including the cold winters associated with persistent negative NAO such as 2009–10, is attributable to the oscillation.
