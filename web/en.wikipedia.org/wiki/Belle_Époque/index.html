Belle Époque

The Belle Époque (French for "Beautiful Epoch") is the period of French and European history, usually dated from the end of the Franco-Prussian War in 1871 to the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. It was characterised by optimism, relative peace, regional stability, economic prosperity and technological, scientific and cultural innovation. In France especially, the arts flourished — Impressionism and Post-Impressionism, Art Nouveau, the birth of cinema and cabaret — and Paris was the cultural capital of the world.

Overview

The name was applied retrospectively, after the horrors of the Great War, to a period that came to be remembered as a golden age. Paris hosted the great Expositions Universelles of 1889 (for which the Eiffel Tower was built) and 1900, showcasing electricity, film and the Métro, which opened in 1900. New department stores, boulevards lined with cafés, and venues such as the Moulin Rouge (opened 1889) defined urban pleasure. Painters from Monet and Renoir to Picasso and Matisse, composers such as Debussy and Ravel, and writers including Proust and Zola shaped modern art and literature.

Technology and society

The era saw the spread of electric light, the telephone, the automobile, the aeroplane and mass-circulation newspapers. Yet prosperity was uneven: industrial workers lived in poverty, the Dreyfus affair exposed deep fractures in French society, and European empires competed violently for colonies abroad. The period's cosmopolitan peace rested on armed rivalries that exploded in 1914.
