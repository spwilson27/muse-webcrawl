Orthopedic cast

An orthopedic cast (American spelling) or orthopaedic cast, is a shell, frequently made from plaster or fiberglass, that encases a limb (or, in some cases, large portions of the body) to stabilize and hold anatomical structures — most often a broken bone (or bones), in place until healing is confirmed. It is similar in function to a splint.

Materials

Plaster

Plaster bandages consist of a cotton bandage impregnated with plaster of Paris (calcined gypsum), which hardens after it has been made wet. Plaster of Paris was popularised for this purpose by the Dutch army surgeon Antonius Mathijsen in 1851. Plaster casts are inexpensive, easy to mould, and radiolucent enough for X-ray follow-up, but they are heavy, not waterproof, and take up to 48 hours to reach full strength.

Fiberglass

Fiberglass casting tape, introduced in the 1970s, consists of a knitted fiberglass fabric impregnated with a polyurethane resin that hardens on contact with water. Fiberglass casts are lighter, stronger, quicker to set, and more durable than plaster, and they come in many colours; they are, however, more expensive and less mouldable for complex reductions.

Application

A stockinette and padding are first applied over the skin to protect it and accommodate swelling. The casting material is then rolled on in overlapping layers while the limb is held in the correct position. Common configurations include the short arm cast, long arm cast, short leg cast, long leg cast, cylinder cast, and body or spica casts for hip and trunk injuries.

Complications

Complications of casting include pressure sores, skin irritation, joint stiffness, muscle atrophy, and — most seriously — compartment syndrome, in which swelling within a tight cast raises tissue pressure enough to compromise circulation. Casts that become too tight, cause increasing pain, numbness or discolouration require urgent medical assessment; a cast may be split, windowed, or removed entirely with an oscillating cast saw, which cuts rigid material while merely vibrating against skin.

History

Immobilisation of fractures with rigid dressings dates to ancient Egypt and the Arab physicians of the medieval period, who used lime and egg white. The modern plaster bandage spread through European military medicine in the 19th century, notably through the work of Mathijsen and the Russian surgeon Nikolai Pirogov, who used it extensively in the Crimean War.
