Attila

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A 19th-century painting of Attila's court receiving Roman envoys]

Attila (c. 406 – 453), frequently called Attila the Hun, was ruler of the Huns from 434 until his death in 453 — co-ruler with his brother Bleda until 445, then sole king of an empire of tribute stretching from the Rhine to Central Asia. From his wooden capital on the Hungarian plain he extorted the Eastern Roman Empire with annual gold payments and punitive invasions of the Balkans, then in 451 invaded Gaul and was checked at the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains by the coalition of Aëtius and the Visigoths; in 452 he invaded Italy, sacked Aquileia, Milan and Padua, and withdrew after meeting Pope Leo I's embassy (and facing famine and disease), dying the next year of a nosebleed on his wedding night. In Christian memory he became the Flagellum Dei, the Scourge of God; in Hungarian and Turkish tradition, a founding hero.

The Hunnic system

Attila's power ran on gold flowing the wrong way: Roman tribute (2,100 pounds of gold yearly after 447) redistributed to a multi-ethnic aristocracy of Huns, Goths, Alans, Gepids and Roman secretaries — the envoy Priscus' history (our best source, surviving in fragments) describes his court in ethnographic detail: wooden palaces, banquet protocol, the king's wooden cup beside his guests' gold. His demands — fugitives returned, markets controlled, a border strip emptied — kept Constantinople paying to protect its richer provinces while the Huns picked the Balkans bare (Naissus, Serdica, Philippopolis sacked; Constantinople's walls, rebuilt after the 447 earthquake, never tested).

Gaul, Italy, death

The 451 invasion — prompted by Valentinian III's sister Honoria's ring and plea (the tradition's romantic strand) and by Frankish succession politics — ended at the Catalaunian Plains near Châlons, antiquity's archetypal bloodbath, whose casualties the sources inflate to hundreds of thousands; Attila withdrew in order, unbroken but checked. Italy in 452 fared worse and recovered stranger: Leo's meeting at the Mincio, famine, disease and Eastern Roman pressure turned him home. His death from a drunken haemorrhage at his wedding feast — the blood, Priscus says, choking him as he slept — dissolved the empire within a year (his sons' defeat at the Nedao, 454): the Huns' unity had been one man's person.
