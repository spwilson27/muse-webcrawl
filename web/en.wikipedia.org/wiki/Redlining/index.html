Redlining

Redlining is a discriminatory practice in which services — most notably mortgages, bank loans, and insurance — are denied to residents of particular areas, or offered on worse terms, based on the racial or ethnic composition of those areas rather than the creditworthiness of individuals. The term derives from the colour-coded maps produced in the United States in the 1930s, on which neighbourhoods deemed "hazardous" for lending were outlined in red.

History

During the Great Depression, the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) created "residential security maps" for over 200 American cities, grading neighbourhoods from A ("best") to D ("hazardous"). The grades explicitly incorporated race: the presence of Black, immigrant, or Jewish residents lowered a neighbourhood's rating regardless of its housing quality. Lenders and the Federal Housing Administration adopted similar practices, systematically channelling mortgage credit toward white suburban developments and away from minority and racially mixed urban neighbourhoods. Private covenants, steering by estate agents, and blockbusting reinforced the pattern.

Effects

Redlining is widely credited by historians and economists with deepening residential segregation and the racial wealth gap: while white families built equity through subsidised home ownership, Black families in redlined districts were largely excluded from appreciating housing markets and were exposed to predatory alternatives such as contract buying. Studies continue to find correlations between historically redlined grades and present-day disparities in income, health, tree canopy, and exposure to heat and pollution.

Legislation

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 prohibited discrimination in housing, and the Community Reinvestment Act of 1977 required banks to meet the credit needs of all communities they serve, including formerly redlined areas. Related modern concepts include "reverse redlining" — the targeting of minority neighbourhoods for predatory, high-cost loans — which was litigated extensively after the subprime mortgage crisis of 2007–2008.
