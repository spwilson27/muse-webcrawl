Kaleb of Axum

Kaleb (Ge'ez: ካሌብ, Kālēb), also known as Ella Asbeha and in Greek sources as Hellesthaeus, was King of Axum (in modern-day Ethiopia and Eritrea) who reigned in the early 6th century AD, roughly from 514 to 543. He is best remembered for his military intervention in Himyar (South Arabia, modern Yemen) on behalf of persecuted Christians, and he is venerated as a saint in the Ethiopian Orthodox and Roman Catholic traditions.

Reign

Kaleb inherited a kingdom that controlled trade between the Roman world and the Indian Ocean through the Red Sea port of Adulis. His inscriptions and coinage — among the first African coins to bear the Christian cross — reflect a confident, expansionist monarchy closely allied with the Byzantine Empire.

Invasion of Himyar

Around 518–523 the Jewish king of Himyar, Dhu Nuwas (Yūsuf Asʾar Yathʾar), persecuted the Christian population of Najran. In response, with Byzantine encouragement and shipping support, Kaleb led an expedition across the Red Sea, defeated and killed Dhu Nuwas, and installed a Christian client king, Sumyafa Ashwa (Esimiphaios). A second expedition followed around 525 after the viceroy Abraha seized power. Axumite garrisons remained in Yemen for decades, although Abraha eventually ruled effectively independently.

Later life and veneration

Ethiopian tradition holds that Kaleb abdicated late in life and retired to a monastery, sending his crown to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. His tomb, and that attributed to his son Gebre Meskel, stand near Axum. His feast day is observed on 24 October in the Catholic Church (as Saint Elesbaan) and in late May in the Ethiopian tradition.
