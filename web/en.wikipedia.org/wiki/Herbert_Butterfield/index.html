Sir Herbert Butterfield (7 October 1900 – 20 July 1979) was a British historian and philosopher of history, who was Regius Professor of Modern History and Vice-Chancellor of the University of Cambridge. He is remembered chiefly for a short volume published in 1931, The Whig Interpretation of History, in which he coined the phrase "Whig interpretation of history" for the tendency of historians to write history as the story of progress toward the present, praising the "winners" who supposedly advanced it and condemning those who stood in its way. The term has passed into general use far beyond the context of English constitutional history for which it was invented.

Early life and education

Butterfield was born in Oxenhope in the West Riding of Yorkshire, the son of a textile mill worker, and was educated at the Trade and Grammar School in Keighley before winning a scholarship to Peterhouse, Cambridge, where he read history and took his degree in 1922. A devout Methodist lay preacher throughout his life, he remained at Peterhouse as a fellow for the rest of his career, becoming its Master in 1955. His early historical works included The Historical Novel (1924) and The Peace Tactics of Napoleon, 1806–1808 (1929).

The Whig interpretation

In The Whig Interpretation of History (1931), Butterfield argued against the practice — named for historians of the Whig tradition who celebrated the triumph of Protestantism, Parliament, and liberty in English history — of studying the past "with one eye upon the present", dividing historical actors into the friends and enemies of progress. Such history, he held, abridges the complexity of the past, produces moralising melodrama instead of analysis, and misunderstands people who did not share modern assumptions. The remedy he proposed was to study the past "for its own sake", recovering the strangeness and unintended outcomes of human action. Later historians have criticised Butterfield's own practice as not always living up to the ideal, but the book remains one of the most influential methodological essays ever written by an English historian.

Later work

Butterfield's The Origins of Modern Science, 1300–1800 (1949), based on lectures delivered in 1948, argued that the Scientific Revolution "outshines everything since the rise of Christianity" in importance, and it helped establish the academic history of science in Britain; he was also a founding editor of the British Journal for the History of Science. As a Christian thinker he wrote Christianity and History (1949), and in international relations theory he is associated, with Martin Wight and others, with the so-called English School and with the concept of the "international society". During the Cold War he advocated a historically minded diplomacy and helped found the British Committee on the Theory of International Politics.

Butterfield was Regius Professor of Modern History at Cambridge from 1963 to 1968, served as Vice-Chancellor in 1959–1961, and was knighted in 1968. He died at Sawston, Cambridgeshire, in 1979. Critics have sometimes charged his later historical writing — for example on George III — with excessive sympathy toward its subjects, a charge he would have regarded as preferable to the Whig vice he had named.
