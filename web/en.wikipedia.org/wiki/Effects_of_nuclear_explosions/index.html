Effects of nuclear explosions

The energy released by a nuclear weapon detonated in the atmosphere is divided among several destructive mechanisms: roughly 50 percent goes into the blast wave, about 35 percent into thermal radiation, about 5 percent into initial ionising radiation, and the remainder into residual radiation, principally fallout. The precise effects depend on the yield of the weapon, the height of burst, the weather, and the terrain.

A nuclear fireball and rising mushroom cloud.

Blast

Most damage to buildings and infrastructure comes from the blast wave: a wall of compressed air that radiates outward faster than the speed of sound, followed by high winds. Overpressure of about 5 psi is sufficient to destroy most residential buildings, while reinforced concrete structures require much higher pressures. People can also be injured by flying debris and by being thrown by the blast winds.

Thermal radiation

The fireball emits intense visible, infrared and ultraviolet radiation. The thermal pulse can ignite flammable materials, cause fires over a wide area — potentially merging into a firestorm in cities — and inflict severe flash burns on exposed skin at considerable distances. Its range grows strongly with yield and in clear weather can exceed the blast-damage radius.

Ionising radiation

Initial radiation, mostly gamma rays and neutrons emitted within the first minute, is lethal at short ranges but is significant mainly for smaller weapons. Residual radiation includes fallout: radioactive debris drawn up into the mushroom cloud that later settles to the ground, contaminating large downwind areas. Exposure can cause acute radiation sickness and long-term increases in cancer risk.

Electromagnetic pulse

A high-altitude detonation can generate an electromagnetic pulse capable of damaging electrical and electronic equipment over a continental-scale area, an effect demonstrated by the Starfish Prime test of 1962.

Other effects

Surface bursts produce radioactive craters and intense local fallout. Large numbers of simultaneous explosions have been hypothesised to cause a "nuclear winter" through soot injected into the stratosphere. Atmospheric tests also produced artificial radiation belts in near-Earth space.
