Geostrategy

Geostrategy is the geographic direction of a state's foreign policy; more precisely, it is the study of how geographic factors — location, terrain, resources, climate, and access to seas and chokepoints — shape political and military strategy. It is a subfield of geopolitics concerned with the practical conduct of statecraft rather than ideological theories of international relations.

Classical theories

Alfred Thayer Mahan's The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890) argued that control of maritime trade routes made great powers. Halford Mackinder's "Heartland" thesis (1904, 1919) held that whoever ruled the interior of Eurasia — the "pivot area" or Heartland, inaccessible to sea power — could command the "World-Island" and thus the world. Nicholas Spykman countered with the "Rimland" theory (1942), emphasising the coastal fringes of Eurasia as the decisive zone, an idea that influenced Cold War containment. Alexander de Seversky stressed air power, and later theorists added nuclear weapons, space, and cyberspace to the geographic calculus.

Modern usage

Today geostrategy informs policy on issues such as control of shipping chokepoints (Suez, Panama, Malacca, Hormuz), competition in the Arctic as sea ice retreats, access to energy and rare-earth resources, and China's Belt and Road Initiative. Critics caution against geographical determinism, noting that technology and economics can overturn supposedly permanent geographic advantages.
