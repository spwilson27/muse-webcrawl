Ignition coil

An ignition coil is an induction coil in an automobile's ignition system that converts the battery's low voltage (typically 12 volts) to the thousands of volts needed to create an electric spark in the spark plugs to ignite the fuel. Some coils have an internal resistor, while others rely on a resistor wire or external resistor to limit current flowing into the coil from the car's 12-volt supply.

Operating principle

The coil consists of two windings of insulated wire around a laminated iron core. The primary winding has relatively few turns of heavy wire; the secondary has many thousands of turns of fine wire. When the primary circuit is suddenly interrupted (by breaker points or an electronic switch), the collapsing magnetic field induces a high voltage in the secondary winding, which is delivered to the spark plug via the distributor or directly in coil-on-plug designs.

Types

Historically a single coil served all cylinders through a distributor. Modern engines typically use coil packs serving paired cylinders (wasted-spark systems) or individual coil-on-plug units mounted directly on each spark plug, allowing more precise electronic control and stronger sparks.

History

The induction-coil ignition system was developed from the work of Nicholas Callan and others in the nineteenth century and was refined by Charles Kettering, whose battery-and-coil system replaced magneto ignition in most cars from the 1910s onward.
