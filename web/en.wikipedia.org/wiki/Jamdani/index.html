Jamdani

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jamdani (Bengali: জামদানি) is a fine muslin textile of Bengal, produced in the region around Dhaka in present-day Bangladesh and in parts of West Bengal, India. It is distinguished by rich floral and geometric motifs woven directly into the fabric on the loom using a discontinuous weft technique, creating patterns that appear to float on the sheer ground cloth. Jamdani is regarded as one of the most labour-intensive forms of hand weaving in the world.

History

The weaving of figured muslins in Bengal is ancient; the fame of Dhaka's muslins reached Roman and later Arab and European markets. The name jamdani is often derived from the Persian jam ("flower") and dani ("vase" or "container"), referring to the floral designs on the cloth. Under Mughal patronage, Jamdani weaving flourished, and the fabric was used for court garments, turbans and scarves. Production declined sharply under British colonial rule as machine-made textiles displaced handloom production, but the craft survived in weaving villages around Narayanganj, particularly in Rupganj and Sonargaon.

Technique

Jamdani is woven on a pit loom using a supplementary weft inserted by hand with small shuttles or bamboo sticks. Unlike embroidery, the motifs are built up thread by thread as the cloth is woven; traditional designs are held in the weaver's memory and transmitted through apprenticeship rather than drawn patterns, though graph-paper designs are now also used. A single fine sari can take from two weeks to nine months to complete, depending on the density of the pattern.

Recognition

In 2013, UNESCO inscribed "the traditional art of Jamdani weaving" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Jamdani from Bangladesh received geographical indication (GI) registration in 2016. The fabric is used principally for saris, as well as scarves, handkerchiefs and furnishing cloth.
