Vellum
A medieval manuscript on vellum

Vellum is a writing material made from specially prepared animal skin, most often calfskin, giving rise to its name from the Old French vélin, "calf". In its strict sense vellum is calfskin only, while parchment is the broader term for prepared skins of other animals such as sheep and goats; in modern usage the terms are often used interchangeably for high-quality prepared skin used for manuscripts, bookbinding and calligraphy.

Preparation

Vellum is made by cleaning the skin, soaking it in a lime solution, and stretching it on a frame (a "hers") where it is scraped with a crescent-shaped knife (a lunarium) and allowed to dry under tension. Unlike leather, vellum is not tanned; the stretching and drying produce a stiff, smooth, pale surface that takes ink well and is remarkably durable. The two sides of the sheet differ: the hair side may show follicle patterns, while the flesh side is whiter and smoother.

Use

Vellum was the prestige writing material of medieval Europe, used for illuminated manuscripts, charters, and the finest copies of texts; a single large Bible could require the skins of several hundred calves. It remained in use for important documents long after paper became cheaper: Acts of the British Parliament were printed on vellum for archival purposes into the 21st century, and vellum is still used for Torah scrolls, diplomas, drumheads, and fine bookbinding. So-called "paper vellum" is a modern synthetic imitation used for drafting.
