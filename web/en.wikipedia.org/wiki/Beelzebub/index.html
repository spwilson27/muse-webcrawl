Beelzebub (also spelled Beelzebuth or Belzebuth) is a name derived from a Philistine deity, Ba'al-zebub, worshipped at Ekron. In the Hebrew Bible, King Ahaziah of Israel sends messengers to consult Ba'al-zebub about his recovery from a fall, provoking the prophet Elijah's rebuke (2 Kings 1). The name is commonly interpreted as "lord of the flies", possibly a mocking distortion of an original title meaning "lord of the (heavenly) dwelling" or "lord prince".

In the New Testament

In the Synoptic Gospels, scribes and Pharisees accuse Jesus of casting out demons "by Beelzebul, the prince of demons" (Matthew 12:24; Mark 3:22; Luke 11:15). Jesus answers with the parable of the divided house: "If Satan casts out Satan, he is divided against himself." In these passages Beelzebub is treated as effectively synonymous with Satan or as his chief subordinate.

Demonology and literature

In later Jewish and Christian demonology, Beelzebub became one of the principal demons of Hell. In the pseudepigraphal Testament of Solomon he is the prince of demons; medieval and early modern grimoires variously rank him among the chief powers of the infernal hierarchy. In John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667), Beelzebub is Satan's second-in-command, the fallen cherub who proposes the corruption of mankind at the infernal council. In William Golding's novel Lord of the Flies (1954), the title translates Beelzebub's name and the "lord of the flies" becomes a symbol of innate human savagery.
