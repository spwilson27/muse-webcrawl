The Cathedral Church of the Holy Trinity, Saint Peter, Saint Paul and Saint Swithun, commonly known as Winchester Cathedral, is a Church of England cathedral in Winchester, Hampshire, England. It is one of the largest cathedrals in England and has the greatest overall length of any Gothic cathedral in Europe. It is the seat of the Bishop of Winchester and the centre of the Diocese of Winchester.

History

A church known as the Old Minster was founded on the site in 642. Saint Swithun, a 9th-century bishop, was buried outside the Old Minster; his shrine made Winchester a major centre of pilgrimage. After the Norman Conquest, Bishop Walkelin began the present cathedral in 1079. The great crossing tower collapsed in 1107—an event contemporaries attributed to the impious burial of William Rufus in the cathedral—and was rebuilt.

The building was repeatedly remodelled in the Gothic styles of the succeeding centuries. Bishop William of Wykeham oversaw the transformation of the nave into the Perpendicular Gothic in the late 14th century. In the early 20th century the cathedral was in danger of collapse because its foundations rested on waterlogged peat; between 1906 and 1912 the diver William Walker worked in flooded trenches under the east end, packing in more than 25,000 bags of concrete and vast quantities of brick by hand, thereby saving the building.

Notable features and burials

The cathedral contains the mortuary chests said to hold the bones of early English kings including Cynegils, Cnut, and William Rufus, and the tombs of bishops such as William of Wykeham and Cardinal Beaufort. Jane Austen, who died in Winchester in 1817, is buried in the north aisle of the nave. The library holds the Winchester Bible, a magnificent 12th-century illuminated manuscript, the largest surviving English medieval Bible. The crypt floods regularly in wet winters and contains Antony Gormley's sculpture Sound II.
