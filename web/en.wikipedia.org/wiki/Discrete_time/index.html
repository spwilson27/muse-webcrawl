Discrete time

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematical dynamics, discrete time and continuous time are two alternative frameworks within which variables that evolve over time are modelled. Discrete time views values of variables as occurring at distinct, separate "points in time", or equivalently as being unchanged throughout each non-zero region of time ("time period") — that is, time is viewed as a discrete variable. Thus a non-time variable jumps from one value to another as time moves from one time period to the next.

By contrast, continuous time views variables as having a particular value for potentially only an infinitesimally short amount of time, so that time is a continuous variable defined over the whole real line.

Usage

Discrete-time models are natural when observations are made at fixed intervals: economic statistics reported quarterly, digital audio sampled at a fixed rate, or simulations advanced in fixed steps. A discrete-time signal is a sequence x[n] indexed by integers, often obtained by sampling a continuous-time signal x(t) at intervals T, giving x[n] = x(nT).

Dynamics in discrete time are described by difference equations (recurrence relations) such as xn+1 = f(xn), whose continuous-time counterparts are differential equations. Tools specific to discrete time include the Z-transform and the discrete Fourier transform. Discrete-time systems are the foundation of digital signal processing and digital control theory.

Discrete-time stochastic processes

In probability and statistics, discrete-time processes — such as Markov chains and autoregressive (AR) time-series models — take values at integer time steps, in contrast to continuous-time processes such as Brownian motion.
