Köroğlu

Köroğlu (literally "son of the blind man"; Azerbaijani: Koroğlu; Turkish: Köroğlu; Turkmen: Görogly) is the hero of a widespread epic cycle of the Turkic peoples of Anatolia, the Caucasus and Central Asia. Part bandit, part minstrel-poet, Köroğlu rebels against unjust rulers and defends the oppressed from his mountain fortress of Çamlıbel, accompanied by his faithful horse Kırat and a band of loyal companions.

The legend

In the best-known version, the hero is born Rövşən (Rushan) Ali, son of a horse groom in the service of a lord (in many versions the Bey of Bolu). When the groom presents a seemingly scrawny foal and honestly predicts its greatness, the enraged lord has him blinded. The son swears vengeance, takes the name Köroğlu — "son of the blind man" — and raises the foal, which becomes the magnificent, nearly magical grey horse Kırat. From Çamlıbel he raids caravans, duels with champions such as Ayvaz, and sings improvised verses accompanied by the long-necked bağlama, defying sultans and beys while sheltering runaways and the poor. Recurring themes include his rivalry and reconciliation with his father, the abduction or winning of his beloved Nigar Hanım, and lamentations, in old age, at the coming of firearms, which he feels end the age of heroes: "the rifle was invented, bravery was ruined".

Tradition and versions

The epic circulated orally among ashik (minstrel) performers from at least the 16th–17th centuries, the period in which a historical Köroğlu — a Jelali rebel leader mentioned in Ottoman documents — appears to have lived. Versions are told in Azerbaijani, Turkish, Turkmen, Uzbek and other traditions, and spread as far as Armenian, Georgian and Kurdish storytelling. In Azerbaijani tradition the dastan is one of the central national epics, set partly around Lake Goycha and the Caucasus.

Legacy

The Azerbaijani composer Uzeyir Hajibeyov based his celebrated opera Koroğlu (1937) on the epic; it remains a staple of the Azerbaijani stage. Streets, a Baku metro station and the Turkmen national epic tradition (Görogly) honour the hero, and the epic's verse duels and songs continue in the repertoire of ashik performers today.
