Heter mekhira

The heter mekhira (Hebrew: היתר מכירה, "permit of sale") is a formal arrangement in Jewish law by which a Jew's leavened products (chametz) are sold to a non-Jew before Passover, so that the Jewish owner does not violate the prohibition on possessing chametz during the holiday. The sale is executed through a rabbi acting as agent, commonly via a written contract or, in modern times, an online form.

Background

The Torah forbids both eating and owning chametz during Passover. While small quantities can be consumed or burned before the festival (biur chametz), merchants and manufacturers holding large stocks faced heavy losses. The sale mechanism, rooted in Talmudic discussion of transactions with non-Jews before Passover, allows the chametz to be legally owned by the non-Jewish buyer for the duration of the holiday and bought back afterwards.

Debate

Some authorities have questioned whether the sale is a genuine transaction or a legal fiction (ha'arama), particularly when it is clear both parties expect the goods to be repurchased. Others defend it as a fully valid sale, noting that the buyer makes a deposit, may in principle take possession, and bears the risk of price changes. The related term is also applied to the heter mekhira for land in Israel during the sabbatical year (Shmita), proposed in 1888–89 and famously supported by Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook, which remains a subject of controversy in Israeli religious life.
