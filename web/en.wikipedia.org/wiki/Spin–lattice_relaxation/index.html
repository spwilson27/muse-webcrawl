Spin–lattice relaxation

Spin–lattice relaxation is the mechanism by which the component of the nuclear (or electron) magnetisation vector along the direction of the external magnetic field, Mz, returns to its equilibrium value after a perturbation. It is characterised by the longitudinal relaxation time T1, and is also called T1 relaxation or longitudinal relaxation.

Mechanism

Relaxation of Mz requires an exchange of energy between the spin system and its surroundings, conventionally called the "lattice" — originally the crystal lattice of a solid, and by extension the whole thermal environment of the sample. The recovery follows an exponential law: after a 180° inverting pulse, Mz(t) = M0(1 − 2e−t/T1). Microscopically, T1 relaxation is driven by fluctuating local magnetic fields at the Larmor frequency, produced chiefly by molecular tumbling in liquids and by lattice vibrations (phonons) in solids. Its efficiency therefore depends strongly on molecular correlation times, field strength, and temperature, following the Bloembergen–Purcell–Pound (BPP) theory.

Applications

Measurement of T1 is fundamental to nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, where it informs on molecular dynamics, and to magnetic resonance imaging, where differences in T1 between tissues provide image contrast; "T1-weighted" MRI scans are a routine diagnostic tool. In electron spin resonance the analogous quantity governs saturation behaviour.
