Jewish bereavement

Bereavement in Judaism is governed by a structured set of rituals and mourning periods intended to honour the dead and gradually return mourners (avelim) to ordinary life. Jewish law (halakha) treats burial promptly, usually within a day, and comfort of the mourners as central obligations.

Stages of mourning

Aninut is the period between death and burial, when the closest relatives are exempt from many commandments to attend to funeral arrangements. After burial begins shiva, seven days of intense mourning at home, during which mourners sit on low stools, cover mirrors, and receive visitors who come to comfort them. Sheloshim, the thirty days after burial, involves a gradual easing of restrictions. For a parent, mourning continues for twelve months (avelut), during which festivities are avoided and the Mourner's Kaddish is recited — traditionally for eleven months.

Customs

Other customs include keriah, the tearing of a garment; washing of the body and watching over it before burial (shemira); the unveiling of the headstone (matzevah); and annual remembrance on the yahrzeit (anniversary of death), when a memorial candle is lit and Kaddish recited. Yizkor memorial services are held on major festivals.
