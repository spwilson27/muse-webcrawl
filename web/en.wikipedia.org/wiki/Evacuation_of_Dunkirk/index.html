The Dunkirk evacuation, codenamed Operation Dynamo and also known as the Miracle of Dunkirk, was the evacuation of Allied soldiers during the Second World War from the beaches and harbour of Dunkirk, in northern France, between 26 May and 4 June 1940. After Nazi Germany invaded the Low Countries and France in May 1940, its armoured spearheads raced to the Channel and cut off the British Expeditionary Force (BEF) and large numbers of French and Belgian troops in a pocket around Dunkirk. The evacuation by sea — carried out by Royal Navy vessels and a hastily assembled fleet of hundreds of civilian "little ships" — rescued 338,226 men, far beyond expectations, though the BEF abandoned nearly all its heavy equipment.

Background

The German offensive of 10 May 1940 bypassed the Maginot Line through the Ardennes, crossed the Meuse at Sedan, and reached the Channel at Abbeville on 20 May, encircling the Allied armies in the north. As the pocket shrank, the BEF commander Lord Gort controversially withdrew toward Dunkirk on 25 May, judging a counter-attack hopeless. The German advance on Dunkirk itself paused for two days — the famous "halt order" of 24 May — partly to conserve armour for the conquest of the rest of France, giving the Allies time to organise a defensive perimeter. The harbour at Dunkirk was heavily bombed, but its long concrete breakwater, the East Mole, proved usable for berthing ships.

The evacuation

Operation Dynamo began on the evening of 26 May under Vice-Admiral Bertram Ramsay in the tunnels beneath Dover Castle. Planners hoped to save perhaps 45,000 men; in the event, favourable weather — unusually calm seas and low cloud that limited the Luftwaffe — and the sacrificial defence of the perimeter by British and French rearguards allowed the lift to grow daily, peaking at over 68,000 men on 31 May. Roughly 850 vessels took part: destroyers, minesweepers, ferries, trawlers, lifeboats, barges and private yachts, many crewed by civilians and naval reservists, ferrying men from the beaches or direct to England. About 68,000 British soldiers were nonetheless lost in the French campaign overall, and some 40,000 French troops covering the final evacuation were taken prisoner.

Aftermath and legacy

All the BEF's tanks, artillery and vehicles were left behind, and over 200 vessels were sunk; yet the rescued army formed the nucleus around which Britain rebuilt its forces, and the nation avoided the catastrophic loss of its trained manpower. Prime Minister Winston Churchill called the deliverance a "miracle" while warning in the same speech, on 4 June 1940, that "wars are not won by evacuations", and delivered his famous vow: "We shall fight on the beaches... we shall never surrender." The "Dunkirk spirit" — defiant improvisation in adversity — remains a touchstone of British national identity, retold in films including Christopher Nolan's Dunkirk (2017).
