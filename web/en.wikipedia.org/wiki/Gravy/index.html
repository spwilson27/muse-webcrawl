Gravy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A jug of brown gravy beside a roast dinner]

Gravy is a sauce made from the juices of meats that run naturally during cooking, often thickened with flour or cornflour and seasoned, and served with the roasted or grilled food. In British and American cooking, gravy is inseparable from the roast dinner — poured over roast meat, potatoes and Yorkshire pudding — and in the American South it appears in forms such as the milk-based "country gravy" served over biscuits.

Making gravy

After meat is roasted, it is rested and the pan juices are skimmed of excess fat. Flour is often cooked briefly in some of the fat in the roasting pan (a roux), the juices and stock or vegetable water are whisked in, and the mixture is simmered until glossy and lightly thickened, dissolving the browned fond from the pan bottom for depth of flavour.

Varieties

Brown gravy accompanies beef and pork; poultry gravy is made from chicken or turkey drippings, famously at Thanksgiving. Onion gravy, enriched with slowly caramelised onions, tops sausages and mash. In Canada, gravy is one of the two toppings (with cheese curds) of poutine. Curry sauces and many pan sauces are gravies in the broad sense, and Indian English uses "gravy" for the sauce of a curry itself.

Instant and vegetarian gravies

Granules and powders (such as Britain's Bisto, sold since 1908) make instant gravy with boiling water. Vegetarian and vegan gravies build flavour from mushrooms, yeast extract, soy sauce and caramelised vegetables instead of meat juices.
