Indian Territory

The Indian Territory was an evolving land area set aside by the United States government for the relocation of Native Americans who held aboriginal title to their land. From the 1830s it comprised most of what is now the state of Oklahoma. It was the destination of the forced removals known as the Trail of Tears, and it ceased to exist as a distinct territory when Oklahoma became a state in 1907.

History

The concept of an Indian territory originated with the Louisiana Purchase of 1803, when President Thomas Jefferson envisioned lands west of the Mississippi as an exchange for eastern tribal lands. The Indian Removal Act of 1830, signed by President Andrew Jackson, authorized the removal of the southeastern tribes. During the 1830s the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole were forced west, suffering thousands of deaths on the Trail of Tears.

The Indian Intercourse Act of 1834 formally defined Indian country. The removed tribes established constitutional governments modelled in part on that of the United States, built schools and towns, and published newspapers such as the bilingual Cherokee Advocate.

Civil War and Reconstruction treaties

During the American Civil War, most of the Five Tribes allied with the Confederacy. The Reconstruction treaties of 1866 forced them to cede their western lands, which were then assigned to other removed tribes, and to accept the emancipation of their enslaved people (the tribal Freedmen).

Allotment and dissolution

The Dawes Act of 1887 began the policy of allotment — dividing communal tribal land into individual parcels. The Curtis Act of 1898 extended allotment to the Five Tribes and abolished their governments' authority over land. Land runs opened "surplus" lands to non-Indian settlers beginning in 1889, and Oklahoma Territory was organized in 1890 from the western half of the region. On 16 November 1907, Indian Territory and Oklahoma Territory were combined to form the State of Oklahoma, ending the Indian Territory's existence.

Legacy

In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the U.S. Supreme Court held that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation had never been disestablished by Congress, a ruling subsequently extended to the other Five Tribes' reservations in eastern Oklahoma, with major consequences for criminal jurisdiction.
