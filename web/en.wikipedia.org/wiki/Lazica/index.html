Lazica (Georgian: ლაზიკა, Lazika; Greek: Λαζική) was the name given in late antiquity to the territory of the ancient kingdom of Colchis on the eastern shore of the Black Sea, in modern western Georgia. Ruled by the Laz people, it was for centuries a contested buffer and client state between the Roman (later Byzantine) Empire and Sasanian Persia.

History

As Colchis's older Greek coastal cities faded, the Laz consolidated power in the interior during the first centuries AD, and by the fourth century their kingdom covered most of Colchis, with its capital at Archaeopolis. Formally subordinate to Rome, Lazica guarded the Caucasus passes and the coastal route between the Black Sea and Iberia. In the 540s, disputes over Roman trade monopolies and religious pressure drove King Gubazes II to appeal to Persia, triggering the Lazic War (541–562) between Justinian I and Khosrow I, chronicled by Procopius and Agathias. The war ended with the Fifty-Year Peace, which left Lazica in the Roman sphere in exchange for an annual payment.

Later history

Lazica was Christianised during the fourth to sixth centuries and became part of the Byzantine cultural world. In the seventh century Arab invasions weakened Byzantine control, and the region was gradually absorbed into the west Georgian polity of Abkhazia, while Laz identity survived longest in the southwestern districts (Lazistan) along the coast.
