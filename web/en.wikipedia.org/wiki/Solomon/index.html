Solomon (Hebrew: שְׁלֹמֹה, Šəlōmō), also called Jedidiah, was, according to the Hebrew Bible, the third king of the United Kingdom of Israel, succeeding his father David. The conventional dates of his reign are c. 970–931 BCE. Solomon is portrayed as wealthy and wise, the builder of the First Temple in Jerusalem, and is regarded as a prophet in Islam (Sulayman). The Hebrew Bible credits him with authoring or inspiring the books of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs.

Reign

After a struggle with his elder half-brother Adonijah, Solomon secured the throne and consolidated his kingdom. At Gibeon, according to 1 Kings, God offered him anything he wished; Solomon asked for wisdom, and was granted wisdom, riches, and honor. The Judgment of Solomon — in which he discerned the true mother of a disputed infant by proposing to divide the child — became the archetype of judicial wisdom.

Building projects

Solomon's most famous project was the First Temple in Jerusalem, built with cedar and labor supplied through his alliance with Hiram I of Tyre, and dedicated with the transfer of the Ark of the Covenant. He also built a royal palace complex, fortified cities including Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer, and is said to have maintained great stables and a trading fleet operating from Ezion-Geber, trading with Ophir and receiving the visit of the Queen of Sheba.

Wealth and decline

The Bible describes annual revenues of 666 talents of gold and a court of legendary splendor. It also rebukes Solomon for his many foreign wives — 700 wives and 300 concubines — who "turned away his heart after other gods". After his death the kingdom split under his son Rehoboam into the northern Kingdom of Israel and the southern Kingdom of Judah, a division the biblical text presents as punishment for Solomon's idolatry.

Historicity

The extent of Solomon's kingdom is debated among archaeologists and historians. No contemporary extra-biblical inscriptions name Solomon, and scholars such as Israel Finkelstein have argued that 10th-century Jerusalem was a modest highland center rather than the capital of a large empire, while others defend a more substantial Solomonic state. The traditional image of Solomon's wisdom endured across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, and later legend credited him with command over demons and the authorship of magical texts such as the Testament of Solomon.
