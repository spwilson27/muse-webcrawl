An auto rickshaw is a motorised version of the traditional pulled rickshaw or cycle rickshaw. Most have three wheels and do not tilt; they are known by many names around the world, including tuk-tuk (in Thailand and widely elsewhere), auto, bajaj (after the manufacturer Bajaj Auto), rickshaw, mototaxi and three-wheeler. They are a ubiquitous form of urban transport, both as vehicles for hire and for private use, across much of Asia and increasingly in Africa and Latin America.

Design

The typical auto rickshaw has a sheet-metal or open frame body on three wheels, a canvas roof, a small cabin for the driver in front and seating for two to four passengers behind. Steering is by handlebar rather than a wheel. Engines are usually small single-cylinder petrol, diesel, CNG or LPG units of around 200 cc, traditionally two-stroke and increasingly four-stroke; electric models ("e-rickshaws") have become common in the 2010s and 2020s. The vehicles are inexpensive to buy and run, highly manoeuvrable in congested streets, but offer little weather or crash protection.

Use around the world

In India, auto rickshaws are a principal means of paratransit, typically metered or hailed informally, and are painted in regional liveries such as the black-and-yellow of Mumbai or the green-and-yellow of Delhi's CNG fleet. Thailand's larger, open-air tuk-tuk has become a national icon and tourist attraction. Versions operate as the bajaj in Indonesia, the mishuk in Bangladesh, the tuk in Sri Lanka, the mototaxi in Peru and parts of Central America, and the keke in Nigeria. Tourist and novelty tuk-tuks also operate in cities in Europe and North America.

Regulation and environment

Cities have repeatedly sought to regulate auto rickshaw numbers, fares and emissions. Two-stroke models were significant sources of urban air pollution, prompting mandates to convert fleets to CNG or LPG in cities such as Delhi and Dhaka, and promoting electric replacements. Debates continue over licensing regimes, driver livelihoods and the role of app-based ride-hailing platforms, which began aggregating auto rickshaw services in the mid-2010s.
