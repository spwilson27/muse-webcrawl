Verse (poetry)

A verse is formally a single metrical line in a poetic composition, though in common usage the word has come to denote a stanza, or any short unit of a poem or song, and by extension poetry in general (writing "in verse", as opposed to prose). The word derives from Latin versus, a furrow or line of writing, from vertere, to turn — the plough turning at the end of the furrow, and the eye at the end of the line.

Forms of verse

Verse may be rhymed or unrhymed, metrical or free. Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter, the characteristic measure of English drama and epic — Shakespeare's plays and Milton's Paradise Lost are largely in blank verse. Free verse (vers libre) abandons regular metre and fixed line lengths while retaining lineation as its organising device; it dominates modern and contemporary poetry. Stanzaic units built from verses include the couplet (two lines), tercet (three), quatrain (four) and longer forms such as the sonnet's octaves and sestets.

Verse and stanza

Strictly, a stanza is a group of verses set off by a space, and the verse is its constituent line; popular usage — especially in song, where a verse alternates with a chorus — reverses this, calling each stanza a "verse". In biblical usage, the chapters of scripture are divided into numbered verses for reference, a separate but related sense of the word.
