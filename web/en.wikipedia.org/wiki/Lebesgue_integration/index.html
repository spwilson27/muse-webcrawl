Lebesgue integration

Lebesgue integration, named after the French mathematician Henri Lebesgue, extends the integral to a larger class of functions than the Riemann integral and makes it possible to interchange limits and integrals under much weaker hypotheses. Roughly, whereas Riemann's approach partitions the domain of a function into small intervals, Lebesgue's approach partitions the range: Lebesgue himself illustrated the difference by saying that with Riemann integration one counts coins in the order they are taken from a pocket, whereas with his integral one sorts the coins by denomination first and then adds them up.

Construction

The Lebesgue integral is built in stages:

  1. Characteristic functions: for a measurable set E, the integral of its indicator function is defined as its measure, ∫1E dμ = μ(E).
  2. Simple functions: finite linear combinations of indicator functions are integrated by the corresponding weighted sum of measures.
  3. Nonnegative measurable functions: the integral is the supremum of the integrals of simple functions below it.
  4. General functions: a measurable function f is split into positive and negative parts, f = f⁺ − f⁻, and is integrable when both parts have finite integral.

On Euclidean space the underlying measure is Lebesgue measure, which generalises the notions of length, area and volume to far more sets than elementary geometry handles; on abstract measure spaces the same construction underlies probability theory, where the integral becomes expectation.

Advantages over the Riemann integral

Every Riemann-integrable function on a compact interval is Lebesgue-integrable with the same value, but the converse is false: the Dirichlet function (1 on rationals, 0 on irrationals) is Lebesgue-integrable with integral 0 but not Riemann-integrable. More importantly, the spaces of Lebesgue-integrable functions behave well under limits: the monotone and dominated convergence theorems give simple conditions under which the limit of integrals equals the integral of the limit, which underlies the completeness of the Lp spaces and hence modern partial differential equations, Fourier analysis and functional analysis.

History

Lebesgue developed the theory in his 1902 doctoral thesis at the University of Paris, building on work on measure by Émile Borel and Camille Jordan, and published his Leçons sur l'intégration in 1904. The theory was initially resisted by some analysts but by the 1920s–30s, in the hands of Frigyes Riesz, Johann Radon and others (the Lebesgue–Stieltjes and Lebesgue–Radon–Nikodym theories), it became the standard foundation for integration.
