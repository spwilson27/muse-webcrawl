In physics, a symmetry of a physical system or of a physical law is a feature that is preserved (invariant) under some transformation. Symmetries may be discrete, like the reflection symmetry of a crystal, or continuous, like the rotational symmetry of a circle. The symmetry principles of physics dictate the form of physical laws and, through Noether's theorem, are directly connected to conservation laws.

Symmetry as invariance

The central idea is invariance: the laws of physics are the same regardless of one's position or orientation, or the time at which an experiment is performed. Translational symmetry (invariance under shifts in space) implies the conservation of linear momentum; rotational symmetry implies conservation of angular momentum; and time-translation symmetry implies conservation of energy. Emmy Noether proved in 1915 that every differentiable continuous symmetry of the action of a physical system corresponds to a conservation law.

Spacetime symmetries

Classical mechanics is invariant under Galilean transformations, while special relativity is built on the Lorentz transformations that preserve the speed of light and the spacetime interval. Together with translations these form the Poincaré group, the symmetry group of Minkowski spacetime. In general relativity, the laws are invariant under arbitrary smooth coordinate transformations (diffeomorphisms).

Discrete symmetries: C, P, and T

Three discrete symmetries are fundamental in particle physics: parity (P, spatial reflection), charge conjugation (C, exchanging particles and antiparticles), and time reversal (T). It was long assumed each was exactly conserved, but in 1956–57 parity was shown to be violated by the weak interaction (following the proposal of Lee and Yang and the experiment of Chien-Shiung Wu), and CP violation was discovered in 1964 in kaon decays. The CPT theorem states that the combination of all three must be conserved in any local Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory.

Gauge symmetry and symmetry breaking

The Standard Model of particle physics is built on internal gauge symmetries, notably the group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1). Spontaneous symmetry breaking — in which the laws retain a symmetry that the ground state does not — explains, via the Higgs mechanism, how the W and Z bosons acquire mass while the photon remains massless. Symmetry arguments also guide the search for physics beyond the Standard Model, such as supersymmetry.
