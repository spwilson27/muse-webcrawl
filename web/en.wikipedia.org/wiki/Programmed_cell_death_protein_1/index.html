Programmed cell death protein 1

Programmed cell death protein 1, also known as PD-1 and CD279, is a protein on the surface of immune cells that functions as an immune checkpoint. In humans it is encoded by the PDCD1 gene. PD-1 was discovered in 1992 by Tasuku Honjo and colleagues at Kyoto University.

Function

PD-1 is expressed on activated T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells. It binds two ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2, and delivers an inhibitory signal that dampens T-cell activity. This mechanism prevents autoimmunity and limits tissue damage during immune responses, but it can also be exploited by tumors: many cancers express PD-L1 to evade immune attack.

Clinical significance

Monoclonal antibodies that block PD-1, such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab, or its ligand PD-L1, are used in cancer immunotherapy and have produced durable responses in melanoma, lung cancer, and other tumors. Blocking PD-1 can also cause immune-related adverse effects. Honjo shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of cancer therapy by inhibition of negative immune regulation.
