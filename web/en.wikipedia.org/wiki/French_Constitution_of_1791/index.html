French Constitution of 1791

The first written constitution of France.

The French Constitution of 1791 was the first written constitution in France, created after the collapse of the absolute monarchy during the French Revolution. Adopted by the National Constituent Assembly on 3 September 1791 and accepted by King Louis XVI on 14 September, it established a constitutional monarchy and embodied the principles of popular sovereignty, separation of powers and the rights proclaimed in the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789, which was prefixed to it as a preamble.

Provisions

The constitution guaranteed civil rights including equality before the law, freedom of speech, freedom of religion and security of property, and abolished the privileges of the nobility and the guild system. Sovereignty was declared to reside in the nation. Legislative power was vested in a single chamber, the Legislative Assembly, elected for two-year terms. The king retained executive power and a suspensive veto, and was styled "King of the French".

Voting rights were restricted to "active citizens" — men over 25 who paid a minimum amount of direct tax — excluding roughly a third of adult men, and women entirely. This distinction between active and passive citizens was one of the constitution's most criticised features.

Collapse

The constitution was undermined by the king's attempted flight to Varennes in June 1791, war with Austria from April 1792, and growing radicalism in Paris. Following the insurrection of 10 August 1792 and the storming of the Tuileries, the monarchy was suspended; the National Convention abolished it in September 1792 and proclaimed the First French Republic. The Constitution of 1791 was thus in force for barely a year, but it remained a landmark in the development of European constitutionalism.
