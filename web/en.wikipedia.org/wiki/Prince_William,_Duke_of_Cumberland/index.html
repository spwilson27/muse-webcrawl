Prince William, Duke of Cumberland

Prince William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland (15 April 1721 – 31 October 1765), was the third and youngest son of King George II of Great Britain and Ireland and Queen Caroline of Ansbach. He is best remembered for his role in putting down the Jacobite Rising of 1745 at the Battle of Culloden, an achievement that earned him lasting popularity in parts of England — where the flower sweet William was named in his honour — and the nickname "the Butcher" in Scotland, owing to the harsh pacification of the Highlands that followed.

Early life and military career

William was created Duke of Cumberland in 1726 and was raised with a military career in view. He served during the War of the Austrian Succession, fighting at the Battle of Dettingen in 1743 and commanding the allied army at the Battle of Fontenoy in 1745, where he was defeated by Marshal Saxe but conducted an orderly retreat.

The Forty-Five

Recalled to Britain to face the Jacobite rising led by Charles Edward Stuart, Cumberland took command of government forces in Scotland in early 1746. On 16 April 1746 he decisively defeated the Jacobite army at Culloden Moor, the last major battle fought on British soil. In the aftermath, his troops hunted down fugitives and harshly repressed Jacobite sympathizers in the Highlands, actions that gave rise to his Scottish epithet "Butcher Cumberland."

Later career

Cumberland returned to the continent during the Seven Years' War but was defeated at the Battle of Hastenbeck in 1757 and signed the Convention of Klosterzeven, which he was forced to repudiate; he resigned all his military offices in disgrace. He remained an influential figure in politics as an adviser to his nephew, George III, until his death in 1765.
