Nikolai Gogol

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Nikolai Gogol in the 1840s: the sharp-featured writer with his distinctive long nose and centre-parted hair]

Nikolai Vasilyevich Gogol (1 April 1809 – 4 March 1852) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer and playwright of Ukrainian origin, one of the founding figures of Russian prose. His work — grotesque, comic and terrifying by turns — founded the Petersburg tradition of Russian realism and surrealism alike; 'We all came out from under Gogol's Overcoat' runs the famous saying attributed to Dostoevsky.

His masterpieces are the novel Dead Souls (1842), the play The Government Inspector (1836), and the Petersburg tales: 'The Nose' (1836), 'The Overcoat' (1842) and 'Diary of a Madman' (1835). Earlier Ukrainian story cycles, Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka (1831–32) and Mirgorod (1835, including 'Taras Bulba'), made his name.

Career

Born in Sorochyntsi, Poltava Governorate, Gogol arrived in St Petersburg in 1828 with literary ambitions and a first poem he was so ashamed of that he bought up and burned the edition. Pushkin became his patron and — Gogol claimed — gave him the plots of both The Government Inspector and Dead Souls. The Inspector, a satire of provincial bureaucracy, delighted Nicholas I ('Everyone gets it — and I most of all', the tsar is said to have remarked) and scandalised officialdom.

Decline

Years of wandering in Europe, chiefly Rome, produced the first part of Dead Souls — Chichikov travelling Russia buying up the names of dead serfs to mortgage — planned as a three-part Divine Comedy of Russian life. In religious crisis under the influence of a fanatical confessor, Gogol burned the manuscript of Part Two in 1852, days before his death at 42, an act Russian culture has never stopped arguing about.
