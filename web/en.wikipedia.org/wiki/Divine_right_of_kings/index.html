Divine right of kings

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The divine right of kings, or divine-right theory of kingship, is a political and religious doctrine of political legitimacy in post-Reformation Western Europe. It asserts that a monarch is subject to no earthly authority, deriving the right to rule directly from the will of God; the king is thus not subject to the will of the people, the aristocracy or any other estate, and only God can judge an unjust king — resistance to him is rebellion against God.

History and refutation

The doctrine matured from medieval sacral kingship (anointed kings, "by the Grace of God" in royal styles) into systematic theory as confessional states centralised: James VI and I's The True Law of Free Monarchies (1598) and Robert Filmer's Patriarcha (published 1680) grounded it in Adam's patriarchal authority. John Locke's First Treatise demolished Filmer point by point, and the Glorious Revolution (1688) and later democratic revolutions replaced divine right with popular sovereignty and constitutionalism in the West — though sacred legitimation persisted elsewhere (Japan's imperial divinity until 1946; the Chinese Mandate of Heaven, by contrast, made legitimacy conditional on just rule and was invoked to justify dynastic overthrow).
