Pupillary light reflex

The pupillary light reflex (PLR) is a reflex that controls the diameter of the pupil in response to the intensity of light falling on the retina. When light is shone into one eye, the pupil of that eye constricts (the direct response) and the pupil of the opposite eye also constricts (the consensual response). The reflex protects the retina from excessive light and increases depth of field, and because its pathway traverses the optic nerve, midbrain and oculomotor nerve, testing it is a fundamental part of the neurological and ophthalmic examination.

Pathway

The afferent limb begins with photosensitive ganglion cells and photoreceptors of the retina; fibres travel through the optic nerve, partially decussate in the optic chiasm, and continue in the optic tract, leaving it before the lateral geniculate nucleus to reach the pretectal area of the midbrain. Pretectal neurons project bilaterally to the Edinger–Westphal nuclei — this bilateral projection explains the consensual response. The efferent limb begins in the Edinger–Westphal nucleus, whose parasympathetic fibres run with the oculomotor nerve (cranial nerve III) to the ciliary ganglion; postganglionic short ciliary nerves then supply the sphincter pupillae muscle of the iris, which constricts the pupil.

Clinical testing

Using a penlight in a dim room, the examiner checks each pupil's direct and consensual response. The swinging flashlight test compares the two eyes: light swung from the normal eye to an eye with an afferent defect (for example in optic neuritis) paradoxically causes dilation — a relative afferent pupillary defect, or Marcus Gunn pupil. A pupil that fails to constrict with a normal afferent input suggests an efferent lesion, such as an oculomotor nerve palsy, classically producing a fixed, dilated pupil. Unreactive pupils are also a sign monitored in head injury and brainstem death testing.

Related reflexes

The light reflex should be distinguished from the near reflex, in which viewing a near target triggers constriction by a pathway involving the visual cortex. In some conditions — notably the Argyll Robertson pupil of neurosyphilis and dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome — the light response is lost while the near response is preserved, a phenomenon called light-near dissociation.
