Hügelkultur

Hügelkultur
Literal meaning"Hill culture" / "mound culture" (German)
OriginGerman and Eastern European gardening
Popularized bySepp Holzer and permaculture writers

Hügelkultur (German pronunciation: [ˈhyːɡl̩kʊlˌtuːɐ̯], literally "hill culture" or "mound culture") is a horticultural technique where a mound constructed from decaying wood debris and other compostable biomass plant materials is later (or immediately) planted as a raised bed. Adopted by permaculture advocates, it is suggested the technique helps to improve soil fertility, moisture retention and soil warming, and thus benefits plants grown on or near such mounds.

Construction

A hügelkultur bed is typically built by digging a shallow trench, laying in logs, branches and woody debris (often in descending size order), then piling on turfs upside-down, grass clippings, compost, manure and topsoil to form a mound commonly about 0.6–1 metre high, with steep sides for maximum planting area. In arid landscapes, the wood may be buried in deep pits (sometimes called "sunken hügelkultur") to conserve moisture. Fresh beds can be planted immediately, though the first year's decomposition may temporarily tie up nitrogen.

Claimed advantages

As the buried wood decomposes over years, it acts like a sponge, absorbing rainwater and slowly releasing it to plant roots, which advocates say reduces or eliminates irrigation after the first year. Decomposition also slowly releases nutrients and generates modest heat, potentially extending the growing season, while the porous structure aerates the soil and provides habitat for soil organisms. The method also gives gardeners a use for woody waste that might otherwise be burned or chipped.

Criticism

Horticultural researchers note that many claimed benefits are supported mainly by anecdote rather than controlled trials, that improperly built mounds can slump, dry out or harvest carpenter ants and termites, and that using logs of allelopathic species (such as black walnut) or diseased wood can harm crops.
