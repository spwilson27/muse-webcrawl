Collective investment scheme

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Investors pool funds, which a manager invests in a portfolio of assets.

A collective investment scheme (CIS), sometimes called a pooled investment fund, is a way of investing money in which a number of investors pool their money together into a fund, which is managed by a fund manager who invests in a portfolio of assets such as stocks, bonds, money market instruments, or real estate. Investors share in the profits and losses of the fund in proportion to their holdings.

Advantages

Pooling allows small investors to obtain diversification across many securities, professional management, and economies of scale in trading costs that would be unavailable to them individually. Schemes offer liquidity, with investors typically able to buy or sell units at prices based on the net asset value of the fund's holdings.

Types

Common forms include open-ended funds such as mutual funds and unit trusts, which continuously issue and redeem units; closed-ended funds such as investment trusts, whose shares trade on exchanges; exchange-traded funds (ETFs); and undertakings for collective investment in transferable securities (UCITS), a European regulatory framework allowing funds to be sold across the European Union under a single passport. Other vehicles include hedge funds, private equity funds, and real estate investment trusts (REITs).

Regulation

Because they deal with the public's money, collective investment schemes are heavily regulated. In the United States, mutual funds are governed principally by the Investment Company Act of 1940; in the United Kingdom the Financial Conduct Authority regulates authorised funds, and UCITS directives govern retail funds across the European Union. Regulation typically covers disclosure, valuation, custody of assets, conflicts of interest, and the separation of the fund's assets from those of its manager.
