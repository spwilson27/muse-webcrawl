Coagulation (blood clotting)

[Hero image: micrograph of a fibrin mesh trapping red blood cells within a blood clot]

Coagulation, also known as blood clotting, is the process by which blood changes from a liquid to a gel, forming a blood clot. It potentially results in hemostasis, the cessation of blood loss from a damaged vessel, followed by repair. The process involves activation, adhesion and aggregation of platelets, as well as deposition and maturation of fibrin.

Mechanism

Primary hemostasis

When endothelium is injured, platelets adhere to exposed collagen via von Willebrand factor, become activated, release granule contents such as ADP and thromboxane A2, and aggregate into a platelet plug.

The coagulation cascade

The secondary phase amplifies clot formation through a cascade of serine proteases. Tissue factor exposed at the injury site binds factor VIIa (the extrinsic pathway), while contact with negatively charged surfaces activates factor XII (the intrinsic pathway). Both converge on the activation of factor X. The prothrombinase complex (factors Xa and Va) converts prothrombin (factor II) to thrombin, which cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin monomers; factor XIIIa then cross-links the fibrin into a stable mesh. Vitamin K is required for the hepatic synthesis of factors II, VII, IX and X and of proteins C and S.

Regulation and fibrinolysis

Antithrombin, tissue factor pathway inhibitor, and the protein C system limit clot propagation. Plasmin, generated from plasminogen by tissue plasminogen activator, dissolves fibrin during fibrinolysis.

Disorders

Defects in clotting cause bleeding disorders such as hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) and hemophilia B (factor IX deficiency), and von Willebrand disease. Excessive clotting produces thrombosis, including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism; disseminated intravascular coagulation combines both processes. Clinical tests of clotting include the prothrombin time (PT/INR) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT). Anticoagulant drugs include heparin, warfarin, and the direct oral anticoagulants.
