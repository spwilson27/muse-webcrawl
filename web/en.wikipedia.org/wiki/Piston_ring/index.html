Piston ring

A piston ring is a metallic split ring fitted into a groove on the outer diameter of a piston in a reciprocating engine or compressor. Piston rings seal the gap between the piston and the cylinder wall, and serve three main functions: sealing combustion gases in the chamber, regulating oil consumption, and transferring heat from the piston to the cylinder wall.

Design

A typical automotive piston carries three rings. The top two are compression rings, which provide the gas seal; their faces may be barrel-shaped, tapered, or coated with molybdenum, chrome, or ceramic materials. The third is an oil control ring — usually a three-piece assembly of two thin rails and a spacer-expander — which scrapes excess oil from the cylinder wall while leaving a thin lubricating film.

Materials and behaviour

Rings are commonly made of cast iron or steel and are manufactured with a free diameter larger than the bore, so their spring tension presses them outward; combustion pressure behind the top ring strengthens the seal. The small gap left at the split ("end gap") allows for thermal expansion. Worn or stuck rings cause blow-by, oil burning, and loss of compression.

History

Effective metallic rings were essential to the steam engine and were perfected for internal-combustion engines in the late nineteenth century; John Ramsbottom's spring ring design of the 1850s was an influential early form.
