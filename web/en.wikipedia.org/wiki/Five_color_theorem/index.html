Five color theorem

FieldGraph theory
ProvedPercy John Heawood, 1890

The five color theorem states that five colors are always sufficient to color any map drawn on a plane or sphere so that no two adjacent regions share the same color. In graph-theoretic terms, every planar graph is 5-colorable. It is a weaker result than the four color theorem, but unlike that theorem it admits a short proof that can be checked by hand.

Proof outline

The result was first proved by Percy John Heawood in 1890, in the same paper that exposed the flaw in Alfred Kempe's attempted proof of the four color theorem. Heawood's proof works by induction using Kempe chains: every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most five; removing such a vertex, coloring the smaller graph, and then — if necessary — swapping colors along two-color chains to free a color for the removed vertex completes the coloring.

Relation to the four color theorem

The stronger claim that four colors suffice was conjectured in 1852 and proved only in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken, using extensive computer checking. The five color theorem remains a standard exercise in introductory graph theory courses.
