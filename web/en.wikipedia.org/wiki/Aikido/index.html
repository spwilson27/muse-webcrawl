Aikido

Aikido (Japanese: 合気道) is a Japanese martial art founded by Morihei Ueshiba (1883–1969) in the early 20th century. Often translated as "the way of harmonious spirit", aikido is primarily defensive: practitioners (nage) blend with an attacker's motion, redirecting its energy into throws and joint locks rather than meeting force with force.

Techniques and training

Training centres on paired practice: one partner (uke) attacks and receives the technique; the other (nage/tori) applies it. Core principles include entering (irimi) and turning (tenkan) movement, circular motion, and maintaining one's centre. Techniques include ikkyō through gokyō (pinning controls), shihōnage, iriminage, koshinage and kokyūnage throws, usually finished with a pin or a safe projection. Weapons training with the wooden sword (bokken), staff (jō) and knife (tantō) is integral in many schools, and students learn to fall safely (ukemi).

Philosophy and spread

Ueshiba, influenced by the Ōmoto religion, framed aikido as a path of reconciliation — "the art of peace" — intending it to neutralise aggression without injuring the attacker. After his death, his son Kisshomaru and grandson Moriteru led the Aikikai, while students such as Koichi Tohei (Ki Society) and Gozo Shioda (Yoshinkan) founded major separate styles. Aikido is now practised worldwide and has no competition in most styles, gradings proceeding through kyū and dan ranks.
