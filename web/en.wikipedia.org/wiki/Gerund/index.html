Gerund

A gerund (abbreviated GER) is a verb form that functions as a noun. In English, a gerund is the -ing form of a verb — identical in shape to the present participle — used in positions typical of noun phrases, as in "Swimming is good exercise" or "I enjoy reading." Traditional grammar distinguishes the gerund (nominal) from the present participle (adjectival or progressive), though many modern grammars treat them as a single form called the gerund-participle.

In English

Gerunds can take objects and adverbial modifiers like verbs ("Eating vegetables quickly is wise") while the whole gerund phrase occupies a noun-phrase position as subject, object, or complement of a preposition. Usage questions include the possessive before a gerund ("I object to his leaving" versus "him leaving"), a topic of long-standing prescriptive debate. In terminology borrowed from Latin grammar, the English possessive-plus-gerund construction is sometimes called the "fused participle" controversy.

In Latin and other languages

In Latin the gerund is a verbal noun in the oblique cases (genitive amandī, "of loving") contrasted with the nominative infinitive, while the gerundive is a passive verbal adjective expressing necessity ("that must be loved"). The Romance languages preserve descendant uses, such as the Spanish gerundio and Italian gerundio, which are chiefly adverbial. Many languages lack a distinct gerund and use infinitives or other nominalisations instead.
