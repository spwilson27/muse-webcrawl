Metaphysics is the branch of Philosophy that investigates the fundamental nature of reality, including the relationship between mind and matter, substance and attribute, and potentiality and actuality. Its name derives from the Greek meta ta physika ('after the Physics'), the title given to works of Aristotle placed after his writings on physics. Central questions include what exists (ontology), what kinds of things exist, how objects persist through change, the nature of time and space, and whether the will is free.

History

Metaphysical speculation begins in the West with the Pre-Socratic philosophers, who sought the underlying arche (principle) of all things. Plato located reality in eternal Forms, while Aristotle grounded it in substances and their forms and matter. Medieval thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas fused Aristotelian metaphysics with Christian theology. In the early modern period, Descartes split reality into thinking and extended substance, Spinoza argued for a single substance, and Immanuel Kant held that metaphysics must be limited by the conditions of possible experience.

In the 20th century, logical positivists dismissed much traditional metaphysics as meaningless, but the field revived in analytic philosophy through work by figures such as W. V. O. Quine and Saul Kripke on modality, identity and possible worlds.

Central problems

Core problems include the mind–body problem, free will, the nature of universals, causation, personal identity over time, and the existence and nature of God, treated by philosophical theology. Ontology, the sub-field concerned with what there is, asks whether abstract objects such as numbers exist and how categories of being are structured.
