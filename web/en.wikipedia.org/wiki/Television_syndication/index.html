Television syndication

Television syndication (more precisely, broadcast syndication) is the practice of leasing the right to broadcast television programmes to multiple television stations, without going through a broadcast network. Syndicated programming is distributed by a syndicator — often the production company or a specialist distributor — to individual stations or station groups, which schedule it in their own local time slots. It is particularly common in the United States, where stations must fill many hours outside network programming.

Types of syndication

First-run syndication refers to programmes produced specifically for syndication rather than for a network. Historically this category has included talk shows such as The Oprah Winfrey Show and Dr. Phil, game shows such as Wheel of Fortune and Jeopardy!, court shows, entertainment news magazines, and, especially in the 1980s and 1990s, action and science-fiction series such as Star Trek: The Next Generation and Xena: Warrior Princess.

Off-network syndication is the sale of reruns of programmes that originally aired on a network. Sitcoms such as Friends, Seinfeld, and The Big Bang Theory have generated enormous revenue in this market. Traditionally a series needed roughly 100 episodes — about four or five seasons — to be viable for daily "stripping" (airing at the same time five days a week), though the threshold has fallen in the streaming era.

Economics

Syndication deals are structured as cash licences, barter (in which the syndicator retains some advertising time to sell nationally in exchange for a lower or zero licence fee), or a combination of both. Successful first-run shows can dominate a time period for decades; successful off-network reruns, in which production companies historically operated at a loss during the network run in hopes of a syndication payoff, redefined television financing. From the 2010s, streaming platforms became the principal buyers of library content, shifting the economics away from local-station syndication while leaving its flagship first-run franchises in place.
