Burlesque

A burlesque stage show, with its characteristic blend of satire, music and variety performance.

Burlesque is a literary, dramatic or musical work intended to cause laughter by caricaturing the manner or spirit of serious works, or by ludicrous treatment of their subjects. The word derives from the Italian burlesco, which itself comes from burla — a joke, ridicule or mockery. In its later, predominantly American usage, the term came to describe a form of variety entertainment featuring comic sketches, chorus numbers and striptease acts.

Literary burlesque

As a literary form, burlesque has two principal modes: high burlesque, in which a lofty style is applied to a trivial subject (as in mock-epic poems such as Alexander Pope's The Rape of the Lock), and low burlesque, in which a serious subject is treated in a deliberately vulgar or ridiculous style. The form flourished in 17th- and 18th-century England and France, often as political and social satire.

Theatrical burlesque

Victorian burlesque was a popular theatrical genre in 19th-century London, presenting parodies of well-known operas, plays and ballets, enlivened by puns, topical songs and women in male roles. Writers such as W. S. Gilbert produced numerous burlesques before turning to comic opera. In the United States, burlesque evolved into a distinct variety format combining comedians, chorus lines and, increasingly, striptease.

American burlesque and striptease

From the late 19th century, American burlesque circuits — notably the Columbia and Mutual wheels — toured companies of comedians and chorus girls. By the 1920s and 1930s the striptease had become the headline attraction, with performers such as Gypsy Rose Lee achieving mainstream fame. Municipal crackdowns, including New York's 1937 ban, and competition from cinema led to the form's decline, though a "neo-burlesque" revival emerged from the 1990s onward.
