Speleology

Speleology is the scientific study of caves and other karst features, as well as their geological, hydrological, biological and historical characteristics. The term derives from the Greek spēlaion (σπήλαιον), meaning cave, and -logia, the study of.

History

Although caves have been explored since prehistory, speleology as a recognised science is generally credited to the French lawyer Édouard-Alfred Martel (1859–1938), who pioneered cave exploration techniques in the late nineteenth century and is often called the father of speleology. Earlier figures such as the Austrian explorer Adolf Schmidl contributed systematic surveys of the Postojna cave system in Slovenia.

Disciplines

Speleology overlaps with several fields:

  • Geospeleology — the study of cave formation (speleogenesis) and cave minerals (speleothems).
  • Hydrospeleology — cave hydrology, including underground rivers and aquifers.
  • Biospeleology — the study of organisms living in caves, including troglobites such as the olm.
  • Paleospeleology and archaeology — cave deposits preserve fossils, sediments and evidence of human occupation.

Techniques

Cave surveying uses compasses, inclinometers, tape measures and increasingly laser rangefinders and LiDAR to produce maps of passages. Vertical caves require single rope technique (SRT). Safety, conservation ethics and minimal-impact practice are central to modern caving organisations such as the Union Internationale de Spéléologie, founded in 1965.
