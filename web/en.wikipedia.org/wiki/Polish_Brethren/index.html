Polish Brethren

The Polish Brethren (Polish: Bracia Polscy) were members of the Minor Reformed Church of Poland, a nontrinitarian Protestant church that broke with the Calvinist Reformed Church in Poland in 1565 during the Protestant Reformation. Also known to their opponents as Arians, and later associated with Socinianism through the influence of Fausto Sozzini (Faustus Socinus), they developed one of the most theologically radical and intellectually productive movements of the Radical Reformation.

Beliefs and institutions

The Brethren rejected the doctrine of the Trinity, denied the pre-existence of Christ, practised believer's baptism, and many refused military service, oaths and participation in government, following a strict reading of the Sermon on the Mount. Their centre at Raków, founded in 1569, housed the Racovian Academy and a famous printing press; the Racovian Catechism of 1605 set out their doctrine and was translated across Europe.

Expulsion and legacy

During the era of the Swedish Deluge, the Brethren were accused of collaborating with the invaders, and in 1658 the Sejm ordered them to convert to Catholicism or leave the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Exiles settled in Transylvania, the Netherlands and Prussia, where their writings influenced early Unitarianism and Enlightenment thinkers including John Locke and Isaac Newton.
