Ghost town

A ghost town, or alternatively a deserted city, is an abandoned settlement, usually one that contains substantial visible remaining structures and infrastructure such as buildings, roads, or railways. A town often becomes a ghost town because the economic activity that supported it — commonly mining, milling, agriculture, or a transport route — has failed or ceased, or because of natural or human-caused disasters such as floods, droughts, government-ordered evacuations, or war.

Causes

The classic ghost towns of the American West were mining boomtowns that flourished for a few years or decades and emptied when the ore ran out or prices collapsed; examples include Bodie in California and Tombstone's less fortunate neighbours in Arizona. Elsewhere, towns died when railways bypassed them, when highways were rerouted, when a single-employer factory or mine closed, or when water supplies failed. Some settlements were abandoned abruptly: Pripyat in Ukraine was evacuated in 1986 after the Chernobyl disaster, and Centralia, Pennsylvania, emptied after an underground mine fire began in 1962.

Preservation and tourism

Many ghost towns survive as heritage sites. Bodie is maintained by California State Parks in a condition of "arrested decay", its interiors left as they were when residents departed. Kolmanskop in Namibia, slowly being swallowed by desert sand, and Hashima Island in Japan, a former undersea coal-mining colony, draw visitors from around the world. Definitions vary — some enthusiasts classify any settlement that has lost most of its population as a ghost town, even where a handful of residents remain — and ghost towns exist on every continent.
