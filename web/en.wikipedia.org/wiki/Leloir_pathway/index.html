Leloir pathway

The Leloir pathway is a metabolic pathway found in many organisms that converts galactose to glucose 1-phosphate. It is named after the Argentine biochemist Luis Federico Leloir, whose laboratory elucidated the pathway in the early 1950s and whose discovery of sugar nucleotides — including the pathway's donor, UDP-galactose — formed part of the work recognized by his 1970 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Reactions

The pathway proceeds in four principal steps:

  1. Galactokinase (GALK) phosphorylates α-D-galactose using ATP to give galactose 1-phosphate.
  2. Galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (GALT) transfers a uridylyl group from UDP-glucose to galactose 1-phosphate, producing UDP-galactose and releasing glucose 1-phosphate.
  3. UDP-galactose 4′-epimerase (GALE) interconverts UDP-galactose and UDP-glucose, regenerating the UDP-glucose consumed in the previous step, so that UDP-glucose acts catalytically.
  4. The glucose 1-phosphate produced can be converted by phosphoglucomutase to glucose 6-phosphate, entering glycolysis or glycogen synthesis.

Before entering the pathway, β-D-galactose (the form released from lactose) is converted to the α anomer by galactose mutarotase (GALM).

Clinical significance

Defects in the enzymes of the Leloir pathway cause the galactosemias, inborn errors of metabolism in which galactose and its metabolites accumulate. Classic galactosemia results from severe deficiency of GALT and presents in newborns fed milk with vomiting, jaundice, hepatomegaly, and susceptibility to sepsis; it is detected by newborn screening in many countries and treated by a galactose-restricted diet. Galactokinase deficiency chiefly causes early cataracts, and UDP-galactose epimerase deficiency ranges from a benign peripheral form to a severe generalized form.
