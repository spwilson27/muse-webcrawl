Principle of double effect

Placeholder image: intended versus merely foreseen effects

The principle of double effect (also known as the rule of double effect, the doctrine of double effect, or DDE) is a set of ethical criteria, originating in the writings of Thomas Aquinas, for determining when it is permissible to perform an action that will have both a good effect and a harmful side effect.

Principle of double effect
OriginScholastic moral theology; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae
FieldEthics, moral philosophy, medical ethics, just war theory

Conditions

In its classical formulation an action with a foreseeable bad effect is permissible only if:

  1. The action itself is good or at least morally neutral.
  2. The agent intends only the good effect; the bad effect is foreseen but not intended, either as an end or as a means.
  3. The bad effect is not a means to the good effect.
  4. The good effect is proportionately weighty enough to justify allowing the bad effect.

Applications

The principle is invoked in medical ethics — for example, administering high-dose analgesics that may foreseeably shorten life — and in just war theory, where it distinguishes permissible attacks on military targets with foreseen civilian harm ("collateral damage") from terror bombing aimed at civilians as a means. It is also discussed in debates about abortion, euthanasia, and self-defence; Aquinas's own example was killing in self-defence, where death of the attacker is beside the intention (praeter intentionem).

Criticism

Critics question whether the intended/foreseen distinction can bear the moral weight placed on it, since effects can often be redescribed, and argue the principle risks licensing foreseeable harms. The related trolley problem literature frequently uses double effect to explain differing judgements between cases.
