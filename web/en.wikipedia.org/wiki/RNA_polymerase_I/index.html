RNA polymerase I

Placeholder image: structure of RNA polymerase I.

RNA polymerase I (also called RNA Pol I and, historically, RNA polymerase A) is, in higher eukaryotes, the polymerase that transcribes only ribosomal RNA (but not 5S rRNA, which is synthesised by RNA polymerase III). This type of RNA is the most abundant, accounting for over 80% of the total RNA in a dividing cell, and is a major component of ribosomes.

RNA Pol I is a large enzyme of about 590 kDa composed of 14 subunits in yeast, several of which are shared with, or homologous to, subunits of RNA polymerases II and III. It is localised in the nucleolus, where the tandemly repeated ribosomal DNA (rDNA) genes are found.

Transcription of ribosomal RNA

Pol I transcribes a single large precursor, the 47S pre-rRNA in mammals (35S in yeast), which is subsequently processed into the mature 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs that form the core of the small and large ribosomal subunits.

Initiation

Initiation at the rDNA promoter requires the upstream binding factor (UBF) and the selectivity factor SL1, which in humans contains the TATA-binding protein (TBP) and several TBP-associated factors. These factors recruit Pol I, together with its initiation factor Rrn3 (TIF-IA), to form the pre-initiation complex. Because ribosome production consumes a large fraction of cellular energy, Pol I activity is tightly regulated in response to nutrients, growth factors and stress, including through the mTOR pathway; deregulated Pol I transcription is a hallmark of many cancers, and the enzyme is a target of investigational anticancer drugs such as CX-5461.
