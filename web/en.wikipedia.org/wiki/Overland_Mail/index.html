Overland Mail

Overland mail refers to mail services carried overland across North America in the mid-19th century, most famously the Butterfield Overland Mail (1858–1861), a United States mail service under contract to John Butterfield's Overland Mail Company, which carried mail by stagecoach from St. Louis and Memphis to San Francisco along the southern "oxbow route". Other overland services included the Central Overland California & Pikes Peak Express Company — operator of the Pony Express — and the Overland Mail Corporation of the 1860s.

Butterfield Overland Mail

Awarded a six-year federal contract in 1857, Butterfield's line ran some 2,800 miles through Texas, New Mexico and Arizona to California, with about 200 relay stations, 1,200 employees and hundreds of horses and mules. Coaches made the journey in about 25 days, a dramatic improvement over the months-long sea route via Panama. The service was suspended in March 1861 as the American Civil War approached, and the route shifted north to the central corridor.

Later services

During and after the Civil War, Ben Holladay's Overland Mail and Express Company dominated the central route, earning Holladay the nickname "Stagecoach King" before he sold out to Wells Fargo in 1866. The completion of the first transcontinental railroad in 1869 ended long-distance stagecoach mail, though overland stage lines continued locally for decades.

Legacy

The Butterfield route is commemorated by the Butterfield Overland Mail Route National Historic Trail and numerous preserved station sites across the Southwest.
