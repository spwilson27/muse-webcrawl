Semiring

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Semiring — a ring-like structure without the requirement of additive inverses.

In abstract algebra, a semiring is an algebraic structure similar to a ring, but without the requirement that each element must have an additive inverse. The term "rig" is sometimes used — for "ring without negatives". Semirings arise naturally in theoretical computer science, optimization, and tropical geometry, where subtraction is unavailable or meaningless.

Definition

A semiring is a set R equipped with two binary operations, addition (+) and multiplication (·), such that (R, +) is a commutative monoid with identity element 0, (R, ·) is a monoid with identity element 1, multiplication distributes over addition from both sides, and 0 annihilates R under multiplication: 0 · r = r · 0 = 0 for all r. Conventions vary between authors on whether the identity elements and commutativity of addition are required.

Examples

The natural numbers with ordinary addition and multiplication form the prototypical semiring that is not a ring. Other major examples include the Boolean semiring used in logic, the tropical semiring (min-plus algebra), which encodes shortest-path problems, the max-plus algebra of scheduling and discrete-event systems, probability semirings used to weight automata and grammars in natural language processing, and sets of languages under union and concatenation.

Applications

Because many graph algorithms, parsing algorithms, and dynamic programs share the same algebraic skeleton, formulating them over abstract semirings yields a single correctness proof and a family of algorithms at once: shortest paths, widest paths, reliability computations, and Viterbi decoding all emerge by changing the semiring. Semirings also underlie provenance tracking in databases and the algebra of formal power series.
