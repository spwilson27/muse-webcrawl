1973 kidnapping of Kim Dae-jung

Date8 August 1973
LocationHotel Grand Palace, Tokyo, Japan
TargetKim Dae-jung, South Korean opposition politician
PerpetratorsAgents of the Korean Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA)
OutcomeKim released near his home in Seoul, 13 August 1973

On 8 August 1973 the South Korean opposition leader Kim Dae-jung was kidnapped from a hotel in Tokyo by agents of the Korean Central Intelligence Agency (KCIA) acting for the government of President Park Chung-hee. Kim was smuggled out of Japan by sea and, according to his own later testimony, came close to being murdered aboard the boat before being released in Seoul five days later. The affair caused a major diplomatic scandal between South Korea and Japan.

Background

Kim had nearly defeated Park in the 1971 presidential election, campaigning against authoritarianism and for reconciliation with North Korea. After surviving a suspicious truck collision widely believed to have been an assassination attempt, he lived in exile in the United States and Japan, where he organised opposition to Park's Yushin Constitution of 1972, which granted Park sweeping dictatorial powers. On 8 August 1973 Kim was meeting fellow opposition figure Yang Il-dong at the Hotel Grand Palace in Tokyo when unidentified men seized him from his room.

Abduction

Kim was beaten, blindfolded and taken by car and then by boat — the vessel later identified as the Yonggeum-ho — from Osaka across the sea towards Korea. He later recounted that aboard the ship he was tied to a plank weighted for drowning, and believed he was about to be thrown overboard, when the plan was aborted: he attributed his survival to the appearance of an aircraft (reported to have been a United States surveillance plane) tracking the vessel, and the resulting fears in Seoul of American and Japanese detection. Rather than being killed, Kim was taken to Korea and, on 13 August, was released, dumped blindfolded near his home in Seoul with the warning to remain silent.

Aftermath

Japan protested the violation of its sovereignty; the fingerprints of a Korean embassy official, Kim Dong-woon, were found in Kim's hotel room, but South Korea denied government involvement and the affair was papered over by a political settlement between Prime Minister Kakuei Tanaka and Seoul, in which South Korean Premier Kim Jong-pil visited Tokyo and expressed regret. Kim Dae-jung remained under house arrest and persecution for years; he was sentenced to death in 1980 after Park's assassination and the Gwangju Uprising, saved by international — especially American — pressure, and eventually elected President of South Korea in 1997, winning the Nobel Peace Prize in 2000. In 2007, South Korea's National Intelligence Service formally admitted that the KCIA had carried out the kidnapping, confirming that the plot to kill Kim at sea had existed and was abandoned.
