Psalmody

Psalmody is the singing or chanting of psalms in worship, and by extension the body of musical practice, chant formulas and printed collections devoted to the Psalter. It is central to Jewish and Christian liturgy: psalms form the core of the daily prayer of the synagogue and church, the monastic Liturgy of the Hours, and much congregational song.

Forms

Psalms may be sung antiphonally (two choirs alternating verses, framed by an antiphon), responsorially (a refrain sung by the congregation between verses sung by a cantor), or directly (through without refrain). In plainchant, each psalm is sung to one of the eight psalm tones according to its mode, ending with the doxology Gloria Patri.

Metrical psalmody

The Protestant Reformation produced metrical psalmody: vernacular paraphrases of the psalms in verse fitted to syllabic tunes for congregational singing. Landmarks include the Genevan Psalter (1562), associated with Calvin and the tunes of Loys Bourgeois, and the Anglo-Scottish tradition of the Scottish Psalter (1650) and the Bay Psalm Book (1640), the first book printed in British North America. Exclusive psalmody — the restriction of sung worship to psalms — remains the practice of some Reformed churches.

In Catholic and Orthodox practice

In Roman Catholic worship, psalmody structures the Liturgy of the Hours and the responsorial psalm of the Mass. Eastern Orthodox practice centres on the chanting of psalm kathismata and psalm verses interleaved with hymnography in Byzantine and Slavonic chant traditions.
