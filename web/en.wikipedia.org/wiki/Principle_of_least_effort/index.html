Principle of least effort

The principle of least effort is a broad theory that seeks to explain animal and human behaviour: an organism will choose, from the alternatives available, the path that requires the least perceived expenditure of effort. The principle was systematically formulated by the linguist George Kingsley Zipf in his 1949 book Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort.

Applications

Zipf applied the principle to language, predicting statistical regularities such as the tendency for frequent words to be short — related to what is now called Zipf's law. In information science, the principle describes why users prefer easily accessible sources over higher-quality but harder-to-obtain ones; J. H. Shera and others applied it to library use. It also informs interface design, economics, and evolutionary biology, where energy minimisation shapes foraging and movement strategies.

Criticism

Critics note that "effort" is difficult to measure independently of the behaviour it is meant to explain, risking circularity, and that organisms frequently choose effortful paths for exploration, learning, or social reasons. Nevertheless, the principle remains a useful heuristic across disciplines.
