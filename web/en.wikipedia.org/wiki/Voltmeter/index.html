Voltmeter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A voltmeter is an instrument used for measuring electric potential difference (voltage) between two points in an electric circuit. It is connected in parallel with the component or section whose voltage is to be measured. Analogue voltmeters move a pointer across a scale; digital voltmeters give a numerical display and are commonly integrated into multimeters.

Analog voltmeter

A moving-coil galvanometer can be made into a voltmeter by adding a high-value series resistor (a "multiplier"). The combination measures voltage while drawing only a small current: sensitivity is expressed in ohms per volt, with 20,000 Ω/V typical of a good instrument. Because an ideal voltmeter would have infinite resistance, practical voltmeters are designed to present a high input resistance so as not to disturb the circuit under test. Electrostatic voltmeters draw essentially no current and can measure very high voltages.

Digital voltmeter

Digital voltmeters (DVMs) convert the input voltage to a digital value using an analogue-to-digital converter, often of the dual-slope integrating type for accuracy and noise rejection. Typical input resistance is 10 MΩ. Bench DVMs can resolve microvolts; specialised types include true-RMS AC voltmeters and vacuum-tube voltmeters (VTVMs) using amplifier stages for very high input impedance.

Usage

The voltmeter is connected across the two points of interest, observing polarity for DC. Measuring in series is a common error that yields meaningless readings because the meter's high resistance then dominates the circuit.
