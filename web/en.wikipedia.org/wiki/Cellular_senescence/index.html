Cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is a stable state of cell-cycle arrest in which cells remain metabolically active but no longer divide. It was first described by Leonard Hayflick in 1961, who showed that normal human fibroblasts divide only a limited number of times in culture (the Hayflick limit), a limit now known to reflect progressive shortening of telomeres.

Mechanisms

Senescence can be triggered by telomere shortening (replicative senescence), by DNA damage, by strong mitogenic signalling and oncogene activation (oncogene-induced senescence), and by cellular stress. The arrest is enforced chiefly by the tumour-suppressor pathways p53/p21 and p16INK4a/Rb. Senescent cells characteristically enlarge, show increased lysosomal β-galactosidase activity, and secrete a cocktail of inflammatory cytokines, growth factors and proteases known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).

Biological roles

Senescence acts as a barrier to cancer by halting the proliferation of damaged cells, and it plays roles in embryonic development and wound healing. However, senescent cells accumulate with age, and their SASP is thought to drive chronic inflammation and many age-related diseases. Drugs that selectively kill senescent cells, called senolytics, are under investigation.
