Uracil: one of the four nucleobases of RNA

Uracil (symbol U or Ura) is one of the four nucleobases in the nucleic acid RNA that are represented by the letters A, G, C and U. The others are adenine, guanine, and cytosine. Uracil is a demethylated form of thymine: in RNA, uracil base-pairs with adenine and replaces thymine during DNA transcription; in DNA, thymine takes uracil's place.

Properties

Uracil is a planar, unsaturated pyrimidinedione with the molecular formula C4H4N2O2. It was first isolated in 1900 by Alberto Ascoli from yeast nuclein. It occurs naturally and is a normal component of RNA; the body breaks down dietary and cellular RNA into uracil derivatives that are further degraded to beta-amino acids. When bonded to a ribose sugar it forms the nucleoside uridine, and its phosphorylated forms (UMP, UDP, UTP) participate in carbohydrate metabolism and RNA synthesis.

Role and significance

In RNA, uracil pairs with adenine through two hydrogen bonds. The presence of uracil instead of thymine in RNA is thought to reflect an evolutionary economy — uracil is cheaper to produce — and a repair consideration: cytosine spontaneously deaminates to uracil, and because uracil is foreign to DNA, repair enzymes can recognize and remove it, whereas a "proper" DNA base such as thymine (5-methyluracil) makes this discrimination possible. Uracil derivatives also serve as cofactors and in pharmaceuticals: 5-fluorouracil is a long-standing chemotherapy drug that interferes with nucleic acid synthesis. Uracil has also been detected in the Murchison meteorite, a finding relevant to discussions of the prebiotic origins of the RNA world.

Synthesis

Laboratory syntheses of uracil include the condensation of maleic acid with urea in fuming sulfuric acid and routes from cytosine hydrolysis. Industrially it can be produced by condensing urea with propiolic acid derivatives or by fermentative methods.
