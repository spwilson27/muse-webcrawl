The Prehistoric Sites and Decorated Caves of the Vézère Valley is a UNESCO World Heritage Site in the Dordogne department of southwestern France, inscribed in 1979. It comprises 147 prehistoric sites and 25 decorated caves along the Vézère River valley, of which the most famous is the cave of Lascaux.

The Vézère Valley is one of the most important concentrations of Paleolithic art and occupation sites in Europe. Humans have lived in the valley's limestone shelters and caves for roughly 400,000 years, and the region gave its name to several prehistoric cultures and type sites, including Le Moustier (Mousterian) and La Madeleine (Magdalenian). The nearby village of Les Eyzies is home to the National Museum of Prehistory.

Component sites

  • Lascaux — discovered in 1940, famous for its vivid polychrome paintings of animals, dating to about 17,000 years ago. The original cave is closed to the public to preserve the art; replicas (Lascaux II and Lascaux IV) are open to visitors.
  • Font-de-Gaume — a cave with engravings and paintings, including polychrome bison.
  • Les Combarelles — known for hundreds of engravings of animals and abstract signs.
  • Cap Blanc — a rock shelter with monumental sculpted friezes of horses.
  • Cro-Magnon rock shelter — the site where the first recognized remains of anatomically modern humans in Europe (Cro-Magnon) were found in 1868.
  • Rouffignac Cave — known as the "cave of the hundred mammoths" for its many depictions of mammoths.

Significance

The decorated caves of the Vézère preserve some of the earliest and finest surviving works of art made by humans. The paintings and engravings, predominantly of large animals such as horses, aurochs, bison, deer, and mammoths, date mainly from the Upper Paleolithic (roughly 17,000 to 12,000 years ago). Together with the valley's rich stratigraphy of occupation layers, the sites provide an exceptional record of the lives and symbolic expression of early modern humans in Europe.
