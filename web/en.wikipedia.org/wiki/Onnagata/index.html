Onnagata

Japanese女形 / 女方 ("woman-form")
Art formKabuki theatre
OriginEdo period, after bans on actresses (1629 onwards)

Onnagata (女形・女方, "woman-form") are male actors who play female roles in Japanese kabuki theatre. The practice arose after the Tokugawa shogunate banned women from the stage in 1629, and subsequently banned the young-male (wakashū) troupes that replaced them, leading by the mid-17th century to all-female roles being performed by adult men.

Technique

The onnagata's art is not simple impersonation but a highly codified idealisation of femininity: stylised gestures, a raised and softened speaking voice, gliding steps with knees pressed together, and manipulation of the kimono's long sleeves convey the character. The treatises of the great Edo-period onnagata Yoshizawa Ayame (1673–1729) argued that the actor should sustain feminine mannerisms offstage as well, and his writings remain foundational to kabuki acting theory. Specialists train for decades, and leading onnagata — such as Bandō Tamasaburō V, designated a Living National Treasure — are among Japan's most celebrated performers.

Although actresses returned to the Japanese stage in other genres after the Meiji Restoration, kabuki retains the onnagata tradition to this day. Comparable conventions exist in other Asian theatres, such as the dan roles of Chinese opera.
