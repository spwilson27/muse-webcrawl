Continuity of government (COG) is the set of plans and procedures by which a state preserves essential government functions during catastrophic events, such as nuclear attack, natural disaster, or decapitation strikes against leadership.

Overview

COG planning typically covers: succession (who replaces incapacitated leaders), devolution (pre-designated successors operating from alternate sites), reconstitution (restoring normal government afterwards), and the protection of essential records.

United States

US COG policy dates to Cold War executive orders under Presidents Eisenhower and Kennedy. The Presidential Succession Act of 1947 defines the line after the Vice President. During State of the Union addresses, one cabinet member is kept away as the "designated survivor."

Other countries

The United Kingdom's secret plans during the Cold War included regional seats of government; the Soviet Union maintained deep bunker complexes. Many modern states keep publicly acknowledged COG frameworks tied to civil defence.

Criticism

Critics argue that secret COG programmes, such as those revealed around Iran-Contra planning in the 1980s, can bypass constitutional checks. Defenders respond that they are dormant insurance policies, not active governments.
