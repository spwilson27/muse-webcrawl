Transcription into Chinese characters

Transcription into Chinese characters is the use of traditional or simplified Chinese characters to phonetically transcribe the sound of terms and names in foreign languages, without regard to the literal meaning of the characters chosen. Because each character represents a syllable, foreign words are re-syllabified to fit Chinese phonology, and the characters selected are usually common ones with neutral or favourable meanings.

History

The practice is ancient: Buddhist missionaries transcribed Sanskrit terms such as nirvana (涅槃, nièpán) and bodhi (菩提, pútí), and Chinese pilgrims like Xuanzang systematised the rendering of Indian names. Jesuit missionaries in the Ming and Qing courts transcribed European names and places; the practice expanded greatly in the 19th century with diplomacy and trade.

Standardisation

Modern China maintains official transcription standards: Xinhua News Agency reference tables govern the characters used for foreign personal and place names in the press, and standard Mandarin readings are used even in Cantonese- or Hokkien-speaking areas, so transcriptions may not match local pronunciation. Taiwan and Hong Kong maintain their own conventions; familiar examples include 華盛頓 (Huáshèngdùn) for Washington and 倫敦 (Lúndūn) for London.

Meaning-based selection

Brand names often choose auspicious characters rather than pure sound: Coca-Cola is rendered 可口可樂 (kěkǒu kělè, roughly "tasty and enjoyable"). Some transcriptions instead translate meaning, or combine both approaches.
