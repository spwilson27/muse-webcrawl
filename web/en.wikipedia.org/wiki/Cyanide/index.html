Cyanide

A cyanide is a chemical compound that contains the group C≡N. This group, known as the cyano group, consists of a carbon atom triple-bonded to a nitrogen atom. In inorganic cyanides, the group is present as the anion CN−; salts such as sodium cyanide and potassium cyanide are highly toxic. In organic chemistry, compounds containing the −C≡N group are called nitriles.

Chemistry

The cyanide ion is isoelectronic with carbon monoxide and molecular nitrogen and binds strongly to metal ions, forming coordination complexes — a property exploited in the industrial extraction of gold and silver from their ores, where dilute sodium cyanide solution dissolves the precious metals as soluble cyano complexes. Hydrogen cyanide (HCN) is a volatile, weakly acidic liquid boiling at 26 °C, historically known as prussic acid.

Toxicity

Cyanide is a potent, fast-acting poison. It binds to the ferric iron in cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV) of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, preventing cells from using oxygen — a form of chemical asphyxiation or "histotoxic hypoxia". Symptoms can appear within minutes and include headache, dizziness, seizures, loss of consciousness, and cardiac arrest. Antidotes include hydroxocobalamin, sodium thiosulfate, and nitrites, which convert haemoglobin to methaemoglobin that scavenges cyanide. A characteristic bitter-almond odour is detectable by only a portion of the population, a genetically determined trait.

Occurrence and uses

Cyanogenic glycosides occur naturally in many plants, including bitter almonds, apple seeds and cassava, and cyanide must be removed from cassava during food preparation. Industrially, cyanide is used in mining, electroplating, plastics and chemical synthesis, and in the production of nitriles and nylon precursors such as adiponitrile. Hydrogen cyanide was used as the pesticide Zyklon B and has a grim history as a chemical weapon and agent of mass murder.
