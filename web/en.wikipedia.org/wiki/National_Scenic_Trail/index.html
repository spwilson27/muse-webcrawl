National Scenic Trail

A National Scenic Trail is a designation for long-distance trails in the United States created under the National Trails System Act of 1968. National Scenic Trails are extended, primarily non-motorized trails of outstanding scenic quality and natural beauty, managed by federal agencies — principally the National Park Service and the United States Forest Service — in cooperation with states, local governments, and non-profit trail organizations.

Designated trails

There are eleven National Scenic Trails. The first two, authorized in 1968, were the Appalachian Trail (about 2,200 miles from Georgia to Maine) and the Pacific Crest Trail (about 2,650 miles from Mexico to Canada along the Sierra Nevada and Cascade ranges). Later additions include the Continental Divide Trail, the North Country Trail — the longest in the system, stretching some 4,800 miles — the Ice Age Trail, the Arizona Trail, the Florida Trail, the Natchez Trace Trail, the New England Trail, the Pacific Northwest Trail, and the Potomac Heritage Trail.

The "Triple Crown"

Hikers who complete the Appalachian Trail, the Pacific Crest Trail, and the Continental Divide Trail are said to have achieved the "Triple Crown of Hiking", a feat accomplished by only a few hundred people. The National Trails System also includes National Historic Trails, National Recreation Trails, and connecting or side trails.
