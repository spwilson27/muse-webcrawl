Factitious disorder

Patients with factitious disorder may seek repeated hospital care for fabricated illness

Factitious disorder is a mental disorder in which a person, without obvious external incentives, deliberately produces, feigns or exaggerates physical or psychological symptoms of illness in themselves or in another person. The DSM-5 distinguishes factitious disorder imposed on self from factitious disorder imposed on another (formerly "by proxy").

The best-known severe form is Munchausen syndrome, described by Richard Asher in 1951, in which patients travel between hospitals with dramatic, fabricated histories. In factitious disorder imposed on another — often called Munchausen syndrome by proxy — a caregiver, most often a parent, fabricates or induces illness in a dependent, which is regarded as a form of abuse of the victim.

Signs and diagnosis

Clues include symptoms that do not fit known disease patterns, inconsistent histories, wounds that do not heal, evidence of tampering with samples or medical devices, eagerness for invasive procedures, and deterioration that occurs only when the patient believes they are observed. The behaviour differs from malingering, in which symptoms are fabricated for external rewards such as money or avoiding work, and from somatic symptom disorder, in which symptoms are not deliberately produced.

Treatment

There is no well-established specific treatment. Management typically involves limiting unnecessary investigations and procedures, careful documentation, coordinated care through a single team, and psychotherapy where the patient accepts it; confrontation rarely leads to engagement, and many patients leave care once suspected. Prognosis varies; the imposed-on-another form requires immediate protection of the victim.
