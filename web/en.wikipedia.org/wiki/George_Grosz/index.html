George Grosz (born Georg Ehrenfried Groß; 26 July 1893 – 6 July 1959) was a German artist known especially for his savagely satirical drawings and paintings of Berlin life in the Weimar Republic. A leading figure of the Berlin Dada movement and of New Objectivity, he caricatured the war profiteers, militarists and bourgeoisie he blamed for Germany's catastrophes.

Early life and war

Grosz studied art in Dresden and Berlin. Conscripted into the German army in 1914, he served at the front and in a sanitary unit before being discharged after a breakdown; the experience of war and military hospitals turned him into a fierce anti-militarist. In 1916 he anglicised his name from Georg Groß to George Grosz in protest against German nationalism.

Weimar satire

After the 1918 revolution Grosz joined the Communist Party briefly and became central to Berlin Dada. His portfolios of drawings — Ecce Homo (1923), The Pillars of Society (1926) and paintings such as The Funeral (1918) — depicted maimed veterans, corrupt officials and bloated industrialists with corrosive precision. He was repeatedly prosecuted for obscenity, blasphemy and insulting the Reichswehr.

Exile and later life

Grosz emigrated to the United States in January 1933, weeks before Hitler came to power, and taught at the Art Students League of New York; the Nazis stripped him of citizenship and displayed his work in the "Degenerate Art" exhibition. His American work turned toward landscape and allegory. He returned to Berlin in 1959 and died there the same year after a fall.
