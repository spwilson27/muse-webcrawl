Cnidus

The ruins of Cnidus at the tip of the Datça Peninsula in modern Turkey.

Cnidus (Greek: Κνίδος, Knidos) was a Greek city of ancient Caria, situated on the Datça Peninsula in present-day southwestern Turkey. A major port and trading centre, it was a member of the Dorian Hexapolis and is noted for its medical school, its sanctuary of Aphrodite, and the famous statue of Aphrodite of Cnidus by the sculptor Praxiteles.

History

Cnidus was founded by Dorian Greeks, traditionally from Sparta, and formed the Dorian Hexapolis together with Halicarnassus, Cos, and the three cities of Rhodes. Its location commanded the sea routes between the Aegean and the eastern Mediterranean. The city possessed two harbours — a military harbour and a larger commercial one — joined by a canal dug through the narrow neck of land connecting the island site of the old city to the mainland.

The Cnidians were renowned in antiquity for medicine: the Cnidian school of medicine was a rival of the school of Cos associated with Hippocrates. The astronomer and mathematician Eudoxus of Cnidus, the historian Ctesias, and Sostratus, the architect credited with the Pharos of Alexandria, were among its famous citizens. The city flourished under Roman rule before declining in late antiquity; its ruins were excavated in the nineteenth century by Charles Thomas Newton, who shipped the colossal Lion of Cnidus and other sculptures to the British Museum.

Archaeology

The extensive ruins include a theatre, temples, stoas, the agora, and the circular temple traditionally associated with the sanctuary of Aphrodite Euploia, where Praxiteles' celebrated nude statue stood. A monumental marble lion from a nearby tomb is now displayed in the British Museum.
