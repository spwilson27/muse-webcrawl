Stalinism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stalinism is the totalitarian political and economic system implemented by Joseph Stalin in the Soviet Union and, after 1945, in the states of the Eastern Bloc, characterised by a single-party state led by a cult around the leader, centralised command economy, forced collectivisation and industrialisation, and mass terror against real and imagined enemies.

Stalin consolidated power after Lenin's death (1924) by defeating Trotsky, Zinoviev and Bukharin in succession struggles, then launched the First Five-Year Plan (1928) and the collectivisation of agriculture, which destroyed the peasantry as a class and contributed to catastrophic famines, including the Ukrainian famine of 1932–33 (the Holodomor) in which several million died. The Great Terror of 1936–38 executed roughly three-quarters of a million people and sent millions to the Gulag. After victory in the Second World War, Stalinism was exported to Eastern Europe; de-Stalinisation under Khrushchev after 1956 moderated but did not dismantle the system.

Interpretations

Historians debate Stalinism's relationship to Leninism and Marxism — continuation, perversion or distinct phenomenon — and the balance between ideology, modernisation pressures and Stalin's personality. The term is also used, loosely, for rigidly bureaucratic and repressive styles of rule within parties and organisations. In Marxist debates, Trotskyists analysed the USSR as a degenerated workers' state, Maoists defended parts of Stalin's record, and Eurocommunists repudiated the model.
