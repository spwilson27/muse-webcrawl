Exoplanet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Exoplanet
Known count5,500+ confirmed (mid-2020s)
First confirmed1992 (pulsar planets); 1995 (51 Pegasi b, first around a Sun-like star)
Key missionsKepler, TESS, JWST
An exoplanet transit (artist's concept).

An exoplanet is a planet outside the solar system. The first confirmed detections came in 1992 around a pulsar; the first around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b — a 'hot Jupiter' nobody had predicted — followed in 1995 and won the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics. The field then exploded: the Kepler space telescope mission's transit census showed planets are the rule rather than the exception, and the confirmed count passed 5,500 in the mid-2020s.

Most exoplanets are found indirectly: by the wobble (radial velocity) or dimming (transit) they induce in their stars, or by gravitational microlensing; a few hundred have been directly imaged. The catalogue spans lava worlds, ocean candidates, gas giants in decade-long orbits, rogue planets with no star at all, and a prized few rocky planets in habitable zones — Proxima Centauri b, the TRAPPIST-1 system, and the TESS discoveries nearest home.

Atmospheres

The current frontier is spectroscopy: the James Webb Space Telescope reads the atmospheres of transiting planets, detecting water, carbon dioxide, methane and — contentiously — possible biosignature gases, while the next flagship observatories are designed to image an Earth twin directly. Exoplanet science is the long game that Kepler began: from three laws of motion to a census of worlds.
