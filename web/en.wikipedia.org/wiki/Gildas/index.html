Gildas

Gildas (c. 500 – c. 570), also known as Gildas the Wise (Latin: Gildas Sapiens), was a 6th-century British monk and the author of De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae ("On the Ruin and Conquest of Britain"), the principal — and almost the only — contemporary written source for the history of Britain in the century after the end of Roman rule. Written as a fiery sermon denouncing the sins of British kings and clergy, the work incidentally preserves crucial evidence for the Anglo-Saxon settlement of Britain and the age associated with the resistance leader Ambrosius Aurelianus.

Life

Details of Gildas's life come mainly from two much later medieval Lives (one by a monk of Rhuys in Brittany, one by Caradoc of Llancarfan), whose reliability is doubtful. Tradition places his birth in the kingdom of Alt Clut (Strathclyde) in the year of the Battle of Mount Badon, his education in Wales under St Illtud, missionary activity in Ireland, and his death at the abbey of Saint-Gildas-de-Rhuys in Brittany, which he is said to have founded. His genuine writings include the De Excidio, a penitential, and fragments of letters on monastic discipline.

De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae

The work, usually dated to the 530s–540s, is not a history in intent but a jeremiad modelled on Old Testament prophets. Its first, historical, part sketches Britain from the Roman conquest through the withdrawal of Roman protection, the Britons' appeal to Aëtius ("the Groans of the Britons"), the invitation of Saxon mercenaries by a "proud tyrant" (later identified as Vortigern), the Saxon revolt, and the British recovery under Ambrosius Aurelianus culminating in the siege of Mount Badon, after which, Gildas says, an uneasy peace lasted into his own day. The rest denounces five contemporary British kings — Constantine of Dumnonia, Aurelius Caninus, Vortiporius of Dyfed, Cuneglasus and Maglocunus (Maelgwn of Gwynedd) — and a corrupt clergy. Gildas never names Arthur, a silence that has fuelled endless debate about the historical Arthur.

Legacy

Bede relied heavily on Gildas for his account of the 5th century, transmitting the narrative framework of invitation, revolt and ruin into the later historical tradition used by the Historia Brittonum and Geoffrey of Monmouth. Despite his rhetorical exaggerations, Gildas remains the essential primary source for sub-Roman Britain.
