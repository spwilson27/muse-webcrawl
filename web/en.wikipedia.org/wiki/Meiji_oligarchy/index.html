The Meiji oligarchy was the name given to the small group of relatively young samurai leaders who directed the Meiji Restoration of 1868 and dominated the government of the Empire of Japan during the first decades of the Meiji period (1868–1912). Drawn mainly from the domains of Satsuma and Chōshū, they dismantled the Tokugawa order, abolished the feudal domains, and drove Japan's rapid programme of modernisation and Westernisation.

Composition

The core of the oligarchy consisted of men from the southwestern domains that had overthrown the Tokugawa shogunate: Saigō Takamori, Ōkubo Toshimichi and Kuroda Kiyotaka from Satsuma; Kido Takayoshi, Itō Hirobumi, Yamagata Aritomo and Inoue Kaoru from Chōshū. Officials from Tosa and Hizen, and court nobles such as Iwakura Tomomi and Sanjō Sanetomi, were also important. Most were in their thirties or forties in 1868 and had been exposed to Western learning through domain schools and, in some cases, travel abroad.

Rule

Acting in the name of the young Emperor Meiji, the oligarchs issued the Charter Oath (1868), abolished the han system (1871), introduced conscription and a land-tax reform (1873), and sent the Iwakura Mission around the world (1871–1873) to study Western institutions. The group survived internal crises — Saigō's resignation in 1873 over the Korea question and his death leading the Satsuma Rebellion (1877), the assassination of Ōkubo in 1878 — and the Political Crisis of 1881, after which Itō Hirobumi emerged as the dominant figure, drafting the Meiji Constitution promulgated in 1889.

Genrō

As the Meiji period progressed, the surviving oligarchs were recognised as genrō ("elder statesmen"), an extraconstitutional body whose members advised the emperor, most importantly in selecting prime ministers. Itō and Yamagata remained the pre-eminent genrō until their deaths in 1909 and 1922 respectively; Saionji Kinmochi, the last surviving genrō, died in 1940.

Criticism

The oligarchy has been criticised as the "Satchō clique" (hanbatsu) — a narrow, domain-based monopoly of power that favoured appointees from Satsuma and Chōshū in the cabinet, bureaucracy and armed forces, and which provoked demands for representative government in the Freedom and People's Rights Movement of the 1870s and 1880s.
