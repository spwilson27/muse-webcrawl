The George Cross (GC) is the highest award bestowed by the British government for non-operational gallantry, or gallantry not in the presence of an enemy. Instituted by King George VI in 1940, during the height of the Blitz, it ranks second in the order of wear, immediately after the Victoria Cross, and is its equal in prestige for acts of civilian and non-combat bravery.

The George Cross, instituted in 1940.

Institution and design

George VI created the award by royal warrant on 24 September 1940 to recognise the courage of civilians and service personnel during the Blitz. The silver cross bears an image of Saint George slaying the dragon, encircled by the inscription "For Gallantry", with the royal cypher at the ends of the arms; it hangs from a dark blue ribbon. Holders are entitled to the post-nominal letters GC and receive an annuity.

Recipients

Fewer than 420 George Crosses have been awarded, about half posthumously. Uniquely, the Cross has been awarded collectively three times: to the island of Malta in 1942 for the gallantry of its people during the siege (the cross is depicted on Malta's flag), to the Royal Ulster Constabulary in 1999, and to the National Health Service in 2021 for its work during the COVID-19 pandemic. Earlier Empire Gallantry Medals and certain other awards were exchanged for the George Cross after its institution. Many recipients earned it for bomb disposal, espionage, and rescue work.
