Substellar object

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Substellar object
Mass rangeBelow the hydrogen-burning limit (~0.075–0.08 solar masses)
ExamplesBrown dwarfs, sub-brown dwarfs, rogue planets, gas-giant planets

A substellar object, sometimes called a substar, is an astronomical object the mass of which is smaller than the smallest mass at which hydrogen fusion can be sustained (approximately 0.08 solar masses). This definition includes brown dwarfs and gas giant planets, but also excludes objects smaller than about 13 Jupiter masses that may fuse deuterium—the boundary often used to distinguish planets from brown dwarfs is itself debated.

Substellar objects do not have stable luminosity from hydrogen fusion; instead they radiate away the heat of their formation and, in the case of brown dwarfs, energy from deuterium and lithium fusion early in their lives. They cool and fade over billions of years, passing through spectral types M, L, T and Y as they do so.

Categories

  • Brown dwarfs – roughly 13 to 75–80 Jupiter masses; fuse deuterium (and, above ~65 Jupiter masses, lithium) but never sustain hydrogen fusion. The first confirmed, Gliese 229B and Teide 1, were announced in 1995.
  • Sub-brown dwarfs (planetary-mass objects) – below the deuterium-burning limit; they form like stars from collapsing gas clouds but have planet-like masses.
  • Planets – substellar objects formed in protoplanetary discs around stars; giant planets have masses overlapping the lowest-mass brown dwarfs, making formation history rather than mass the essential distinction.
  • Rogue planets – planetary-mass objects free-floating in interstellar space, either formed alone or ejected from systems.

Observation

Because they are faint and emit mostly in the infrared, substellar objects are found by infrared surveys such as 2MASS and WISE. The coldest known brown dwarfs (Y dwarfs) have temperatures below 300 K—comparable to a warm day—and are among the Sun's nearest neighbours: the Luhman 16 system, a pair of brown dwarfs, lies only 6.5 light-years away.
