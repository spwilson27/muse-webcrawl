Lava tube

A lava tube is a natural conduit formed by flowing lava from a volcanic vent that moves beneath the hardened surface of a lava flow. When the supply of lava stops, the tube can drain, leaving a long hollow tunnel known as a lava cave.

Formation

Lava tubes form in fluid, low-viscosity basaltic flows, typically of the pāhoehoe type. As lava advances, its surface cools and crusts over while the interior remains molten and continues to flow. The crust insulates the lava, allowing it to travel great distances. Two main mechanisms are recognised: roofing over of a surface channel, and the progressive extension of a flow behind a solidifying front.

Features and examples

Drained tubes may contain lava stalactites, flow lines, and benches marking former flow levels. Openings called skylights form where the roof collapses. Notable examples include Kazumura Cave in Hawaiʻi, one of the longest known lava tubes in the world, and caves in Iceland, the Canary Islands, Australia, and the western United States. Similar structures have been identified on the Moon and Mars, where they are of interest as potential sheltered habitats.
