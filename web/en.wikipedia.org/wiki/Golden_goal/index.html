Golden goal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The golden goal was a rule used in association football, field hockey, ice hockey and other sports to decide the winner of a match in a knockout tournament where the scores were level at the end of normal time. Under the rule, during extra time the first team to score wins the match immediately — a form of sudden death.

History

FIFA introduced the golden goal in 1993 in an effort to encourage attacking play during extra time and to reduce the number of matches decided by penalty shoot-outs. It was first used at a World Cup in 1998, when Laurent Blanc's golden goal for France against Paraguay was the first at that level. David Trezeguet's golden goal decided the Euro 2000 final for France against Italy, and Oliver Bierhoff had scored the first golden goal in a major final at Euro 1996.

Criticism and abolition

Contrary to its intention, the rule often made teams more cautious, fearing that a single mistake would end the match. A short-lived variant, the "silver goal" — where the team leading at half-time of extra time won — was used at Euro 2004. The IFAB abolished both rules in 2004, returning knock-out matches to full extra time followed by penalties. Similar sudden-death overtime remains standard in the NHL and the NFL.
