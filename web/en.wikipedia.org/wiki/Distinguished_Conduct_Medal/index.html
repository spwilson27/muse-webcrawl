Distinguished Conduct Medal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Distinguished Conduct Medal (DCM) was a British military decoration awarded to non-commissioned officers and other ranks of the British Army and, historically, of other Commonwealth forces, for distinguished conduct in the field. Instituted by Queen Victoria in 1854 during the Crimean War, it was the oldest British award for gallantry by other ranks and ranked second only to the Victoria Cross for soldiers; it was often described as the other ranks' equivalent of the Distinguished Service Order.

History

The medal was established by Royal Warrant on 4 December 1854 to recognise acts of conspicuous gallantry by non-commissioned officers and privates for which no medal then existed. Recipients were entitled to the post-nominal letters DCM and, at various times, to a gratuity or annuity. Bars could be awarded for further acts of gallantry.

Design

The medal is silver and circular. The obverse bears the effigy of the reigning monarch; the reverse bears the inscription "For Distinguished Conduct in the Field" within a laurel wreath, surmounted by the Royal Crest. The ribbon is crimson with a dark blue central stripe. The recipient's name, rank, and unit are impressed or engraved on the rim.

Awards and discontinuation

More than 25,000 DCMs were awarded during the First World War alone. Following the 1993 review of the British honours system, which removed distinctions of rank in gallantry awards, the DCM was discontinued and replaced by the Conspicuous Gallantry Cross, open to all ranks.
