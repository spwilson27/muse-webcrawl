Novena

A novena (from Latin: novem, "nine") is an ancient tradition of devotional praying in Christianity, consisting of private or public prayers repeated for nine successive days or weeks. The nine days between the Feast of the Ascension and Pentecost, when the disciples gathered in the upper room and devoted themselves to prayer, is often considered the first novena.

History

The practice is said to derive from the period of prayer of the Apostles and the Virgin Mary in the Cenacle of Jerusalem between the Ascension of Jesus and the descent of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost. In the Roman Catholic tradition, novenas became especially popular during the Middle Ages and the Counter-Reformation, and indulgences were attached to many of them. Novenas are most often addressed to the Virgin Mary or to a particular saint, asking for intercession.

Types

Novenas fall into four broad categories: novenas of mourning, of preparation, of petition and of indulgence. Novenas of petition, the most common, ask God or a saint for a particular favour, such as healing, employment or the resolution of a difficulty. Well-known examples include the novena to Saint Jude, patron of hopeless causes; the novena to Our Lady of Perpetual Help; and the Divine Mercy novena, prayed beginning on Good Friday.

Practice

A novena may be prayed at home, in church, or in a group, often using printed booklets, pamphlets or prayer cards that prescribe a set prayer for each of the nine days. Some are prayed at particular times of year, such as the Christmas novena (the O Antiphons from 17 to 23 December), while others are begun whenever a need arises. Novenas are also practised in the Eastern Orthodox, Anglican and Lutheran traditions.
