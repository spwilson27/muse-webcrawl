Bicycle drivetrain systems

Bicycle drivetrain systems transmit power from the rider's legs to the drive wheel of a bicycle. Most systems use a chain drive, but alternatives include belt drives, shaft drives, and direct drive mechanisms.

Overview

A conventional drivetrain consists of pedals, cranks, chainrings (the crankset), a chain, rear sprockets (the cassette or freewheel), and usually front and rear derailleurs that move the chain between gears. Gear ratios are determined by the relative sizes of the chainring and sprocket engaged. Shifters on the handlebars control the derailleurs via cables or electronic signals.

Types

Hub gears enclose the gearing inside the rear hub and are common on city bikes. Single-speed and fixed-gear bicycles dispense with multiple gears entirely. Belt drives using toothed carbon belts are quieter and cleaner than chains but require a splittable frame. Shaft drives, used historically and on some modern city bikes, transmit power through bevel gears. Human power is converted with an efficiency that depends on gear selection and cadence, typically favouring 80–100 rpm for sustained effort.

Maintenance

Chains wear and stretch with use and require lubrication and periodic replacement to avoid damaging chainrings and cassettes.
