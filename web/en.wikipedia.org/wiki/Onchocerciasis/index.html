Onchocerciasis, also known as river blindness, is a parasitic disease caused by infection with the filarial worm Onchocerca volvulus. It is transmitted through repeated bites by black flies of the genus Simulium, which breed near fast-flowing rivers and streams. It is the second-most common cause of blindness due to infection, after trachoma.

Signs and symptoms

Adult worms live in fibrous nodules under the skin and produce millions of microscopic larvae (microfilariae) that migrate through the skin and eyes. Most symptoms result from the body's inflammatory response to dying microfilariae: intense itching, dermatitis with depigmentation ("leopard skin"), skin thickening, and, when larvae invade the eye, inflammation that can progress to irreversible blindness. Heavy infection is also associated with epilepsy in some regions ("nodding syndrome" is under investigation for links to the parasite).

Distribution and control

More than 99% of infections occur in sub-Saharan Africa; smaller foci historically existed in Yemen and in Latin America. Prevention relies on mass drug administration with ivermectin, donated by Merck under the Mectizan programme since 1987, which kills microfilariae and, given once or twice yearly for many years, interrupts transmission. The endosymbiotic Wolbachia bacteria the worms depend on can be targeted with doxycycline, which sterilises and shortens the life of adult worms. Several Latin American countries have been verified by the WHO as having eliminated transmission, and a number of African foci have also achieved elimination.

History

The parasite was described in the 19th century in West Africa; the role of black flies was established in the 1920s by Donald Blacklock in Sierra Leone. The disease emptied fertile river valleys of people, with major agricultural and social consequences that control programmes have gradually reversed.
