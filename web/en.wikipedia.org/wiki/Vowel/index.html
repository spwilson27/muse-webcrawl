A vowel is a speech sound pronounced with an open vocal tract so that air flows relatively unobstructed from the lungs through the mouth. Vowels are distinguished by the position of the tongue (high or low, front or back), the shape of the lips (rounded or unrounded), and features such as tenseness, length, and nasalization. With consonants, vowels are one of the two principal classes of speech sounds, and together they form the syllables of spoken language — nearly every syllable contains exactly one vowel as its nucleus.

Examples of vowels in English include the sounds in beet [iː], bat [æ], and boot [uː]. The word vowel derives from the Latin vocalis, meaning "vocal" or "sounding", via Old French vouel.

Definition and articulation

The traditional articulatory definition of a vowel is a sound produced without the obstruction of airflow that characterises consonants. Vowels are typically voiced — the vocal folds vibrate during their production — though voiceless vowels occur in some languages, such as Japanese and French under certain conditions.

Three parameters are used to describe vowel quality:

  • Height: the vertical position of the tongue — close (high), mid, or open (low).
  • Backness: the horizontal position of the tongue — front, central, or back.
  • Roundedness: whether the lips are rounded during articulation.

These parameters give rise to the vowel quadrilateral, a schematic representation of the space within which vowel sounds can be produced. The cardinal vowels, defined by Daniel Jones, provide reference points for this space.

Acoustics

Acoustically, vowels are characterised by their formants — resonant frequencies of the vocal tract. The first formant (F1) correlates inversely with vowel height, while the second formant (F2) correlates with vowel backness. Formant analysis underlies modern speech recognition and synthesis technologies.

Vowels in writing

In alphabetic writing systems, vowels are typically represented by dedicated letters: in the Latin alphabet, a, e, i, o, u (and sometimes y and w). Abjads such as Arabic and Hebrew historically omit most vowels, while abugidas such as Devanagari mark vowels with diacritics attached to consonant letters. Some writing systems, notably Korean Hangul, give vowels equal graphic standing with consonants.
