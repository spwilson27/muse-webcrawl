Book of Leviticus

Leviticus
Hebrew name: Vayikra ("And He called")
Position: Third book of the Torah and Old Testament
Chapters: 27
Contents: Priestly law, sacrifice, purity, holiness code

The Book of Leviticus (from Greek Leuitikon, "relating to the Levites") is the third book of the Torah (the Pentateuch) and of the Old Testament. Its Hebrew name, Vayikra, is drawn from its first word, "And He called." The book consists almost entirely of legal material spoken by God to Moses on Mount Sinai concerning sacrifice, priestly duties, ritual purity, and moral conduct.

Contents

The first sixteen chapters concern the sacrificial system (burnt, grain, peace, sin, and guilt offerings), the consecration of Aaron and his sons as priests, the purity laws distinguishing clean and unclean foods, childbirth, skin diseases, and bodily discharges, and the ritual of the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), including the scapegoat sent to Azazel. Chapters 17–26 form the so-called Holiness Code, beginning "You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy," covering sexual ethics, neighbourly duties (including "love your neighbour as yourself"), festivals, sabbatical and jubilee years, and blessings and curses; a final chapter deals with vows and tithes.

Composition and interpretation

Most critical scholars attribute Leviticus largely to priestly sources composed or edited during and after the Babylonian exile (6th–5th centuries BCE), though it incorporates older ritual traditions. In Jewish tradition the book is central to the study of Torah law; in Christian interpretation its sacrificial system is often read typologically, while its ethical commandments remain influential.
