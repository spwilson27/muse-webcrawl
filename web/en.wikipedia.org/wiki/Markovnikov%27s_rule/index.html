Markovnikov's rule

Markovnikov's rule is an empirical rule in organic chemistry, formulated by Russian chemist Vladimir Markovnikov in 1870, that predicts the regiochemistry of electrophilic addition reactions to unsymmetrical alkenes. In modern terms it states that, in the addition of a protic acid HX to an alkene, the hydrogen atom attaches to the carbon that already bears the greater number of hydrogen atoms, while the halide (X) attaches to the more substituted carbon — often summarised as "the rich get richer".

Explanation

The rule follows from carbocation stability. Protonation of the double bond can place the positive charge on either carbon; the pathway through the more substituted, more stable carbocation (tertiary > secondary > primary) is faster, so the nucleophile ultimately bonds to that carbon.

Anti-Markovnikov additions

Certain conditions give the opposite regiochemistry. The classic example is the peroxide-initiated addition of HBr, which proceeds by a free-radical mechanism (the Kharasch effect); hydroboration–oxidation likewise delivers the anti-Markovnikov alcohol. These exceptions are described as anti-Markovnikov behaviour.

History

Markovnikov presented the rule in his 1869 doctoral work and an 1870 paper, based on observations of hydrohalic acid additions. It was initially little noticed, in part because it appeared in Russian, but it became a standard predictive tool of organic synthesis.
