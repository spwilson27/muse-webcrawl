Gene family

FieldMolecular biology, genomics
OriginGene duplication of an ancestral gene
ExamplesGlobin family, Hox genes, olfactory receptors

A gene family is a set of several similar genes, formed by duplication of a single original gene, that generally share biochemical functions and significant sequence similarity. Members of a gene family may be clustered together on the same chromosome or dispersed throughout the genome.

Formation and evolution

Gene families expand through duplication events — unequal crossing over, retrotransposition, or whole-genome duplication. After duplication, copies may accumulate mutations and diverge: one copy may retain the original function while another acquires a new function (neofunctionalisation), the copies may partition the ancestral functions (subfunctionalisation), or a copy may degenerate into a pseudogene. Large families, called superfamilies, group together families whose relationship is more distant.

Examples

Well-known gene families include the globin genes, whose members (such as the alpha- and beta-globins and myoglobin) are expressed at different developmental stages; the Hox genes controlling body plans in animals; the immunoglobulin superfamily; and the olfactory receptor genes, the largest gene family in many mammalian genomes, numbering in the hundreds to over a thousand in some species.
