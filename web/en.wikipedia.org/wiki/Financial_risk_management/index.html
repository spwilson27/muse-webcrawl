Financial risk management is the practice of protecting economic value in a firm by identifying sources of financial risk, measuring exposure to them, and managing that exposure using financial instruments and organisational controls. It is commonly carried out by specialised risk managers, treasury departments, and quantitative analysts ("quants").

Types of financial risk

  • Market risk — losses from movements in prices: interest rates, exchange rates, equity and commodity prices.
  • Credit risk — losses when a borrower or counterparty fails to meet obligations.
  • Liquidity risk — inability to meet payments or to trade assets without large price concessions.
  • Operational risk — losses from failed processes, people, systems, or external events, including fraud and model error.

Measurement

Value at risk (VaR) estimates the maximum loss over a horizon at a given confidence level (e.g., a one-day 99% VaR); expected shortfall averages losses beyond the VaR threshold and is favoured in newer regulation. Firms supplement statistical measures with scenario analysis and stress tests, which probe performance under severe but plausible shocks, and with sensitivity measures ("the Greeks") for derivatives portfolios.

Management techniques

Risks may be avoided, retained, reduced, or transferred. Hedging with futures, options, and swaps offsets adverse price moves; diversification reduces concentration; limits and collateral control counterparty exposure; insurance transfers specified risks. The goal is rarely to eliminate risk — returns are compensation for bearing it — but to keep it within an institution's stated risk appetite.

Regulation and the 2008 crisis

The 2007–2008 financial crisis exposed failures of risk models that underestimated correlations and tail dependence, prompting reforms: the Basel III framework raised capital and liquidity requirements, central clearing was mandated for standardised derivatives, and supervisory stress testing became routine. Critics continue to debate the limits of quantitative risk measurement, warning against over-reliance on models whose assumptions break down precisely in crises.
