Securitization is the financial practice of pooling various types of contractual debt — such as mortgages, auto loans, credit card receivables, or student loans — and selling their related cash flows to third-party investors as securities. The securities created are generically called asset-backed securities (ABS); those backed by mortgages are mortgage-backed securities (MBS).

How it works

In a typical transaction, an originator (such as a bank) sells a pool of loans to a special-purpose vehicle (SPV), a legally separate, bankruptcy-remote entity. The SPV issues securities in tranches with different levels of seniority: senior tranches are paid first and carry the highest credit ratings and lowest yields, while junior and equity tranches absorb losses first and earn higher returns. A servicer collects payments from borrowers and passes them through the structure. Credit ratings from agencies such as Moody's, S&P, and Fitch play a central role in marketing the securities.

Economics and risks

Securitization lets originators move loans off their balance sheets, freeing capital to make new loans, diversify funding, and transfer credit risk to investors seeking yield. Modern securitization began in the United States in the 1970s with agency mortgage pass-throughs from Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac, and expanded to almost every asset class with predictable cash flows. However, the "originate-to-distribute" model weakened underwriting incentives, since originators no longer bore default risk. Mis-rated structured products backed by subprime mortgages — especially MBS and CDOs — were at the centre of the 2007–2008 financial crisis.

Post-crisis regulation

Reforms after the crisis included risk-retention ("skin in the game") rules requiring sponsors to keep a share of the credit risk, enhanced disclosure, and, in Europe, the "simple, transparent and standardised" (STS) framework intended to revive high-quality securitization for funding the real economy.
