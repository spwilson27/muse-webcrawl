The George Medal (GM) is a decoration of the United Kingdom and Commonwealth, instituted by King George VI's namesake descendant, George VI, by royal warrant on 24 September 1940. It is awarded for gallantry "not in the face of the enemy" that does not reach the standard of the George Cross, and it ranks second in the British honours system's order of precedence for bravery awards.

History

The medal was created during the Blitz, when the king wished to recognise the many acts of civilian bravery during German bombing that the existing honours did not cover. It was instituted together with the George Cross, which was placed immediately below the Victoria Cross. Awards were made extensively to civilians, air-raid wardens, bomb disposal personnel and members of the armed forces for gallantry not in direct contact with the enemy; recipients are entitled to the post-nominal letters GM.

Description

The medal is a circular silver medal. The obverse bears the crowned effigy of the reigning monarch; the reverse depicts Saint George slaying the dragon, with the inscription "The George Medal". It is suspended from a crimson ribbon shot through with five narrow blue stripes, said to represent the blue riband of the Order of the Garter. Bars may be awarded for further acts of gallantry.

Notable recipients

Among thousands of recipients were wartime bomb disposal officers and civilian rescuers; notable peacetime recipients include police officers and members of the public recognised for life-saving bravery. In total more than 2,000 George Medals have been awarded.
