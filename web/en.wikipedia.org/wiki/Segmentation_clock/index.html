Segmentation clock

The segmentation clock is a molecular oscillator that controls the periodic formation of somites — the paired blocks of embryonic tissue that give rise to the vertebrae, ribs, and associated musculature — during vertebrate development. In the "clock and wavefront" model, proposed by Jonathan Cooke and Erik Christopher Zeeman in 1976, cells of the presomitic mesoderm oscillate in their gene expression, and a moving wavefront of maturation determines where and when each oscillation cycle is converted into a new somite boundary.

Molecular mechanism

The oscillator is driven by negative feedback loops in the Notch, Wnt, and FGF signalling pathways. In mice, the transcription factor Hes7 represses its own transcription and that of the Notch modulator Lunatic fringe, generating oscillations with a period of about two hours — matching the rate at which somites form. The period differs between species: roughly 30 minutes in zebrafish, 90 minutes in chick, and about five hours in humans, correlating with overall developmental tempo and linked to the biochemical kinetics of the feedback loop, including protein degradation rates.

Evidence and significance

Oscillatory expression of genes such as c-hairy1 in the chick presomitic mesoderm was demonstrated in 1997 by Isabel Palmeirim and colleagues, providing direct evidence for the clock. Mutations in clock components cause vertebral malformations: in humans, mutations in DLL3, HES7, LFNG, and related genes cause spondylocostal dysostosis. In 2020, researchers recapitulated the human segmentation clock in vitro using induced pluripotent stem cells, confirming its longer period and dependence on protein turnover.
