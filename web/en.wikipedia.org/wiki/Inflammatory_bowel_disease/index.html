Inflammatory bowel disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a group of inflammatory conditions of the colon and small intestine, occurring as a result of an inappropriate immune response to the gut microbiota in genetically susceptible individuals. The two main types are Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.

Types

Crohn's disease can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract from mouth to anus, characteristically in a patchy ("skip lesion") distribution with transmural inflammation that can cause strictures, fistulas and abscesses. Ulcerative colitis is limited to the colon and rectum, with continuous superficial inflammation of the mucosa; it typically presents with bloody diarrhoea and urgency. Cases that cannot be clearly classified are termed indeterminate colitis.

Symptoms and complications

Common symptoms include abdominal pain, diarrhoea (often bloody), weight loss, fatigue and fever. Extraintestinal manifestations affect the joints, skin, eyes and liver (including primary sclerosing cholangitis). Long-standing colitis increases the risk of colorectal cancer.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on colonoscopy with biopsy, imaging and laboratory markers such as faecal calprotectin. Treatment aims to induce and maintain remission using aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunomodulators (thiopurines, methotrexate), and biologic therapies such as anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab), vedolizumab and ustekinumab, as well as small molecules such as JAK inhibitors. Surgery — colectomy in ulcerative colitis, or resection in Crohn's — remains necessary for some patients.

Epidemiology

IBD is most common in Europe and North America but is rising rapidly in newly industrialised countries. Several million people are affected worldwide.
