Beaux-Arts architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Palais Garnier, Paris (1861–1875), Charles Garnier

Beaux-Arts architecture (French: [bozaʁ]) was the academic architectural style taught at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, particularly from the 1830s to the end of the 19th century. Drawing upon principles of French neoclassicism, it incorporated Renaissance and Baroque elements and used modern materials such as iron and glass. It was widely used for grand public buildings — railway stations, museums, libraries, courthouses and opera houses — in France and the United States, where it flourished between about 1880 and 1920.

Characteristics

Beaux-Arts buildings are typically massive and symmetrical, with a raised first storey, rusticated stonework at ground level, a clear hierarchy of spaces expressed in plan, and rich sculptural decoration concentrated around entrances and the roofline. Facades use classical orders, arched windows, paired columns, balustrades and prominent domes or pavilions; interiors feature grand staircases, axial sequences of decorated halls and integrated painting and sculpture. In the United States the style was associated with the City Beautiful movement and the "White City" of the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 in Chicago.

History

The style emerged from the teaching of the Paris École, whose Prix de Rome system shaped generations of architects. Charles Garnier's Palais Garnier (opened 1875) and the Grand Palais (1900) became canonical examples. American architects trained in Paris — among them Richard Morris Hunt, Charles Follen McKim and Daniel Burnham of McKim, Mead & White — carried the style home, producing landmarks such as the New York Public Library, Grand Central Terminal, the Boston Public Library and, in Washington, D.C., Union Station. In Britain the idiom appears in "Edwardian Baroque" civic buildings. The style fell from favour with the rise of modernism after the First World War, though Beaux-Arts training influenced architectural education well into the 20th century.
