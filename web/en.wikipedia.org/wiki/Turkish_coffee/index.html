Turkish coffee (Turkish: Türk kahvesi) is a style of coffee prepared in a cezve using very finely ground coffee beans without filtering. It is commonly consumed in the Middle East, North Africa, the Caucasus, the Balkans, and in former Ottoman territories. Turkish coffee culture and tradition were inscribed in 2013 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Preparation

Turkish coffee is made by bringing powdered coffee beans and water, and usually sugar, to a boil in a special pot called a cezve in Turkey and an ibrik elsewhere. As soon as the mixture begins to froth, and before it boils over, it is taken off the heat; it may be briefly reheated twice more to increase the amount of foam. The coffee is traditionally served in a small porcelain cup called a kahve fincanı. The grounds left after drinking Turkish coffee are sometimes used for fortune-telling.

History

Coffee drinking spread through the Islamic world in the 15th and 16th centuries. The world's first coffeehouses are believed to have opened in Constantinople (Istanbul) in the mid-16th century, including the famous Kiva Han in 1475 by some accounts. In 1543, Özdemir Pasha, the Ottoman governor of Yemen, is said to have introduced coffee to the court of Suleiman the Magnificent. Turkish coffee became an integral part of Turkish culture; the Turkish word for breakfast, kahvaltı, literally means "before coffee".

Names and variants

The same method of preparation is found under different names across the region: Greek coffee, Armenian coffee, Arabic coffee (a related but distinct preparation), Bosnian coffee, and Cypriot coffee, among others. The name "Turkish coffee" was historically used because of its Ottoman origins; after political tensions, some countries adopted alternative names for the identical preparation.
