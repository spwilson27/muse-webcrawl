Divided highway

A divided highway is a class of highway with two separated roadways for traffic moving in opposite directions, divided by a median strip (central reservation in British terminology) that may be a paved area, a grass strip, or a physical barrier such as a concrete Jersey barrier, guardrail, or cable barrier. The separation reduces the risk of head-on collisions, generally regarded as the most dangerous type of crash on undivided roads.

Characteristics

Divided highways range from full freeways (motorways), in which access is completely controlled and all crossings are grade-separated, to arterial roads with at-grade intersections, driveways, and traffic signals. A divided highway with at least partial control of access is sometimes called a dual carriageway in British usage, where the term refers to the two separate carriageways irrespective of access control. Divided highways are the standard design for the United States Interstate Highway System and for high-capacity rural arterials in many countries.

Median types

Medians may be wide depressed grass medians common on rural freeways, narrow raised medians on urban arterials, or barrier medians where right-of-way is constrained. Wide medians provide recovery space for errant vehicles and room for future lanes, while barrier medians prevent cross-median crashes where traffic volumes and speeds are high. Median openings allow turning movements on non-freeway divided highways, and designs such as the Michigan left and the restricted crossing U-turn (RCUT) intersection manage those movements to improve safety.
