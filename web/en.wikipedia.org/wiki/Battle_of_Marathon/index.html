Battle of Marathon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Soros, the burial mound of the Athenian dead on the Marathon plain]

The Battle of Marathon was fought on the plain of Marathon in September 490 BC between the citizens of Athens, aided by Plataea, and a Persian expeditionary force commanded by Datis and Artaphernes, sent by Darius I to punish Athens and Eretria for supporting the Ionian Revolt. The outnumbered Athenians — some 10,000 hoplites — charged the Persian line at a run, enveloped its stronger wings, and drove it to its ships with heavy loss (Herodotus gives 6,400 Persian dead against 192 Athenians). The victory became a founding myth of Athenian democracy and gave its name, via the legend of a runner to Athens, to the modern marathon race.

Course

The Persian force (perhaps 20,000–30,000 infantry with cavalry, figures are guesswork) landed in Marathon bay, a day's march from Athens, where the plain suited its cavalry. For days the armies faced off; then — possibly because the Persian cavalry had re-embarked — Miltiades persuaded the Athenian council of generals to attack. The Athenians advanced at a trot or run over roughly a mile, their thinned centre giving ground while the reinforced wings routed the Persian flanks and closed inward. The Persians fled to their ships, losing seven vessels; the fleet then rounded Cape Sunium hoping to find Athens undefended, but the army force-marched home in time to face it down.

Legacy

The 192 dead were buried on the field under the Soros mound, still visible today, and honoured above all other Athenian war dead. Marathon proved hoplite infantry could defeat the empire's troops and emboldened Athens for the greater invasion to come. The marathon race itself rests on a conflated legend: Herodotus records the runner Pheidippides' 240-km run to Sparta before the battle; the fatal run from Marathon to Athens appears only in later authors (Plutarch, Lucian) — yet it inspired the 1896 Olympic event and the modern 26.2-mile standard.
