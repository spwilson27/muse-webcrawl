Film studio

A film studio is a major entertainment company or a motion picture company that has its own privately owned studio facility or facilities for the production of films. In common usage the term denotes both the production company and the physical premises — the sound stages, backlots, workshops, and technical facilities — at which films are shot.

History

The first dedicated film studio is generally considered to be Thomas Edison's Black Maria in West Orange, New Jersey (1893), a small tar-paper building mounted on a turntable to follow the sun. As the industry grew, producers moved to Southern California for its sunlight and varied scenery, and the great Hollywood studios — purpose-built lots with multiple stages — emerged in the 1910s and 1920s. The studio system of the Golden Age of Hollywood placed actors, directors, and technicians under long-term contract and gave the major studios control of production, distribution, and exhibition.

Facilities

A modern studio complex typically includes sound stages (large, acoustically treated, windowless buildings), a backlot of permanent exterior sets such as streets and building façades, production offices, costume and property departments, post-production and editing suites, and increasingly water tanks and virtual-production (LED volume) stages. Famous complexes include Warner Bros. Studios in Burbank, Universal Studios Lot, Pinewood Studios and Shepperton Studios in England, Babelsberg in Germany, and Cinecittà in Rome.

Major and independent studios

The "major studios" — historically Paramount, Warner Bros., Columbia, Universal, and Disney, among others — dominate global film distribution, while "mini-major" and independent studios occupy smaller niches. Many productions rent studio facilities from companies that do not produce films themselves.
