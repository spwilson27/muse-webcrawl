Subjectivity and objectivity (philosophy)

Subjectivity / Objectivity
BranchesEpistemology, metaphysics, philosophy of mind
Related conceptsQualia, intersubjectivity, fact–value distinction

In philosophy, subjectivity refers to the quality of pertaining to a subject's perspective, feelings, beliefs, and experiences — things that exist in the mind of a perceiving subject. Objectivity, by contrast, refers to the quality of being true or real independently of any individual's perception or bias. The distinction between subjective and objective claims is central to epistemology and to debates about knowledge, truth, and values.

Subjectivity

A statement is subjective if its truth depends on the attitudes or experiences of the person making it, as with judgements of taste ("this soup is delicious"). Subjective experiences, such as the felt quality of pain or the redness of red (qualia), are accessible from the first-person point of view and pose a well-known problem for purely physical accounts of the mind, explored in Thomas Nagel's essay "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"

Objectivity

Objective claims — such as measurements of mass or temperature — are in principle verifiable by any observer regardless of perspective. In science, objectivity is pursued through reproducibility, peer review, and methods designed to cancel individual bias. Philosophers disagree over whether complete objectivity is attainable: Nagel characterised the objective standpoint as a "view from nowhere," while others, including feminist epistemologists and philosophers of science, argue that knowledge is always situated and that objectivity is better understood as a product of critical, intersubjective scrutiny.

History

The modern contrast crystallised in early modern philosophy. Descartes treated the thinking subject as the foundation of certainty, while Kant distinguished phenomena (things as they appear to subjects) from noumena (things in themselves). In medieval scholastic usage the terms were nearly reversed: objective referred to things as objects of thought, and subjective to things as they exist in themselves.
