Fluoroacetic acid

Formula: C2H3FO2 (FCH2COOH)
Molar mass: 78.04 g/mol
Appearance: White crystalline solid
Melting point: about 35 °C

Fluoroacetic acid is an organofluorine compound with the chemical formula FCH2COOH. It is the simplest fluorinated carboxylic acid and a highly toxic metabolic poison. Its sodium salt, sodium fluoroacetate, is the pesticide known as "1080", used against mammalian pests, notably in Australia and New Zealand. Fluoroacetate occurs naturally in a number of plants, including the South African gifblaar (Dichapetalum cymosum) and Australian poison peas (Gastrolobium species), where it acts as a defence against herbivores.

Toxicity and mechanism

Fluoroacetic acid itself is not the direct toxin; it undergoes "lethal synthesis" in the body: it is converted to fluoroacetyl-CoA and then to fluorocitrate, which blocks the enzyme aconitase in the citric acid (Krebs) cycle, halting cellular energy production and causing citrate to accumulate. Signs of poisoning — vomiting, convulsions, cardiac arrhythmia and respiratory failure — may be delayed for hours while this conversion proceeds, and the lethal dose for humans is only a few milligrams per kilogram of body weight. There is no specific antidote; treatment is supportive, though glycerol monoacetate (monacetin) and ethanol have been used historically.

Uses and regulation

Sodium fluoroacetate (1080) is used in baits to control introduced predators and herbivores — foxes, wild dogs, cats, possums and rabbits — and is controversial because of risks to non-target animals and animal-welfare concerns. Fluoroacetic acid has otherwise limited use, as a reagent and intermediate in research. It is a stronger acid than acetic acid (pKa ≈ 2.6) owing to the electron-withdrawing fluorine atom.
