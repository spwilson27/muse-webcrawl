The opium poppy (Papaver somniferum) is a species of flowering plant in the family Papaveraceae. It is the source of opium, the dried latex obtained by scoring the unripe seed capsules, from which the alkaloids morphine, codeine and thebaine are derived. The plant is also cultivated for its edible seeds, used in baking and for oil, and as an ornamental garden flower.

Description

Papaver somniferum is an annual herb growing to about 100 cm tall, with glaucous blue-green foliage and large showy flowers in white, pink, red or purple. The fruit is a hairless, nearly spherical capsule topped by a disc of stigmatic rays; when unripe it exudes a milky latex when incised, which darkens and dries to form raw opium.

Cultivation and history

Domesticated in the western Mediterranean region in prehistoric times, the opium poppy spread across Eurasia and was known to the Sumerians, Egyptians, Greeks and Romans as a medicine and soporific. Legal cultivation for pharmaceutical alkaloids takes place under licence in countries such as Australia, Turkey and India, while illicit cultivation for opium and heroin production has historically been concentrated in Afghanistan and the "Golden Triangle" of Southeast Asia. Poppy seeds contain negligible alkaloid levels and are a common food ingredient, although they can cause positive results in sensitive drug tests.
