Mozzarella

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Fresh mozzarella balls in liquid, with basil and tomatoes]

Mozzarella is a fresh, soft Italian cheese made by the pasta filata (stretched curd) method, traditionally from the milk of the Italian Mediterranean buffalo and now more commonly from cow's milk (sold as fior di latte). It is eaten fresh and milky, and in a drier, low-moisture form it is the definitive pizza cheese, prized for melting into long, elastic strands. The name comes from mozzare, "to cut off", referring to the pinching-off of individual balls of curd.

Production

Curd is acidified, then stretched and kneaded in hot water until smooth and elastic, formed into balls or braids, and stored in brine or whey. Fresh mozzarella is perishable and best eaten within days; it weeps liquid and has a delicate, lactic flavour. Low-moisture mozzarella is aged briefly, saltier and firmer, designed to melt evenly and brown on pizza.

Types

Mozzarella di Bufala Campana, made from buffalo milk in Campania and nearby regions, holds PDO status. Variants include burrata (a mozzarella shell filled with stracciatella and cream), smoked mozzarella (affumicata), bocconcini (small balls) and the braided treccia. Pizza cheese outside Italy is often a blend engineered for melt and cost.

Uses

Fresh mozzarella stars in the Caprese salad with tomato and basil — the colours of the Italian flag — while low-moisture mozzarella covers most of the world's pizzas and tops lasagne, eggplant parmigiana and cheese sticks (breaded and deep-fried).
