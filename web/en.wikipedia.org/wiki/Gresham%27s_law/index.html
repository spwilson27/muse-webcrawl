Gresham's law

FieldMonetary economics
Named afterSir Thomas Gresham (1519–1579)
Principle"Bad money drives out good"

In economics, Gresham's law is a monetary principle stating that "bad money drives out good". If a new coin ("bad money") is issued equal in face value to an older coin ("good money") that is composed of a more valuable metal, people will hoard or melt down the old coins and spend the new ones, so the new coins circulate and the old disappear from circulation.

History

The principle was named in the 19th century after Sir Thomas Gresham, financial agent of Queen Elizabeth I, who explained the disappearance of undebased English coins following earlier debasements. Versions of the idea appear earlier in Aristophanes' The Frogs, in medieval writers such as Nicole Oresme, and in Copernicus's monetary treatise of 1519–1526; for this reason some economists call it the Gresham–Copernicus law.

Mechanism and limits

The law works when an official exchange ratio between two monies differs from their market ratio: the legally overvalued money is spent, and the undervalued money is hoarded, exported, or melted. When both monies float freely at market rates, the law does not apply, and good money can instead drive out bad — an outcome sometimes called Thiers' law.

Applications

The principle has been invoked for bimetallism, clipped and debased coinage, and modern cases where weak and strong currencies circulate side by side.
