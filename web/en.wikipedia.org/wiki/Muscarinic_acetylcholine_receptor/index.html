Muscarinic acetylcholine receptor

Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChRs)
Type: G protein-coupled receptors
Subtypes: M1–M5
Endogenous agonist: Acetylcholine
Named for: Muscarine

Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChRs) are G protein-coupled receptors activated by the neurotransmitter acetylcholine and by the alkaloid muscarine, from which they take their name. Together with the ion-channel-type nicotinic receptors, they mediate cholinergic signalling throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems.

Subtypes and signalling

Five subtypes, M1–M5, are known. M1, M3, and M5 couple to Gq/11 proteins, activating phospholipase C; M2 and M4 couple to Gi/o proteins, inhibiting adenylyl cyclase and opening potassium channels. M2 receptors in the heart slow the heart rate; M3 receptors drive glandular secretion, smooth-muscle contraction in gut and airways, and bladder emptying — the main effectors of parasympathetic "rest and digest" responses.

Pharmacology

Muscarinic antagonists include atropine, scopolamine, and oxybutynin, used for indications from motion sickness to overactive bladder; agonists such as pilocarpine treat dry mouth and glaucoma. Blockade of muscarinic receptors explains the anticholinergic side-effect burden of many older drugs, and M1/M4 agonism (for example, xanomeline) is a therapeutic target in schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease.
