Eadburh

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Died: after 802

Spouse: Beorhtric of Wessex

Father: Offa of Mercia

Mother: Cynethryth

Eadburh (Old English: Ēadburh; fl. 787–802) was the daughter of King Offa of Mercia and Queen Cynethryth, and the wife of King Beorhtric of Wessex. She is one of the most strikingly portrayed women of the Anglo-Saxon period, chiefly through the hostile account of Asser, biographer of Alfred the Great.

Marriage and rule

Eadburh married Beorhtric in 789, a marriage that sealed Mercian dominance over Wessex. According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and Asser, she wielded unusual power at court, ordering arrests and executions and acting, in Asser's words, "like a tyrant after the manner of her father". Asser claims that in 802 she accidentally poisoned her husband while attempting to poison a young courtier of whom she was jealous; Beorhtric died, as did the young man.

Exile

After Beorhtric's death, Eadburh fled to the court of Charlemagne. Asser tells the story that Charlemagne offered her a choice between himself and his son, and that when she chose the son — "because he is younger" — the emperor replied that she should have neither, and instead made her abbess of a convent. She was later expelled from the convent, the account says, for misconduct, and ended her life begging in the streets of Pavia in Italy.

Reputation

Asser adds that Eadburh's notoriety was why the wives of West Saxon kings were afterwards denied the title of queen and a seat beside the king. Modern historians treat his story with caution: it reads as a moralising tale directed against powerful queens, and its more dramatic details are probably embellished. Eadburh's mother Cynethryth was herself exceptional — the only Anglo-Saxon queen to issue coinage in her own name.
