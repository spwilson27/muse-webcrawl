A beta-lactam (β-lactam) is a lactam — a cyclic amide — with a four-membered ring containing one nitrogen and one carbonyl carbon. The "beta" indicates that the nitrogen atom is attached to the β-carbon relative to the carbonyl. β-lactam rings form the structural core of the β-lactam antibiotics, the most widely used class of antibiotics in medicine.

Chemistry

The four-membered ring is highly strained, which makes it unusually reactive for an amide and underlies both its biological activity and its chemical instability. The first β-lactam was synthesised by Hermann Staudinger in 1907 by the [2+2] cycloaddition of a ketene with an imine, a reaction now known as the Staudinger synthesis. In antibiotic molecules the ring is fused to a second ring in penicillins (with a thiazolidine ring), cephalosporins and carbapenems, or stands alone in monobactams such as aztreonam.

Biological role

β-lactam antibiotics kill bacteria by acylating penicillin-binding proteins, enzymes that cross-link the peptidoglycan cell wall, thereby blocking cell wall synthesis. Bacteria resist them principally by producing β-lactamase enzymes that hydrolyse the ring, and by altering the target proteins, as in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Outside antibiotics, β-lactam compounds include the cholesterol absorption inhibitor ezetimibe.
