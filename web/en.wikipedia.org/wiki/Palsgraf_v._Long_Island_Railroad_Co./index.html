Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co.

Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co., 248 N.Y. 339 (1928), is a leading case in American tort law on the question of liability to an unforeseeable plaintiff. The opinion by Chief Judge Benjamin Cardozo of the New York Court of Appeals established that a defendant's duty of care is owed only to plaintiffs within the foreseeable zone of danger, and the case remains a staple of first-year torts courses in American law schools.

Facts

Helen Palsgraf was standing on a platform of the Long Island Rail Road at the East New York station in Brooklyn, waiting to board a train to Rockaway Beach. As a train left the station, two men ran to catch it; one jumped aboard safely, but the second, carrying a package wrapped in newspaper, stumbled. Railroad guards pushed and pulled him aboard, dislodging the package, which fell to the tracks and exploded — it contained fireworks. The shock of the explosion toppled a coin-operated scale at the far end of the platform, which fell on Palsgraf and injured her. She sued the railroad for negligence.

Decision

A jury found for Palsgraf and the Appellate Division affirmed, but the Court of Appeals reversed in a 4–3 decision. Cardozo reasoned that "the risk reasonably to be perceived defines the duty to be obeyed": the guards' conduct, if negligent toward the passenger carrying the package, was not negligent toward Palsgraf, who was outside the foreseeable orbit of danger, and negligence in the abstract is not actionable — there must be a violation of a duty owed to the plaintiff. Judge William Shankland Andrews dissented, arguing that a duty is owed to society at large and liability should be limited instead by proximate cause — a famous debate that shaped subsequent tort doctrine.

Legacy

The case anchors the doctrinal distinction between duty/foreseeability (Cardozo) and proximate cause (Andrews) approaches. Most American jurisdictions follow Cardozo's view in analyzing duty.
