Humane Slaughter Act

The Humane Slaughter Act, or the Humane Methods of Livestock Slaughter Act (P.L. 85-765; 7 U.S.C. 1901 et seq.), is a United States federal law designed to decrease suffering of livestock during slaughter. It was passed on August 27, 1958. The law was amended by the Humane Methods of Slaughter Act of 1978.

The Act requires that livestock be rendered insensible to pain before slaughter.

Content

Congress found that the use of humane methods in the slaughter of livestock prevents needless suffering, results in safer and better working conditions for persons engaged in the slaughtering industry, brings about improvement in the products and economies in slaughtering operations, and produces other benefits for producers, processors, and consumers.

The Act declares two methods of slaughter and handling to be humane. In the first, livestock are rendered insensible to pain by a single blow or gunshot or an electrical, chemical, or other means that is rapid and effective, before being shackled, hoisted, thrown, cast, or cut. In the second, slaughter follows ritual requirements of a religious faith — such as Jewish shechita or Islamic halal slaughter — whereby the animal suffers loss of consciousness by anaemia of the brain caused by the simultaneous severance of the carotid arteries with a sharp instrument.

Scope and enforcement

The 1958 Act applied initially only to slaughterhouses selling meat to the federal government. The 1978 amendments extended its requirements to all federally inspected slaughterhouses and declared humane slaughter standards applicable to imported meat as well. Enforcement is by the Food Safety and Inspection Service of the U.S. Department of Agriculture. The Act covers cattle, calves, horses, mules, sheep, swine, and other livestock, but not poultry, which account for the vast majority of land animals slaughtered for food in the United States.

Criticism

Animal welfare advocates have criticized the Act for weak enforcement, its exemption of ritual slaughter, and the exclusion of poultry. Investigations and audits have periodically found inconsistent inspection and under-reporting of violations, leading to repeated calls for stronger enforcement.
