Udana

The Udāna is a Buddhist scripture, part of the Khuddaka Nikaya of the Sutta Pitaka in the Pali Canon of Theravada Buddhism. Its name means "inspired utterances," and the text consists of eighty short prose narratives, each culminating in a verse spoken by the Buddha on that occasion. It is considered among the oldest strata of the Pali Canon.

Structure and content

The Udāna is organized into eight chapters (vagga) of ten discourses each, including the Bodhi Vagga on the awakening, the Meghiya Vagga, and the Patali Vagga. Famous passages include the story of the blind men and an elephant, used as a parable of sectarian dispute over partial truths; the Buddha's visits to the Bodhi tree in the weeks after his enlightenment; and the discourse to Bahiya of the Bark Garment, whose brief instruction — "in the seen, merely the seen" — led to his immediate awakening and which remains influential in meditation traditions.

Udāna verses on nibbana

Two utterances in the eighth chapter are among the most quoted descriptions of nibbana in the canon: "There is, monks, an unborn, unbecome, unmade, uncompounded. If there were not that unborn... no escape from the born, become, made, and compounded would be discerned." These verses are central evidence in scholarly discussions of how early Buddhism characterized the goal of practice.

Translations

The Udāna was translated into English by F. L. Woodward for the Pali Text Society (published 1935) and has since been rendered by several translators, including John Ireland and Bhikkhu Ṭhānissaro, making it widely available to lay readers.
