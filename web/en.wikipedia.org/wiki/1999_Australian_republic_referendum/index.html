The 1999 Australian republic referendum was held on November 6, 1999, asking voters whether Australia should become a republic with a president appointed by a two-thirds majority of the federal Parliament, replacing the Queen and the Governor-General. The proposal was defeated, with 54.87 percent voting No and the Yes side failing to carry any state—the double-majority requirement for constitutional amendment (a national majority plus majorities in at least four of six states) was missed on both counts. A second question, on inserting a new preamble into the Constitution, was also rejected.

Background

Republican sentiment grew under Prime Minister Paul Keating, who championed a republic for the centenary of federation in 2001. His successor, John Howard—a monarchist—convened a Constitutional Convention in February 1998, half elected and half appointed, which endorsed the "bipartisan appointment" model associated with the Australian Republican Movement (ARM), under which the prime minister would nominate a president after consultation, to be confirmed by a joint sitting of Parliament.

Campaign

The Yes campaign united the ARM, much of the Labor Party, and most media under slogans such as "an Australian head of state." It was fatally divided by "direct election" republicans—who wanted a popularly elected president and urged a No vote to keep that option alive—and by the official No campaign of Australians for Constitutional Monarchy, which argued "don't know—vote no" and portrayed the model as a "politicians' republic." Support for a republic in principle consistently outpolled support for the specific model on offer.

Result and aftermath

Only the Australian Capital Territory recorded a Yes majority. The defeat stalled the republican movement for a generation; although debate periodically revives, no subsequent government has held another referendum on the question, and Australia remains a constitutional monarchy.
