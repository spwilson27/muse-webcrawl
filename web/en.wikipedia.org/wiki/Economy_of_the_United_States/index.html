The **economy of the United States** is the world's largest by nominal GDP and the second-largest by purchasing power parity (after China). It is a mixed economy characterised by abundant natural resources, a large consumer market, dominant technology and finance sectors, and the US dollar's role as the global reserve currency, which underpins deep Treasury markets and the Federal Reserve's influence worldwide.

The US has the world's largest consumer market and consumer spending drives about 70% of GDP. It leads in technology (Silicon Valley), entertainment, higher education, aerospace and pharmaceuticals, is a top producer of oil and natural gas, and remains a manufacturing power, second to China. Weaknesses debated by economists include high healthcare costs, income inequality, large federal deficits and public debt (over 120% of GDP including intragovernmental holdings), and regional decline in old industrial areas (the 'Rust Belt').

History in outline

From the nineteenth-century railroad and steel booms through post-1945 suburban mass affluence, the crises of the 1970s, the 2008 financial crisis (centred on US housing finance) and the post-COVID inflation surge of 2021–23, the US economy has repeatedly reorganised around new technologies while retaining unusual dynamism and deep capital markets.
