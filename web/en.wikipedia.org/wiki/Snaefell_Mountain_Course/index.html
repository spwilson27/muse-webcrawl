Snaefell Mountain Course

The Snaefell Mountain Course is a road-racing circuit used for the Isle of Man TT and the Manx Grand Prix motorcycle races held on the Isle of Man. Riders race individually against the clock (a time trial) on closed public roads. At 37.73 miles (60.72 km) it is the longest circuit still used in top-level motorcycle racing, and riders complete up to six laps in the Senior TT.

Course description

The course starts at the TT Grandstand in Douglas and follows the A2 through Onchan, up through Glen Helen (A3), Kirk Michael and Ramsey, before climbing the A18 over the shoulder of Snaefell — the "Mountain" section — reaching the highest point on the course near the Bungalow at about 1,385 ft (422 m), then descends past the Creg-ny-Baa and back into Douglas.

Famous named points, many from early competitors, pubs or accidents, include Bray Hill, Quarterbridge, Braddan Bridge, Union Mills, Glen Helen, Kirk Michael, Ballaugh Bridge (one of the few remaining jumps), Ramsey Hairpin, the Gooseneck, the Bungalow, Windy Corner, Kate's Cottage, the Creg-ny-Baa, Brandish Corner and Governor's Bridge. The course has over 200 corners, dozens of them named.

History and records

The Mountain Course was first used in 1911, replacing the shorter St John's course, and has hosted the TT ever since except during the two World Wars and cancellations such as the 2001 foot-and-mouth outbreak and the 2020–21 COVID-19 pandemic. It was part of the motorcycle World Championship from 1949 to 1976, when the races were removed from the championship on safety grounds and the British round moved to Silverstone.

Average speeds have climbed relentlessly: the first 60 mph lap came in the 1920s, the 100 mph lap in 1957 (Bob McIntyre), and the outright lap record now stands above 136 mph average, set by Peter Hickman in 2023, with top speeds over 200 mph down Sulby Straight.

Safety

The course is recognised as the most dangerous in motorsport: more than 260 competitors have been killed in the TT and Manx Grand Prix since 1907, along with officials and spectators. Defenders argue the event is a unique test freely undertaken by riders; safety work continues on signage, padding, marshal coverage (over 500 marshals are required before racing can start), and helicopter medevac response.
