Lark Quarry

Lark Quarry Conservation Park
Location: near Winton, Queensland, Australia
Known for: Dinosaur Stampede fossil trackway
Age of tracks: about 95 million years (Cretaceous)

Lark Quarry Conservation Park, near Winton in central Queensland, Australia, preserves the site of the Dinosaur Stampede: a fossil trackway of thousands of small dinosaur footprints dating to about 95 million years ago, in the mid-Cretaceous Winton Formation. It is often described as the world's only known fossil evidence of a dinosaur stampede.

The trackway

The tracks were made on the muddy shore of a freshwater lake. Some 3,300 footprints are preserved, left by an estimated 150 or more small bipedal dinosaurs—small theropods (coelurosaurs, about the size of chickens to emus) and small ornithopods—running in one direction at speeds estimated at up to 12 km/h. Crossing their path are the tracks of a single much larger theropod, around 9–10 metres long, walking slowly in the opposite direction; a few metres short of the big predator's tracks, the small footprints swerve and scatter.

The classic interpretation, developed after the site was excavated in the 1970s by Glen Seymour and palaeontologists including Tony Thulborn and Mary Wade, is that the large theropod's approach panicked the flock of small dinosaurs, causing the stampede. Later researchers have suggested alternative readings—for example that the "large theropod" tracks were made by a big herbivorous iguanodontian such as Muttaburrasaurus, and that the small dinosaurs were scurrying around a waterhole rather than fleeing in a single event.

Discovery and conservation

The footprints were first noticed in the 1960s by station manager Glen Seymour during quarrying of stone (hence "Lark Quarry", after the Lark family of Seymour's wife's side in some accounts). Excavation revealed the full trackway, which was heritage-listed, and in 2002 a purpose-built, earth-sheltered conservation building was opened over the site to protect the fragile surface from temperature extremes and weathering. The tracks famously inspired the stampede scene of the film Jurassic Park, whose makers consulted the Queensland researchers.
