Hereditary neuropathy

SpecialtyNeurology, medical genetics
TypesHereditary motor and sensory neuropathies (CMT), hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSAN), hereditary motor neuropathies (HMN)
CausesInherited gene mutations affecting peripheral nerve myelin or axons

Hereditary neuropathy is a group of inherited disorders of the peripheral nervous system in which genetic mutations damage the long peripheral nerves, causing progressive weakness, wasting, and loss of sensation, most prominently in the feet and hands. They are among the most common inherited neurological conditions, collectively affecting roughly 1 in 2,500 people.

Classification

The hereditary neuropathies are conventionally divided by which fibres predominate:

  • Hereditary motor and sensory neuropathies (HMSN), better known as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) — by far the commonest group. CMT1 is demyelinating (most often a duplication of the PMP22 gene), CMT2 is axonal; dozens of genetic subtypes are now recognised. Typical features are distal weakness and wasting ("inverted champagne bottle" legs), pes cavus, foot drop, reduced reflexes, and mild sensory loss, usually beginning in the first two decades.
  • Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSAN) — rare disorders dominated by loss of pain and temperature sensation, painless injuries and ulcers, and autonomic disturbance; the best-known gene is NTRK1 in HSAN type IV (congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis).
  • Hereditary motor neuropathies (HMN), or distal spinal muscular atrophies — pure motor syndromes without sensory involvement.
  • Hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (HNPP) — caused by deletion of PMP22, the reciprocal of the CMT1A duplication; patients develop transient palsies (e.g., wrist drop or foot drop) after minor nerve compression.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on clinical examination, family history, nerve conduction studies (which separate demyelinating from axonal forms), and increasingly on genetic testing panels that can identify the causative gene in a majority of cases. No disease-modifying treatment exists for most forms; care is supportive — physiotherapy, orthotics and ankle-foot braces, orthopaedic correction of foot deformity, occupational therapy, and avoidance of neurotoxic drugs such as vincristine, which can dramatically worsen CMT.
