Natural history

Natural history museums preserve and display specimens for study and education.

Natural history is a domain of inquiry involving organisms including animals, plants, and fungi in their environment, and also including geological and environmental processes. It leans more towards observational than experimental methods of study. Historically, natural history was the principal scientific study of the living world, encompassing what are now the separate disciplines of zoology, botany, geology, palaeontology, and ecology. A person who studies natural history is called a naturalist or natural historian.

History

The term derives from the Latin naturalis historia, the title of Pliny the Elder's encyclopaedic first-century work surveying the natural world. Natural history flourished in the Renaissance with the revival of classical texts and the flood of new species from global exploration. In the eighteenth century, Carl Linnaeus devised the binomial system of taxonomy, while the Comte de Buffon's Histoire naturelle attempted a complete account of nature. The nineteenth century saw natural history professionalise and fragment into specialised sciences, a transformation embodied in the work of naturalists such as Alexander von Humboldt, Charles Darwin, and Alfred Russel Wallace, whose field observations underpinned the theory of evolution by natural selection.

Modern scope

Today natural history persists in natural history museums — such as the Natural History Museum in London and the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History — which curate collections of specimens used in taxonomy, conservation biology, and environmental monitoring. It also survives as a mode of field-based inquiry practised by both scientists and amateurs in fields such as ornithology, entomology, and botany, and has experienced renewed prominence in citizen science projects that record biodiversity.

Societies and journals

Learned societies devoted to natural history include the Linnean Society of London (founded 1788) and numerous regional natural history societies. Journals such as the Journal of Natural History and American Midland Naturalist continue to publish descriptive and field-based research.
