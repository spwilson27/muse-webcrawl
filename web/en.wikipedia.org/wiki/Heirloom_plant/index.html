Heirloom plant

An heirloom plant, heirloom variety, heritage fruit (in the United Kingdom), or heirloom vegetable is an old cultivar of a plant used for food that is grown and maintained by gardeners and farmers, particularly in isolated communities of the Western world. These were commonly grown during earlier periods in human history, but are not used in modern large-scale agriculture.

Heirloom tomatoes display far more colour and shape diversity than commercial hybrids.

Definition and requirements

Definitions vary, but heirloom varieties are generally open-pollinated, meaning they are pollinated by insects, birds, wind or human hands rather than controlled hybridisation, and their seeds "breed true", producing plants with the parent characteristics. Many seed-savers require a variety to have been introduced before 1951, or to have been passed down within a family or community for several generations, like a family heirloom. Famous examples include 'Brandywine' and 'Cherokee Purple' tomatoes, 'Kentucky Wonder' beans, and countless regional apple, maize and squash varieties.

History and conservation

As commercial agriculture consolidated around a narrow range of hybrid varieties bred for yield, shelf life and uniform appearance, many older varieties fell out of cultivation and were at risk of extinction. From the 1970s, organisations such as Seed Savers Exchange (founded 1975 in the United States) and Garden Organic's Heritage Seed Library (in the UK) built networks of growers maintaining thousands of varieties, motivated by genetic diversity, local adaptation, flavour and cultural heritage.

Advantages and criticism

Advocates prize heirlooms for flavour, diversity, adaptation to local conditions and the independence of saving one's own seed; critics note they are often less productive, less disease-resistant and less uniform than modern hybrids. Heirloom varieties are nonetheless valuable reservoirs of genetic traits for plant breeding.
