Æthelstan or Athelstan (c. 894 – 27 October 939) was King of the Anglo-Saxons from 924/925 to 927 and the first King of the English from 927 until his death in 939. The son of Edward the Elder, he completed his father's and his aunt Æthelflæd's work of unifying the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, and modern historians regard him as the first king of England and one of its greatest Anglo-Saxon rulers.

Æthelstan, shown in a manuscript presentation miniature, was a noted patron of learning and the Church.

Reign

On Edward's death in 924, Æthelstan was accepted as king in Mercia, though his half-brother Ælfweard briefly held Wessex before dying within weeks. He was crowned at Kingston upon Thames in September 925. In 927 he conquered the last Viking kingdom, York, following the death of its ruler Sihtric, whose widow — Æthelstan's sister — he had married the previous year. Thereafter his charters styled him rex Anglorum, "King of the English", and at Eamont Bridge the kings of Scotland, Strathclyde and Bamburgh, and the Welsh princes, submitted to his overlordship.

In 934 Æthelstan invaded Scotland, apparently after Constantine II broke their agreement. Three years later Constantine joined an alliance with Olaf Guthfrithson of Dublin and Owain of Strathclyde and invaded England; Æthelstan crushed the coalition at the Battle of Brunanburh in 937, one of the most celebrated victories of the Anglo-Saxon age, commemorated in an Old English poem in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. The battle confirmed English unity but did not permanently subdue the north.

Government and legacy

Æthelstan's court was a centre of government innovation: he issued extensive law codes, centralised control of coinage (requiring that each borough have a mint and one currency for the whole kingdom), and maintained diplomatic relations with rulers across Europe, marrying his half-sisters into continental royal houses. A pious ruler and relic collector, he richly endowed churches and monasteries. He never married and was succeeded by his half-brother Edmund. He was buried at Malmesbury Abbey, whose patron saint, Aldhelm, he particularly venerated.
