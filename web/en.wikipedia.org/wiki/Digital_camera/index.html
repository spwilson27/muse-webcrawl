Digital camera

TypeCamera recording images digitally
Sensor typesCCD; CMOS (predominantly)
StorageMemory cards (SD, CFexpress, etc.); internal memory

A digital camera is a camera that captures photographs in digital memory rather than on photographic film. Light entering the lens is recorded by an electronic image sensor — historically a charge-coupled device (CCD) and now almost universally a CMOS sensor — converted into digital data, processed in-camera, and stored on removable memory cards or internal storage for viewing, transfer and printing.

History

The first digital camera was built in 1975 by Kodak engineer Steven Sasson; it weighed about 3.6 kg, recorded a 0.01-megapixel black-and-white image to a cassette tape, and took 23 seconds to do so. Commercial digital cameras appeared at the end of the 1980s, and through the 1990s resolution rose and prices fell: the Kodak DCS series served professionals, while consumer models such as the Apple QuickTake (1994) and the megapixel compacts of the late 1990s brought digital photography to the mass market. Digital single-lens reflex cameras (DSLRs) dominated professional work in the 2000s, followed by mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras in the 2010s. The rise of smartphones, which integrated capable cameras from the mid-2000s onward, collapsed the market for dedicated compact cameras.

How it works

The sensor is covered by a colour filter array, usually a Bayer pattern, so each photosite records one colour; demosaicing software reconstructs a full-colour image. Processors apply white balance, noise reduction and sharpening, saving images as compressed JPEG/HEIF files or as minimally processed "raw" files preserving sensor data for later editing. Key specifications include sensor size (from small compact sensors through APS-C to full-frame 35 mm and medium format), resolution in megapixels, sensitivity (ISO), and autofocus and stabilisation systems.

Types

Compact (point-and-shoot) cameras, bridge cameras, DSLRs, mirrorless cameras, digital rangefinders, medium-format systems, action cameras and 360-degree cameras all share the same basic principle. Digital technology also underlies camcorders, webcams, and the cameras in phones, drones and doorbells.
