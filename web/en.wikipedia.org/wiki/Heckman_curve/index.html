Heckman curve

The Heckman curve is a graphical representation, associated with the Nobel laureate economist James Heckman of the University of Chicago, of the finding that the rate of return on human capital investment is highest in the earliest years of life and declines with age. It is widely used to argue that public investment in early childhood — especially high-quality early education for disadvantaged children — yields higher economic and social returns than later remediation.

The curve

The graph plots rates of return against the age at which investment is made. Returns are highest (often estimated around 7–10% per annum, or higher in some studies) for investment in the prenatal period and the first five years of life, and fall steadily for school-age spending, job training and adult programmes, whose returns are frequently estimated to be low or even negative for the most disadvantaged groups.

Evidence base

The curve draws chiefly on long-run evaluations of model early-childhood programmes in the United States, above all the Perry Preschool Program and the Abecedarian Project, whose participants were followed into middle age. These studies found lasting effects on earnings, employment, health and reduced criminality, with benefit–cost ratios estimated at several dollars returned per dollar spent. Heckman summarises the logic with the principle that "skills beget skills": early cognitive and non-cognitive (character) skills raise the productivity of later learning, a property economists call dynamic complementarity and self-productivity.

Influence and criticism

The Heckman curve has been influential in policy debates in many countries, cited by finance ministries, the World Bank and early-years advocates. Critics caution that the original evidence rests on small, intensive, decades-old American programmes whose effects may not scale to universal provision; that effect sizes from larger public programmes such as Head Start appear smaller; and that a focus on economic returns risks undervaluing later education and the intrinsic worth of schooling. Heckman and colleagues respond that the curve is about the declining marginal return to remediation, not about abandoning later investment.
