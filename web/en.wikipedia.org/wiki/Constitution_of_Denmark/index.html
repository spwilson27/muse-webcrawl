Constitution of Denmark

The Constitutional Act of Denmark (Danish: Grundloven, lit. "the fundamental law") is the constitution of the Kingdom of Denmark, adopted on 5 June 1953. It is the successor of the previous constitutions of 1849 (the June Constitution) and 1915, and was signed on Constitution Day, 5 June, commemorating the anniversary of the first constitution.

History

The first Danish constitution, the June Constitution of 1849, established Denmark as a constitutional monarchy and ended absolute monarchy, which had been in place since 1660. It was drafted partly in response to the revolutions of 1848 across Europe. The constitution was revised in 1866 (creating a more conservative upper chamber), again in 1915 (which introduced women's suffrage and proportional representation), and in 1920 (following the reunification with North Schleswig).

After the Second World War, pressure grew to modernise the constitution. The 1953 revision abolished the upper house, the Landsting, making the Folketing the sole legislative chamber; allowed female succession to the throne; and created the office of the Danish ombudsman, the Folketingets Ombudsmand. It also strengthened the protection of civil rights and made it harder to amend the constitution.

Main principles

The constitution rests on the principle of the separation of powers, set out in Section 3: legislative power is vested jointly in the monarch and the Folketing, executive power in the monarch (exercised through the government), and judicial power in the courts. In practice the monarch's role is largely ceremonial, as Section 12–14 vest authority in ministers who are responsible to the Folketing.

Key features include:

  • Parliamentary system: a government cannot remain in office against the will of a majority of the Folketing (Section 15).
  • Referendums: certain decisions, including changes to the voting age and transfers of sovereignty to international authorities, can or must be put to a referendum (Sections 20, 29 and 42).
  • Civil rights: Sections 70–84 protect personal liberty, freedom of speech and assembly, freedom of religion, and the inviolability of the home and property.
  • Difficult amendment procedure: changes require passage by two successive parliaments with an election in between, followed by a referendum in which at least 40% of the entire electorate must vote in favour (Section 88).

Structure

The Constitutional Act consists of 89 sections divided into eleven chapters, covering the form of government and the monarch, the Folketing and elections, the government, the courts, the established Church (the Evangelical Lutheran Church, supported by the state under Section 4), local government, civil rights, and the Faroe Islands and Greenland, which form part of the Realm together with Denmark.
