Art therapy

FieldPsychotherapy, creative arts therapies

Art therapy is a distinct form of psychotherapy that uses art media, the creative process and the resulting artwork as a means of communication, assessment and treatment. Practised by trained, credentialed therapists, it is used to help people explore emotions, reduce anxiety, manage behaviour and addictions, resolve conflicts, increase self-awareness and improve social skills. It forms part of the wider group of expressive or creative arts therapies, alongside music therapy, dance/movement therapy and drama therapy.

History

The term "art therapy" was coined in 1942 by the British artist Adrian Hill, who had discovered the therapeutic benefits of drawing and painting while recovering from tuberculosis in a sanatorium, and who went on to promote art work among hospital patients. In the United States, Margaret Naumburg, an educator and psychologist, developed "dynamically oriented art therapy" from the 1940s, emphasising the spontaneous image as a form of symbolic speech arising from the unconscious. Edith Kramer, an Austrian-born artist who worked with children in New York, emphasised the healing potential of the creative process itself, a view sometimes summarised as "art as therapy," in contrast to Naumburg's "art in therapy," in which artwork serves as an adjunct to verbal psychotherapy.

Practice

Art therapy takes place in hospitals, psychiatric and rehabilitation facilities, schools, prisons, care homes, private practices and community settings. Clients — who need no artistic skill — work with materials such as paint, clay, collage and drawing media. The therapist may invite the client to discuss the finished image, or may treat the art-making itself as the therapeutic agent. Approaches draw on psychoanalytic, humanistic, cognitive-behavioural and developmental theories.

Populations and evidence

Art therapy is used with children, adolescents, adults and older people; with people experiencing trauma, bereavement, dementia, schizophrenia, cancer and other conditions. Research evidence is mixed and often based on small studies: systematic reviews suggest possible benefits for quality of life and some symptoms, but call for larger, better-controlled trials.

Professional regulation

In the United Kingdom, art therapists (officially "art psychotherapists" or "art therapists") are regulated by the Health and Care Professions Council. In the United States, art therapists typically hold a master's degree and may be credentialed as registered art therapists (ATR) by the Art Therapy Credentials Board; requirements for state licensure vary.
