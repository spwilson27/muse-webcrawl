Quasicrystal

A quasicrystal, formally a quasiperiodic crystal, is a structure that is ordered but not periodic: its atomic arrangement displays long-range orientational order and diffraction patterns with sharp Bragg peaks, yet it lacks translational symmetry. Quasicrystals can display symmetries forbidden to ordinary crystals, most famously fivefold (icosahedral) symmetry.

Diffraction pattern of an icosahedral quasicrystal, showing forbidden tenfold symmetry.

Discovery

Dan Shechtman discovered an aluminium–manganese alloy with an icosahedral diffraction pattern in 1982; published in 1984 with Ilan Blech, John Cahn and Denis Gratias, the finding defied the crystallographic restriction theorem and was initially met with scepticism — Linus Pauling notably opposed it, proposing twinned ordinary crystals instead. The discovery ultimately redefined the concept of a crystal: in 1991 the International Union of Crystallography broadened its definition to any solid with an essentially discrete diffraction diagram. Shechtman received the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery.

Structure and mathematics

Quasicrystals are modelled by quasiperiodic tilings such as the Penrose tiling, or by cutting a higher-dimensional periodic lattice and projecting it into physical space (the cut-and-project method). Their atomic positions can be described by a small number of interpenetrating sublattices, and properties such as phason modes — rearrangements unique to quasiperiodic order — arise from the higher-dimensional description.

Properties and occurrences

Most known quasicrystals are metallic alloys (e.g. Al–Cu–Fe, Al–Pd–Mn) combining high hardness, low friction, low thermal and electrical conductivity and corrosion resistance, suggesting uses in coatings. Natural quasicrystals were identified in 2009 in the Khatyrka meteorite, proving that such structures can form and persist in nature; synthetic quasicrystals have also formed in the debris of the first atomic bomb test at Trinity.
