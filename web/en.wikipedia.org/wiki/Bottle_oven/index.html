Bottle oven

A bottle oven or bottle kiln is a type of kiln. The word 'bottle' refers not to the kiln's products but to its shape. Bottle ovens were typical features of the British pottery industry, especially in the Staffordshire Potteries, and were used to fire pottery and tiles. They consisted of the inner firing chamber (the oven proper) surrounded by a tall, bottle-shaped brick chimney known as the hovel, which protected the kiln from the weather and created the draught for the fires.

Operation

Ware was placed inside protective fireclay containers called saggars and stacked within the oven. Fires were lit in firemouths around the base, and firing with coal could take several days, followed by a long cooling period. Conditions inside were harsh and uneven, and skilled firemen judged temperature by experience and by the colour of the flames.

History and preservation

Coal-fired bottle ovens were built in their thousands in Stoke-on-Trent during the 18th and 19th centuries; their smoke was a notorious source of air pollution. Clean air legislation in the 1950s and the adoption of gas and electric tunnel kilns ended their commercial use; the last firing of a bottle oven in Stoke took place in 1978. Around fifty bottle ovens survive as listed structures, and preserved examples can be seen at the Gladstone Pottery Museum in Longton.
