Shot (filmmaking)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In filmmaking and video production, a shot is a series of frames that runs for an uninterrupted period of time. It is a basic unit of film grammar: shots are joined by editing into scenes, and scenes into sequences and the whole film. Film shots are an essential aspect of a movie, where angles, transitions and cuts are used to further express emotion, ideas and movement. The term "shot" can refer both to the material recorded in a single take and to the unit as it appears in the finished, edited work.

Shot sizes

Shots are conventionally classified by the size of the subject in frame:

  • Extreme long shot / wide shot — the subject appears small within a vast landscape or setting.
  • Long shot — the whole human figure fits the frame, with surroundings prominent.
  • Medium long (American) shot — the figure framed roughly from the knees up.
  • Medium shot — the figure from about the waist up; common for dialogue.
  • Medium close-up — chest and head.
  • Close-up — the face or an object fills the frame.
  • Extreme close-up — a detail such as eyes or lips fills the frame.

Angles, movement and special shots

Shots are also described by camera angle (high-angle, low-angle, Dutch angle, bird's-eye), by point of view (POV shot, over-the-shoulder shot, two-shot), and by camera motion (pan, tilt, tracking/dolly shot, crane shot, handheld, Steadicam). A long take or plan-séquence is an unusually extended shot, sometimes choreographed to cover an entire scene or, by concealed editing, an entire film. Other named forms include the establishing shot, master shot, insert, cutaway, reaction shot and the "one-shot" music or action sequence.

Shot length and style

Average shot length (ASL) is a measurable stylistic signature: classical Hollywood films averaged roughly 8–11 seconds per shot, whereas contemporary mainstream films often average 4–6 seconds, and action sequences far less, while art-cinema directors such as Andrei Tarkovsky or Theo Angelopoulos built films from shots lasting minutes. The choice of shot scale, angle and duration shapes narrative emphasis and audience emotion, and mismatches between shots are governed by continuity rules such as the 180-degree rule.
