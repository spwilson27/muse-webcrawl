The Lwów Ghetto (German: Ghetto Lemberg) was a Nazi ghetto in the city of Lwów (now Lviv, Ukraine) established in 1941 for the Jewish population of the city and refugees from elsewhere in occupied Poland. It was one of the largest ghettos in Nazi-occupied Europe, holding well over 100,000 Jews under conditions of extreme overcrowding, starvation, and disease.

Establishment

German forces occupied Lwów on 30 June 1941 after the Soviet withdrawal, and pogroms in July 1941 — encouraged by the occupiers — killed thousands of Jews. In November 1941 the Germans decreed a Jewish quarter in the poor northern districts of Zamarstynów and Kleparów; Jews were forced to move there by December 1941, and the crossing over Pełtewna Street, the infamous "gate" under the railway bridge, became a site of repeated selections and killings.

Deportations and liquidation

In March 1942 deportations to the Bełżec extermination camp began, taking some 15,000 Jews. The "Great Aktion" of August 1942 deported roughly 50,000 more to Bełżec, and thousands were murdered in the city or sent to the Janowska forced-labour camp. The ghetto was progressively reduced and finally liquidated in June 1943; remaining residents were shot at the Janowska camp and the Piaski ("Sands") ravine, or deported. In total, of Lwów's pre-war Jewish population of over 100,000, together with refugees, only a few hundred had survived by the time the Red Army retook the city in July 1944.

Resistance and rescue

Armed resistance, escapes to the "Aryan side", and hiding with Polish and Ukrainian rescuers saved a small number of people. Notable survivors' accounts include those of Leopold Socha, a Polish sewer worker who hid a group of Jews in the city's sewers for fourteen months — a story told in the film In Darkness (2011).
