Cauchy–Riemann equations

In the field of complex analysis in mathematics, the Cauchy–Riemann equations, named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Bernhard Riemann, consist of a system of two partial differential equations which form a necessary and sufficient condition for a complex function of a complex variable to be complex differentiable, that is, holomorphic.

Suppose that f(x + iy) = u(x, y) + iv(x, y) is a function of a complex variable z = x + iy, where u and v are real-valued functions of two real variables. The Cauchy–Riemann equations on u and v are:

∂u/∂x = ∂v/∂y   and   ∂u/∂y = −∂v/∂x

History

The equations first appeared in the work of Jean le Rond d'Alembert in 1752. Later, Leonhard Euler connected this system to analytic functions. Cauchy then used these equations to construct his theory of functions in the early 19th century, and Bernhard Riemann's dissertation of 1851 gave the equations their modern name and central role.

Interpretation

Geometrically, the equations express the condition that the Jacobian matrix of the function be a scalar multiple of a rotation matrix, so that the function is conformal (angle-preserving) wherever its derivative does not vanish. Physically, if u and v are interpreted as the components of a velocity potential and stream function of a steady two-dimensional incompressible fluid flow, the equations express that the flow is irrotational.

Related concepts

Functions satisfying the equations are connected to harmonic functions: both u and v satisfy Laplace's equation. Closely related topics include Cauchy's integral formula, the Cauchy integral theorem, and Morera's theorem.
