Ballistic missile

A ballistic missile is a type of missile that uses projectile motion to deliver warheads to a target. Such weapons are powered only during relatively brief periods — most of the flight is an unpowered, gravity-determined (ballistic) trajectory through or above the atmosphere — in contrast to cruise missiles, which are aerodynamically guided in sustained powered flight.

Flight profile

A ballistic missile flight has three phases: the boost phase, in which rocket motors propel the missile and its guidance sets the course; the midcourse phase, an unpowered coast — for intercontinental missiles, exoatmospheric, during which multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs) and decoys may be released; and the terminal phase, in which the reentry vehicle plunges through the atmosphere to its target at hypersonic speed.

Categories by range

  • Short-range (SRBM): under 1,000 km;
  • Medium-range (MRBM): 1,000–3,000 km;
  • Intermediate-range (IRBM): 3,000–5,500 km;
  • Intercontinental (ICBM): over 5,500 km;
  • Submarine-launched (SLBM) and air-launched variants.

History

The first operational ballistic missile was Germany's V-2 rocket of 1944, developed under Wernher von Braun. After the Second World War, the United States and Soviet Union built on V-2 technology; by the late 1950s both fielded ICBMs, which became the principal delivery systems for nuclear weapons during the Cold War. Ballistic missile defence — from Nike Zeus to modern systems such as THAAD, Aegis BMD and Patriot — developed in response.
