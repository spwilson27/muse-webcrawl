Auburn system

The Auburn system (also known as the New York system and the congregate system) is a penal method of the nineteenth century in which prisoners worked during the day in groups and were kept in solitary confinement at night, with enforced silence at all times. It was developed at Auburn Prison in Auburn, New York, in the 1820s, and rivalled the separate system of Pennsylvania for influence over prison design worldwide.

Characteristics

Under the Auburn system, inmates slept alone in small cells at night but laboured together in workshops during the day, eating meals in common—always under a strict rule of silence enforced by corporal punishment. Prisoners wore striped uniforms, moved in the famous "lockstep", and were forbidden to communicate even by glances. The system relied on productive labour, and the sale of prison-made goods, to offset the costs of confinement.

History

After an experiment with continuous solitary confinement at Auburn in 1821–1823 produced deaths and mental illness, the prison under keepers such as Elam Lynds adopted the congregate-by-day regime. Sing Sing prison was built by Auburn inmates under Lynds from 1825 using the same model. European observers debated the merits of Auburn versus the Pennsylvania separate system; Auburn prevailed in most American states because it was cheaper to build and operate and produced revenue from inmate labour.

Legacy

The Auburn system shaped the design of many prisons, including tiered cellblocks with small inside cells. Its strict silence rule gradually eroded in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, but elements—congregate work, classification, and prison industries—persisted in American corrections.
