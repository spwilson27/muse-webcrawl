The 1975 Kalapana earthquake occurred on November 29, 1975, off the south coast of the island of Hawaii. With a moment magnitude of 7.7, it was the largest Hawaiian earthquake since 1868 and generated a locally devastating tsunami that killed two people at Halapē.

The earthquake

The quake struck at 4:47 a.m. local time and originated from sudden seaward movement of Kīlauea volcano's south flank along a near-horizontal fault, a motion driven by magma intruding into the volcano's rift zones. The Kalapana coastline subsided abruptly by as much as 3.5 metres (11.5 feet), and horizontal displacements reached several metres toward the sea. A smaller eruption of Kīlauea began shortly after the main shock.

Tsunami

The abrupt seafloor subsidence generated a tsunami with run-up heights reaching about 14.6 metres (48 feet) at Halapē, where a group of campers and Boy Scouts were swept inland; two people were killed and 19 injured. Waves of 2–3 metres were recorded around the Hawaiian Islands, and measurable waves reached California, Japan, and other Pacific shores. Property damage totaled about $4.1 million.

Scientific significance

The event provided the clearest demonstration that Kīlauea's south flank is sliding seaward in episodic, earthquake-generating movements—a process later monitored continuously by GPS—and it underpins assessments of Hawaii's local tsunami hazard, which can arrive within minutes of the earthquake.
