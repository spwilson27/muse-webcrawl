Nonparametric statistics

Nonparametric statistics is a type of statistical analysis that makes minimal assumptions about the underlying distribution of the data being studied. Often these models are infinite-dimensional, rather than finite dimensional, as in parametric statistics. Nonparametric statistics can be used for descriptive statistics or statistical inference. Nonparametric tests are often used when the assumptions of parametric tests are evidently violated.

Definitions and characteristics

The term "nonparametric statistics" has been defined in at least two ways. In one usage, it refers to methods that do not rely on data belonging to any particular parametric family of probability distributions — these include distribution-free methods based on ranks, such as the Mann–Whitney U test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. In another usage, it encompasses models whose structure is not fixed in advance but grows in complexity with the amount of data, such as kernel density estimation.

Because nonparametric methods make fewer assumptions, their applicability is much wider than that of the corresponding parametric methods. In particular, they may be applied in situations where less is known about the application in question. Also, due to this reliance on fewer assumptions, nonparametric methods are more robust, though they may have less statistical power when parametric assumptions do hold.

Applications and purpose

Nonparametric methods are widely used for studying populations that take on a ranked order, such as movie reviews receiving one to four stars. They are also used when the data consist of counts or proportions, when sample sizes are small, or when the data contain outliers that make means misleading. Classical nonparametric tests include the sign test, the Kruskal–Wallis test, and Spearman's rank correlation coefficient as an alternative to Pearson's product-moment correlation.

History

Early nonparametric methods emerged in the eighteenth century with the sign test used by John Arbuthnott in 1710. The field took modern shape in the mid-twentieth century with rank tests developed by Frank Wilcoxon (1945), Henry Mann and Donald Whitney (1947), and William Kruskal and W. Allen Wallis (1952), alongside the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for goodness of fit to a distribution.
