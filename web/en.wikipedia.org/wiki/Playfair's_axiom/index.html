Playfair's axiom

Playfair's axiom is an axiom of Euclidean geometry that can be used in place of Euclid's fifth postulate (the parallel postulate). It is named after the Scottish mathematician John Playfair, who popularized it in his 1795 edition of Euclid's Elements, although the statement itself had been given earlier, for example by the ancient commentator Proclus.

Statement

In a plane, given a line and a point not on it, at most one line parallel to the given line can be drawn through the point.

Equivalently: if a line segment intersects two straight lines forming two interior angles on the same side that sum to less than two right angles, then the two lines, if extended indefinitely, meet on that side on which the angles sum to less than two right angles—a consequence that follows from the axiom together with the other postulates. Playfair's axiom combined with the first four Euclidean postulates is logically equivalent to Euclid's fifth postulate, so it can serve as its replacement in axiomatizing Euclidean geometry.

Relation to other statements

Many familiar statements are equivalent to Playfair's axiom within neutral geometry, including: the angles of a triangle sum to two right angles; there exists a rectangle; similar but non-congruent triangles exist; and through any point inside an angle there is a line intersecting both sides of the angle. In hyperbolic geometry, Playfair's axiom fails: infinitely many parallels to a given line pass through a point not on it. In elliptic geometry it fails differently, since there are no parallels at all.

History

For two thousand years mathematicians attempted to prove the fifth postulate from the others. Playfair's reformulation, often written "at most one parallel," made the assumption particularly transparent and became the standard form stated in geometry textbooks. The eventual development of non-Euclidean geometries in the nineteenth century by Lobachevsky, Bolyai, and Riemann showed that the postulate (in Playfair's form) is independent of the others.
