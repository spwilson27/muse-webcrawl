U.S. Customs and Border Protection

U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) is the largest federal law enforcement agency of the United States Department of Homeland Security (DHS). It is charged with regulating and facilitating international trade, collecting import duties, and enforcing U.S. regulations, including trade, customs and immigration law, at and between the nation's ports of entry.

History

CBP was created on 1 March 2003 when DHS merged the inspection functions of the former U.S. Customs Service, the Immigration and Naturalization Service's inspectors and Border Patrol, and agricultural quarantine inspection into a single border agency under the slogan "one face at the border". Its customs lineage reaches back to the Tariff Act of 1789 and the creation of the Customs Service that same year.

Components

The agency employs more than 60,000 people. Its principal operational components include the Office of Field Operations, whose officers staff some 328 ports of entry; the U.S. Border Patrol, which secures the land and coastal borders between ports of entry; Air and Marine Operations, which flies and sails a large fleet of aircraft and vessels; and the Office of Trade, which administers customs revenue and trade enforcement.

Activities and controversies

CBP processes hundreds of millions of travellers and billions of dollars of imports annually, operates trusted-traveller programmes such as Global Entry, and interdicts narcotics, counterfeit goods and inadmissible agricultural products. It has also been at the centre of political debate over border enforcement, migrant detention conditions and its 100-mile border zone authority.
