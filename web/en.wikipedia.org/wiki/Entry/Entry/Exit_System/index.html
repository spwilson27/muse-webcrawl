The Entry/Exit System (EES) is an automated information technology system of the European Union for registering travellers from third countries — both short-stay visa holders and visa-exempt travellers — whenever they cross the external borders of the European countries using the system. The EES replaces the manual stamping of passports and records the date, time, and place of entry and exit, together with the traveller's biometric data (a facial image and, on first registration, fingerprints). The system is intended to modernise border management, help identify travellers who overstay their authorised period of stay, and strengthen security within the Schengen Area.

The EES was established by Regulation (EU) 2017/2226 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 30 November 2017. It is operated centrally by eu-LISA, the European Union Agency for the Operational Management of Large-Scale IT Systems in the Area of Freedom, Security and Justice, in cooperation with the border authorities of the participating states. Following several postponements of the original launch dates, the system began a progressive start of operations on 12 October 2025, with a phased rollout at border crossing points over the following months.

Self-service kiosks are used at many border crossing points to pre-register travellers' data.

Background

For decades, the authorised length of a short stay in the Schengen Area — 90 days in any 180-day period for visa-exempt third-country nationals — was tracked by border guards manually examining the entry and exit stamps in a traveller's passport. This method was slow, error-prone, and made it difficult to detect overstayers systematically: stamps can be illegible, missing, or inconsistent, and no central record existed. The European Commission first proposed a "smart borders" package in 2013, and following the 2015 European Agenda on Security and the migration and security debates of the mid-2010s, the project was relaunched in revised form in 2016, leading to the adoption of the EES Regulation in 2017.

The system is one of several large-scale EU justice and home-affairs IT systems, alongside the Schengen Information System (SIS), the Visa Information System (VIS), Eurodac, and the European Travel Information and Authorisation System (ETIAS), with interoperability components designed to let authorised users query several systems at once.

Legal basis and scope

Regulation (EU) 2017/2226 establishes the EES and defines the categories of data to be recorded, the purposes for which they may be used, and the rights of travellers. The system applies to third-country nationals admitted for a short stay (or refused entry) at the external borders of the EU and Schengen states that operate the system. Citizens of the EU, EEA, and Switzerland, and their family members exercising free-movement rights, are not registered in the EES. The countries using the system include the Schengen states; Ireland, which is not part of the Schengen Area, does not participate, and Cyprus initially remained outside while its accession to Schengen was pending.

Access to EES data is granted to border, immigration, and visa authorities for border-management purposes. Law-enforcement access — including by Europol — is permitted under stricter conditions, for the prevention, detection, or investigation of terrorist offences or other serious criminal offences.

How the system works

When a third-country national subject to the EES first crosses an external border, a border guard or a self-service kiosk creates an individual file in the system. The file contains the data from the travel document (name, date of birth, nationality, document number), the date, time, and place of entry, a facial image, and four fingerprints. On subsequent crossings, the traveller's identity is verified against the stored biometrics, and a new entry or exit record is linked to the file.

Each entry and exit is recorded electronically, and the system automatically calculates the remaining authorised stay for each traveller. Where no exit is recorded by the expiry of the authorised stay, the system produces a list of presumed overstayers that can be consulted by competent authorities. Entries of refusals of entry are also recorded. Because entries and exits are logged centrally, the manual passport stamp is no longer required for travellers registered in the EES, although stamping may continue during transitional periods and for categories of travellers outside the system.

Data recorded and retention

The EES stores alphanumeric data from the travel document, biometric data (facial image and fingerprints), and the entry/exit or refusal records. In general, records are retained for three years from the date of the exit record (or from the expiry of the authorised stay where no exit was recorded). Longer retention — up to five years — applies in specific cases, including for the records of travellers who have not left by the end of their authorised stay. Travellers have rights under EU law to access their data and to request the correction of inaccurate data or the deletion of unlawfully recorded data, addressed to the state that recorded the data.

Interplay with ETIAS and other systems

The EES is complemented by ETIAS, the travel authorisation system for visa-exempt travellers, modelled loosely on the United States ESTA. While ETIAS performs pre-travel screening and issues travel authorisations, the EES records actual movements at the border. The two systems exchange data: ETIAS checks EES records as part of its screening, and border guards performing EES checks can consult ETIAS authorisations. Through the interoperability framework adopted in 2019 — including a European search portal, a shared biometric matching service, and a common identity repository — border and law-enforcement users can query multiple systems, though the full interoperability architecture was rolled out in stages alongside the EES itself.

Rollout and delays

The launch of the EES was postponed repeatedly. Initial plans envisaged entry into operation in 2022, but delays in the development of the central system and in member states' preparations — including concerns from transport operators about queues at juxtaposed controls such as the Port of Dover and the Channel Tunnel terminals — pushed the date back several times. In late 2024 the Commission proposed a progressive start, allowing member states to phase in the system at their border crossing points over a period of months rather than switching everything on at once. The progressive start of operations began on 12 October 2025, with participating states progressively registering a growing share of travellers, and full operation — at which point passport stamping for EES travellers ends entirely — following the end of the transition period.

Criticism and concerns

The EES has attracted criticism on civil-liberties and practical grounds. Privacy advocates and the EU's own consultative bodies raised concerns about the collection and retention of biometric data from millions of travellers who are not suspected of any offence, and about the risks of function creep toward general law-enforcement use. Airlines, ferry operators, and the authorities at the UK–France juxtaposed borders warned of long queues during initial registration, since capturing fingerprints and facial images takes longer than stamping a passport. Defenders of the system respond that it replaces an unreliable paper process, improves security screening, and includes safeguards on access and retention in the Regulation.
