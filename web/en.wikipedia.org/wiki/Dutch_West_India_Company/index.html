The Dutch West India Company (Dutch: Westindische Compagnie, WIC) was a chartered company of Dutch merchants granted a monopoly of trade in the Dutch West Indies by the Republic of the Seven United Netherlands in 1621. Modelled on the Dutch East India Company (VOC), it was empowered to make treaties, build forts, maintain armies and wage war — in practice, to attack Spanish and Portuguese interests across the Atlantic world.

Activities

The company founded the colony of New Netherland in North America (including New Amsterdam, later New York), seized northeastern Brazil in 1630 (Dutch Brazil, held until 1654), and took Curaçao, Aruba and Bonaire in the Caribbean. On the West African coast it captured Portuguese forts including Elmina on the Gold Coast, and it became deeply involved in the Atlantic slave trade, shipping enslaved Africans to its plantation colonies and to Brazil.

Its greatest windfall came in 1628, when admiral Piet Hein captured the Spanish silver fleet off Cuba — the only time the entire treasure fleet was taken — a prize worth some 11.5 million guilders that financed the invasion of Brazil.

Decline and dissolution

Chronic warfare, the loss of Brazil in 1654 and of New Netherland to England (1664–1674) left the first WIC bankrupt; it was dissolved in 1674 and replaced by a second, more purely commercial company, which managed the remaining colonies until its own dissolution in 1791.
