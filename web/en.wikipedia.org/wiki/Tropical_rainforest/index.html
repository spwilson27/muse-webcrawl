Tropical rainforests are lush, warm, wet forests found in the tropics, roughly between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, characterised by high annual rainfall, high average temperatures, and the greatest biodiversity of any terrestrial biome. Despite covering only about 6–7% of Earth's land surface, they are home to an estimated half of all plant and animal species. The largest rainforests are the Amazon rainforest in South America, the Congo rainforest in central Africa, and the rainforests of Southeast Asia and New Guinea.

Characteristics

Rainfall generally exceeds 1,750 mm per year with little seasonal drought, and humidity and temperature remain high throughout the year. The vegetation forms distinct layers: emergent trees reaching 45–55 m or more above a continuous canopy at 30–40 m, a shaded understory, and a dark forest floor where little grows. Trees are typically evergreen broadleaf species with drip-tip leaves and buttress roots; epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads, and woody vines (lianas), are abundant. Soils are usually nutrient-poor — most nutrients are locked up in the vegetation itself and rapidly recycled from leaf litter — which makes cleared rainforest land poor for sustained agriculture.

Ecology and climate role

Tropical rainforests are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth and historically served as major carbon stores; deforestation converts them into significant carbon sources. Their evapotranspiration generates much of their own rainfall and influences weather far beyond the tropics — the Amazon, for example, produces "flying rivers" of moisture that water farmland in southern South America.

Threats

Rainforests are being cleared and degraded by logging, mining, cattle ranching, and commodity agriculture (soy, oil palm), and by smallholder shifting cultivation. Clearance rates peaked in several regions in the early 2000s, fell in Brazil for a time, and have risen again; scientists warn that combined deforestation and climate change could push the Amazon past a tipping point toward savanna-like conditions in its east and south. Many rainforest species face extinction before they are even described, and indigenous peoples who depend on the forests are displaced by their destruction.
