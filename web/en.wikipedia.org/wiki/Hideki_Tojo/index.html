Hideki Tojo (東條 英機, Tōjō Hideki, 30 December 1884 – 23 December 1948) was a Japanese politician and general of the Imperial Japanese Army who served as Prime Minister of Japan and President of the Imperial Rule Assistance Association from 1941 to 1944, during World War II. He assumed the premiership while concurrently holding the office of Army Minister, and later also served as Chief of the Army General Staff.

Tojo was a leading advocate of Japan's expansionist policies in Asia and of the Tripartite Pact with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy. Under his government, Japan carried out the attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, bringing the United States into the war, and continued its offensives across Southeast Asia and the Pacific. After a string of Japanese defeats, culminating in the fall of Saipan in July 1944, Tojo resigned as prime minister.

Early life and military career

Tojo was born in the Kojimachi district of Tokyo, the third son of Hidenori Tojo, a lieutenant general in the Imperial Japanese Army. He graduated from the Imperial Japanese Army Academy in 1905 and from the Army War College in 1915. He served as a military attaché in Germany after the First World War and rose through staff and command appointments, becoming associated with the Tōseiha (Control Faction) of the army.

In 1935 Tojo became commander of the military police (Kempeitai) of the Kwantung Army in Manchuria, and in 1937 he was appointed its chief of staff. He returned to Tokyo in 1938 as vice minister of war and became a prominent spokesman for a hard line against China and the Soviet Union.

Road to the premiership

Tojo became Minister of War in July 1940 in the second cabinet of Fumimaro Konoe. He supported the occupation of French Indochina and the conclusion of the Tripartite Pact. When negotiations with the United States deadlocked in the autumn of 1941, Konoe resigned, and Emperor Hirohito approved Tojo's appointment as prime minister on 18 October 1941, in the expectation that Tojo's standing in the army would allow him to restrain it while a final diplomatic effort was made. Instead, the government resolved on war.

World War II

During the first months of the war, Japanese forces overran Malaya, Singapore, the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies and Burma. Tojo consolidated political control at home through the Imperial Rule Assistance Association and a militarised cabinet. The turning points of Midway (June 1942) and Guadalcanal gradually put Japan on the defensive. In February 1944 Tojo took the additional post of Chief of the Army General Staff, an unprecedented concentration of power. The American capture of Saipan, which brought the home islands within range of B-29 bombers, forced his resignation on 22 July 1944.

Arrest, trial and execution

After Japan's surrender in September 1945, Tojo attempted suicide at his home in Setagaya as American troops arrived to arrest him; he was saved by American doctors and nursed back to health in order to stand trial. The International Military Tribunal for the Far East convicted him of war crimes, including waging wars of aggression and ordering or permitting atrocities against prisoners of war. He was sentenced to death and hanged at Sugamo Prison on 23 December 1948. His remains are among those of the Class-A war criminals enshrined at Yasukuni Shrine, a fact that remains politically controversial.
