Main battle tank

A main battle tank (MBT), also known as a battle tank or universal tank, is a tank that fills the role of armour-protected direct-fire and manoeuvre in many modern armies. It was originally intended as a replacement for separate classes of light, medium, heavy and super-heavy tanks.

History

The development of the main battle tank arose from Cold War advances in engines, suspension and armament that allowed a single medium-weight design to combine the firepower of heavy tanks, the mobility of cruiser tanks and the protection needed for breakthrough operations. The British Centurion, introduced in 1945, is often regarded as the first true MBT; it was followed by the Soviet T-54/T-55 series, the American M60 Patton, the German Leopard 1 and Leopard 2, the French Leclerc, the British Challenger 2 and the American M1 Abrams.

Characteristics

Firepower

Most Western MBTs use a 120 mm smoothbore gun (often the Rheinmetall L/44 or L/55), while Russian and Chinese designs typically use a 125 mm smoothbore gun with an autoloader. Ammunition includes armour-piercing fin-stabilised discarding sabot (APFSDS) rounds and high-explosive anti-tank (HEAT) or programmable multipurpose rounds.

Protection

Modern MBTs use composite armour such as Chobham/Dorchester, often supplemented by explosive reactive armour (ERA), slat armour and active protection systems (APS) such as Trophy, which intercept incoming projectiles.

Mobility

Despite weights of 55–70 tonnes, MBTs can reach road speeds of 60–70 km/h using powerful diesel engines or, in the case of the M1 Abrams, a gas turbine.

Role

The MBT remains the principal land-based weapon system for armoured manoeuvre warfare, though debates continue about its vulnerability to top-attack missiles, loitering munitions and drones, as highlighted in the Russo-Ukrainian War.
