Ribbon microphone

TypeDynamic microphone (velocity microphone)
InventorWalter H. Schottky and Erwin Gerlach (early 1920s)
Polar patternFigure-eight (bidirectional)
PrincipleElectromagnetic induction in a corrugated metal ribbon

A ribbon microphone, also known as a ribbon velocity microphone, is a type of dynamic microphone that uses a thin aluminium, duraluminium or nanofilm ribbon placed between the poles of a permanent magnet to convert sound into an electrical signal. Sound waves move the ribbon within the magnetic field, inducing a voltage proportional to the velocity of the air particles — hence the term "velocity microphone".

History

The ribbon microphone principle was patented in the early 1920s by Walter H. Schottky and Erwin Gerlach. In the United States, Harry F. Olson at RCA developed practical ribbon microphones in the late 1920s and early 1930s; the RCA 44 (introduced 1931) and the smaller 77 series became standard tools of radio broadcasting, recording and film sound. In Britain, the BBC–Marconi ribbon microphones and later the Coles 4038 (originally STC) became studio staples, while in Germany Beyerdynamic produced compact ribbon designs. Ribbon microphones fell from favour in the 1970s as condenser microphones rose, but interest revived in the 1990s and 2000s with new designs from Royer Labs, AEA, Coles and others, using stronger magnets and tougher ribbon materials.

Characteristics

Because the ribbon is exposed to sound on both sides, classic ribbon microphones have a bidirectional (figure-eight) polar pattern, picking up sound equally from front and rear while rejecting sound from the sides. Their frequency response is typically smooth with a natural high-frequency rolloff, giving them a warm sound prized on brass, guitar amplifiers, strings and vocals. Ribbons exhibit a strong proximity effect, boosting bass at close distances.

Handling and fragility

Traditional ribbons are extremely delicate: a blast of air can stretch or tear the foil, and passive ribbons produce a low output level that demands a quiet, high-gain preamplifier. Phantom power applied incorrectly through a faulty cable can damage some older models. Modern designs are considerably more robust, and "active" ribbon microphones incorporate buffer amplifiers that raise output and protect the ribbon electrically, though the ribbon element itself remains mechanically delicate.

Applications

Ribbon microphones are widely used as drum overheads and room microphones, on electric guitar cabinets, brass and woodwind instruments, and in classical recording, where paired figure-eight ribbons in a Blumlein configuration capture both the ensemble and the hall acoustics.
