The United States attorney general is the head of the United States Department of Justice and the chief law enforcement officer of the federal government. The attorney general is a member of the Cabinet, appointed by the president with the advice and consent of the Senate.

History

The office was created by the Judiciary Act of 1789; Edmund Randolph was the first to hold it. Originally a part-time post advising the president and representing the United States before the Supreme Court, the office grew with the creation of the Department of Justice in 1870, which consolidated federal legal functions and supervision of United States attorneys and marshals.

Duties

The attorney general oversees the Federal Bureau of Investigation, the Drug Enforcement Administration, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, the U.S. Marshals Service, and the federal Bureau of Prisons, sets federal law-enforcement priorities, and advises the president and executive departments on legal questions. Notable holders include Robert F. Kennedy (1961–1964), Robert Jackson, and Janet Reno, the first woman to serve (1993–2001).
