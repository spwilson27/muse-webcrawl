United States Colored Troops

The United States Colored Troops (USCT) were regiments of the United States Army during the American Civil War composed principally of African American soldiers, including many formerly enslaved men, usually under white commissioned officers. The Bureau of Colored Troops was created in May 1863 to organize their recruitment and administration.

Service

By the end of the war about 175,000–180,000 Black men had served in the Union Army, roughly a tenth of its strength; others served in the Navy, which was not organized under the USCT designation. More than one hundred USCT infantry regiments were raised, along with cavalry, artillery and engineer units. They fought at Port Hudson, Milliken's Bend, Fort Wagner, the Petersburg Campaign, Nashville and numerous smaller engagements, and performed extensive garrison, labour and occupation duties.

Discrimination and risks

Black soldiers initially received lower pay than white soldiers and protested until Congress equalized pay in 1864. They were often assigned disproportionate fatigue duty and suffered high mortality from disease. Captured Black soldiers faced enslavement or death rather than normal prisoner-of-war treatment, and Confederate forces massacred USCT troops in incidents such as Fort Pillow. Black officers were rare, though chaplains, surgeons and a small number of line officers were commissioned.

Legacy

Military service strengthened African American claims to citizenship and political rights after emancipation. Frederick Douglass argued that once a Black man wore the brass letters “U.S.” and carried a musket, no power could deny he had earned citizenship. USCT veterans became community leaders, officeholders and pension claimants, and their service is commemorated by the African American Civil War Memorial in Washington, D.C.
