In mathematics, the Dirichlet convolution is a binary operation defined for arithmetic functions — functions from the positive integers to the complex numbers. It is a central tool of multiplicative number theory and was used extensively by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet.

Definition

If f and g are arithmetic functions, their Dirichlet convolution f ∗ g is defined by

(f ∗ g)(n) = Σd | n f(d) · g(n/d) = Σab = n f(a) · g(b)

where the sum extends over all positive divisors d of n.

Properties

The Dirichlet convolution is commutative and associative, and distributes over addition. The constant function 1 convolved with the Möbius function μ gives the delta function, which is the identity for the operation; this yields the Möbius inversion formula. The convolution of two multiplicative functions is multiplicative. Under convolution, arithmetic functions with f(1) ≠ 0 form an abelian group, the group of units being exactly those functions invertible via a Dirichlet inverse.

Examples

Writing 1 for the constant function and Id for the identity function n ↦ n: the divisor summatory function satisfies σ = Id ∗ 1, and Euler's totient satisfies φ = Id ∗ μ. The convolution 1 ∗ 1 gives d(n), the number of divisors of n.

Dirichlet series

Dirichlet convolution corresponds to ordinary multiplication of the associated Dirichlet series: if F(s) and G(s) are the series of f and g, then F(s)G(s) is the series of f ∗ g. This correspondence underlies many proofs in analytic number theory, including manipulations of the Riemann zeta function.
