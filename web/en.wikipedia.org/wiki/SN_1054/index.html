SN 1054 is a supernova that was first observed on 4 July 1054 and remained visible until 6 April 1056. It is one of the best-documented "guest stars" in pre-telescopic astronomy: Chinese astronomers recorded it near the star Tianguan (ζ Tauri) in the constellation Taurus, and it was also likely recorded by Japanese observers and in rock art by Ancestral Puebloans in North America. The explosion created the Crab Nebula, the first astronomical object identified with a historical supernova.

Historical records

Chinese court astronomers of the Song dynasty noted that the guest star appeared in the southeast and was visible in daylight for 23 days, shining, by some accounts, as brightly as Venus; it remained visible at night for nearly two years. The star was considered an omen and its appearance was recorded in official histories. No unambiguous contemporary European record survives, though proposed references include a coin of Constantine IX and a report by the physician Ibn Butlan in Constantinople.

The remnant

In 1731 the English astronomer John Bevis observed the nebula now known as the Crab Nebula; Charles Messier independently rediscovered it in 1758, making it the first entry (M1) in his catalogue. In the 1920s, astronomers including Knut Lundmark and Edwin Hubble noted that the nebula's expansion rate, traced backwards, matched the 1054 event, establishing the identification. In 1968 the Crab Pulsar — a rapidly rotating neutron star spinning about 30 times per second — was discovered at the nebula's centre, the collapsed core of the progenitor star, which is thought to have had a mass of roughly 8–10 solar masses.
