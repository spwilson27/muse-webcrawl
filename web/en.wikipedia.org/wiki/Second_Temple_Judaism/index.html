Second Temple Judaism

Second Temple Judaism is the Jewish religion as it developed during the Second Temple period, which began with the construction of the Second Temple around 516 BCE and ended with its destruction by the Romans in 70 CE. This roughly 600-year span covers the Persian, Hellenistic and Roman eras, and saw the formation of the Hebrew Bible, the rise of the synagogue, and the emergence of diverse Jewish movements — Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes and Zealots — as well as early Christianity.

History

After the Babylonian exile, the Persian king Cyrus permitted the Jews to return to Judah (Yehud) and rebuild the Temple, which was dedicated around 516 BCE. The community came under Hellenistic rule after the conquests of Alexander the Great; the Maccabean revolt against the Seleucids (167–141 BCE) produced the independent Hasmonean kingdom. Rome conquered Jerusalem in 63 BCE, and Herod the Great, installed as king, rebuilt the Temple on a monumental scale. The period ended with the First Jewish–Roman War and the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE.

Religious life

Worship centred on the Temple sacrifices performed by the priesthood, alongside prayer, Torah study and synagogue gatherings in towns throughout Judea and the diaspora. The period produced a rich literature, including the final shaping of the Hebrew Bible, its Greek translation (the Septuagint), the Dead Sea Scrolls and numerous apocalyptic works. Beliefs in the resurrection of the dead, angels and a coming messiah developed or became widespread during this era.

Legacy

Second Temple Judaism is the matrix from which both rabbinic Judaism and Christianity emerged. After 70 CE, the Pharisaic-rabbinic tradition reshaped Judaism around the synagogue, prayer and study in place of sacrifice.
