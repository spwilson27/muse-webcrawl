Cross-laminated timber

A CLT panel showing its crosswise layers of lumber boards.

Cross-laminated timber (CLT) is a subcategory of engineered wood: a wood panel product made from gluing together layers of solid-sawn lumber, each layer oriented perpendicular to adjacent layers. Developed in Europe in the early 1990s, in Austria and Germany, CLT panels are used as prefabricated structural elements — walls, floors and roofs — in residential, commercial and high-rise "mass timber" construction.

Manufacture and properties

Panels typically consist of three, five, seven or nine layers of kiln-dried boards, most often spruce, pine or Douglas fir, bonded with structural adhesives under pressure. The crosswise arrangement gives the panel dimensional stability and strength in two directions, allowing it to span and carry loads comparably to concrete or steel elements of much greater weight. Panels are cut to size by computer-controlled machinery, including openings for doors, windows and services, before delivery to site.

Use in construction

CLT has been used in some of the world's tallest timber buildings, including the 18-storey Mjøstårnet in Norway and the 25-storey Ascent tower in Milwaukee. Advocates point to its lower embodied carbon compared with concrete and steel, rapid on-site assembly, and the sequestration of carbon in the wood itself. Building codes in many jurisdictions have been revised to permit taller mass-timber structures, subject to fire-performance requirements, since CLT chars predictably and retains structural capacity in fire.
