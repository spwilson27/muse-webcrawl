Solar flare

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A brilliant solar flare erupting from the Sun's surface in ultraviolet false colour
A brilliant solar flare erupting from the Sun's surface in ultraviolet false colour

A solar flare is a relatively intense, localised eruption of electromagnetic radiation in the Sun's atmosphere — a sudden flash of increased brightness, usually near sunspots, lasting minutes to hours. Flares release the energy of millions of hydrogen bombs across the spectrum (radio to gamma rays), heat plasma to tens of millions of kelvin, accelerate particles to near light speed, and often accompany Coronal mass ejections; the largest (X-class) can disrupt radio communications and endanger satellites and astronauts.

Mechanism

Flares are powered by Magnetic reconnection in stressed active-region fields: twisted loops snap into lower-energy configurations, dumping stored magnetic energy into particles and heat. The first observed flare — Carrington and Hodgson's white-light sighting of 1 September 1859 — preceded the great geomagnetic storm by a day, the link that founded space-weather science.

Classification and effects

Flares are classed A, B, C, M, X by peak X-ray flux (each letter tenfold; X-class events sometimes exceed X10, and the 2003 'Halloween' flare saturated detectors at about X28+). Radio blackouts on Earth's dayside are routine for M and X flares; the particle storms threaten aviation routes and space hardware, and flare forecasting (probabilistic, from active-region magnetic complexity) is a growth industry of the space age.
