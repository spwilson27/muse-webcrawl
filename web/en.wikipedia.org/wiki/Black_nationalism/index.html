Black nationalism

Black nationalism is a political and social movement that advocates the self-determination, unity, and cultural pride of Black people, sometimes including the creation of a separate Black nation-state and often emphasising Black economic independence and control of institutions within Black communities. It has taken religious, cultural, and revolutionary forms in the United States, the Caribbean, and Africa.

History

Early expressions included 19th-century emigrationism — Martin Delany and others promoted Black settlement in Africa or the Caribbean as an escape from American racism. Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association (founded 1914) built the largest mass movement, with its Black Star Line shipping company, Negro World newspaper, and "Back to Africa" vision. The Nation of Islam, led by Elijah Muhammad from the 1930s, combined Black nationalist economics and separatism with its own theology.

Black Power era

In the 1960s, Malcolm X after his break with the Nation of Islam, Stokely Carmichael's call for "Black Power" (1966), and the Black Panther Party carried nationalist ideas into the civil rights era, stressing community control, cultural nationalism (as in Maulana Karenga's creation of Kwanzaa), and alliances with anti-colonial movements. Pan-Africanism — associated with W. E. B. Du Bois and Kwame Nkrumah — is a closely related internationalist strand.

Criticism and legacy

Critics, including integrationist civil rights leaders, have questioned separatism and some groups' doctrines; supporters credit Black nationalism with fostering racial pride, Black-owned businesses, and political organisation whose legacy runs through later movements, including aspects of Black Lives Matter.
