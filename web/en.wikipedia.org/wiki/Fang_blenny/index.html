Fang blenny

Fang blennies are small marine fishes of the genus Meiacanthus in the family Blenniidae, found in the Indian and Pacific Oceans. They are notable for possessing large, grooved canine teeth (fangs) in the lower jaw connected to venom glands — one of the few groups of venomous fishes with a true venom delivery system in their bite.

Venom

The venom of Meiacanthus is unusual. Rather than causing pain like most defensive venoms, experiments on the striped fang blenny (Meiacanthus grammistes) show it contains opioid-like peptides that lower the victim's blood pressure and cause dizziness, slowing a predator's coordination and allowing the blenny to escape. The bite itself is reported to be relatively painless.

Mimicry

Fang blennies are central to a network of mimicry on coral reefs. Venomous Meiacanthus species serve as models for harmless mimics such as the blenny Petroscirtes species and the juvenile cardinalfish, which gain protection from resembling them. Some fang blennies, such as the forktail blenny, mimic cleaner wrasses and use the disguise to approach larger fish and nip at their skin and scales.

Behaviour

Fang blennies shelter in empty worm tubes and rock crevices. Males guard eggs laid in these cavities. Several species are popular in the marine aquarium trade because of their coloration and small size.
