Nanofluidics

Nanofluidics is the study of the behaviour, manipulation, and control of fluids confined in structures with characteristic dimensions in the nanometre range (typically 1–100 nm). It is a subfield of microfluidics and nanotechnology, emerging from advances in nanofabrication in the early 21st century.

Physical phenomena

At nanometre scales, physical characteristics normally dominated by bulk behaviour — such as viscosity and volumetric flow — are superseded by surface-dominated effects. The electrical double layer (EDL) at a charged channel wall has a thickness characterised by the Debye length, typically 1–100 nm in aqueous solutions; in nanochannels the double layers from opposite walls can overlap, making ion transport strongly dependent on surface charge. Consequences include ion selectivity, ionic current rectification in asymmetric (for example conical) nanopores, concentration polarisation, and electrokinetic phenomena such as electro-osmotic flow.

In the narrowest channels — sometimes called extended nanospace (10–1000 nm) — water near surfaces shows altered properties, including changed viscosity, proton mobility and reaction kinetics, which researchers exploit for chemical analysis.

Fabrication

Nanofluidic devices are fabricated using electron-beam lithography, focused ion beam milling, nanoimprint lithography, track etching of polymer membranes, and the use of naturally nanoscale structures such as carbon nanotubes, biological pores (e.g. α-hemolysin) and 2D-material slits (graphene, MoS2). Sealing fluidic networks at this scale without collapsing or blocking channels is a principal technical challenge.

Applications

Applications include single-molecule DNA analysis and sequencing (notably nanopore sequencing), protein and ion sensing, desalination membranes, osmotic ("blue") energy harvesting, nanofluidic diodes and transistors, and lab-on-a-chip analytical systems with zeptolitre-to-attolitre sample volumes.
