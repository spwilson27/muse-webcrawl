Australopithecus afarensis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A reconstruction of 'Lucy', the famous Australopithecus afarensis skeleton, in a museum display]
A reconstruction of 'Lucy', the famous Australopithecus afarensis skeleton, in a museum display

Australopithecus afarensis is an extinct species of australopithecine hominin that lived in East Africa between about 3.9 and 2.9 million years ago. It is best known from 'Lucy' (AL 288-1), the 40%-complete skeleton found at Hadar in 1974, and from the fossilised footprints at Laetoli, which together show a creature that walked upright on the ground while retaining adaptations for climbing.

Discovery

Donald Johanson's team found Lucy on 24 November 1974 — named for the Beatles' 'Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds' playing in camp. With Mary Leakey's Laetoli footprints (1978) and the 'First Family' group at Hadar, Johanson, Tim White and Yves Coppens named the species in 1978, uniting specimens from Ethiopia and Tanzania.

Anatomy and behaviour

Afarensis stood 1.1–1.5 m, with a small brain (roughly 380–550 cm³, ape-sized), long arms, curved fingers and a pelvis and knee adapted for habitual bipedalism — a mosaic anatomy central to debates over how bipedalism evolved. Strong sexual dimorphism is claimed by some and disputed by others. The Dikika child ('Selam', 2000) added a nearly complete juvenile, including a hyoid bone, and cut-marked bones from Dikika (contested) hint at early tool-assisted butchery before stone tools are otherwise known (Lomekwi).

Phylogeny

Afarensis is widely regarded as ancestral to later australopithecines and probably to Homo, though the discovery of contemporaneous species (such as Australopithecus deyiremeda and Kenyanthropus) suggests a bushier mid-Pliocene hominin radiation. The species spans nearly a million years with little change.
