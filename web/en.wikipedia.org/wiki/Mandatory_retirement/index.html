Mandatory retirement

Mandatory retirement is the compelled retirement of employees who reach a specific age (the mandatory retirement age), at which they must leave their employment. The practice is justified by some on grounds of safety (for occupations such as airline pilots and firefighters), organizational renewal, or career progression for younger employees; it is criticized as age discrimination where workers remain capable.

By jurisdiction

In the United States, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 progressively raised and then, in 1986, largely abolished mandatory retirement ages for most occupations, with exceptions including airline pilots (subject to a statutory age limit, raised from 60 to 65 in 2007), certain public-safety officers, and some executives. Tenured university professors lost their exemption in 1994.

The United Kingdom abolished its default retirement age of 65 in 2011. Canada has eliminated mandatory retirement in most jurisdictions and occupations. Many European countries historically combined pension eligibility ages with mandatory retirement clauses, which have been progressively restricted under EU age-discrimination law. Japan traditionally features mandatory retirement at a set age (often 60–65) followed in many cases by re-employment at reduced wages.

Debate

Economists debate the "lump of labour" assumptions behind mandatory retirement and its effects on youth employment, pension finances, and productivity. Demographic aging and longer healthy life expectancy have driven the global trend toward later and more flexible retirement.
