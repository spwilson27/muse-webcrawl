Toxoplasmosis

Toxoplasmosis is a parasitic disease caused by the protozoan Toxoplasma gondii. Cats and other felids are the definitive hosts, in whose intestines the parasite reproduces sexually; virtually all warm-blooded animals, including humans, can be intermediate hosts carrying tissue cysts.

Transmission

Humans are infected mainly by eating undercooked meat containing cysts, ingesting oocysts from soil, water, or cat faeces, or congenitally from mother to fetus. A large fraction of the world's population — estimates range from roughly a quarter to a half — carries latent infection.

Clinical features

In immunocompetent people infection is usually asymptomatic or a mild flu-like illness, after which the parasite persists latently in tissue cysts. In immunocompromised patients, reactivation can cause life-threatening encephalitis. Congenital infection, especially in early pregnancy, can cause miscarriage or the classic triad of chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, and intracranial calcifications.

Treatment and prevention

Severe disease is treated with combinations such as pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine, or spiramycin in pregnancy. Prevention centres on cooking meat thoroughly, washing produce, and hygiene around cat litter, particularly for pregnant people and the immunocompromised.
