Dermatology is the branch of medicine dealing with the skin. It is a speciality with both medical and surgical aspects. A dermatologist is a specialist medical doctor who manages diseases related to skin, hair, nails, and some cosmetic problems.

Scope

The skin is the largest organ of the body, and dermatologists diagnose and treat more than 3,000 conditions, including eczema (atopic dermatitis), psoriasis, acne, rosacea, skin infections such as impetigo and cellulitis, allergic contact dermatitis, autoimmune blistering diseases, and skin cancers including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma. Dermatology also covers hair disorders such as alopecia, nail diseases, and sexually transmitted infections presenting on the skin — the field's historical pairing with venereology persists in many countries.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on visual examination, aided by dermatoscopy (examination of skin lesions with a magnifying device that improves detection of melanoma), patch testing for allergies, skin biopsy and dermatopathology, and mycology and microbiology. Treatments range from topical therapies (corticosteroids, retinoids, calcineurin inhibitors) and systemic drugs (including biologic agents targeting immune pathways in psoriasis and eczema) to phototherapy, cryotherapy, laser treatment and surgery. Mohs micrographic surgery is a specialised technique for removing skin cancers with maximal sparing of healthy tissue.

Training

Dermatologists typically complete medical school followed by a residency in dermatology (three to four years in the United States), with optional fellowships in dermatopathology, procedural dermatology or paediatric dermatology. The specialty is among the most competitive in medicine.

History

Skin diseases were described in ancient Egyptian and Greek medicine; modern dermatology developed in the 19th century in Vienna and London, with figures such as Ferdinand von Hebra establishing systematic classification of skin diseases.
