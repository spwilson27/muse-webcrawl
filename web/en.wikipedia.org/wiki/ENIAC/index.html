ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer) was the first programmable, electronic, general-purpose digital computer, completed in 1945 and unveiled at the University of Pennsylvania in February 1946. Designed by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert for the United States Army, it was built to calculate artillery firing tables for the Ballistic Research Laboratory, though its first large problem was a feasibility calculation for the hydrogen bomb.

Design and operation

ENIAC contained over 17,000 vacuum tubes and occupied about 167 m². It performed arithmetic in decimal using twenty 10-digit accumulators and could execute about 5,000 additions per second — roughly a thousand times faster than electromechanical machines. Programming originally meant physically rewiring plugboards and setting switches, a task carried out largely by six women programmers — Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Meltzer, Fran Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman — who were long unrecognised. In 1948 ENIAC was adapted to run stored programs in a limited fashion.

Legacy

ENIAC was moved to Aberdeen Proving Ground in 1947 and ran until 1955. Its design influenced the EDVAC and the von Neumann architecture. A 1973 court ruling invalidated the ENIAC patent, finding that Mauchly had derived ideas from John Vincent Atanasoff's earlier ABC computer. Sections of the machine are preserved at the Smithsonian and the University of Pennsylvania.
