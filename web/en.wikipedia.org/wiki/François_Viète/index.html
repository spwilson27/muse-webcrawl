François Viète, Seigneur de la Bigotière (1540 – 23 February 1603), known in Latin as Franciscus Vieta, was a French mathematician, lawyer, and privy councillor to Kings Henry III and Henry IV of France. He is regarded as one of the founders of modern algebra for his systematic use of letters to represent both unknowns and known parameters in equations — a decisive step beyond the rhetorical and syncopated algebra of earlier writers — and he is remembered for Viète's formulas, relating the coefficients of a polynomial to sums and products of its roots.

Life

Viète was born in Fontenay-le-Comte in Poitou and trained as a lawyer at the University of Poitiers. He served as a councillor in the Parlement of Brittany at Rennes and later as a royal councillor in Tours and Paris. A Huguenot by family background, he nonetheless enjoyed the confidence of the Catholic Henry IV. During the war with Spain, Viète rendered a famous service by deciphering intercepted Spanish correspondence written in a complex cipher of more than 500 characters; the Spanish, convinced that no human could break it, complained to the Pope that the French must be using sorcery.

Mathematical work

Viète pursued mathematics as a private passion, publishing at his own expense. His In artem analyticem isagoge ("Introduction to the Analytic Art," 1591) laid out his new algebra: vowels denoted unknown quantities and consonants known parameters, allowing general rules for manipulating equations. He gave his name to the formulas connecting polynomial coefficients with the roots, published (in modern form) in his posthumous works, and in 1593 he produced the first infinite product in mathematics — his formula expressing 2/π as an infinite product of nested radicals, derived from approximating the circle by polygons.

Viète also worked on trigonometry, producing extensive tables and the cosine law for plane triangles, solved the famous 45th-degree challenge problem posed by Adriaan van Roomen, and proposed reforms of the calendar. His collected works, Opera Mathematica, were published in 1646 by Frans van Schooten. Through Descartes and others, Viète's analytic art became the foundation of modern symbolic mathematics.
