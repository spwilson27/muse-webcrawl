Kaluza–Klein theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Illustration of a curled-up extra dimension: at each point of ordinary space, a tiny circle]
Kaluza–Klein: a fifth dimension curled into a tiny circle at every point.

Kaluza–Klein theory is a classical Unified field theory of gravitation and electromagnetism built around the idea of a fifth dimension beyond the usual four of spacetime. In 1921 Theodor Kaluza, a mathematician at Königsberg, wrote to Albert Einstein with the observation that General relativity in five dimensions automatically contains Maxwell's equations: the components of the 5D metric split into the 4D metric (gravity), a vector (electromagnetism) and a scalar (the 'dilaton').

In 1926 Oskar Klein gave the fifth dimension a quantum interpretation: it is compactified — curled into a circle of radius near the Planck length — which explains why it is unseen and quantises electric charge. Einstein was initially enthusiastic, then cooled. The idea lay dormant until the 1970s–80s, when supergravity and String theory required compact extra dimensions for entirely different reasons; Kaluza–Klein reduction became their standard tool, and the massive 'Kaluza–Klein tower' of states is a searched-for signature of extra dimensions at colliders.

Legacy

Kaluza–Klein theory was the first unification by geometry — the template for all later higher-dimensional physics, including braneworlds and the compactified Calabi–Yau spaces of string theory. Klein's explanation of charge quantisation from momentum in the compact dimension is the same logic Dirac applied to monopoles: topology and quantum mechanics conspiring to make charge discrete.
