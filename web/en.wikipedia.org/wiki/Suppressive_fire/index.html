Suppressive fire

Suppressive fire degrades the enemy's ability to shoot back effectively.

In military science, suppressive fire is "fire that degrades the performance of an enemy force below the level needed to fulfill its mission". When used to protect exposed friendly troops advancing on the battlefield, it is commonly called covering fire. Suppression is usually only effective for the duration of the fire; it is one of three types of fire support, alongside neutralisation and destruction.

Purpose

The aim of suppressive fire is not necessarily to kill enemy personnel but to force them to take cover, reducing the accuracy and volume of their return fire and preventing them from observing or manoeuvring freely. This creates a window in which friendly forces can move — for example, in the "fire and movement" tactic, where one element fires while another advances. Artillery, machine guns, mortars, and automatic cannon are commonly used to deliver suppressive fire.

History

The importance of suppression grew with the firepower of modern weapons. In the First World War, creeping barrages suppressed defenders in their trenches while infantry advanced behind the curtain of shells. Modern doctrine institutionalizes suppression at every level, from a rifleman's aimed shots to artillery "suppression of enemy air defenses" (SEAD) missions by aircraft.

Effects

Studies of combat effectiveness note that suppression is primarily psychological as well as physical: troops under accurate, sustained fire tend to keep their heads down and fire less effectively even when casualties are few. Once the suppressive fire lifts, however, the effect dissipates quickly, so timing with the assaulting element is critical.
