Chorea

Chorea is an abnormal involuntary movement disorder, one of a group of neurological disorders called dyskinesias. The term chorea derives from the Ancient Greek word χορεία (a kind of dance), as the quick movements of the feet or hands are comparable to dancing. Chorea is characterised by brief, irregular, unpredictable, non-stereotyped movements that flow from one body part to another.

Causes

The most famous cause of chorea is Huntington's disease, an inherited neurodegenerative disorder. In children, the most common cause is Sydenham's chorea, a manifestation of rheumatic fever following group A streptococcal infection. Other causes include stroke affecting the basal ganglia, Wilson's disease, hyperthyroidism, systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid syndrome, and adverse effects of levodopa or antipsychotic drugs (tardive dyskinesia). Chorea gravidarum occurs during pregnancy.

Presentation

Patients show motor impersistence — an inability to sustain a contraction, such as tongue protrusion ("flycatcher tongue") or grip ("milkmaid's grip"). The movements disappear during sleep and worsen with stress or attempted voluntary movement.

Pathophysiology

Chorea is thought to result from dysfunction of the indirect pathway of the basal ganglia, leading to reduced inhibitory output and excessive thalamocortical drive. In Huntington's disease, degeneration of striatal medium spiny neurons is the principal substrate.
