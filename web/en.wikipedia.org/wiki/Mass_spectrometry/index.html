Mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry (MS) is an analytical technique that measures the mass-to-charge ratio of ions. A sample is ionised, the ions are accelerated and sorted by mass (by magnets, electric fields or time of flight), and a detector records a spectrum of peaks at each mass.

MS identifies unknown compounds, determines molecular formulas from exact masses, reads protein sequences, dates and sources materials by isotope ratios, and detects substances at parts-per-trillion. It is usually coupled to chromatography (GC–MS, LC–MS) to analyse mixtures.

[Image: A mass spectrometer instrument and a sample mass spectrum printout]

History

J. J. Thomson's parabola spectrograph (1912–13) first revealed isotopes (neon-20 and neon-22); Francis Aston's mass spectrograph (1919) measured masses precisely and won the 1922 Nobel Prize. Soft ionisation methods (electrospray, MALDI, 1980s) extended MS to fragile biomolecules.

Reading a spectrum

The molecular ion peak gives the molar mass; fragment peaks are a structural puzzle chemists solve like a jigsaw; isotope patterns (e.g. chlorine's 3:1 pair at M and M+2) betray which elements are present.
