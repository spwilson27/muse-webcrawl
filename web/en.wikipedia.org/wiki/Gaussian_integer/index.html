Gaussian integer

A Gaussian integer is a complex number whose real and imaginary parts are both integers. The set of Gaussian integers, denoted Z[i], forms an integral domain under the usual addition and multiplication of complex numbers, and is a central object of algebraic number theory. Gaussian integers were introduced by Carl Friedrich Gauss in his work on biquadratic reciprocity (1828–1832), in which extending arithmetic from the integers to Z[i] proved essential.

Basic properties

The norm of a Gaussian integer a + bi is N = a² + b², the square of its distance from the origin. The norm is multiplicative. The units of Z[i] are the four elements of norm 1: ±1 and ±i. Crucially, the ring admits a Euclidean division algorithm using the norm: for any two Gaussian integers α and β ≠ 0, there exist q and r with α = qβ + r and N(r) < N(β). Hence Z[i] is a Euclidean domain, and therefore a principal ideal domain and a unique factorisation domain — factorisation into Gaussian primes is unique up to units and ordering.

Primes

An ordinary prime number p ≡ 3 (mod 4) remains prime in Z[i], while 2 and primes p ≡ 1 (mod 4) split into products of non-unit Gaussian primes: 2 = −i(1 + i)² and, for example, 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i). This splitting behaviour underlies Fermat's theorem that a prime p ≡ 1 (mod 4) is a sum of two squares, and conversely.

Applications

Gaussian integers are used to characterise representations of integers as sums of two squares, to find Pythagorean triples, and in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem for exponent 4. They also appear in signal processing and coding theory, where constellations of Gaussian-integer-like lattice points are used in modulation schemes.
