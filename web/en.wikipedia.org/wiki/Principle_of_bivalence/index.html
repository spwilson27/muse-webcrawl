Principle of bivalence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, the principle of bivalence states that every declarative sentence expressing a proposition (of a theory under inspection) has exactly one truth value, either true or false. A logic satisfying this principle is called a two-valued logic or bivalent logic.

The principle of bivalence should not be confused with the law of excluded middle, which states that for any proposition, either that proposition is true or its negation is true. The law of excluded middle is a principle of proof expressible within a logic, while bivalence is a semantic principle about truth values; some non-classical logics accept the law of excluded middle while rejecting bivalence, and vice versa.

Relation to other principles

Bivalence is connected with the laws of non-contradiction and excluded middle that characterise classical logic as developed from Aristotle. Aristotle himself appears to have hesitated over bivalence for statements about future contingent events — the famous sea-battle argument of De Interpretatione, chapter 9 — since holding that "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true or false seems to entail fatalism.

Rejections of bivalence

Many non-classical logics reject bivalence:

  • Intuitionistic logic rejects it in favour of provability-based semantics; a proposition is not taken to have a truth value independent of our ability to prove it.
  • Many-valued logics, such as the three-valued logics of Łukasiewicz and Kleene, admit an intermediate value (e.g. "indeterminate" or "unknown").
  • Fuzzy logic assigns degrees of truth throughout the interval between 0 and 1.
  • Supervaluationism, in the philosophy of vagueness, treats borderline statements ("this man is bald") as neither true nor false.
