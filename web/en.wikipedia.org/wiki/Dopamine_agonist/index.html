A dopamine agonist is a compound that activates dopamine receptors, mimicking the neurotransmitter dopamine. They are used chiefly in Parkinson's disease, restless legs syndrome and prolactin-secreting pituitary tumours.

Medical uses

In Parkinson's disease, agonists such as pramipexole, ropinirole and the rotigotine patch stimulate D2/D3 receptors in the striatum, providing symptomatic relief, often before levodopa is started in younger patients. Ergot-derived agonists (bromocriptine, cabergoline) powerfully suppress prolactin and are used for prolactinomas; apomorphine is given by injection or infusion as rescue therapy for severe "off" episodes.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include nausea, somnolence and sudden sleep attacks, orthostatic hypotension, hallucinations and peripheral oedema. A clinically important complication is impulse control disorders — pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping and binge eating — linked especially to D3-preferring agonists. Ergot agonists can cause fibrotic reactions, including cardiac valvulopathy, which has curtailed their use in Parkinson's disease.
