A polyamine is an organic compound having more than two amino groups. Alkyl polyamines occur naturally but some are also synthetic. Natural polyamines are small aliphatic polycations found in virtually all living cells, where they occur in millimolar concentrations and play essential roles in cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation. The most common polyamines in eukaryotes are putrescine, spermidine, and spermine; prokaryotes and plants also produce cadaverine and a variety of longer polyamines.

Biological roles

Because they are positively charged at physiological pH, polyamines bind strongly to negatively charged molecules such as DNA, RNA, proteins, and phospholipids. They modulate chromatin structure, DNA replication and transcription, mRNA translation, and ion channel function. Spermidine is the sole donor of the aminobutyl group used to form hypusine in the translation factor eIF5A, a modification essential for cell viability. Polyamines also protect cells against oxidative damage and are involved in stress responses in plants.

Biosynthesis

In animals and fungi, the pathway begins with the decarboxylation of ornithine by ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) to form putrescine. ODC is one of the most tightly regulated enzymes in metabolism, controlled by antizymes and antizyme inhibitors. Decarboxylated S-adenosylmethionine provides aminopropyl groups that spermidine synthase and spermine synthase transfer to putrescine and spermidine respectively. Plants and many bacteria can also synthesise putrescine from arginine via agmatine.

Catabolism and interconversion

Polyamines are interconverted and degraded by enzymes including spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase (SSAT), acetylpolyamine oxidase, and spermine oxidase. Acetylated polyamines are excreted in urine, and elevated levels have been investigated as biomarkers. Catabolism generates hydrogen peroxide and reactive aldehydes, contributing to both normal signalling and, when unbalanced, tissue damage.

Medical significance

Because rapidly dividing cells demand high polyamine levels, the pathway is a target for therapy. The ODC inhibitor eflornithine (α-difluoromethylornithine, DFMO) is used against African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) and hirsutism, and was approved for maintenance treatment of high-risk neuroblastoma. Polyamine analogues and transport inhibitors are investigated as anticancer agents. Parasites such as Trypanosoma brucei depend on spermidine to make trypanothione, their unique redox cofactor.

Chemical and industrial uses

Synthetic polymeric polyamines, such as polyethyleneimine, are used as flocculants in water treatment, in paper manufacture, as chelating agents, and as transfection reagents for delivering nucleic acids into cells. Alkyl polyamines also serve as epoxy curing agents and corrosion inhibitors.
