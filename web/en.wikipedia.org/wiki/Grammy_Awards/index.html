Grammy Awards

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Grammy trophy: a gilded gramophone, its bell flaring gold against the stage lights]

The Grammy Awards (stylised as GRAMMYs; originally called the Gramophone Awards) are awards presented by the Recording Academy of the United States to recognise outstanding achievements in the music industry. First held on 4 May 1959, the televised ceremony is music's biggest night; the trophy — a gilded gramophone — is one of the entertainment industry's four EGOT pillars (Emmy, Grammy, Oscar, Tony).

The awards span dozens of categories across genres plus the 'General Field': Album of the Year, Record of the Year, Song of the Year and Best New Artist. Beyoncé holds the record for most Grammy wins (32, reached in 2023); Quincy Jones and others trail in the high twenties by most counts.

The institution

The Recording Academy (founded 1957 by Los Angeles record executives, partly to counter rock and roll's disrepute with craft legitimacy) votes through its membership of performers, producers and engineers. The ceremony's modern form — wall-to-wall live performance rather than award-giving — made it pop's annual summit meeting and reset concert staging standards yearly.

Arguments

The Grammys' recurring controversies — genre ghettoes, generational blindness (the Academy's slowness to honour hip-hop in the top categories became the 2010s' standing complaint), opaque voting committees (reformed after 2020's public rupture with its own CEO) — are the tax on being the industry's establishment. The ceremony's power in sales and legacy-making remains unmatched in music.
