Sub-replacement fertility

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sub-replacement fertility is a total fertility rate (TFR) below the replacement level — the average number of children per woman needed for each generation to exactly replace itself in the absence of migration. In low-mortality societies the replacement level is approximately 2.1 children per woman, slightly above two because not all children survive to reproductive age and slightly more boys than girls are born.

Extent

If sustained over the long term, sub-replacement fertility leads to population ageing and eventually to population decline. More than half of the world's countries now have fertility below replacement, including nearly all of Europe and East Asia. Some countries have recorded exceptionally low rates: South Korea's TFR fell below 0.8 in the early 2020s, the lowest national figure ever recorded, while China, Japan, Italy, and Spain have all reported rates around or below 1.3 — a threshold demographers have termed "lowest-low" fertility.

Causes

Sub-replacement fertility is the late stage of the demographic transition, driven by urbanisation, the rising cost of housing and child-rearing, higher female education and labour-force participation, delayed marriage and childbearing, widespread access to contraception, and changing social norms about family size. In some countries, economic insecurity and work–family conflict are emphasised; in others, partnership decline among young adults plays a central role.

Consequences and policy responses

Consequences include a rising old-age dependency ratio, pressure on pension and healthcare systems, and, in some countries, school closures and regional depopulation. Governments have responded with pro-natalist measures such as child allowances, subsidised childcare, parental leave, and housing support, though evidence suggests such policies typically raise fertility only modestly. Immigration can offset population decline in the shorter term but does not by itself stabilise age structure.
