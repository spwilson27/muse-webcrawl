Isotopes of nickel

Stable isotopes: ⁵⁸Ni, ⁶⁰Ni, ⁶¹Ni, ⁶²Ni, ⁶⁴Ni

Most abundant: ⁵⁸Ni (68.077%)

Notable: ⁵⁹Ni, ⁶³Ni, ⁵⁶Ni (supernovae)

Naturally occurring nickel (₂₈Ni) is composed of five stable isotopes: ⁵⁸Ni, ⁶⁰Ni, ⁶¹Ni, ⁶²Ni, and ⁶⁴Ni, with ⁵⁸Ni the most abundant at about 68 percent. Nickel-62 has the highest binding energy per nucleon of any nuclide, slightly above iron-58 and iron-56.

Radioisotopes

Nickel-59, with a half-life of about 76,000 years, is produced by cosmic-ray activation and is used in dating meteorites and ice samples. Nickel-63 (half-life ~100 years) is used in electron capture detectors and betavoltaic devices. Nickel-56, though it decays in days, is produced in enormous quantities in core-collapse supernovae, and its decay chain to cobalt-56 and iron-56 powers supernova light curves.

Applications

Nickel isotope ratios are used in geochemistry to trace Earth's early history and the origin of meteorites. The near-extinct radionuclide ⁶⁰Fe decays to ⁶⁰Ni, and excesses of ⁶⁰Ni in meteorites provide evidence about the early Solar System.
