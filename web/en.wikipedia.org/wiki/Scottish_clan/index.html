Scottish clan

A Scottish clan (from the Gaelic clann, "children, progeny") is a kinship group among the Scottish people, historically centred in the Highlands, which gives its members a sense of shared identity and descent. Clans were the principal social and political structure of Highland Scotland from the Middle Ages until the 18th century, each traditionally headed by a chief and associated with a territory, a surname, tartan patterns, a crest badge and a plant badge.

Structure and history

The clan system combined kinship with the lordship structures of Highland society: the chief (ceann-cinnidh) provided protection and leadership, while clansmen owed loyalty and military service. Clans such as Campbell, MacDonald, Mackenzie and MacLeod amassed great power, and clan feuds were endemic in parts of the Highlands. Lowland families and Border "riding surnames" developed similar structures, though the formal recognition of clans today derives largely from the Highland system.

Decline and revival

After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the British government dismantled the military and judicial foundations of the clans through measures including the Heritable Jurisdictions Act 1746 and the Act of Proscription (which banned Highland dress until 1782). The Highland Clearances further dispersed clan populations. In the 19th century a romantic revival — associated with the novels of Sir Walter Scott and the state visit of George IV to Edinburgh in 1822 — popularised tartan and clan identities, much of it codified by works such as the Vestiarium Scoticum.

Modern recognition

Clan chiefs are recognised in Scots law, with the Lord Lyon King of Arms granting arms to chiefs; the Standing Council of Scottish Chiefs represents them. Clan societies flourish among the Scottish diaspora in North America, Australia and New Zealand, and clan tartans are registered in the Scottish Register of Tartans. Not every Scottish surname is a clan name, and historians note that many tartan–clan associations are Victorian inventions.
