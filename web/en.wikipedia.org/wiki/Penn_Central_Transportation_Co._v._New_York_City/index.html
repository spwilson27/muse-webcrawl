Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104 (1978), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision on the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The Court upheld New York City's Landmarks Preservation Law as applied to Grand Central Terminal, holding that a regulation restricting development is not a taking merely because it denies the most profitable use of property, and it established the multi-factor Penn Central test that remains the default framework for regulatory takings claims.

Background

Grand Central Terminal, completed in 1913, was designated a New York City landmark in 1967 under the city's Landmarks Preservation Law. Its owner, the Penn Central Transportation Company, agreed to lease the air rights above the terminal to a developer for construction of a large office tower designed by Marcel Breuer. The Landmarks Preservation Commission rejected the proposals in 1968. Penn Central sued, arguing that preventing construction of the tower was a taking of its property requiring just compensation.

Holding and reasoning

Writing for a 6–3 majority, Justice William J. Brennan Jr. held there was no taking. The Court noted it had been unable to develop a "set formula" for regulatory takings and that inquiries are essentially ad hoc, guided by several factors of particular significance: (1) the economic impact of the regulation on the claimant; (2) the extent to which the regulation interferes with distinct investment-backed expectations; and (3) the character of the governmental action.

Applying these factors, the Court reasoned that the landmarks law did not interfere with the terminal's primary use as a railroad station, which remained economically viable; that Penn Central retained valuable transferable development rights in nearby parcels; and that the regulation was part of a comprehensive preservation programme distributing benefits broadly rather than singling out one owner. Justice William Rehnquist dissented, arguing the decision effectively appropriated the owner's air rights for public benefit.

Significance

Penn Central safeguarded the legality of historic preservation and land-use regulation generally, and its three-factor balancing test became the principal analysis for takings claims outside the narrow categories of physical appropriations and total economic wipeouts (addressed later in Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, 1992). The survival of Grand Central is widely credited to the campaign led by preservationists including Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis.
