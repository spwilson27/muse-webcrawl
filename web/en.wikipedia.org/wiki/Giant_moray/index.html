Giant moray

Scientific nameGymnothorax javanicus
FamilyMuraenidae (moray eels)
Max. lengthc. 3 m
RangeIndo-Pacific, Red Sea to Hawaii

The giant moray (Gymnothorax javanicus) is a species of moray eel and the largest of its kind by mass, widespread in the Indo-Pacific. It inhabits lagoons and seaward reefs, sheltering in crevices by day and hunting at night.

Description

Reaching 3 metres in length and 30 kg in weight, the giant moray has a brownish body covered with dark leopard-like spots that become denser with age, a black patch around the gill opening, and a yellowish head. Like other morays it has a second set of jaws in the throat (pharyngeal jaws) that thrust forward to pull prey into the gullet.

Behaviour

Giant morays feed mainly on fish and cephalopods, and occasionally crustaceans. They are famous for cooperative hunting with roving coral groupers: the two predators signal to each other and hunt together, the eel flushing prey from crevices the grouper cannot enter. Although generally shy, they can bite severely if provoked or hand-fed.

Relationship with humans

Large individuals may accumulate ciguatera toxin, so the species is not widely eaten. It is a popular sight for divers throughout its range and is assessed as Least Concern by the IUCN.
