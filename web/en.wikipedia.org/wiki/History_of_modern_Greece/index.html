The history of modern Greece begins with the establishment of the independent Greek state in 1830, following the Greek War of Independence against the Ottoman Empire. Modern Greek history is marked by territorial expansion, political instability, and eventual integration into the European Union.

War of Independence and the early kingdom

The revolt of 1821, aided by philhellenic opinion abroad and the intervention of Britain, France, and Russia—decisively at the Battle of Navarino (1827)—led to recognition of Greek independence in 1830. The young kingdom was ruled first by Governor Ioannis Kapodistrias and, after his assassination, by King Otto, a Bavarian prince. Athens became the capital in 1834.

Territorial expansion and the Megali Idea

Guided by the Megali Idea of uniting all Greek lands, the kingdom absorbed the Ionian Islands (1864), Thessaly (1881), and, after the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, Macedonia, Epirus, Crete, and the Aegean islands. The Greco-Turkish War of 1919–22 ended in disaster in Asia Minor and the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey.

Interwar period and the Second World War

Political division between republicans and royalists (the National Schism) plagued the interwar years, culminating in the Metaxas dictatorship of 1936. Italy's invasion in 1940 and subsequent German occupation brought fierce resistance and famine. Liberation was followed by the Greek Civil War (1946–49), won by the government side with Western support.

Postwar Greece

Greece joined NATO in 1952 and pursued rapid economic growth. The military junta of 1967–74 collapsed after the Cyprus crisis, restoring democracy in 1974. Greece joined the European Community in 1981 and adopted the euro in 2001. The sovereign debt crisis after 2009 brought severe austerity and political upheaval, from which the economy gradually recovered in the late 2010s.
