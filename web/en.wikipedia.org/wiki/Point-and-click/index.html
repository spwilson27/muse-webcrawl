Point and click

Point and click are the actions of a computer user moving a pointer to a certain location on a screen (pointing) and then pressing a button on a mouse or other pointing device (clicking). It is the fundamental interaction style of the graphical user interface (GUI), and by extension a general term for interfaces and entertainments operated principally through this gesture — most notably the point-and-click adventure game genre.

Pointing and clicking with a mouse, the basic gestures of the graphical user interface.

In computing interfaces

The metaphor developed with the mouse, invented by Douglas Engelbart in the 1960s and popularised by the Xerox Alto, the Apple Macintosh (1984), and Microsoft Windows. Point-and-click interaction lets users select objects, activate controls, follow hyperlinks, and manipulate direct-manipulation interfaces without memorising commands. Variants include single-click, double-click, right-click, drag and drop, and, on touchscreens, the corresponding tap. The speed with which users can point at targets is modelled by Fitts's law, which predicts acquisition time from target distance and size and underlies many interface design guidelines.

Point-and-click adventure games

In video games, "point and click" denotes a subgenre of adventure games in which the player explores environments and solves puzzles by pointing at and clicking on hotspots, characters, and inventory objects rather than typing commands (as in earlier text adventures and parser games) or directly controlling movement. The genre was defined commercially by LucasArts and Sierra On-Line in the late 1980s and early 1990s: titles include Maniac Mansion (1987), The Secret of Monkey Island (1990), King's Quest, Sam & Max Hit the Road, and the phenomenally successful Myst (1993), whose pre-rendered scenes were navigated entirely by clicking. The genre declined in the late 1990s as real-time 3D games rose, but persisted through studios such as Telltale Games and Wadjet Eye, independent developers, and remasters of the classics, and it adapted naturally to touchscreens.

Other uses

Cameras described as "point-and-shoot" borrow the idiom to indicate fully automatic operation (see Point-and-shoot camera). In photography and shooting sports the phrase contrasts simple automatic operation with manually controlled technique, and in everyday speech "point and click" often simply means any easy, self-explanatory interface.
