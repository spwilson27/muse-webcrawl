Battery electric vehicle

A battery electric vehicle (BEV), pure electric vehicle or all-electric vehicle is a type of electric vehicle that exclusively uses chemical energy stored in rechargeable battery packs, with no secondary source of propulsion such as a hydrogen fuel cell or petrol generator. BEVs use electric motors and motor controllers instead of internal combustion engines for propulsion, and are charged from external power sources such as home charging points and public charging stations. The category covers electric cars, buses, trucks, bicycles, motorcycles, trains, aircraft and watercraft.

History

Battery electric vehicles predate the internal combustion engine: practical electric cars appeared in the 1880s and were popular around 1900, before cheap mass-produced petrol cars displaced them. Interest revived with the energy crises of the 1970s and the environmental concerns of the 1990s, producing vehicles such as the GM EV1. The modern era began with lithium-ion battery vehicles such as the Tesla Roadster (2008) and the Nissan Leaf (2010), the first mass-market highway-capable BEV. By the early 2020s, global annual sales of plug-in vehicles exceeded ten million, with China the largest market, and many governments and manufacturers announced phase-out dates for new internal-combustion car sales.

Technology

Most modern BEVs use lithium-ion traction batteries, increasingly with lithium iron phosphate (LFP) or nickel-rich chemistries, mounted flat in the floor in "skateboard" layouts. Typical passenger-car packs range from about 40 to over 100 kWh, giving ranges of roughly 250–600 km. Electric motors offer instant torque and high efficiency (often above 85%, compared with roughly 20–40% for combustion engines), and regenerative braking recovers energy during deceleration. Charging standards include Type 1/Type 2 (Mennekes), CCS, CHAdeMO and Tesla's NACS connector, which has been adopted as a North American standard.

Advantages and disadvantages

BEVs produce no tailpipe emissions, are quiet, have lower per-kilometre energy and maintenance costs, and can be powered by low-carbon electricity. Disadvantages include higher upfront cost (narrowing over time), longer refuelling times than petrol, dependence on charging infrastructure, battery degradation, and environmental impacts of battery raw-material extraction and manufacturing. Total lifecycle emissions are generally substantially lower than those of equivalent petrol or diesel vehicles, especially on cleaner grids.
