Anatolian rug

An Anatolian rug is a term for hand-knotted pile rugs and flat-woven kilims produced in Anatolia (modern Turkey) and its adjacent regions. Anatolian weaving traditions extend back more than a thousand years, shaped by the region's position between Europe and Asia and by the succession of cultures — Phrygian, Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Seljuk, and Ottoman — that occupied the peninsula.

History

Carpet weaving arrived in Anatolia with the Seljuk Turks in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Surviving Seljuk carpets of the thirteenth century, preserved in mosques at Konya and Beyşehir, display bold geometric designs in a limited palette. Marco Polo praised the carpets of Anatolia as among the finest in the world. During the Ottoman period, weaving centres such as Uşak, Hereke, Gördes, Kula, and Bergama produced rugs exported widely to Europe, where they appear in Renaissance paintings by artists including Hans Holbein and Lorenzo Lotto — whose names are still attached to particular design types.

Materials and technique

Traditional Anatolian rugs are woven of sheep's wool on a wool foundation, using the symmetric "Turkish" or Gördes knot; finer Hereke pieces may use silk or cotton warps. Natural dyes — madder red, indigo blue, weld yellow, and walnut brown — characterised production until synthetic dyes arrived in the late nineteenth century. Designs are typically geometric in village and nomad (Yörük) weavings, with stylised floral patterns in court and town production.

Types

  • Prayer rugs (namazlık) with a mihrab arch motif pointing toward Mecca
  • Kilims, flat-woven tapestry textiles used as floor coverings and hangings
  • Uşak carpets, with large-scale medallion and star designs
  • Hereke carpets, extremely fine silk rugs originally produced for the Ottoman court
