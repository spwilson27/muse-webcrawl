Lost river

A lost river is a river that loses water as it flows downstream, instead of gaining it. A lost river might be one that flows into a sinkhole or other opening in an underground passage, disappearing from the surface — common in karst landscapes of limestone. Or a river might lose water gradually through evaporation, transpiration, and seepage into its bed, particularly in arid regions, until its flow diminishes and it vanishes into the desert or into a dry lake bed without reaching the sea or another body of water.

Sinking streams

In karst terrain, surface streams frequently flow into ponors (swallow holes) and continue underground through cave systems, sometimes resurging kilometres away as a spring. Rivers that disappear underground in this way are also called sinking streams or losing streams. A classic example is the Lost River in Indiana, United States, which flows underground for much of its course through a cave system before rising again. The Danube itself "sinks" in the Swabian Jura, part of its water diverting underground through the Danube Sinkhole to emerge at the Aachtopf spring and ultimately drain into the Rhine.

Arid-region lost rivers

In dry climates, rivers fed by distant mountains may lose so much water to evaporation and infiltration that they peter out in endorheic (closed) basins, salt pans, or sand seas. Such rivers flow only seasonally or vanish altogether in dry years.
