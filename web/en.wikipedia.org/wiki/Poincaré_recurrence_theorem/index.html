Poincaré recurrence theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics and physics, the Poincaré recurrence theorem states that certain dynamical systems will, after a sufficiently long but finite time, return to a state arbitrarily close to their initial state. Precisely: for a measure-preserving transformation of a finite-measure space, almost every point of any measurable set returns to that set infinitely often. Henri Poincaré proved a version of the theorem in 1890 in his prize-winning work on the three-body problem; Constantin Carathéodory gave the modern measure-theoretic proof in 1919.

Implications for statistical mechanics

Applied to a gas in a sealed container (an isolated Hamiltonian system, which preserves phase-space volume by Liouville's theorem), the theorem says the gas will eventually return arbitrarily close to any initial configuration — for example, with all molecules momentarily in one corner. Ernst Zermelo used this to argue against Boltzmann's statistical explanation of irreversibility (Zermelo's paradox, 1896). Boltzmann's celebrated reply was that the recurrence time for a macroscopic system, while finite, vastly exceeds the age of the universe, so such fluctuations are never observed; the argument became a classic illustration of the statistical character of the second law of thermodynamics.

Recurrence time

Kac's lemma quantifies the theorem: for an ergodic system, the expected return time to a set is inversely proportional to the measure of that set. For fine-grained states of macroscopic systems the Poincaré recurrence time is astronomically large — often written as exponential in the number of particles — which reconciles recurrence with observed irreversibility.
