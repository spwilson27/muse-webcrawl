Walden inversion

Field: Stereochemistry

Discovered by: Paul Walden, 1896

Related concept: SN2 reaction, backside attack

The Walden inversion is the inversion of configuration at a stereogenic centre in a molecule during a chemical reaction — that is, the conversion of one enantiomer or stereoisomer into its mirror-image arrangement at the reacting carbon atom. It is named after the Latvian-German chemist Paul Walden, who reported the phenomenon in 1896.

Discovery

Walden found that (−)-malic acid could be converted with phosphorus pentachloride into (+)-chlorosuccinic acid, which with silver oxide gave (+)-malic acid; he could thus interconvert the two enantiomers of malic acid through a cycle of substitution reactions, demonstrating that some steps in the cycle proceeded with inversion of configuration and others with retention. At the time, the result was startling because it showed that configuration at a stereocentre was not an immutable property of the molecule but could be overturned by substitution.

Mechanism

The inversion is now understood as the hallmark of the bimolecular nucleophilic substitution (SN2) reaction: the nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group ("backside attack"), passing through a planar transition state in which the three remaining groups lie in a plane, and the configuration "flips" like an umbrella turned inside out. Reactions proceeding with retention or racemisation involve other mechanisms, such as neighbouring-group participation or the formation of a planar carbocation in SN1 reactions. The Walden inversion remains a foundational concept in stereochemistry and a key diagnostic tool for assigning reaction mechanisms.
