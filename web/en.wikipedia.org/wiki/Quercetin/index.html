Quercetin

IUPAC name: 2-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-3,5,7-trihydroxy-4H-chromen-4-one
Formula: C15H10O7
Molar mass: 302.24 g/mol
Appearance: Yellow crystalline powder
Classification: Flavonol

Quercetin is a plant flavonol from the flavonoid group of polyphenols. It is found in many fruits, vegetables, leaves, seeds, and grains; capers, red onions, and kale are especially rich sources. Quercetin has a bitter flavour and is used as an ingredient in dietary supplements, beverages, and foods. Its name derives from quercetum (oak forest), after the oak genus Quercus.

Chemistry and occurrence

Quercetin is the aglycone form of a number of other flavonoid glycosides, such as rutin, which are found in citrus fruit, buckwheat, and onions. In foods, quercetin typically occurs bound to sugars; on ingestion, glycosides are hydrolysed and the aglycone is extensively metabolised in the intestine and liver, so little free quercetin circulates in plasma.

Research

Quercetin has been studied in basic research for its antioxidant activity in vitro and for possible effects on inflammation, blood pressure, and athletic performance. However, neither the European Food Safety Authority nor the US Food and Drug Administration has approved any health claim for quercetin, and evidence from human clinical trials remains preliminary and inconsistent. Quercetin is also investigated as a senolytic agent—in combination with dasatinib—for its ability to clear senescent cells in model systems.

Uses

Quercetin is sold as a dietary supplement, commonly in doses of 500–1000 mg per day, and is used as a food colouring and flavouring component in some products. It is generally regarded as safe at dietary levels, though high doses have been associated in animal studies with kidney effects, and it can interact with certain medications by affecting drug-metabolising enzymes and transporters.
