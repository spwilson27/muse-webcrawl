Forensic anthropology is the application of the anatomical science of anthropology and its various subfields, including forensic archaeology and forensic taphonomy, in a legal setting. A forensic anthropologist can assist in the identification of deceased individuals whose remains are decomposed, burned, mutilated or otherwise unrecognisable, as might happen in a mass disaster.

Methods

Working from skeletal remains, the anthropologist assembles a biological profile: estimating sex (chiefly from the pelvis and skull), age at death (from bone fusion, dental development, and degenerative changes), stature (from long-bone lengths), and population affinity, together with individualising traits such as healed fractures or surgical implants. Analysis of perimortem trauma can bear on cause and manner of death. Because soft-tissue methods used in forensic pathology are unavailable, the skeleton itself becomes the record.

Applications

Forensic anthropologists work in medical examiner systems, mass-fatality response, the recovery and identification of missing military personnel, and human-rights investigations of mass graves — for example in the former Yugoslavia, Rwanda and Latin America. Research facilities such as the University of Tennessee's Anthropological Research Facility (the "Body Farm") study decomposition to improve time-since-death estimates. In the United States, practitioners may be certified by the American Board of Forensic Anthropology.
