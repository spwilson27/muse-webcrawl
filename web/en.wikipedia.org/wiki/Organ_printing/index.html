Organ printing

Organ printing

Field: Tissue engineering, regenerative medicine

Techniques: Inkjet, extrusion and laser-assisted bioprinting

Organ printing is the use of three-dimensional bioprinting technology — layer-by-layer deposition of living cells in "bioinks" — to fabricate organs and tissues for research and, prospectively, for transplantation. The concept, developed in the early 2000s by researchers including Gabor Forgacs and Vladimir Mironov, treats multicellular aggregates as a printable living material that can fuse and mature into functional tissue, analogous to embryonic development.

Approach

A typical organ-printing workflow begins with imaging or design of the target organ's geometry, preparation of cell-laden hydrogel bioinks, and printing of a construct on a bioprinter. Cells are deposited as droplets (inkjet), continuous filaments (extrusion), or with laser guidance. The printed construct is then cultured in a bioreactor, where cell aggregates fuse, deposit extracellular matrix, and remodel. A central strategy is to print sacrificial materials that are later removed to leave perfusable channels mimicking blood vessels.

Challenges

The principal obstacle to printing whole organs is vascularisation: cells more than a few hundred micrometres from a nutrient supply die, so a functional printed organ needs a hierarchical network from large vessels down to capillaries, which is beyond current printing resolution. Other challenges include printing at clinically relevant speed without damaging cells, sourcing the enormous numbers of cells required, integrating nerves, and ensuring long-term mechanical and physiological function.

Current state

Simple tissues have progressed furthest: bioprinted skin, cartilage, bone, corneal tissue and small vascular grafts have been tested in animals and, in a few cases, in early human use as patches or grafts. Printed miniature tissue models ("organs on chips" and tumour models) are already used in drug screening. Printing a whole transplantable heart, liver or kidney remains a long-term goal rather than a clinical reality, and commentators caution against the hype that has sometimes surrounded the field.
