The Western Ghats, also known as the Sahyadri, are a mountain range running parallel to India's western coast for about 1,600 km through the states of Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu. One of the world's eight "hottest" biodiversity hotspots, they were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012.

Geology and geography

The Ghats formed as the western edge of the Deccan Plateau was uplifted when India broke away from Madagascar about 150 million years ago; the range is therefore older than the Himalayas. The highest peak is Anamudi (2,695 m) in Kerala. The Palghat Gap is the major break in the chain.

Climate

The mountains intercept the southwest monsoon, wringing heavy orographic rainfall (often over 3,000 mm) from the winds on the western slopes and casting a rain shadow over the Deccan to the east. Most of peninsular India's great rivers — the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri — rise in the Ghats.

Biodiversity

The Ghats harbour at least 7,400 species of flowering plants, 1,800 of them endemic, plus 139 mammals, 508 birds and 179 amphibians, most amphibians found nowhere else. Notable fauna include the Bengal tiger, Asian elephant, Nilgiri tahr, lion-tailed macaque and Malabar giant squirrel.

Conservation

Threats include deforestation, plantations, mining, dams and tourism pressure. The Gadgil and Kasturirangan committee reports' proposals for ecologically sensitive zoning sparked intense regional debate.
