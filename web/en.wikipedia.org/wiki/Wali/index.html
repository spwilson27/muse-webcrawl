Wali

Wali (Arabic: ولي, plural awliyā أَوْلِيَاء) is an Arabic word with several related meanings, most commonly rendered in English as "guardian", "protector", "custodian", "governor", or "friend of God". Its meaning depends heavily on context, and it is used in Islamic theology, Islamic law, and the administration of Muslim states.

Etymology and senses

The word derives from the Arabic root w-l-y, conveying nearness and alliance. In the Qur'an, God is described as the wali of the believers, and believers are urged to take God and the Prophet as their protectors rather than the enemies of the faith. In classical usage, a wali is someone entrusted with authority over a person or thing.

Islamic law

In Islamic jurisprudence, the wali is the legal guardian of a minor, an incapacitated person, or — in the classical marriage contract — the woman being married. The guardian's consent was traditionally required for a valid marriage contract in most schools of law, with debate over which relatives qualify and in what order.

Sufism

In Sufi tradition, a wali Allah ("friend of God") is a saint — a person believed to have attained closeness to God through piety and devotion. The veneration of saints and the visiting of their shrines is a prominent feature of popular Islam in many regions, though it is contested by reformist movements. The doctrine of wilaya (sainthood) was systematised by writers such as al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi in the 9th century.

Administrative use

Historically, a wali was also a provincial governor in the Islamic empires, notably under the Umayyads and Abbasids and later in the Ottoman Empire, where a vilayet (province) was administered by a vali. The title survives in modern usage in several countries, including as the title of the governor of a province in Algeria and Tunisia.

Other uses

The name Wali is also used as a personal name, and appears in the names of figures such as Wali Khan, the Pashtun nationalist politician, and in place names across the Muslim world.
