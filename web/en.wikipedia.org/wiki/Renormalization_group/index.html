Renormalization group

The renormalization group (RG) is a mathematical apparatus in theoretical physics that allows systematic investigation of the changes of a physical system as viewed at different scales. In particle physics, it reflects the changes in the underlying force laws (encoded in a quantum field theory) as the energy scale at which physical processes occur varies; in condensed matter physics and statistical mechanics, it is bound up with phase transitions and critical phenomena.

History

The idea originated in quantum electrodynamics in the work of Stückelberg and Petermann (1953) and Gell-Mann and Low (1954). It was transformed in the 1970s by Kenneth Wilson, whose momentum-space and real-space RG — including the explanation of the Kondo problem and of universality at critical points — earned him the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Main ideas

RG transformations coarse-grain a system: short-distance degrees of freedom are integrated out, lengths are rescaled, and couplings "flow" according to beta functions. Fixed points of the flow describe scale-invariant behaviour; around them, quantities divide into relevant, irrelevant and marginal, explaining why very different materials share identical critical exponents (universality). In quantum field theory, RG flow explains the running of coupling constants, such as the decrease of the strong coupling at high energies (asymptotic freedom).
