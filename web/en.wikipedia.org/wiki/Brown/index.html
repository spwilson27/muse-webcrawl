Brown

Brown is a colour produced by mixing red, yellow and black pigments, or by combining complementary colours. It is a composite colour associated in nature with earth, wood, soil, tree bark, and human hair, skin and eye colour. Brown pigments — earth colours such as umber, ochre and sienna — are among the oldest used by humans, appearing in prehistoric cave paintings.

Perception

Brown is perceived when a surface reflects light in a way that would appear orange or yellow at higher intensity but is seen at low luminance relative to its surroundings; it is thus a "dark orange" in perceptual terms. In the RGB colour model used on screens, browns are produced with lower-intensity combinations of red and green with little or no blue. In CMYK printing, brown is typically made by combining all three chromatic inks with black.

In nature

Brown is the colour of most soils, caused by iron oxides and organic matter, and of the bark and heartwood of many trees. Melanin pigments give brown colouring to the skin, hair and eyes of many humans and other mammals, and brown feathers and fur provide camouflage for countless animals. Autumn leaves turn brown as chlorophyll breaks down.

History and culture

Burnt and raw umber and sienna were staple pigments from antiquity through the Renaissance; the seventeenth-century Dutch masters made extensive use of earth browns. Brown has carried connotations of humility and poverty (the undyed robes of Franciscan friars), of the earth and autumn, and in the twentieth century, controversially, of the Nazi paramilitary SA, nicknamed the Brownshirts. Surveys in Europe and North America regularly place brown among the least favourite colours, yet it remains ubiquitous in food, furniture and clothing.

Shades

  • Beige, tan, khaki (light browns)
  • Chestnut, auburn, russet (reddish browns)
  • Chocolate, coffee, walnut (dark browns)
  • Umber, sienna, ochre (pigment browns)
