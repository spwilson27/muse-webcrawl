Jamshid

Jamshid (Persian: جمشید, Jamshēd; Middle Persian: Jam; Avestan: Yima) is the fourth and greatest king of the Pishdadian dynasty in Iranian mythology and tradition. He is described in the Shahnameh of Ferdowsi as a culture hero who reigned for centuries, taught mankind the arts of civilization, instituted the festival of Nowruz, and finally lost his glory and his kingdom through pride, falling to the tyrant Zahhak.

In the Avesta

In the Zoroastrian Avesta, Yima (Xšaēta, "the radiant") is the first mortal to converse with Ahura Mazda. He refuses the role of prophet but accepts the duty of nourishing and ruling the world, making it prosper and expand. In the second chapter of the Vendidad, Yima builds a vara — an underground enclosure — to preserve the seeds of every living thing through a devastating winter, a story closely paralleling flood and ark traditions elsewhere. Ahura Mazda warns him with a golden seal and a poniard to press against the earth.

In the Shahnameh

Ferdowsi's Jamshid reigns for 700 years. Under him, mankind learns metalworking, armour and weapons, weaving and clothing, building with brick, mining of gems, perfume-making, medicine and sailing; society is organised into classes of priests, warriors, farmers and artisans. Jamshid institutes Nowruz, the New Year festival celebrated at the spring equinox, and sits on a jewelled flying throne raised by demons. At the height of his power he proclaims himself the creator of the world; for this hubris the farr (divine glory) departs from him, his subjects revolt, and he flees before Zahhak, who eventually captures and saws him in half.

Cup of Jamshid

Persian tradition credits Jamshid with a magical seven-ringed cup, the Jām-e Jam, filled with an elixir of immortality and used for divination, in which the whole world could be seen. The cup became a favourite image of classical Persian poetry, notably of Hafez.

Legacy

Jamshid remains a central figure of Iranian cultural memory: the ruins of Persepolis are popularly called Takht-e Jamshid ("Throne of Jamshid"), and Nowruz continues to mark his legendary accession. His story is a standard moral exemplum on the ruin that follows pride.
