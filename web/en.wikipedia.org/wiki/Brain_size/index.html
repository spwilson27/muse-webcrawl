Brain size is a frequently studied aspect of the anatomy of animals and humans, measured by brain mass or volume, or in fossil species by endocranial capacity — the volume inside the braincase. Absolute sizes vary enormously: a mouse brain weighs about 0.4 g, an adult human brain about 1.3–1.4 kg, an elephant's about 5 kg, and a sperm whale's about 7.8 kg, the largest of any animal. Brain size tends to increase with body size across species, but not proportionally, which has led researchers to develop relative measures such as the encephalization quotient (EQ), comparing actual brain size with that expected for an animal of a given body mass.

Evolution

In the human lineage, cranial capacity increased from roughly 400–500 cm³ in australopithecines to about 600–750 cm³ in Homo habilis, 900–1,100 cm³ in Homo erectus, and 1,300–1,500 cm³ in Homo sapiens and Neanderthals. This expansion is associated with bipedalism freeing the hands, tool use, dietary change (including cooking), and social complexity, though it required trade-offs: the brain consumes about 20% of resting metabolic energy in adult humans, and large-brained infants impose obstetric constraints. Brain size in modern humans has changed little, and may have decreased slightly, over the last tens of thousands of years.

Brain size and intelligence

Within modern humans, total brain volume correlates only modestly with measured intelligence (correlations around 0.3–0.4 in MRI studies). Across species, absolute brain size, EQ, cortical neuron number and measures such as gyrification each predict some aspects of cognitive ability, but none is a complete explanation: corvids and parrots achieve complex cognition with small but neuron-dense brains, while much of the size difference between male and female human brains reflects body-size differences rather than cognitive ones. Modern research therefore emphasises neuron counts, connectivity and cortical organisation alongside raw size.

Measurement

Brain size is measured by weighing fresh or fixed specimens, by displacement of water, by cranial capacity estimates using seeds or shot in skulls, and in living subjects by structural MRI volumetry. Developmental disorders such as microcephaly (abnormally small brain) and macrocephaly are defined against population distributions of head circumference and brain volume.
