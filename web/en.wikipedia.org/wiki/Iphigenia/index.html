Iphigenia

Iphigenia (Ancient Greek: Ἰφιγένεια, Iphigéneia) is a figure in Greek mythology, the daughter of King Agamemnon and Queen Clytemnestra, and a sister of Orestes, Electra and Chrysothemis. She appears principally in connection with the Trojan War: in the most famous version of the story, her father sacrifices her at Aulis to appease the goddess Artemis, whose wrath has becalmed the Greek fleet. In other versions, Artemis rescues her at the last moment, substituting a deer, and carries her off to Tauris, where she serves as priestess of Artemis's temple.

Her name means "strong-born" or "she who gives birth to strong offspring". Iphigenia is one of the most frequently dramatised figures of Greek tragedy, appearing in works by Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, and in later adaptations by Racine, Goethe and Gluck.

In mythology

The sacrifice at Aulis

As the Greek fleet assembled at Aulis to sail against Troy, the winds failed. The seer Calchas announced that Artemis was angry — in some accounts because Agamemnon had killed a sacred deer and boasted of it, in others because of an offence by his father Atreus — and that the goddess demanded the sacrifice of Iphigenia. Agamemnon sent for his daughter on the pretext that she was to marry Achilles. In Euripides' Iphigenia in Aulis, Iphigenia first pleads for her life and then, in a famous reversal, accepts death willingly for the glory of Greece.

Rescue and Tauris

In the version followed by Euripides in Iphigenia in Tauris, Artemis snatched Iphigenia from the altar, leaving a deer in her place, and made her a priestess in the land of the Taurians (the Crimea), where it was the custom to sacrifice shipwrecked strangers to the goddess. Years later her brother Orestes, pursued by the Furies after killing Clytemnestra, came to Tauris charged by Apollo with stealing the sacred image of Artemis. Brother and sister recognised one another, escaped with the image, and returned to Greece.

In literature and art

Iphigenia's story is told in the lost epic Cypria, in Aeschylus's Agamemnon (where the sacrifice is described in the parodos), and in Sophocles' Electra. Euripides devoted two plays to her. Later treatments include Racine's Iphigénie (1674), Goethe's Iphigenie auf Tauris (1787), and Gluck's operas Iphigénie en Aulide (1774) and Iphigénie en Tauride (1779). The sacrifice was a popular subject in Roman wall painting and sarcophagus relief.
