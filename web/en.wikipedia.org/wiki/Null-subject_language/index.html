Null-subject language

A null-subject language is a language whose grammar permits an independent clause to lack an explicit subject; in sentences with a null subject there is no subject word present, yet the clause is not grammatically incomplete. Such languages are also called pro-drop languages, after the theoretical pronoun pro posited in generative grammar. Examples include Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Greek, Arabic, Japanese, and Chinese, whereas English and French are non-null-subject languages that normally require an overt subject (as in "it rains", with dummy "it").

Types

Linguists distinguish consistent null-subject languages, where rich verbal agreement identifies the subject (Spanish hablo, "I speak"), from radical pro-drop languages such as Japanese and Chinese, which lack agreement but allow subjects and objects alike to be omitted when recoverable from discourse. Partial null-subject languages, such as Finnish and Hebrew, allow omission in some persons or tenses only.

The null subject parameter

In principles-and-parameters theory, the null subject (pro-drop) parameter proposed by Noam Chomsky and studied by Luigi Rizzi was linked to a cluster of properties including free subject inversion and the absence of expletive pronouns. Later research has questioned whether these properties always co-occur, treating null subjects as arising from several distinct mechanisms.

English comparison

Standard English requires subjects even for weather verbs and in embedded clauses, though informal English allows limited omission in diaries and imperatives ("Went to the store", "Watch your step").
