Snake

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Snakes are elongated, limbless, carnivorous reptiles of the suborder Serpentes, distinguished by overlapping scales, forked tongues that sample scent for the vomeronasal organ, unhinged jaws that swallow prey whole, and the absence of eyelids and external ears. There are over 3,900 known species, on every continent except Antarctica, from 10-cm threadsnakes to the ~7-metre reticulated python.

Snakes evolved from lizard ancestors over 100 million years ago and are supreme specialists: constrictors kill by coiling (recent work shows they induce circulatory arrest, not just suffocation), while about a fifth of species are venomous, using modified saliva delivered by fangs to subdue prey and for defence.

Venom and humans

Snakebite is a major neglected health problem, killing an estimated 80,000–140,000 people a year, mostly rural farmers in Africa and Asia; antivenom — produced by immunising horses — is the specific treatment. Most snakes are harmless to humans and vital predators of rodents; fear of them (ophidiophobia) is one of the commonest phobias.

Biology

Snakes shed their skin whole as they grow, hear through bone conduction, sense infrared with pit organs in some families, and include sea snakes fully adapted to the ocean and burrowing forms that barely surface. Boas and pythons retain vestigial hind-limb spurs — evidence of their four-legged ancestry.
