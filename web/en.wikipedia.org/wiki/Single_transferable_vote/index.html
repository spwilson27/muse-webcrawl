Single transferable vote

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The single transferable vote (STV) is a multi-winner electoral system in which voters rank candidates in order of preference in multi-member districts, and candidates who reach a quota (usually the Droop quota: votes/(seats+1), plus one) are elected, with surplus votes and the votes of eliminated candidates transferred according to next preferences until all seats are filled. Developed in the 19th century (Carl Andræ in Denmark; Thomas Hare and John Stuart Mill's advocacy in Britain), STV aims to give minorities representation in rough proportion to their support while maximising voter choice among individual candidates, including within parties.

Where used and how it behaves

STV elects Ireland's Dáil and Malta's parliament (its two national strongholds, producing strongly constituency-focused deputies), the Australian Senate, Northern Ireland's Assembly, and many local and university bodies elsewhere; Scotland uses it for local councils. Counting is conducted by increasingly standardised rules (fractional transfers of surpluses), with computer counts resolving earlier practical objections. Properties praised by supporters include high proportionality without party lists, voter choice of persons, and few wasted votes; critics cite intra-party cannibalisation of campaigns, weak party cohesion, counting complexity and opaque final transfers — a classic trade-off between candidate-centred and party-centred representation.
