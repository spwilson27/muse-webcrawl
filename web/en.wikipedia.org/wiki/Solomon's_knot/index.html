Solomon's knot

Solomon's knot (Latin: nodus Salomonis) is a decorative motif consisting of two interlaced oval loops, used since antiquity in many cultures around the world. Despite its name, it is classified as a link rather than a true knot in knot theory, because it involves two closed components rather than a single knotted strand. It should not be confused with the Solomon's seal, a star-shaped motif.

The basic Solomon's knot: two interlaced ovals forming a fourfold design.

Structure

The most common form of Solomon's knot has a central four-sided figure formed by two interlacing ovals, with the loops extending in four directions, producing a pattern of rotational symmetry of order four. The design can be extended indefinitely into complex woven patterns, and elaborated versions with multiple interlacings appear frequently in mosaics and textiles.

History and distribution

Solomon's knot appears in ancient Roman mosaics, in early Christian and Byzantine churches, in synagogues, in Islamic art, and in Celtic and Lombardic stone carving in Italy, where it was a favourite motif of the Comacine masters. It is found on objects ranging from the stone floors of Roman villas to medieval manuscripts, Ethiopian processional crosses, and West African textiles. The name associates the motif with King Solomon, famed for his wisdom, and the design has variously been interpreted as a symbol of eternity, immortality, and the union of opposites.

In knot theory

In mathematical knot theory, Solomon's knot is the two-component link with four crossings, often denoted 421 or the Solomon link. It is a doubling of the Hopf link and is a Brunnian-like construction in decorative applications, though not itself Brunnian.
