Shingle style architecture

The Shingle style is an American architectural style made popular by the rise of the New England school of architecture, which eschewed the highly ornamented patterns of the Eastlake and Queen Anne styles. Influenced by the British Arts and Crafts movement and by a renewed interest in American colonial vernacular building, the Shingle style flourished between about 1880 and 1900, chiefly in seaside resorts of the northeastern United States.

Characteristics

The style is defined by the cladding of the entire building — walls and often the roof planes as well — in wooden shingles (usually cedar), left unpainted to weather to a silvery grey, which unifies the mass of the house into a continuous, sculptural skin. Houses tend to be large and informal, with rambling, asymmetrical plans, broad gambrel or gable roofs that sweep low over wide verandas, eyebrow dormers, and irregular rooflines. Towers and turrets inherited from the Queen Anne style are absorbed into the overall volume rather than standing apart. Masonry is confined to foundations and massive chimneys, windows are grouped in horizontal bands, and exterior ornament is restrained; the interior features an open "living hall" around a central fireplace, flowing spaces, and inglenooks.

History and examples

Leading practitioners included McKim, Mead & White, Bruce Price, William Ralph Emerson, John Calvin Stevens, and the young H. H. Richardson, whose William Watts Sherman House in Newport, Rhode Island (1874–76) is often credited as a prototype. Famous examples include the Kragsyde (1882) in Manchester-by-the-Sea, Massachusetts, and the seaside "cottages" of Newport, Long Island, and Mount Desert Island built as summer houses for wealthy clients. The style's emphasis on natural materials, integration with the landscape, and free planning influenced the Prairie School, the Craftsman bungalow, and later American domestic architecture.
