Commutator (electric)

The commutator of a small DC motor, showing its copper segments and brushes.

A commutator is a rotary electrical switch in certain types of electric motors and electrical generators that periodically reverses the direction of current between the rotor and the external circuit. In a DC motor, it applies power to the rotor windings in such a way that the torque always acts in the same direction; in a DC generator, it rectifies the alternating current induced in the armature windings into direct current at the terminals.

Construction

A commutator consists of a cylinder of copper segments mounted on the rotating shaft, insulated from each other by mica, with each segment connected to a tap on the armature winding. Stationary spring-loaded brushes, usually of carbon or graphite, slide against the rotating segments and carry current to and from the external circuit. As the shaft turns, the brushes successively contact different segments, reversing the connection to each coil just as the coil passes the position where its induced current changes sign.

Limitations

Brush–commutator friction causes wear, so brushes require periodic replacement and commutators resurfacing. Switching under load can produce sparking, electrical noise, and energy losses, and the sliding contact limits speed and voltage. For these reasons, traditional commutator machines have been displaced in many applications by brushless DC motors and by AC induction motors driven by electronic inverters, although brushed DC motors remain common in small, cheap, and high-starting-torque applications such as starter motors, power tools, and toys.

History

The commutator was developed in the 1830s in the earliest electromagnetic motors and dynamos, following William Ritchie's and Hippolyte Pixii's designs, and became a defining feature of nineteenth- and twentieth-century DC machinery, including the Gramme ring dynamo.
