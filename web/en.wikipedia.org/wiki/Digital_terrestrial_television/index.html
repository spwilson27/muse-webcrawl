Digital terrestrial television (DTTV, DTT, or DTV) is a technology for terrestrial television in which television stations broadcast digital content via radio waves to televisions in consumers' residences. It is a substantial technological advancement over analog terrestrial television, which broadcast an analog signal using the same medium.

Digital terrestrial television uses spectrum more efficiently than analog systems, allowing multiple program channels (a "multiplex") to occupy the bandwidth of a single analog channel, and freeing radio spectrum for other services such as mobile communications after the digital transition (the "digital dividend").

Technical overview

DTT signals are transmitted as a digital bitstream using modulation schemes such as COFDM (used by DVB-T and ISDB-T) or 8-VSB (used by ATSC in North America). Video and audio are compressed using codecs such as MPEG-2, H.264/MPEG-4 AVC, and HEVC, allowing several standard-definition channels, or one or more high-definition channels, per frequency channel.

Viewers require either a television set with an integrated digital tuner or an external set-top box (a digital television adapter) to decode the signals. Reception is typically via a conventional rooftop or indoor antenna.

Standards

  • DVB-T and DVB-T2 — used in most of Europe, much of Asia, Africa, and Oceania.
  • ATSC — used in North America and South Korea; ATSC 3.0 ("NextGen TV") is a successor standard.
  • ISDB-T — developed in Japan and adopted in much of Latin America and the Philippines.
  • DTMB — used in China and a few other countries.

Transition

The switch from analog to digital terrestrial broadcasting ("digital switchover" or "analog switch-off") took place worldwide from the 2000s through the 2020s. Luxembourg and the Netherlands were among the first countries to complete national switchovers in 2006; the United States ended full-power analog broadcasts in 2009, and the United Kingdom completed its switchover in 2012.
