Blancmange (from French blanc manger, "white food") is a sweet dessert popular throughout Europe, commonly made with milk or cream and sugar, thickened with rice flour, gelatin, corn starch, or Irish moss (a source of carrageenan), and often flavoured with almonds. It is usually set in a mould and served cold.

History

The medieval blancmange was a savoury dish of capon or chicken pounded with rice flour and almond milk, seasoned with sugar and spices, and eaten during Lent and by invalids; versions of it appear in recipe collections across medieval Europe, including The Forme of Cury (c. 1390). Only in the seventeenth century did the dish evolve into an exclusively sweet dessert of milk, cream, and almonds, set with isinglass or later gelatin, and often elaborately moulded. In the Victorian era blancmange became a nursery staple, sometimes brightly coloured.

Related desserts

Blancmange belongs to a family of set milk puddings that includes Italian panna cotta, Bavarian cream, the Middle Eastern muhallebi, and the Hawaiian coconut dessert haupia. Modern packet versions are made by whisking flavoured powder into hot milk.
