Magnetic tape data storage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Magnetic tape data storage is a system for storing digital information on magnetic tape using digital recording. Tape was a primary medium for early computer data storage, and it remains in widespread use for offline, archival and backup storage because of its low cost per bit, high capacity, longevity and low energy consumption when idle.

History

The first commercial computer tape drive was the Uniservo for the UNIVAC I in 1951, using metal tape. IBM's 726 tape unit followed in 1952 with plastic tape, establishing the half-inch, seven-track and later nine-track reel formats that dominated mainframe computing for decades. Later formats included cartridges such as the IBM 3480, Digital Linear Tape (DLT), and from 2000 the consortium-developed Linear Tape-Open (LTO) standard.

Modern use

Modern LTO cartridges store many terabytes natively (LTO-9 holds 18 TB uncompressed) at streaming rates of hundreds of megabytes per second. Tape libraries automate thousands of cartridges for hyperscale archives. Because tape is sequential-access and usually kept offline ("air-gapped"), it is valued for resilience against ransomware and for cold data archiving by organisations such as CERN and large cloud providers.
