The Guide for the Perplexed

The Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic: Dalālat al-ḥāʾirīn; Hebrew: Moreh Nevukhim) is a work of Jewish philosophy by the medieval rabbi and philosopher Moses Maimonides (1138–1204), written in Judeo-Arabic around 1190 and addressed to his student Joseph ben Judah. It is one of the most influential works of medieval philosophy.

Contents

The Guide seeks to reconcile Aristotelian philosophy with the Hebrew Bible and rabbinic tradition, for readers "perplexed" by apparent conflicts between reason and scripture. It argues that anthropomorphic descriptions of God must be read allegorically, develops a negative theology in which God can only be described by what He is not, and discusses creation, prophecy, providence, the problem of evil and the reasons for the commandments.

Influence

Translated into Hebrew by Samuel ibn Tibbon in the author's lifetime, the Guide shaped Jewish thought for centuries and provoked the Maimonidean controversies. It also influenced Christian scholastics such as Thomas Aquinas and later philosophers including Spinoza and Leibniz.
