Varicella zoster virus (VZV), also known as human herpesvirus 3 (HHV-3), is one of the nine known herpesviruses that infect humans. It causes chickenpox (varicella) as a primary infection, typically in children, and shingles (herpes zoster) when it reactivates later in life from latency in sensory nerve ganglia.

Virology

VZV is an enveloped virus with a linear double-stranded DNA genome of about 125,000 base pairs encoding roughly 70 genes — one of the smallest genomes among the human herpesviruses. Its structure comprises an icosahedral nucleocapsid surrounded by a tegument layer and a lipid envelope studded with glycoproteins. The virus is genetically stable and relatively conserved worldwide, though distinct clades can be defined by sequence variation.

Disease

Primary infection spreads by the respiratory route and produces the generalized itchy vesicular rash of chickenpox after an incubation period of about two weeks. Following the primary illness, the virus establishes lifelong latency in dorsal root and cranial nerve ganglia. If cell-mediated immunity wanes — with age or immunosuppression — the virus can reactivate and travel down a sensory nerve, causing the painful dermatomal rash of shingles. Complications include postherpetic neuralgia, bacterial superinfection, pneumonia, and, rarely, encephalitis.

Prevention and treatment

A live attenuated varicella vaccine (the Oka strain) is used in childhood immunization programmes in many countries, and a recombinant zoster vaccine is recommended for older adults to prevent shingles. Antiviral drugs such as aciclovir, valaciclovir, and famciclovir are effective, particularly when started early in the course of illness.
