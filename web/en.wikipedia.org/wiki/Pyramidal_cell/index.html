Pyramidal cell

LocationCerebral cortex, hippocampus, amygdala
ShapeTriangular (pyramidal) soma
FunctionExcitatory projection neuron
NeurotransmitterGlutamate

Pyramidal cells, or pyramidal neurons, are a type of multipolar neuron found in areas of the brain including the cerebral cortex, the hippocampus, and the amygdala. They are the principal excitatory neurons of the mammalian forebrain and are named for the roughly triangular shape of their cell body (soma). Pyramidal neurons were first described by Santiago Ramón y Cajal.

Structure

A pyramidal cell has a conical soma, a single long apical dendrite that extends toward the cortical surface, multiple shorter basal dendrites radiating from the base, and a single axon that projects to other cortical or subcortical targets. The dendrites are densely covered with small protrusions called dendritic spines, which receive most of the excitatory synaptic input to the cell. In the hippocampus, pyramidal cells form the principal cell layer of the CA fields; in the neocortex they are found mainly in layers II/III and V. The giant pyramidal cells of the primary motor cortex are known as Betz cells.

Function

Pyramidal neurons use the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate. Cortical pyramidal cells in layer V give rise to the corticospinal tract and other major output pathways, while those in layers II/III mediate cortico-cortical communication. Hippocampal pyramidal cells, especially place cells in CA1, are central to spatial memory and navigation. Because of their abundance, regular arrangement and large dendritic trees, pyramidal cells dominate signals recorded by electroencephalography.
