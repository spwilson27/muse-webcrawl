David Sarnoff (February 27, 1891 – December 12, 1971) was an American businessman and pioneer of American radio and television. He founded the National Broadcasting Company (NBC) and throughout most of his career led the Radio Corporation of America (RCA) in a variety of senior executive positions from 1919 until his retirement in 1970.

Early life

Sarnoff was born in the Jewish shtetl of Uzlyany near Minsk and emigrated with his family to New York in 1900. Supporting his family by selling newspapers and delivering telegrams, he joined the Marconi Wireless Telegraph Company of America as an office boy and taught himself telegraphy, rising to operator and station manager. Legend — promoted by Sarnoff himself — holds that he was the operator who received the first distress signals from the sinking Titanic in April 1912 and relayed the news for 72 hours; historians have shown the story to be substantially embellished, though it cemented his public image.

RCA and NBC

When RCA was formed in 1919 to take over American Marconi's assets, Sarnoff became its commercial manager. His "radio music box" memorandum had already proposed broadcasting entertainment into homes as a way to sell receivers. As RCA's general manager and, from 1930, its president, he built the NBC radio networks (1926), championed television — dramatically unveiling RCA's electronic TV system at the 1939 New York World's Fair — and fought a long patent and standards war with FM inventor Edwin Armstrong.

Under Sarnoff, RCA also became a major defence contractor and electronics manufacturer, and he personally directed the introduction of compatible colour television, approved by the FCC in 1953, defeating CBS's mechanical system. Commissioned a brigadier general in the Army Signal Corps during the Second World War, he was known thereafter as "the General".

Legacy

Sarnoff ruled RCA as one of America's most formidable corporate autocrats, building the dominant broadcasting and electronics empire of the mid-20th century while feuding with rivals such as Philo Farnsworth, the inventor of electronic television whose patents RCA was ultimately forced to license. The Sarnoff Corporation research centre in Princeton, New Jersey, bore his name until 2011.
