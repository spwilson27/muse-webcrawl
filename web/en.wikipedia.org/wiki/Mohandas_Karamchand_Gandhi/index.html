Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (2 October 1869 – 30 January 1948), honoured in India as Mahatma ('great-souled') and as Bapu ('father'), was an Indian lawyer and anti-colonial nationalist who employed nonviolent resistance to lead the successful campaign for India's independence from British rule. He inspired movements for civil rights and freedom across the world. Born and raised in a Hindu family in coastal Gujarat, he trained in law in London and first employed nonviolent resistance as an expatriate lawyer in South Africa.

South Africa and return to India

Gandhi spent 21 years in South Africa, where he developed his political method of satyagraha — 'devotion to truth' — in campaigns against racial discrimination affecting the Indian community there. Returning to India in 1915, he organised peasants, farmers, and urban labourers to protest against excessive land-tax and discrimination.

Assuming leadership of the Indian National Congress in 1921, Gandhi led nationwide campaigns for easing poverty, expanding women's rights, building religious and ethnic amity, ending untouchability, and, above all, achieving swaraj (self-rule).

Independence campaigns

Gandhi's most famous campaigns included the Non-cooperation movement of 1920–22, the Salt March of 1930 — a 240-mile march to the Arabian Sea to protest the British salt tax — and the Quit India movement of 1942. He was imprisoned many times and for many years in both South Africa and India.

India gained independence in August 1947, accompanied by the partition into India and Pakistan, which Gandhi had opposed. He undertook fasts to promote communal harmony amid the violence of partition.

Assassination and legacy

On 30 January 1948, Gandhi was assassinated in New Delhi by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who believed Gandhi had been too accommodating to Muslims. His birthday, 2 October, is commemorated in India as Gandhi Jayanti, a national holiday, and worldwide as the International Day of Non-Violence.

Gandhi's philosophy of nonviolent resistance influenced civil rights leaders including Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela, and his writings, including the autobiography The Story of My Experiments with Truth, remain widely read.
