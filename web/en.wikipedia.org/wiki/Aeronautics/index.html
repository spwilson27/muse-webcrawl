Aeronautics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A wind tunnel test of an aircraft model, smoke streams flowing over the wing]
A wind tunnel test of an aircraft model, smoke streams flowing over the wing

Aeronautics is the science and practice of travel through the air — the study, design and manufacture of aircraft and the techniques of operating them. It divides into heavier-than-air flight (aeroplanes and rotorcraft), lighter-than-air flight (balloons and airships) and, in broader usage, the atmospheric portion of spaceflight. As an engineering discipline it rests on aerodynamics, propulsion, structures and flight dynamics, distinct from Astronautics, its spacefaring sibling.

History

Scientific aeronautics begins with George Cayley, who identified the four forces of flight and built the first true aeroplane concepts (1799–1850s); Otto Lilienthal's glider experiments fed the Wright brothers' powered flight of 1903. Two world wars industrialised aerodynamics (the wind tunnel, the NACA airfoil programme), and the jet age added transonic and supersonic aerodynamics, fly-by-wire and composite structures.

Branches

Aerodynamics studies airflow (subsonic to hypersonic); propulsion covers piston, jet, turboprop and electric systems; structures and materials balance weight against fatigue and damage tolerance; avionics and flight control integrate the aircraft as a system. Aeronautics research is anchored by agencies and laboratories — NASA's aeronautics directorate (heir to NACA), ONERA, DLR — and by the great manufacturers' test establishments.
