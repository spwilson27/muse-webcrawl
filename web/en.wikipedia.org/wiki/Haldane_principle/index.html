Haldane principle

The Haldane principle is the principle that decisions about how to spend research funds should be made by researchers rather than politicians. It is named after Richard Burdon Haldane, Viscount Haldane, who chaired the Machinery of Government Committee whose 1918 report (the Haldane Report) set out the case for separating research funding decisions from day-to-day ministerial direction.

Origins

The principle is usually traced to two sources. In 1904, and again as Secretary of State for War, Haldane promoted the idea that universities and research needed a degree of insulation from political control. More formally, the 1918 report of his committee on the machinery of government recommended that research intended to serve general purposes should be organised under councils independent of the departments concerned with particular applications. This became the constitutional basis for the British research councils, beginning with the Medical Research Council and the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research.

Content

In its modern form, the principle holds that ministers may legitimately decide the overall level and broad priorities of public research spending, but that decisions about which specific projects and proposals to fund should rest with experts — typically through peer review in the research councils — rather than with politicians or civil servants. It underpins the "dual support" system of UK research funding, in which block funding to universities is combined with competitive project grants.

Debates

The principle has been periodically debated and reinterpreted. The Rothschild reforms of 1971 introduced a "customer–contractor" principle for applied government research, which some saw as diluting Haldane. More recently, the creation of UK Research and Innovation (UKRI) in 2018 renewed discussion about ministerial direction of research agendas, and the idea has been invoked in debates over mission-oriented funding, industrial strategy and directions requiring ministers to state explicitly how their powers of direction are exercised consistently with the principle. Comparable arrangements, in which autonomous funding agencies allocate money at arm's length from government, exist in many other countries.
