An arrow is a fin-stabilised projectile launched with a bow. It is one of the oldest projectile weapons of humanity, predating recorded history, and remains in use today for hunting, sport (archery) and, historically, warfare.

Construction

A typical arrow consists of four parts:

  • Arrowhead — the functional tip, historically of stone, bone, bronze or steel, and in forms ranging from simple points to broadheads for hunting and bodkins for piercing armour.
  • Shaft — the long spine of the arrow, traditionally wood (such as cedar or pine), today often aluminium, carbon fibre or composites. Its stiffness ("spine") must be matched to the bow.
  • Fletching — feathers or plastic vanes near the rear that stabilise flight by inducing slight rotation and drag.
  • Nock — the notch at the rear that engages the bowstring.

History

Stone points consistent with arrows date back at least 60,000 years in Africa, and bows and arrows were in use across most of the world by the late Palaeolithic. Arrows remained central to warfare from ancient Egyptian and Assyrian armies through the medieval longbow — famously at Agincourt (1415) — until firearms displaced them in the 16th–18th centuries. Composite-bow archery by horse archers of the Eurasian steppe, such as the Mongols, made the arrow one of history's most consequential weapons.

Types

Modern arrows include target arrows, hunting arrows with broadheads, flu-flu arrows with oversized fletching for bird hunting or short-range shots, and specialised designs for bowfishing and traditional flight shooting.
