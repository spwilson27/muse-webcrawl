Amarna Period
Periodc. 1353 – c. 1336 BC (late 18th Dynasty)
Key rulersAkhenaten, Nefertiti, Smenkhkare, Neferneferuaten
CapitalAkhetaten (modern el-Amarna)
Defining featuresAtenism; Amarna art style; Amarna Letters diplomacy

The Amarna Period was an era of ancient Egyptian history during the second half of the Eighteenth Dynasty, named after the modern site of el-Amarna, where Pharaoh Akhenaten (reigned c. 1353–1336 BC) founded his new capital Akhetaten. The period is distinguished by Akhenaten's radical religious revolution — the elevation of the sun-disc Aten to supreme, effectively sole, god — and by a strikingly naturalistic, exaggerated style in art.

Religion: Atenism

Early in his reign Akhenaten promoted the Aten, the visible disc of the sun, above the traditional pantheon, building open-air temples at Karnak. Around his fifth regnal year he changed his name from Amenhotep IV, moved the court to the virgin site of Akhetaten, and in later years ordered the names of Amun and other gods hacked out of monuments — a persecution that made Atenism the most uncompromising religious policy of pharaonic history. The Great Hymn to the Aten, preserved in the tomb of Ay, is the era's most famous text, celebrating the sun as universal creator in terms sometimes compared to Psalm 104.

Art and society

Amarna art broke with three thousand years of convention: the royal family is shown with elongated skulls, full lips, narrow waists and swelling bellies, in informal domestic scenes without precedent — the king kissing a child, the queen Nefertiti dandling daughters under the Aten's life-giving rays. Sculptors' workshops at Amarna, including that of Thutmose, produced masterpieces such as the painted bust of Nefertiti (now in Berlin). Language also shifted: official texts moved toward Late Egyptian, the spoken tongue.

Diplomacy: the Amarna Letters

The royal archives at Amarna preserved some 380 clay tablets of diplomatic correspondence in Akkadian cuneiform — the Amarna Letters — exchanged with Babylonia, Assyria, Mitanni, the Hittites and Egypt's restive vassals in Canaan, a unique window onto Bronze Age great-power politics.

End and aftermath

Akhenaten died after 17 years on the throne and was succeeded briefly by Smenkhkare and Neferneferuaten (possibly Nefertiti ruling as pharaoh) before the boy-king Tutankhaten — renamed Tutankhamun — restored the old cults and abandoned Amarna. Later king lists erased the Amarna pharaohs as heretics; Horemheb dismantled Akhenaten's temples, reusing their small blocks (talatat) as fill. The city of Akhetaten, occupied for barely a generation, was never rebuilt over, making it Egyptology's most informative pharaonic city site.
