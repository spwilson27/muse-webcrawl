Penitentiary

Eastern State Penitentiary, Philadelphia (1829).

A penitentiary is a prison, especially in North America, for people convicted of serious crimes. The word derives from "penitence": early nineteenth-century reformers intended such institutions to induce reflection and repentance in prisoners through order, labour, and, in the influential Pennsylvania system, solitary confinement.

History

The penitentiary emerged from late eighteenth-century prison reform in Britain and the United States. The Walnut Street Jail in Philadelphia added a "penitentiary house" in 1790, and Eastern State Penitentiary (opened 1829) became the model of the separate system, in which prisoners lived, worked, and exercised in isolation. The rival Auburn system in New York combined solitary cells at night with silent congregate labour by day. Both models were widely copied internationally. In the United States, the term came to denote large state and federal prisons, such as the United States Penitentiaries at Leavenworth, Atlanta, and Terre Haute, and state institutions like Angola (Louisiana State Penitentiary) and San Quentin.

Other uses

In the Roman Catholic Church, a penitentiary is also an official or tribunal concerned with matters of conscience and indulgences: the Apostolic Penitentiary is one of the three tribunals of the Roman Curia, and a major penitentiary is a cardinal heading it. Cathedral chapters also have canon penitentiaries. In some jurisdictions the term survives in the names of correctional agencies, such as the New Mexico Penitentiary.
