Layering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Layering is a method of asexual plant propagation in which a stem is encouraged to form roots while still attached to the parent plant, after which the rooted stem is detached to become a new individual. Because the developing roots remain supplied with water and nutrients by the parent, layering succeeds with many species that root poorly as cuttings.

Methods

  • Simple layering — a flexible low branch is bent to the ground, a portion buried and wounded or notched to stimulate rooting, and the tip left exposed.
  • Tip layering — the tip of a shoot (as in black raspberries) is buried and roots at its end.
  • Mound (stool) layering — a plant is cut back and soil is heaped over the base of the new shoots, which root in the mound; used for apple rootstocks.
  • Serpentine layering — a long stem is buried at several points, producing multiple new plants.
  • Air layering — a stem is wounded, wrapped in moist sphagnum moss and plastic while still in the air, and roots form inside the wrap; an ancient technique used for figs, rubber plants, and many ornamentals.

Natural layering

Layering occurs naturally where trailing stems touch the soil, as in strawberries (which propagate by runners), brambles, and many ground-cover plants. Gardeners exploit the same behaviour when propagating plants vegetatively to preserve the characteristics of a cultivar.
