Probability-generating function

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In probability theory, the probability-generating function (PGF) of a discrete random variable is a power-series representation of its probability mass function. It is widely used in the analysis of branching processes, queueing theory and risk theory because convolutions of distributions become products of generating functions.

Definition

If X is a random variable taking values in the non-negative integers {0, 1, 2, ...} with probabilities pk = P(X = k), its probability-generating function is

G(z) = E(zX) = Σk≥0 pk zk,

defined for all z with |z| ≤ 1 (and often beyond, up to the radius of convergence). In particular G(1) = 1 for a proper distribution.

Properties

The probabilities are recovered by differentiation: pk = G(k)(0)/k!. The mean is E(X) = G′(1), and the variance is Var(X) = G″(1) + G′(1) − G′(1)2. Factorial moments are given directly: E(X(X−1)···(X−k+1)) = G(k)(1).

If X and Y are independent, the PGF of their sum is the product of their PGFs: GX+Y(z) = GX(z) GY(z). For a random sum of i.i.d. variables with a random count N, the PGF is the composition GN(GX(z)).

Examples

For a Poisson variable with mean λ, G(z) = eλ(z−1). For a binomial(n, p) variable, G(z) = (1 − p + pz)n. For a geometric variable counting failures before the first success, G(z) = p/(1 − (1−p)z).

Related functions

The PGF is closely related to the moment-generating function M(t) = G(et) and to the characteristic function φ(t) = G(eit). In the theory of Galton–Watson branching processes, the extinction probability is the smallest fixed point of the offspring PGF in [0, 1].
