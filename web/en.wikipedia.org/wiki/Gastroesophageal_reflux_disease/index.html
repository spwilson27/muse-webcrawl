Gastroesophageal reflux disease

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a chronic upper gastrointestinal disease in which stomach content persistently and regularly flows up into the oesophagus, resulting in symptoms or complications such as heartburn, regurgitation, and inflammation of the oesophageal lining (oesophagitis). GERD is common in Western populations, affecting roughly 10–20% of adults chronically.

Reflux occurs when stomach contents pass back through the lower oesophageal sphincter.

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark symptoms are heartburn — a burning sensation behind the breastbone — and acid regurgitation. Other symptoms include difficulty swallowing, chest pain, chronic cough, hoarseness and a sensation of a lump in the throat. Symptoms often worsen after meals and when lying down. In infants, reflux is common and usually resolves, but persistent disease requires evaluation.

Causes

GERD results from failure of the anti-reflux barrier at the junction of the oesophagus and stomach, most often through transient relaxations or weakness of the lower oesophageal sphincter. Hiatal hernia, obesity and delayed stomach emptying contribute. Obesity raises intra-abdominal pressure and is strongly associated with the condition.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is often clinical, based on typical symptoms and response to acid-suppressing therapy. Investigations include upper endoscopy to detect oesophagitis and complications, ambulatory pH monitoring, and oesophageal manometry when surgery is considered or the diagnosis is uncertain.

Treatment

Management begins with lifestyle modification — weight loss, avoiding late meals, and elevating the head of the bed. Medications include antacids, histamine H2-receptor blockers and, most effectively, proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs) such as omeprazole. For selected patients, anti-reflux surgery such as laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication is an option. Long-term complications of untreated disease include Barrett's oesophagus, a precancerous change in the oesophageal lining.
