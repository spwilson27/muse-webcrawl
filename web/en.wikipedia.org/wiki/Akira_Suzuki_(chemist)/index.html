Akira Suzuki (chemist)

Akira Suzuki (鈴木 章, Suzuki Akira; born 12 September 1930) is a Japanese chemist and professor emeritus at Hokkaido University, best known for discovering the palladium-catalysed cross-coupling reaction of organoboron compounds that bears his name — the Suzuki reaction (Suzuki–Miyaura coupling). He shared the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Richard F. Heck and Ei-ichi Negishi "for palladium-catalyzed cross couplings in organic synthesis".

Life and career

Suzuki was born in Mukawa, Hokkaido, in 1930. He studied at Hokkaido University, receiving his PhD in 1959, and joined its faculty. From 1963 to 1965 he was a postdoctoral researcher with Herbert C. Brown at Purdue University in the United States, where he became familiar with the chemistry of organoboranes that Brown had developed. Returning to Japan, he became a full professor at Hokkaido University in 1973 and worked there until his retirement in 1994, afterwards holding posts at Okayama University of Science and Kurashiki University of Science and the Arts.

The Suzuki reaction

In 1979, with Norio Miyaura, Suzuki reported that organoboron compounds — specifically alkenylboranes — could be coupled with organic halides in the presence of a palladium catalyst and base to form carbon–carbon bonds. The reaction was later generalised to arylboronic acids, which are stable, easy to handle, and of low toxicity. The Suzuki–Miyaura coupling proved exceptionally mild and versatile, and it is now one of the most widely used reactions in synthetic chemistry: it is employed on an industrial scale in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals (including the blood-pressure drug losartan's biphenyl unit), agrochemicals, liquid crystals, and organic electronic materials.

Honours

In addition to the 2010 Nobel Prize, Suzuki received the Japan Academy Prize (2004), the Tetrahedron Prize (2014, shared), and the Order of Culture from the Emperor of Japan in 2010, and he was designated a Person of Cultural Merit.
