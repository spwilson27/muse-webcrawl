A thin-film transistor (TFT) is a special type of field-effect transistor (FET) made by depositing thin films of an active semiconductor layer, a dielectric layer, and metallic contacts over a supporting — but non-conducting — substrate, commonly glass. This contrasts with the conventional transistor, in which the substrate itself (typically a silicon wafer) is the semiconductor. Because TFTs can be fabricated at low temperatures over large areas of cheap substrates such as glass or plastic, they are the enabling technology of active-matrix flat-panel displays.

Structure and operation

A TFT has three terminals — source, drain, and gate — like other FETs. Applying a voltage to the gate controls the flow of current between source and drain through the semiconductor channel; the transistor acts as a switch or amplifier. Devices are built in several geometries, including bottom-gate (inverted) and top-gate configurations, and as staggered or coplanar structures. In an active-matrix display, one or more TFTs at each pixel store the image signal on a capacitor during the addressing interval and hold it between refresh cycles, allowing far better image quality than passive-matrix addressing.

Materials

  • Amorphous silicon (a-Si:H) — the long-dominant material, cheap and uniform over large panels, but with low electron mobility (~0.5–1 cm²/V·s) and instability under bias stress.
  • Low-temperature polysilicon (LTPS) — much higher mobility, permitting driver circuits to be integrated on the glass; used in high-resolution mobile displays.
  • Metal oxides, notably indium gallium zinc oxide (IGZO) — mobilities intermediate between a-Si and LTPS, very low leakage, and optical transparency; commercialised in large and high-resolution LCD and OLED panels from the 2010s.
  • Organic semiconductors — flexible, low-cost devices for applications such as electronic paper and flexible electronics, though with lower performance.

History

The field-effect principle behind the TFT was patented by Julius Edgar Lilienfeld in the 1920s and by Oskar Heil in 1934, but practical thin-film devices came only decades later: Paul Weimer at RCA reported working TFTs using cadmium sulfide in the early 1960s. In 1973, T. Peter Brody and colleagues at Westinghouse demonstrated an active-matrix electroluminescent display driven by CdSe TFTs, and in 1979 LeComber, Spear and Ghaith demonstrated switching in amorphous silicon devices. Commercial TFT-LCDs arrived in the late 1980s and 1990s and came to dominate laptops, monitors, televisions, and mobile phones.

Applications

The best-known application is the TFT-LCD: every pixel of an active-matrix liquid-crystal display is controlled by its own transistor. TFT backplanes also drive OLED displays, electronic paper, digital radiography detectors, fingerprint and image sensors, and are researched for flexible and transparent electronics.
