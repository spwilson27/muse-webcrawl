Fault scarp

A fault scarp is a small step or offset on the ground surface where one side of a fault has moved vertically with respect to the other. It is the topographic expression of faulting that may occur during an earthquake, or more generally of recent displacement along a fault. Fault scarps are often located at the base of mountain ranges fronted by normal faults, as in the Basin and Range Province of the western United States.

Formation

Scarps form when surface rupture offsets the ground, most commonly along normal faults where the hanging wall drops down. Fresh scarps can range from centimetres to several metres in height — the 1954 Fairview Peak earthquake in Nevada produced scarps up to about 7 metres. Over time, erosion and deposition soften the scarp profile; measuring this degradation ("scarp diffusion") allows geologists to estimate the age of faulting events.

Significance

Fault scarps are key evidence in paleoseismology: trenching across scarps exposes buried faulted layers used to reconstruct the timing and size of prehistoric earthquakes. Some scarps, called fault-line scarps, instead arise from differential erosion along an old fault and do not reflect the most recent displacement direction. Well-known examples include scarps formed by the 1959 Hebgen Lake earthquake in Montana and those along the Wasatch Fault in Utah.
