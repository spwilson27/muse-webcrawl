Catalepsy

Catalepsy is a nervous condition characterised by muscular rigidity, decreased sensitivity to pain, and the maintenance of postures imposed by an examiner — a limb placed in a position will remain there for some time ("waxy flexibility"). It is a symptom rather than a disease in itself.

Causes

Catalepsy occurs as a feature of catatonia, which is associated with schizophrenia and other psychiatric disorders, and in neurological conditions such as Parkinson's disease and epilepsy. It can be induced by antipsychotic drugs acting on dopamine receptors and by other agents; in animal research, drug-induced catalepsy in rodents is a standard test for the extrapyramidal side effects of antipsychotics.

History

Reports of cataleptic states date to antiquity and were a subject of fascination in 19th-century neurology and hypnotism. Fear of being buried alive while in a cataleptic trance fed the Victorian preoccupation with premature burial, and catalepsy features in literary works such as Edgar Allan Poe's "The Premature Burial" and "The Fall of the House of Usher". Treatment addresses the underlying condition; catatonia responds to benzodiazepines and electroconvulsive therapy.
