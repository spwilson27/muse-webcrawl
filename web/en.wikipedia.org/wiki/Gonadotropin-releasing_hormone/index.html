Gonadotropin-releasing hormone

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) is a releasing hormone responsible for the release of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) from the anterior pituitary gland. GnRH is a tropic peptide hormone synthesized and released from GnRH neurons within the hypothalamus. It is the initial step in the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis that controls reproduction.

Structure and secretion

Mammalian GnRH is a decapeptide with the sequence pyroGlu-His-Trp-Ser-Tyr-Gly-Leu-Arg-Pro-Gly-NH2. It is cleaved from a larger preprohormone. GnRH neurons release the peptide in pulses into the hypophyseal portal circulation; pulsatility is essential, because continuous exposure down-regulates pituitary GnRH receptors and suppresses gonadotropin secretion — a principle exploited pharmacologically by long-acting GnRH agonists.

Function

Binding of GnRH to its G-protein-coupled receptor on pituitary gonadotrophs stimulates the synthesis and release of LH and FSH, which in turn drive gonadal steroid production (estrogen, progesterone, testosterone) and gametogenesis. Pulse frequency modulates the relative output of LH (faster pulses) versus FSH (slower pulses). At puberty, increasing GnRH pulse amplitude and frequency initiates reproductive maturation; upstream regulators include kisspeptin neurons.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of GnRH or its receptor causes hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, including Kallmann syndrome when combined with anosmia. Synthetic GnRH analogues are widely used: agonists such as leuprolide in prostate cancer, endometriosis, and precocious puberty, and antagonists such as cetrorelix in assisted reproduction. Pulsatile GnRH therapy can restore fertility in hypothalamic amenorrhea.

History

GnRH was isolated and characterized in 1971 by the laboratories of Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally, who shared the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work on hypothalamic peptide hormones.
