Jazz improvisation

Jazz improvisation is the spontaneous creation of melodic lines over the chord progressions and forms of jazz tunes. It is the defining practice of jazz: soloists invent new melodies in real time while the rhythm section maintains the harmonic framework, typically in repeated choruses of a song form such as the 12-bar blues or 32-bar AABA standard.

Practice

Improvisers draw on scales and modes related to each chord, arpeggios, guide tones, and a vocabulary of learned phrases ("licks"), shaped by swing rhythm, phrasing and interaction with the band. Bebop in the 1940s greatly expanded the harmonic language, adding rapid chord substitutions and chromatic passing chords, exemplified by Charlie Parker's solos.

Approaches

Different eras emphasise different approaches: collective improvisation in early New Orleans jazz, motivic development in swing, chord-scale theory in post-bop pedagogy, modal improvisation after Miles Davis's Kind of Blue (1959), and free improvisation in free jazz.
