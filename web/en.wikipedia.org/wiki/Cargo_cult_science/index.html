Cargo cult science is a term coined by physicist Richard Feynman for practices that adopt the appearance and rituals of the scientific method without its substance — experiments that look like science but lack the "utter honesty" needed to bend over backwards to show how one might be wrong. Feynman introduced the idea in his 1974 commencement address at the California Institute of Technology, later reprinted in his memoir Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!

"In the South Seas there is a cargo cult of people. During the war they saw airplanes land with lots of good materials... they arranged to have things like runways... but the planes don't land. So I call these things cargo cult science, because they follow all the apparent precepts and forms of scientific investigation, but they're missing something essential." — Richard Feynman, 1974

Meaning

Feynman used the term for work such as parapsychology experiments and poorly controlled psychology studies, in which researchers reported only favourable results, failed to quantify alternative explanations, or ignored failed replications. The central virtue he demanded was scientific integrity: reporting everything that could invalidate one's own result, including all the factors one tried to rule out.

The metaphor has since been applied more broadly — to management fads, software practices ("cargo cult programming") and any field that copies the trappings of successful practice without understanding the underlying causes.
