The Buddha and His Dhamma

The Buddha and His Dhamma is a book by the Indian jurist, economist, and social reformer B. R. Ambedkar, treating the life and teachings of Gautama Buddha and the nature of Buddhism. Ambedkar completed the manuscript shortly before his death on 6 December 1956; it was published posthumously in 1957 by the People's Education Society. For followers of Ambedkar's Navayana (Dalit Buddhist) movement, the book is a sacred text — the scripture of the community that converted with him to Buddhism in October 1956.

Ambedkar presents the Buddha's teaching as a rational, socially engaged path.

Contents

The book is arranged in eight parts. The first narrates the Buddha's life "from birth to parinirvana"; subsequent parts present the Dhamma under Ambedkar's interpretive scheme — distinguishing the Buddha's own rational teaching from later accretions, and recasting central doctrines. Ambedkar famously rejected elements of traditional doctrine he considered later additions or incompatible with reason, including a literal doctrine of rebirth and karma as cosmic moral accounting, and he reinterpreted the Four Noble Truths' emphasis, arguing the Buddha's message was fundamentally social: the removal of suffering through moral conduct, equality, liberty, and fraternity.

Background

Ambedkar, born into the Mahar caste and a lifelong campaigner against caste discrimination, had declared in 1935 that he would not die a Hindu. After two decades of study, he and roughly half a million followers formally embraced Buddhism at Nagpur on 14 October 1956, taking 22 vows that renounced Hindu deities and rituals. The Buddha and His Dhamma was the doctrinal fruit of that decision and the capstone of his project of reconstructing Buddhism — Navayana, the "new vehicle" — for oppressed communities.

Reception and influence

The book is revered in Dalit Buddhist households and viharas across India, where readings from it form part of religious practice, and it has been translated into many languages. Scholars of Buddhism have debated Ambedkar's selective, modernist reading — comparing it with other modern reinterpretations — while historians treat it as a foundational document of anti-caste thought, standing alongside Annihilation of Caste in Ambedkar's canon.
