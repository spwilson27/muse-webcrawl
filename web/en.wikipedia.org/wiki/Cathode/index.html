Cathode

A cathode is the electrode from which a conventional current leaves a polarised electrical device. The term was coined by William Whewell at Michael Faraday's request in 1834, from the Greek kathodos, 'way down'. By definition, reduction occurs at the cathode.

In different devices

In a discharging battery (galvanic cell), the cathode is the positive terminal; in a charging battery or electrolytic cell, it is negative. In vacuum tubes, the cathode emits electrons — by thermionic emission from a heated filament or a separate heater — toward the anode, as in the cathode-ray tube. In a diode, the cathode is the terminal electrons flow toward under forward bias.

Types

Hot cathodes use heating to emit electrons; cold cathodes use field emission or secondary emission. Cathode materials include tungsten, oxide-coated metals, and, in lithium-ion batteries, layered metal oxides.
