Audio mastering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mastering studio console

Mastering, a form of audio post production, is the process of preparing and transferring recorded audio from a source containing the final mix to a data storage device (the master), the source from which all copies will be produced (via methods such as pressing, duplication or replication). In recent years digital masters have become usual, although analogue masters—such as audio tapes—are still being used by the manufacturing industry, particularly by a few engineers who specialize in analogue mastering.

Process

Mastering requires critical listening; however, software tools exist to facilitate the process. Results depend upon the intent of the engineer, the skills of the engineer, the accuracy of the speaker monitors, and the listening environment. Mastering engineers often apply equalization and dynamic range compression in order to optimize sound translation on all playback systems. Other processes include adjusting levels and fades between tracks, sequencing tracks into their final running order, editing minor flaws, applying noise reduction, and adding metadata such as ISRC codes.

History

The discipline emerged with the advent of recording itself: early masters were the wax or lacquer discs from which records were pressed, and the transfer of performances to those discs shaped the sound of releases. The introduction of magnetic tape in the late 1940s allowed the mastering process to become a separate step from recording, and dedicated mastering engineers such as those at major label studios developed the craft. The compact disc era brought digital mastering and the ability to make bit-exact copies, while the competition for perceived loudness in the 1990s and 2000s led to the so-called loudness war, in which dynamic range was sacrificed for higher average levels. Streaming services' adoption of loudness normalization has since reduced that incentive.

Techniques

  • Equalization – balancing the frequency spectrum of the mix.
  • Compression and limiting – controlling dynamics and peak levels; a limiter prevents clipping at the target ceiling.
  • Stereo enhancement – adjusting the width and mono compatibility of the stereo image.
  • Sequencing and spacing – ordering tracks and setting gaps for albums.
  • Dithering – adding low-level noise when reducing bit depth, e.g. from 24-bit to 16-bit CD audio.

Mastering for different media

Different distribution media impose different requirements. Vinyl mastering must account for the physical limitations of the groove, restricting bass content and extreme stereo bass. CD mastering targets the Red Book standard, while streaming masters are typically delivered as high-resolution files and measured against platform loudness targets expressed in LUFS.
