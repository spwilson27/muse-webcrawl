Lake Champlain is a natural freshwater lake in North America lying mainly within the borders of the United States (states of Vermont and New York) but also straddling the Canada–U.S. border in the Canadian province of Quebec. It is the sixth-largest lake in the United States by surface area. The lake was named after the French explorer Samuel de Champlain, who encountered it in July 1609.

Geography

The lake extends about 172 km (107 mi) from near Whitehall, New York, in the south to its outlet at the Richelieu River in Quebec in the north, which drains it into the St. Lawrence River. It lies in the Champlain Valley between the Green Mountains of Vermont to the east and the Adirondack Mountains of New York to the west. Major tributaries include Otter Creek, the Winooski, Lamoille, and Missisquoi rivers in Vermont and the Great Chazy, Saranac, and Ausable rivers in New York. Its largest city is Burlington, Vermont; other ports include Plattsburgh, New York, and Ticonderoga.

History

The lake basin was inhabited by Iroquoian and Algonquian peoples long before European contact; the Mohawk called it Caniaderi-Guarunte, "the lake that is the gate of the country." During the French and Indian War, the Revolutionary War, and the War of 1812 it was a strategically vital corridor: Fort Ticonderoga guarded its southern narrows, Benedict Arnold's fleet fought the Battle of Valcour Island in 1776, and the American victory at the Battle of Plattsburgh in 1814 helped end the War of 1812. The Champlain Canal (1823) and Hudson River links made it a major commercial waterway.

Ecology

The lake supports more than 80 fish species, including lake trout, landlocked Atlantic salmon, and the invasive sea lamprey, whose control is a long-standing management priority. It is famous in folklore for "Champ," a lake-monster legend. In 1998 the lake was briefly designated by statute as one of the Great Lakes, a designation rescinded weeks later after public debate.
