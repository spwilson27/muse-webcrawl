Solar barque

The solar barque (also spelled solar bark or solar boat) is the vessel in ancient Egyptian mythology by which the sun god Ra travels across the sky by day and through the underworld (the Duat) by night. The concept is one of the central images of Egyptian religion: the daily rebirth of the sun represented cosmic order (maat) triumphing over chaos.

Mythology

According to the myth, Ra sails in the Mandjet, the "Barque of Millions of Years," across the sky during the day. At sunset he transfers to the Mesektet barque for the nightly journey through the twelve hours of the Duat, where he brings light to the dead and is ritually reborn. During the night voyage the serpent Apep (Apophis), embodiment of chaos, attacks the barque; Ra is defended by other gods, notably Set standing at the prow with a spear, and by Mehen, the serpent coiled protectively around the god's cabin. If Apep were ever to prevail, the sun would fail to rise.

Funerary significance

Deceased pharaohs were believed to join Ra in his barque. Full-size boats were buried beside royal tombs so the king could make the journey: the most famous surviving examples are the two boats of Khufu (c. 2500 BCE) found in pits beside the Great Pyramid of Giza, one of which was reassembled and displayed in the Giza Solar Boat Museum before being moved to the Grand Egyptian Museum. Smaller model boats were placed in tombs of officials, and depictions of the barque appear in the underworld books carved and painted in New Kingdom royal tombs, such as the Amduat and the Book of Gates.
