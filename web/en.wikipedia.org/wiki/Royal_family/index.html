Royal family

A royal family is the immediate family of a king or queen regnant, and sometimes the term is extended to the wider family of a monarch. Where a member of a royal family is of sovereign rank, they often carry the style of "Royal Highness". Although in republics the term is not strictly applicable, families descended from former monarchs are often still referred to as royal families.

A royal family typically includes the spouse of the reigning monarch, surviving spouses of deceased monarchs, children, grandchildren, siblings and sometimes more distant relatives of the monarch. Royal families may also be associated with a dynasty, a sequence of rulers from the same lineage.

Functions and roles

Members of royal families perform ceremonial, representative and charitable functions on behalf of the state. In constitutional monarchies, working members of a royal family undertake official engagements such as state visits, national commemorations, and the patronage of charities and regiments, while the monarch acts as head of state. In absolute or semi-absolute monarchies, members of the royal family may exercise real governmental power, holding ministerial portfolios or commanding the armed forces.

Composition and styles

The composition of a royal family is usually fixed by law, custom, or the monarch's letters patent. In the United Kingdom, for example, the style "Royal Highness" and the title of prince or princess are governed by letters patent of 1917 issued by George V, restricting them to the children of the monarch, children of sons of the monarch, and the eldest son of the eldest son of the Prince of Wales (extended by later instruments). Other European monarchies, including those of the Netherlands, Sweden and Spain, have in recent decades restricted membership of the royal house to the monarch's closest relatives.

Royal families today

Surviving reigning royal families include those of the United Kingdom and the Commonwealth realms (the House of Windsor), Saudi Arabia (the House of Saud, numbering thousands of princes), the Netherlands (the House of Orange-Nassau), Spain (the House of Bourbon), Sweden (the House of Bernadotte), Denmark (the House of Glücksburg), Norway, Belgium, Japan (the Imperial House, the world's oldest continuing hereditary monarchy), Thailand (the Chakri dynasty), Morocco, Jordan, and the Gulf emirates. Non-reigning royal families, such as those of Greece, Italy, Romania and Bulgaria, retain a social and sometimes quasi-official role in their countries.

Dynastic orders and households

Royal families are often associated with dynastic orders of chivalry, palaces and court households, and with crown estates. The head of a royal family may be the sovereign of such orders and grant membership to family members and distinguished subjects.
