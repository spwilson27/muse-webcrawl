"Address to a Haggis" is a Scots-language poem by Robert Burns (1759–1796), written in 1786. It is a mock-heroic ode celebrating haggis, the traditional Scottish savoury pudding, and is traditionally recited at Burns suppers on 25 January (Burns Night) as the haggis is brought to the table and ceremonially cut open.

[Image: A haggis being piped in at a Burns supper]

Text and structure

The poem consists of eight six-line stanzas in the Standard Habbie (or "Burns stanza") metre. It opens with the famous lines:

Fair fa' your honest, sonsie face,
Great chieftain o' the puddin-race!
Aboon them a' ye tak your place,
Painch, tripe, or thairm:
Weel are ye wordy o' a grace
As lang's my airm.

Burns personifies the haggis as a rustic warrior, contrasting its hearty plainness with pretentious French and Italian dishes ("ragouts", "olio", "fricassee") which he mocks as fit only to make a pig vomit. As the reciter reaches the lines "His knife see rustic Labour dight, / An' cut you up wi' ready slight, / Trenching your gushing entrails bright", the haggis is sliced open. The poem closes with a toast to the dish's rustic honour and a prayer of thanks.

History

According to tradition, Burns improvised the address in 1786 at a dinner in the Edinburgh home of merchant Andrew Bruce, astonished at the sight of a haggis after a period away from Scotland. It was first printed in the Caledonian Mercury on 20 December 1786 and included the following year in the Edinburgh edition of his poems. The poem helped elevate haggis from a humble peasant dish to an emblem of Scottish national identity.

Burns suppers

The Address forms the centrepiece of the modern Burns supper, where the haggis is piped in by a bagpiper, addressed with dramatic gestures, stabbed open at the appropriate line, and toasted with whisky before being served with neeps and tatties (turnips/swede and potatoes).
