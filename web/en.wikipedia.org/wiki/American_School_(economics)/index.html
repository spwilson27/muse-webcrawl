The American School, also known as the American System or the National System, is an economic programme and school of thought that dominated American economic policy from the 1790s into the 1970s, in varying degrees. It rests on three interlocking pillars: protective tariffs to foster manufacturing, a national bank to stabilise currency and provide credit, and federal subsidies for internal improvements — roads, canals, railways — to build a national market.

Origins

The programme originated with Alexander Hamilton's reports as the first Secretary of the Treasury — his Report on Manufactures (1791) argued for tariffs and subsidies to build industry — and was named and championed by Senator Henry Clay, whose "American System" was the Whig Party platform. The German economist Friedrich List, who lived in the United States in the 1820s, systematised the ideas in his National System of Political Economy (1841), and Henry Charles Carey became its leading mid-century theorist. Abraham Lincoln, a Whig turned Republican, implemented much of it during the Civil War era, including high tariffs, the National Banking Acts, and land-grant support for transcontinental railroads.

Principles and decline

The American School opposed laissez-faire free trade, holding that national independence and prosperity required a government role in developing productive powers and "harmony of interests" between agriculture, commerce, and manufacture. Its tariff pillar survived into the 20th century; the programme's influence waned after the Second World War as the United States became the champion of global free trade.
