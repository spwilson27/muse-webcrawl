The sacred–profane dichotomy is a central concept in the sociology of religion, most famously articulated by Émile Durkheim in his 1912 book The Elementary Forms of Religious Life. Durkheim argued that all religions divide the world into two fundamentally opposed domains: the sacred — things set apart, forbidden, and surrounded by ritual respect — and the profane — the ordinary, everyday, utilitarian sphere. For Durkheim, this distinction, rather than belief in gods or the supernatural, is the defining feature of religion, and sacred things are essentially symbols of society itself and its collective representations.

Features of the distinction

In Durkheim's account, sacred and profane are absolutely heterogeneous: passage between the two requires ritual, and contact between them is governed by prohibitions (taboos). Which objects count as sacred is arbitrary in itself — a rock, a flag, a totemic animal or a text can be sacralised by collective practice — but once set apart they inspire awe and become foci of ritual and moral community. Rites periodically renew the group's attachment to its sacred symbols, producing what Durkheim called collective effervescence.

Influence and criticism

The historian of religion Mircea Eliade adopted and transformed the dichotomy in The Sacred and the Profane (1957), treating the sacred as an irreducible mode of human consciousness manifesting in sacred space and time. Later scholars criticised the dichotomy as too rigid and too universal: in many cultures the sacred and mundane interpenetrate, and critics such as Jack Goody questioned whether the binary applies outside the traditions Durkheim and Eliade studied. Nonetheless the distinction remains a standard reference point in religious studies.
