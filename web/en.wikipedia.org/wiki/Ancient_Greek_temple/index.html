Ancient Greek temple

The ancient Greek temple (Greek: ναός, naos, "dwelling") was a structure built to house the cult image of a deity within Greek sanctuaries. Unlike places of congregational worship, temples were not primarily for communal prayer; sacrifices and rituals took place outdoors at an altar before the temple, and the building itself served as the god's house and as a treasury for votive offerings. Greek temples are among the most influential buildings in the history of architecture.

The Parthenon (447–432 BC), the archetypal Doric temple

Development

Early temples of the eighth and seventh centuries BC were built of wood and mud brick; by the sixth century BC they were monumentalized in limestone and marble. The canonical plan comprises a central rectangular chamber (the cella or naos) housing the cult statue, a porch (pronaos) in front, often a rear chamber, and a surrounding colonnade (peristasis) on a stepped platform, the crepidoma.

The orders

Greek temple design is defined by the architectural orders. The Doric order, dominant on the mainland, features sturdy fluted columns without bases and a frieze of triglyphs and metopes. The Ionic order, developed in Ionia and the Aegean, uses slender columns with bases and voluted capitals and a continuous frieze. The later Corinthian order adds an ornate acanthus-leaf capital. Famous examples include the Parthenon in Athens, the Temple of Hephaestus, the temples of Paestum and Agrigento in Italy, and the great Ionic temples of Ephesus and Didyma.

Decoration and optics

Temples were originally painted in vivid colors, with sculpture filling the pediments and friezes. Builders employed subtle refinements — the slight upward curvature of the stylobate and the entasis (gentle swelling) of columns — to counteract optical illusions and give the structure a sense of life.
