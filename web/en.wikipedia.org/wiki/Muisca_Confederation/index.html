Muisca Confederation

The Muisca Confederation was a loose confederation of states (cacicazgos) that united the Muisca people of the Altiplano Cundiboyacense, the high plateau of the Eastern Ranges of the Colombian Andes, before the Spanish conquest of the 1530s. It was not a kingdom or empire but an association of independent chiefdoms linked by language (Muisca, a Chibchan language), religion and trade.

Organisation

The two paramount rulers were the zipa of Bacatá (near modern Bogotá), who dominated the southern savanna, and the zaque of Hunza (modern Tunja) in the north. Other important centres included the religious city of Sugamuxi (Sogamoso), governed by the iraca, and Tundama (Duitama), governed by the tundama. The Muisca farmed maize, potatoes, quinoa and cotton on the fertile high plain, mined salt at Zipaquirá and Nemocón, traded for emeralds and gold, and worked gold into the votive figurines (tunjos) for which they are famed.

Religion and El Dorado

Muisca religion centred on the sun god Sué and the moon goddess Chía, with great temples at Sugamuxi and Bacatá. The famous El Dorado legend originated in a Muisca ceremony at Lake Guatavita, in which a newly invested zipa, covered in gold dust, made offerings of gold and emeralds from a raft — a scene depicted by the Muisca raft, the best-known piece of pre-Columbian goldwork.

Conquest

The confederation was conquered between 1536 and 1539 by the expedition of Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada, who founded Bogotá in 1538. The last zipa, Sagipa, and the last zaque were executed or died in Spanish custody, and the Muisca were incorporated into the New Kingdom of Granada, their language dying out by the 18th century.
