William Kemmler


Born May 9, 1860, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, U.S.
Died August 6, 1890 (aged 30), Auburn Prison, New York
Known for First person executed in the electric chair

William Francis Kemmler (May 9, 1860 – August 6, 1890) was an American murderer who became the first person executed by means of the electric chair.

Crime and trial

Kemmler, a produce merchant in Buffalo, New York, murdered his common-law wife, Matilda "Tillie" Ziegler, with a hatchet on March 29, 1889, while drunk. He was convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to death under New York's new Electrical Execution Law, which had replaced hanging with electrocution effective January 1, 1889.

Appeals and the war of the currents

His appeal against the sentence became entangled in the commercial "war of the currents" between Thomas Edison's direct-current interests and George Westinghouse's alternating-current company. Westinghouse funded the appeal, arguing electrocution was cruel and unusual punishment; Edison's camp promoted AC as the deadly current suitable for executions. The appeal failed, and the sentence stood.

Execution

Kemmler was executed at Auburn Prison on August 6, 1890. The first application of current, lasting about 17 seconds, failed to kill him, and witnesses reported signs of life; a second, longer application followed. Contemporary accounts described the procedure as gruesome, and Westinghouse remarked that "they would have done better using an axe."
