The National Council of the Churches of Christ in the United States of America, usually shortened to the National Council of Churches (NCC), is the largest ecumenical organisation in the United States, bringing together 38 Christian communions — mainline Protestant, Orthodox, African American, evangelical and historic peace churches — representing tens of millions of adherents.

History

The NCC was founded in 1950 in Cleveland, Ohio, through the merger of a dozen interdenominational agencies, succeeding the earlier Federal Council of Churches (1908). From its offices for decades in the Interchurch Center on Riverside Drive in New York — nicknamed the "God Box" — the Council coordinated work in missions, relief, broadcasting and social advocacy. In 2013 it moved its headquarters to Washington, D.C., and restructured around advocacy and interfaith dialogue.

Work and positions

The Council is best known to the public as the sponsor of two widely used Bible translations, the Revised Standard Version (1952) and the New Revised Standard Version (1989). Through Church World Service it long ran refugee and disaster relief work. The NCC has historically taken liberal positions on civil rights, poverty, peace and the environment — supporting the civil rights movement in the 1960s and opposing the Vietnam and Iraq wars — which has made it a frequent target of conservative criticism even as its member denominations' membership has declined.
