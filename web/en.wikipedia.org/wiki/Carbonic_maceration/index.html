Carbonic maceration

Whole bunches of Gamay grapes undergoing carbonic maceration for Beaujolais wine. (Image placeholder)

Carbonic maceration is a winemaking technique, often associated with the French wine region of Beaujolais, in which whole, uncrushed grapes are fermented in a carbon dioxide-rich environment before being crushed. It produces light-bodied, fruity red wines with soft tannins and distinctive aromas of banana, bubblegum, cherry and kirsch, intended to be drunk young.

Process

Whole bunches of hand-harvested grapes are placed in a sealed tank that is filled with carbon dioxide to exclude oxygen. In this anaerobic environment, fermentation begins inside each intact berry, driven by the grape's own enzymes rather than by yeast: sugars are converted to ethanol (reaching about 2% alcohol within the berry) while malic acid is broken down and aromatic compounds are formed. After several days to a few weeks, the berries are pressed; the released juice then completes a conventional yeast fermentation.

A related, looser technique called semi-carbonic maceration omits the deliberate addition of CO₂: the weight of the fruit crushes the grapes at the bottom of the tank, whose fermentation produces the carbon dioxide that triggers intracellular fermentation in the whole berries above.

Wines

The classic application is Beaujolais nouveau, made from the Gamay grape and released each year on the third Thursday of November, only weeks after harvest. Carbonic and semi-carbonic maceration are also used for other early-drinking wines in the Languedoc, Rioja (for young cosechero wines) and elsewhere, and have been adopted by many natural and experimental winemakers. Wines made this way are generally not suited to ageing, because their appeal lies in fresh primary fruit and low tannin.
