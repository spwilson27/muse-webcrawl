Human capital

Human capital
Field: Economics
Key thinkers: Adam Smith, Theodore Schultz, Gary Becker, Jacob Mincer
Components: Education, training, health, skills, experience

Human capital is a concept in economics used to designate the personal attributes — knowledge, skills, education, training, health, and experience — considered useful in the production process. It is the stock of habits and capabilities embodied in people that can be augmented by investment, analogous to physical capital.

Theory

Adam Smith noted acquired abilities as a form of capital. The modern theory was developed in the 1960s by Theodore Schultz and Gary Becker, whose Human Capital (1964) treated education and training as investments yielding returns in higher earnings; Jacob Mincer's earnings function linked wages to schooling and experience. Human capital helps explain wage differences, economic growth, and migration.

Measurement and criticism

Measures include years of schooling, test scores, and lifetime-earnings approaches such as the World Bank's Human Capital Index. Critics argue the concept reduces people to economic instruments, and that signalling, credentials, and social context also drive returns to education.
