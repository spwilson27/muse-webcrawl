Luminism is an American landscape painting style of the 1850s to 1870s, characterised by clear, detailed, almost photographic renderings of light in which the effects of light on water and sky are calm, reflective and crystalline, and the brushwork is invisible. Luminist works emphasise tranquillity and a feeling of the sublime serenity of nature, often with nearly mirror-still water, a low horizon, and an intense, clear light. The term was introduced by art historian John I. H. Baur in 1954 to describe a tendency within 19th-century American landscape painting rather than a self-conscious movement; the artists themselves never used the name.

Characteristics

Luminist canvases typically depict coastal scenes, rivers, lakes and marshes at dawn, midday or dusk, rendered with exacting detail and glassy surfaces. Artists concealed their brushstrokes to create an "invisible" technique, used cool, clear colours, and organised their compositions around horizontal calm rather than dramatic movement. Unlike the grand wilderness panoramas of the Hudson River School, with which Luminism overlaps, Luminist scenes are intimate, ordered and suffused with stillness; human figures, when present, are small and incidental.

Artists

The principal Luminists include the marine painter Fitz Henry Lane of Gloucester, Massachusetts, whose harbour scenes are canonical examples; Martin Johnson Heade, known for his marsh scenes and haystacks in changing light; John Frederick Kensett, painter of Lake George and coastal subjects; and Sanford Robinson Gifford. Jasper Francis Cropsey and others associated with the Hudson River School also produced works with Luminist qualities. Luminism is related to, but distinct from, the Russian landscape tradition and from the Australian Heidelberg School's treatment of light.
