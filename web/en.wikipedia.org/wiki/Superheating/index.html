Superheating

Superheated water can remain liquid above its boiling point until disturbed.

In physics, superheating (sometimes referred to as boiling retardation, or boiling delay) is the phenomenon in which a liquid is heated to a temperature higher than its standard boiling point, without actually boiling. This may occur if a substance is heated in a clean vessel, in a microwave oven, or if it is disturbed, jostled, or simply reaches nucleation conditions under which bubbles can form.

Occurrence

Superheating occurs when a liquid is heated without the nucleation sites — scratches, particles, or dissolved gases — that normally allow bubbles of vapor to form. Very pure water heated in a smooth container in a microwave oven is a classic example: the water can exceed 100 °C without visible boiling. When the container is moved or a foreign object is introduced, boiling can begin explosively, ejecting scalding water; safety warnings about this hazard accompany most microwave ovens.

Industrial use

In steam engineering, superheating has a different, deliberate meaning: steam heated above its saturation temperature is called superheated steam and is used in steam turbines, because it increases thermal efficiency and prevents condensation droplets from damaging turbine blades. Superheaters are standard components of steam locomotive boilers and power station boilers.

Related phenomena

The counterpart phenomenon for cooling a liquid below its freezing point without solidifying is called supercooling. Both are examples of metastable states.
