Mudrocks are a class of fine-grained siliciclastic sedimentary rocks. They are composed predominantly of silt-sized and clay-sized particles (less than 0.0625 millimetres in diameter) and account for more than half of all sedimentary rocks in the geological record. Mudrocks include mudstone, shale, siltstone and claystone, distinguished chiefly by grain size and by whether the rock splits along bedding planes (fissility).

Classification

Mudrocks are classified by the proportions of silt and clay they contain and by their texture. Claystone is dominated by clay-sized particles and lacks fissility; mudstone contains roughly equal silt and clay and also breaks into blocky fragments; siltstone is dominated by silt; and shale is a laminated, fissile mudrock that splits readily along its bedding. The term argillite is sometimes applied to slightly metamorphosed mudrocks.

Formation

Mud is deposited in low-energy environments where fine particles can settle out of suspension: deep marine basins, floodplains, lakes, lagoons and delta plains. After burial, compaction expels water and transforms the soft mud into rock through lithification. Organic-rich mudrocks deposited in oxygen-poor settings become the source rocks for petroleum, generating oil and natural gas as they are heated during deeper burial.

Significance

Because mudrocks are so abundant and relatively impermeable, they act as seals over hydrocarbon reservoirs, as aquitards in groundwater systems, and as hosts to economically important deposits. Their fine grain preserves delicate fossils and detailed records of past environments, making them central to palaeontology and palaeoclimate studies. Weathering of mudrocks produces clay-rich soils, and their tendency to slake and swell causes engineering difficulties in foundations and tunnels.
