Ushr


Agricultural land subject to the ushr levy.

Ushr (Arabic: ʿushr, عشر, "one tenth") is a traditional Islamic levy of one-tenth on agricultural produce, historically applied to land owned by Muslims. It is one of the classical taxes of Islamic fiscal law, alongside kharaj (a land tax levied mainly on non-Muslim landholders or on conquered land), jizya (a poll tax on non-Muslim subjects), and zakat (obligatory almsgiving).

Rates and rules

Classical jurists held that land watered by rain, rivers, or springs without costly irrigation owed the full tenth (10%) of its produce, while land irrigated by artificial means — wells, wheels, or purchased water — owed half the tenth (5%), following a hadith of the Prophet Muhammad: "On that which is watered by the sky and springs... a tenth; on that which is watered by drawing water, half a tenth." Liability generally arose when the harvest reached a minimum threshold (nisab), commonly set at five wasq (roughly 653 kg of produce) in Hanafi and other schools, though the Hanafis famously applied ushr to produce of any quantity.

Ushri land

Land classified as ushri — for example, land owned by Muslims at the time of conquest whose owners embraced Islam, or previously uncultivated land revived by Muslims — paid ushr, whereas kharaji land paid the generally heavier kharaj. The classification of particular provinces (such as the Sawad of Iraq, treated as kharaji by the caliph Umar) shaped agrarian taxation in the early Islamic world and the Ottoman Empire, where ushr (Turkish: öşür) remained a major tithe on peasants until its abolition in Turkey in 1925.

Modern practice

In some modern states with Islamic taxation systems, such as Pakistan, ushr has been revived as a formal levy on agricultural landowners as part of zakat administration, though assessment and collection have often been irregular.
