Arthurian legend

The Arthurian legend, also known as the Matter of Britain, is the body of stories and medieval romances centred on King Arthur, the legendary British leader who, according to medieval histories and romances, led the defence of Britain against Saxon invaders in the late fifth and early sixth centuries. Developed over centuries by Welsh, Breton, French, English and other European writers, the legend encompasses Arthur's court at Camelot, the Knights of the Round Table, the wizard Merlin, Queen Guinevere, Sir Lancelot, the sword Excalibur, and the quest for the Holy Grail.

Origins

The earliest references to Arthur appear in Welsh and Latin sources such as the Historia Brittonum (c. 830), which lists twelve battles fought by Arthur, and the Annales Cambriae, which record the Battle of Badon and the Battle of Camlann. Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136) transformed Arthur into a great conqueror-king and introduced Merlin and much of the familiar narrative framework. French poets, above all Chrétien de Troyes in the late twelfth century, added Lancelot, Camelot, the Grail quest and the romance of Lancelot and Guinevere, while the vast thirteenth-century Vulgate Cycle systematised the story from Arthur's birth to his death.

Principal elements

Central episodes include Arthur's drawing of the sword from the stone (or receiving Excalibur from the Lady of the Lake), his marriage to Guinevere, the fellowship and adventures of the Knights of the Round Table, the Grail quest undertaken by Galahad, Perceval and Bors, the adulterous love of Lancelot and Guinevere, the treachery of Mordred, and Arthur's final battle and departure to the Isle of Avalon — from which, legend says, he will one day return.

Later literature and adaptations

Sir Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485), printed by Caxton, became the standard English account. The legend was revived by the Victorians, notably Tennyson's Idylls of the King, and reshaped in the twentieth century by T. H. White's The Once and Future King (1958). It continues to inspire novels, films, musicals (such as Camelot and Monty Python and the Holy Grail), games and scholarship, and the question of a historical Arthur remains debated without consensus.
