Stepped-up basis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stepped-up basis is a provision of United States federal income tax law under which the tax basis of an asset inherited from a decedent is reset — usually "stepped up" — to the asset's fair market value at the date of the decedent's death (or an alternate valuation date six months later). The rule is codified principally at Section 1014 of the Internal Revenue Code. Its effect is that capital gains accrued during the decedent's lifetime are never subject to income tax: an heir who sells the asset shortly after inheritance owes tax only on appreciation occurring after the date of death.

Mechanism

In general, a taxpayer's basis in property is its cost, and tax on a sale is computed on the difference between the sale price and the basis. For property acquired by gift, the donee normally takes the donor's basis (carryover basis). For property acquired from a decedent, by contrast, the basis becomes the fair market value at death. Although popularly called a "step-up," the adjustment can also be a step-down for assets that have declined in value. In community property states, both halves of community property generally receive a new basis, whereas only the decedent's share of jointly held property in common-law states is adjusted.

Rationale and criticism

Supporters argue that the rule simplifies tax administration (heirs need not reconstruct a decedent's historical cost), avoids double taxation alongside the estate tax, and prevents forced sales of family farms and businesses. Critics contend that it is one of the largest tax expenditures in the code, disproportionately benefits wealthy households, encourages holding appreciated assets until death (the "lock-in" effect), and allows large lifetime gains to escape income tax entirely. Various reform proposals — including treating death as a taxable realization event, carryover basis at death, and capping the step-up — have been advanced repeatedly; a brief carryover-basis regime enacted in 1976 was repealed before taking effect, and a limited carryover system applied for decedents dying in 2010, when the estate tax was temporarily repealed.

Related rules

The step-up does not apply to items of income in respect of a decedent, such as traditional retirement account balances, which retain their taxable character in the heir's hands. Property passing to a surviving spouse generally qualifies for the marital deduction from the estate tax while still receiving the new basis.
