Vertical-lift bridge

TypeMovable bridge
MechanismSpan raised vertically between towers by cables and counterweights
Notable exampleArthur Kill Vertical Lift Bridge (New York)

A vertical-lift bridge is a type of movable bridge in which a span rises vertically between two towers while remaining parallel to the deck, allowing ships to pass beneath. The span is suspended from wire ropes passing over sheaves at the tops of the towers and is balanced by counterweights, so comparatively little power is needed to lift it.

Design

The lifting span is guided by the towers at each end. In the raised position the bridge offers a fixed vertical clearance and practically unlimited horizontal clearance for navigation, unlike bascule and swing bridges, which constrain the channel or require a pivot pier in the waterway. Lift height is limited by tower height, and the span must be raised fully for tall vessels, so operations can take several minutes.

History and examples

The design was developed in the 1890s, with J. A. L. Waddell's 1894 bridge over the Chicago River's South Branch an early landmark. Large examples include the Arthur Kill Vertical Lift Bridge between Staten Island and New Jersey (1959), with a 558 ft (170 m) lift span, and the Marine Parkway–Gil Hodges Memorial Bridge in New York. Many smaller lift bridges serve canals in the Netherlands and northern France.
