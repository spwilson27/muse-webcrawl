Sterile insect technique

The sterile insect technique (SIT) is a method of biological pest control in which large numbers of sterile male insects are released into the environment. The sterile males mate with wild females, which then produce no offspring, so the pest population declines and can be eradicated from an area. Because the insects themselves seek out mates, the technique is species-specific and leaves no chemical residues.

How it works

Target insects are mass-reared in factories that can produce hundreds of millions of individuals per week. The males are separated from the females (increasingly by genetic sexing strains) and sterilised with ionising radiation, typically from cobalt-60 or caesium-137 sources, at a dose high enough to damage their sperm but low enough to leave them competitive. They are then released, often from aircraft, at ratios that swamp the wild male population — commonly ten sterile males for every wild male.

History and successes

The technique was pioneered in the 1950s by American entomologists Edward F. Knipling and Raymond C. Bushland. Its first triumph was the eradication of the New World screwworm, a devastating livestock parasite, from the island of Curaçao and then from the United States, Mexico and Central America; a permanent barrier zone of sterile-fly releases is maintained in Panama. SIT has since been used against Mediterranean fruit flies, codling moths, pink bollworm, and tsetse flies — notably eradicating the tsetse from Zanzibar in 1997 — and is being adapted to mosquitoes that transmit malaria, dengue and Zika.

Limitations

SIT works best against low-density populations and isolated areas, and requires sustained releases, quality control of the reared insects, and suppression of the wild population beforehand, often with insecticide bait sprays. It is a cornerstone of area-wide integrated pest management programmes promoted by the FAO and the IAEA.
