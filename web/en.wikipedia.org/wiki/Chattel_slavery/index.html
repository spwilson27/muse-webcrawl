Chattel slavery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Historical depiction of a slave auction in the American South]

Chattel slavery is the form of slavery in which the enslaved person is legally treated as the personal property — a chattel — of the enslaver, who may buy, sell, trade, lease, or bequeath them like any other movable asset. In most chattel slavery systems the status was hereditary, passing from mother to child, and permanent for life unless the enslaver granted manumission or the state intervened.

Characteristics

Chattel slavery is distinguished from other forms of coerced labour, such as indentured servitude, serfdom, debt bondage, or penal labour, by the completeness of the property right claimed over the person: the enslaved individual had no legal personhood, could not own property, marry with legal effect, or testify against the enslaver in most jurisdictions, and their children were automatically the enslaver's property.

History

Forms of chattel slavery existed in many ancient societies, including Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome. Its most extensive early-modern form was the system built around the transatlantic slave trade, in which millions of Africans were transported to the Americas between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries to labour on plantations producing sugar, tobacco, cotton, and other commodities. In the United States, chattel slavery was entrenched in law in the Southern states until abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865, following the Civil War.

Abolition and legacy

The nineteenth century saw successive abolitions: the British Empire abolished slavery in most of its territories in 1833–34, France in 1848, the United States in 1865, and Brazil — the last major American destination — in 1888. Slavery in all its forms is now prohibited under international law, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), though illegal enslavement persists in various forms. The economic, social, and racial legacies of chattel slavery remain subjects of historical study and public debate, including discussions of reparations.
