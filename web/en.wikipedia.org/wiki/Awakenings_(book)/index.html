Awakenings is a 1973 non-fiction book by the British neurologist Oliver Sacks. It recounts the life histories of a group of patients who had survived the encephalitis lethargica epidemic of the 1920s and had spent decades in a trance-like, frozen state at Beth Abraham Hospital in the Bronx, New York, before being "awakened" in 1969 by the experimental drug L-DOPA.

Background

The patients described in the book were survivors of the worldwide epidemic of encephalitis lethargica (also called "sleepy sickness") that spread between roughly 1916 and 1927. Many survivors developed severe post-encephalitic parkinsonism, becoming effectively imprisoned within their own bodies — motionless, speechless, and dependent on others for all care. Sacks began working at Beth Abraham Hospital in 1966 and encountered dozens of these patients there.

The L-DOPA trials

In 1969, influenced by George Cotzias's work with the dopamine precursor L-DOPA in Parkinson's disease, Sacks began administering the drug to his post-encephalitic patients. The results were at first miraculous: patients who had not moved or spoken in decades suddenly rose, walked, talked, and resumed lives interrupted forty years earlier. Sacks documented these transformations in case studies that form the core of the book.

The awakenings proved temporary and complicated. Many patients developed severe side effects — tics, fluctuations between responsiveness and unresponsiveness, and psychological disturbances — and the narrative explores the ethical and existential dimensions of giving people back a world that had moved on without them.

Reception and adaptations

Awakenings was Sacks's second book and the work that first brought him wide attention. W. H. Auden called it a masterpiece. The book was adapted into a 1990 feature film, Awakenings, directed by Penny Marshall and starring Robin Williams as the physician (renamed Malcolm Sayer) and Robert De Niro as a patient, Leonard Lowe. The film was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Picture. The book also inspired a documentary by British television in 1974 and a play by Harold Pinter, A Kind of Alaska (1982).

Editions

Sacks substantially revised the book for later editions, adding extensive footnotes and appendices reflecting on the patients' subsequent courses and on subsequent developments in neuroscience. A notable revised edition appeared in 1990 alongside the film.
