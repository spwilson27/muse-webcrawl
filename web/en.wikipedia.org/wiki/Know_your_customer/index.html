Know your customer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Know your customer (KYC) is the process by which banks and other financial institutions verify the identity of their clients and assess their suitability, along with the potential risks of illegal intentions such as money laundering in the business relationship. The term is also used for the regulations governing these activities, which form a central element of anti-money-laundering (AML) compliance worldwide.

Components

KYC controls commonly include:

  • Customer identification programme — collecting and verifying identifying information such as name, address, date of birth, and official identification documents.
  • Customer due diligence (CDD) — understanding the nature and purpose of the customer's activities and developing a risk profile; enhanced due diligence (EDD) applies to higher-risk customers such as politically exposed persons (PEPs).
  • Beneficial ownership identification — establishing who ultimately owns or controls corporate customers, in order to detect shell companies used to conceal illicit funds.
  • Ongoing monitoring — scrutinizing transactions for consistency with the customer's profile and reporting suspicious activity.

Legal frameworks

The global standard is set by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendations, notably Recommendation 10 on customer due diligence. In the United States, KYC obligations derive from the Bank Secrecy Act 1970 and were strengthened by the USA PATRIOT Act 2001; FinCEN's CDD Rule (effective 2018) requires identification of beneficial owners of legal entity customers. In the European Union, successive Anti-Money Laundering Directives have progressively tightened KYC and beneficial ownership requirements. Cryptocurrency exchanges and other virtual asset service providers have increasingly been brought within KYC rules, including the FATF "travel rule" requiring originator and beneficiary information to accompany transfers.

Criticism and challenges

KYC processes are criticized for their cost, for friction in customer onboarding, for excluding people who lack standard documentation ("de-risking" and financial exclusion), and for privacy concerns arising from the mass collection of personal data. Conversely, weak KYC controls have been at the centre of major laundering scandals in which banks paid large fines.
