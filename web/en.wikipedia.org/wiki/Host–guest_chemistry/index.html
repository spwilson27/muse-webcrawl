Host–guest chemistry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A guest molecule bound within a macrocyclic host

Host–guest chemistry is the branch of supramolecular chemistry describing complexes in which a larger molecule, the host, binds a smaller molecule or ion, the guest, through non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effects, van der Waals forces, and metal coordination. Host–guest binding mimics the molecular recognition at the heart of enzyme–substrate and antibody–antigen interactions in biology.

Hosts and binding

Classic host families include crown ethers (which bind alkali metal cations), cryptands, cyclodextrins (toroidal sugars that bind hydrophobic guests in water), calixarenes, cucurbiturils, and cyclophanes. The strength and selectivity of binding, expressed as an association constant, depend on the complementarity of size, shape, and interaction sites between host and guest, and on effects such as preorganisation — the host paying the geometric cost of binding in advance. Cyclodextrin inclusion complexes are widely used in pharmaceuticals to solubilise poorly soluble drugs, and in foods and cosmetics to mask tastes and stabilise fragrances.

History

The field grew from the work of Charles Pedersen (crown ethers, 1967), Jean-Marie Lehn (cryptands), and Donald Cram (preorganisation and container molecules), who shared the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing molecules with structure-specific interactions of high selectivity. Host–guest principles underlie molecular machines — rotaxanes and catenanes in which guests are mechanically trapped — sensors, drug delivery systems, and self-healing materials.
