Sue (dinosaur)

Catalog no.FMNH PR 2081
SpeciesTyrannosaurus rex
Age~67–66 million years (Late Cretaceous)
Place discoveredCheyenne River Indian Reservation, South Dakota, USA
Date discovered12 August 1990
Discovered bySusan Hendrickson

Sue (catalog number FMNH PR 2081) is one of the largest, most extensive, and best-preserved Tyrannosaurus rex specimens ever found. Discovered in South Dakota in 1990 by paleontologist Susan Hendrickson — after whom the specimen is named — Sue measures about 12.3–12.8 metres (40–42 ft) long and stands roughly 3.7–4 metres (12–13 ft) tall at the hips. The specimen is more than 90% complete by bulk, an exceptional figure for a T. rex, and includes a superbly preserved skull over 1.5 metres long.

Discovery

On 12 August 1990, while working with a crew from the Black Hills Institute of Geological Research near Faith, South Dakota, Susan Hendrickson spotted several large vertebrae eroding from a cliff face on the Cheyenne River Indian Reservation, on land belonging to rancher Maurice Williams. Excavation over the following weeks exposed a remarkably complete articulated skeleton. The find quickly became the subject of a bitter legal dispute over ownership, involving the Institute, Williams, the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe, and the federal government.

Legal battle and auction

In 1992 the FBI and South Dakota National Guard seized the fossil in a widely publicized raid. Courts ultimately ruled that the land — and thus the fossil — was held in trust for Williams, meaning the Institute's purchase payment was invalid without federal approval. Williams then consigned Sue to Sotheby's, and on 4 October 1997 the skeleton sold at auction for US$8.36 million (including buyer's premium), at the time the highest price ever paid for a fossil. The buyers were a consortium of the Field Museum of Natural History, McDonald's, and Walt Disney World Resort, which funded the purchase for the Field Museum in Chicago.

Exhibition and research

After two years of preparation, Sue was unveiled at the Field Museum on 17 May 2000 and displayed in the museum's main Stanley Field Hall until 2018, when the mount was moved to a dedicated suite in the Evolving Planet exhibition, with updated posture and the addition of the gastralia (belly ribs). Research on Sue has yielded extensive information about T. rex growth, pathology, and biomechanics: the skeleton shows numerous healed injuries and pathologies, including broken ribs, a damaged shoulder blade, jaw lesions possibly caused by a parasitic infection similar to trichomoniasis, and evidence that the animal lived to an advanced age — Sue is estimated to have been about 28 years old at death, among the oldest known T. rex individuals.
