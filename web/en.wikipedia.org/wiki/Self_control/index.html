Self-control

Self-control is the ability to regulate one's emotions, thoughts, and behaviour in the face of temptations and impulses. As an executive function, it is the process by which one brings the self into line with standards such as ideals, values, morals, and social expectations, enabling longer-term goals to prevail over immediate desires.

Research

The most famous research programme is Walter Mischel's delay-of-gratification work, begun in the late 1960s: in the "marshmallow test", preschool children could eat one treat immediately or wait and receive two. Follow-ups reported that children who waited longer had better adolescent and adult outcomes, though later replications with larger, more representative samples (such as Watts, Duncan, and Quan, 2018) found the predictive effects much reduced once family background and early cognitive ability were controlled — indicating that the ability to wait partly reflects the child's environment and the reliability of promised rewards.

Terrie Moffitt and colleagues' Dunedin longitudinal study found that childhood self-control predicted adult health, wealth, and criminal offending in a graded fashion, even after accounting for intelligence and social class. Criminologists Michael Gottfredson and Travis Hirschi made low self-control the central construct of their general theory of crime (1990). Roy Baumeister's strength model proposed that self-control draws on a limited resource ("willpower") depleted by use — the ego-depletion hypothesis — though multi-lab replications in the 2010s largely failed to confirm a sizable effect, and the model remains contested.

Improving self-control

Strategies with empirical support include precommitment (such as Ulysses contracts), implementation intentions ("if–then" plans), avoiding or restructuring tempting situations rather than resisting them in the moment, monitoring behaviour, and treating sleep, stress, and habits as foundations of regulation. Self-control is related to, but narrower than, self-regulation: it typically refers to the conscious, effortful inhibition of impulses, whereas self-regulation also covers automatic and physiological processes.
