William Pickett (December 5, 1870 – April 2, 1932) was an American cowboy, rodeo performer, and actor, best known for inventing the rodeo event of bulldogging, now called steer wrestling. Pickett was of African American and Cherokee descent and became one of the most celebrated figures of the Wild West shows of the early twentieth century.

Early life

Pickett was born in Travis County, Texas, the second of thirteen children born to Thomas Jefferson Pickett, a former slave, and Mary Elizabeth Gilbert. He left school in the fifth grade to work as a ranch hand and soon developed remarkable skill with cattle.

Bulldogging

Pickett's signature technique involved leaping from a galloping horse onto the back of a steer, then wrestling it to the ground by biting its upper lip — a method he said he adapted from watching herding dogs subdue cattle. The stunt, billed as "bulldogging," made him a star attraction with the Miller Brothers 101 Ranch Wild West Show, with which he toured across the United States and internationally, performing in venues including Madison Square Garden.

Legacy

Pickett died in 1932 after being kicked by a horse at the 101 Ranch. In 1971 he became the first Black person inducted into the Rodeo Hall of Fame of the National Cowboy and Western Heritage Museum, and in 1989 he was inducted into the ProRodeo Hall of Fame.
