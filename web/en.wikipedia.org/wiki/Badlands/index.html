Badlands

Badlands are a type of dry terrain where sedimentary rocks and clay-rich soils have been deeply eroded by wind and water into a landscape of steep slopes, gullies, hoodoos, and ravines, largely bare of vegetation. The name derives from the Lakota mako sica, "bad land", also expressed in French as les mauvaises terres, terms applied to the region of present-day South Dakota.

Formation

Badlands form where soft sedimentary strata — shales, mudstones, siltstones — are exposed in arid or semi-arid climates. Intense but infrequent rainstorms carve dense networks of rills and gullies faster than soil can develop or vegetation take hold, while alternating hard and soft layers produce striped, multicoloured slopes. The resulting terrain is difficult to cross, unsuitable for agriculture, but often rich in fossils, since erosion continuously exposes buried remains.

Notable examples

  • Badlands National Park, South Dakota, USA — the type example, famous for Oligocene mammal fossils.
  • Theodore Roosevelt National Park, North Dakota, USA.
  • Drumheller and Dinosaur Provincial Park, Alberta, Canada.
  • Ischigualasto and the Valle de la Luna, Argentina.
  • The Bardenas Reales, Spain; the badlands of Basilicata, Italy; and formations in Taiwan and New Zealand.

In culture

The forbidding character of badlands has made the term figurative for any desolate or lawless region, and the landscapes feature prominently in Western films and in Terrence Malick's 1973 film Badlands.
