Russian conquest of Siberia

The Russian conquest of Siberia was the process by which the Tsardom of Russia expanded across northern Asia from the 1580s to the late 17th century, absorbing the lands between the Ural Mountains and the Pacific Ocean. Driven chiefly by the fur trade, it was carried out by small bands of Cossacks and promyshlenniki (trappers and traders) exacting yasak (fur tribute) from indigenous peoples.

Yermak and the Khanate of Sibir

In 1581 or 1582 the Cossack ataman Yermak Timofeyevich, hired by the Stroganov merchant family, crossed the Urals with about 840 men and defeated Khan Kuchum of the Khanate of Sibir, taking his capital Kashlyk (Isker) near modern Tobolsk. Yermak drowned in 1585, but Russian forces returned permanently, founding Tyumen (1586) and Tobolsk (1587).

Advance to the Pacific

Moving along the great Siberian rivers — Ob, Yenisei, Lena — the Russians built a chain of fortified posts (ostrogs): Tomsk (1604), Krasnoyarsk (1628), Yakutsk (1632), and Okhotsk on the Pacific (1647). Ivan Moskvitin reached the Sea of Okhotsk in 1639, only some sixty years after Yermak. Expansion brought clashes with indigenous groups including the Khanty, Evenks, Yakuts, Chukchi and Koryaks; the Chukchi resisted successfully for over a century. In the Amur valley, expeditions by Vasily Poyarkov and Yerofey Khabarov collided with Qing China, and the Treaty of Nerchinsk (1689) fixed the border, excluding Russia from the Amur until the 1850s.

Consequences

Siberian furs — especially sable — became a mainstay of Muscovite state revenue. The conquest devastated many native communities through violence, tribute demands, alcohol, and epidemics such as smallpox, while integrating Siberia's resources and, later, its exile system into the Russian state. Exploration continued into Kamchatka and across to Alaska in the 18th century.
