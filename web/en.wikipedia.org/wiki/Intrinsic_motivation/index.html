Intrinsic motivation

Intrinsic motivation is the act of doing something without any obvious external rewards — engaging in a behaviour because it is inherently interesting or enjoyable, rather than for some separable consequence. It stands in contrast to extrinsic motivation, in which an activity is performed to attain an outcome such as money, grades, or praise.

Self-determination theory

Within self-determination theory, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, intrinsic motivation flourishes when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: autonomy (a sense of volition), competence (feeling effective), and relatedness (connection to others). Cognitive evaluation theory, a sub-theory, explains how external events such as rewards, deadlines, and evaluations affect intrinsic motivation depending on whether they are experienced as controlling or informational.

The overjustification effect

Research beginning in the 1970s showed that expected tangible rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for an activity a person already enjoys — the overjustification effect. In a classic study by Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973), children who were promised a certificate for drawing — an activity they already liked — later spent less time drawing in free play than children who received no reward or an unexpected one. The phenomenon has been extensively debated, but meta-analyses generally find that expected, tangible, engagement-contingent rewards tend to reduce later free-choice persistence, while verbal praise often enhances intrinsic motivation.

Applications

The distinction matters in education, where autonomy-supportive teaching is associated with deeper engagement; in workplaces, where job design emphasizing autonomy and mastery can raise performance quality; and in game design and gamification, where poorly designed reward systems may crowd out genuine interest. The related concept of flow, described by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, captures the state of full absorption in an intrinsically rewarding activity.
