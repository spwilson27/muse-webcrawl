Lucius Annaeus Cornutus (Greek: Κορνοῦτος; c. AD 20 – c. 65) was a Stoic philosopher who flourished during the reign of Nero. He was a native of Leptis Magna in Libya and lived chiefly at Rome, where he taught philosophy and rhetoric. His most famous pupils were the poets Persius and Lucan, and he was connected with the family of Seneca the Younger.

Life

Cornutus was a member of the Annaea gens, the family of Seneca, and may have been a freedman of Seneca the Elder or of the philosopher Seneca. The poet Persius, who studied under him from the age of sixteen, became his close friend and left his library and writings to Cornutus at his death in AD 62. Cornutus edited Persius's satires for publication, toning down passages that might offend the emperor, and also prepared an edition of the works of Lucan.

According to later biographical tradition, Cornutus was banished by Nero around AD 65, reportedly after giving an evasive or discourteous answer when consulted about the emperor's projected epic poem on Roman history. The date and circumstances of his death are uncertain.

Works

Cornutus wrote extensively on rhetoric, grammar, and philosophy, including commentaries and a work On the Nature of the Gods. Most of his writings are lost. His surviving work is the Compendium of Greek Theology (Επιδρομὴ τῶν κατὰ τὴν Ἑλληνικὴν θεολογίαν παραδεδομένων), a handbook for his students that interprets the myths and divine names of Greek religion allegorically, largely within a Stoic framework of physical and ethical allegory.

The treatise is an important source for Stoic methods of allegorical interpretation and for the etymologies of the Greek gods. Fragments of his other works, including rhetorical writings in Latin, survive through quotations in later authors such as Aulus Gellius and Charisius.
