Glycoprotein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Glycoprotein
DefinitionProtein with covalently attached oligosaccharide chains (glycans)
Linkage typesN-linked (to asparagine); O-linked (to serine/threonine)
Site of synthesisEndoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus

Glycoproteins are proteins which contain oligosaccharide chains (glycans) covalently attached to amino acid side-chains. The carbohydrate is attached to the protein in a cotranslational or posttranslational modification known as glycosylation. Secreted extracellular proteins and the extracellular parts of membrane proteins are very often glycosylated.

Types of glycosylation

In N-linked glycosylation, a preassembled oligosaccharide is transferred to the amide nitrogen of an asparagine residue in the endoplasmic reticulum, then trimmed and elaborated in the Golgi. In O-linked glycosylation, sugars are added one at a time to serine or threonine hydroxyls, mainly in the Golgi; mucins are heavily O-glycosylated. A distinct cytosolic form, O-GlcNAc, acts as a dynamic regulatory modification.

Functions

Glycoproteins serve structural roles (collagens, mucins), transport roles (transferrin), hormonal roles (erythropoietin, hCG), enzymatic roles, and cell-recognition roles: the glycocalyx of cell-surface glycoproteins determines ABO blood group antigens and mediates cell–cell adhesion and immune recognition. Viral envelope glycoproteins — influenza haemagglutinin, HIV gp120, and the coronavirus spike protein — are frequent targets of neutralising antibodies and vaccines.

Clinical significance

Defects in glycan assembly cause congenital disorders of glycosylation. Changes in glycosylation patterns accompany cancer and inflammation, and engineered glycoproteins (such as epoetin) are major biopharmaceutical products whose efficacy depends on their glycan structures.
