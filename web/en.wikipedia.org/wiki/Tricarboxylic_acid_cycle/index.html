Tricarboxylic acid cycle

The tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle) is another name for the citric acid cycle, also known as the Krebs cycle. It is the central metabolic pathway in aerobic organisms, in which acetyl-CoA derived from carbohydrates, fats, and proteins is oxidized to carbon dioxide, generating the reducing equivalents NADH and FADH2 and the high-energy compound GTP that together drive ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation.

Outline

The cycle runs in the mitochondrial matrix of eukaryotes. Acetyl-CoA (2 carbons) condenses with oxaloacetate (4 carbons) to form citrate (6 carbons) — a tricarboxylic acid, giving the cycle its name. Through a series of steps involving isocitrate, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, succinate, fumarate, and malate, two carbons are released as CO2 and oxaloacetate is regenerated. Each turn yields roughly 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 GTP.

History and significance

The cycle was elucidated by Hans Adolf Krebs in 1937, earning him the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Beyond energy production, the cycle is a hub of metabolism: its intermediates feed the synthesis of amino acids, nucleotides, and porphyrins, and are replenished by anaplerotic reactions.
