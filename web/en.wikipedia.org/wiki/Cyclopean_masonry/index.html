Cyclopean masonry is a type of stone construction found in Mycenaean architecture, characterised by the use of massive limestone boulders, roughly fitted together with minimal clearance between adjacent stones and with clay mortar or no use of mortar. The term derives from the belief of classical Greeks that only the mythical Cyclopes — a race of giants — could have moved the enormous stones.

Description

Cyclopean walls are best known from the fortifications of Mycenae, Tiryns, and other Late Bronze Age citadels of Greece (c. 1400–1200 BC). The Lion Gate at Mycenae is framed by such masonry. Similar megalithic techniques appear independently in other cultures, including the polygonal walls of Inca Sacsayhuamán in Peru, Nuragic Sardinia, and other sites around the Mediterranean.

Construction

The boulders, some weighing several tons, were minimally worked and wedged together with smaller stones filling the gaps. Walls could be many metres thick, often built as two faces with a rubble core. Their mass made them resistant to both battering and earthquake, and many survive substantially intact after more than three thousand years.
