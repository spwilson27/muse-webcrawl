In anatomy, the atlas (C1) is the most superior (first) cervical vertebra of the spine. It is named for Atlas of Greek mythology, who in later myth bore the weight of the heavens on his shoulders — just as the atlas bears the weight of the skull.

Structure

The atlas differs from other vertebrae in lacking a vertebral body and a spinous process. Instead it is ring-shaped, composed of an anterior arch, a posterior arch, and two lateral masses. The superior articular facets of the lateral masses form cup-shaped surfaces that receive the occipital condyles of the skull, forming the atlanto-occipital joint — the joint that allows the head to nod "yes".

Function

Together with the axis (C2) beneath it, the atlas forms the joint that connects the skull and the spine. The atlanto-axial joint, in which the dens of the axis rotates within the ring of the atlas, permits the head to shake "no".

Clinical significance

Fractures of the atlas, such as the Jefferson fracture — a burst fracture of the anterior and posterior arches typically caused by an axial load on the head — account for a small but significant proportion of cervical spine injuries. Congenital variations and assimilation of the atlas to the occipital bone also occur.
