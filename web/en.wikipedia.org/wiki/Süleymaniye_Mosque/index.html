The Süleymaniye Mosque (Turkish: Süleymaniye Camii) is an Ottoman imperial mosque located on the Third Hill of Istanbul, Turkey. Commissioned by Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent and designed by the imperial architect Mimar Sinan, it was built between 1551 and 1557 and is considered one of the masterpieces of classical Ottoman architecture. At the time of its construction it was the largest mosque in the Ottoman Empire.

History

Suleiman ordered the mosque at the height of his reign, intending it as a monument to both his power and his piety, and as a counterpart to the Byzantine Hagia Sophia. Sinan, then in his sixties, designed a vast complex (külliye) occupying some 70 acres. The mosque was damaged by fire in 1660 and restored under Sultan Mehmed IV; it was partly restored again in the 19th century, and underwent major conservation in the 20th and 21st centuries. It forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of the Historic Areas of Istanbul.

Architecture

The mosque is preceded by a monumental courtyard with a central ablutions fountain. The prayer hall is covered by a dome 53 metres high and 27.5 metres in diameter, carried on four massive piers and flanked by semi-domes in the direction of the qibla and the entrance, in a composition that develops the scheme of Hagia Sophia. Four slender minarets with ten balconies in total mark the corners of the courtyard; tradition links the four minarets to Suleiman being the fourth sultan of Istanbul, and the ten balconies to his being the tenth Ottoman sultan. The interior is noted for its restrained decoration, Iznik tiles around the mihrab, stained glass by the master İbrahim, and exceptional acoustics — Sinan is said to have placed resonating jars within the dome.

The complex

Around the mosque stand the charitable and educational buildings of the külliye: four madrasas, a medical school, a hospital (darüşşifa), a soup kitchen (imaret), a caravanserai, a hammam, shops whose rents supported the foundation, and the tombs of Suleiman and his wife Hürrem Sultan (Roxelana). Sinan's own modest tomb lies at the edge of the complex he designed to house his patron in death.
