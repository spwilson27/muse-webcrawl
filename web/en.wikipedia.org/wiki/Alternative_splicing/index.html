Alternative splicing

Alternative splicing is a process during gene expression that allows a single gene to code for multiple proteins. In this process, particular exons of a gene may be included in, or excluded from, the final processed messenger RNA (mRNA) produced from that gene. Consequently, the proteins translated from alternatively spliced mRNAs differ in their amino acid sequences and often in their biological functions.

Mechanism and types

Splicing is carried out by the spliceosome, which removes introns and joins exons. Alternative splicing patterns include exon skipping (the most common type in mammals), mutually exclusive exons, alternative 5′ or 3′ splice sites, and intron retention. Splicing decisions are regulated by cis-acting sequence elements (enhancers and silencers) and trans-acting splicing factors such as SR proteins and hnRNPs.

Significance

Alternative splicing greatly expands proteomic diversity: the great majority of human multi-exon genes are alternatively spliced, helping explain how roughly 20,000 genes produce a far larger repertoire of proteins. The fruit fly gene Dscam can generate tens of thousands of isoforms. Defects in splicing cause or contribute to many diseases, including spinal muscular atrophy — treated by the antisense drug nusinersen, which modifies splicing of the SMN2 gene — and numerous cancers.
