Tyrant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tyrant is an absolute ruler who seized or exercises power unjustly and cruelly, governing by their own will rather than by law. The Greek word tyrannos originally lacked the full modern stigma — many archaic Greek tyrants (Peisistratos of Athens, Polycrates of Samos) were popular strongmen who broke aristocratic oligarchies — but Plato and Aristotle fixed the pejorative sense: in the Republic and Politics, tyranny is the worst constitution, the perversion of kingship, where the ruler governs for himself through fear.

Classical and Renaissance thought debated resistance to tyrants: the doctrine of tyrannicide (John of Salisbury, the monarchomachs) held that a ruler who violates law and the common good may legitimately be deposed or even killed — a theory applied from the murder of Julius Caesar to the execution of Charles I. “Tyranny” in early modern usage named both a regime type and specific abuses (taxation without consent styled as tyranny by American revolutionaries). Modern usage is rhetorical as much as analytical: “tyrant” labels rulers such as Hitler, Stalin or Saddam Hussein in common speech, while political science prefers the measurable categories of dictatorship and totalitarianism.
