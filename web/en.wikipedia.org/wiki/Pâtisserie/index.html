Pâtisserie

TypePastry-making; bakery specialising in pastries
OriginFrance
Key productsCroissants, éclairs, macarons, mille-feuille, tarts

Pâtisserie (French pronunciation: [pɑtisʁi]) refers both to the art of French pastry-making and to the shop in which such pastries are sold. In France and Belgium, the title pâtisserie is legally controlled: only bakeries that employ a licensed maître pâtissier (master pastry chef) may formally use it. A pâtissier is a professionally trained pastry chef, whose apprenticeship traditionally includes years of practice and examinations.

Products

Classic pâtisserie includes laminated doughs such as the croissant and pain au chocolat; choux pastry items such as éclairs and profiteroles; macarons; fruit tarts; the mille-feuille; opéra cake; and madeleines. Pâtisserie is distinguished from boulangerie (bread baking) and from viennoiserie, the category of enriched, yeasted breakfast pastries, although in practice a single shop may cover all three.

History

French pastry developed from medieval sweetmeats and was elevated in the 19th century by chefs such as Marie-Antoine Carême, regarded as a founder of French grande cuisine, who systematised doughs, creams and elaborate sugar showpieces called pièces montées. In the 20th century chefs such as Gaston Lenôtre modernised the repertoire, emphasising lighter textures and fresh fruit.
