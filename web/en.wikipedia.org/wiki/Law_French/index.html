Law French

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Law French (lawe frensch) is the archaic form of the French language used in the law courts of England and in legal literature from the Norman Conquest in 1066 until the early 18th century. Derived principally from Anglo-Norman, it gradually diverged from the French spoken in France, accumulating English vocabulary, English grammatical constructions, and specialised legal meanings.

History

After 1066, French became the language of the English royal court and of pleading in the higher courts. Although the Statute of Pleading of 1362 directed that pleas be made in English, court records continued to be enrolled in Latin and law reports — the celebrated Year Books — were written in Law French from the late 13th century until the 16th century. Printed law reports in French continued into the reign of Queen Anne; the last were published in the early 1700s.

An act of 1731 finally required court proceedings to be in English, ending the official use of Law French, though by then it had been largely a written fossil for two centuries.

Legacy

Law French left a deep mark on English legal vocabulary. Words and phrases such as attorney, plaintiff, defendant, jury, verdict, tort, estoppel, laches, voir dire, culprit, and the order of word pairs such as "cease and desist" and the fossilised adjective-after-noun order in terms like attorney general, court martial, and fee simple all descend from it. Many Law French forms were progressively corrupted by English-speaking scribes who no longer understood French, producing spellings that survive in modern legal English.
