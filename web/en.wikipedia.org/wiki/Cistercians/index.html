The Cistercians, officially the Order of Cistercians (Latin: Ordo Cisterciensis, abbreviated OCist), are a Catholic religious order of monks and nuns founded in 1098 at Cîteaux Abbey in Burgundy, France. Nicknamed the "White Monks" for their undyed white habits, the Cistercians sought a return to the literal observance of the Rule of St Benedict, rejecting the wealth and elaboration of contemporary Cluniac monasticism in favour of austerity, manual labour and remote sites.

History

The founders — Robert of Molesme, Alberic and the Englishman Stephen Harding, author of the order's constitution, the Carta Caritatis (Charter of Charity) — established a system of mother and daughter houses linked by annual general chapters and visitations. The arrival of Bernard of Clairvaux in 1112 ignited explosive growth: by the mid-12th century there were over 300 Cistercian abbeys across Europe, and by 1300 nearly 700. Lay brothers (conversi) worked granges — model farms — making the order a pioneer of medieval agriculture, sheep-rearing, wool trading, water management and early ironworking; its distinctive, severely plain architecture embodied its spiritual ideals.

Decline and reform

From the later Middle Ages the order declined through war, plague, commendatory abbots and the Reformation, which dissolved its English houses (Fountains, Rievaulx, Tintern) under Henry VIII. In the 17th century Armand-Jean de Rancé's reform at La Trappe inspired the stricter observance that in 1892 became a separate order, the Trappists (Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance, OCSO) — today the larger branch, known for figures such as the writer Thomas Merton and for monastic products including Trappist beer. Both branches survive worldwide.
