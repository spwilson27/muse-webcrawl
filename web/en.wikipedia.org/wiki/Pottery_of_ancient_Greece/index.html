Pottery of ancient Greece

The pottery of ancient Greece, owing to the durability of fired clay, forms a large part of the archaeological record of ancient Greece, and its painted decoration provides much of our knowledge of Greek art and daily life. Vases were produced from the Neolithic period through the Hellenistic age, with the finest painted wares made in Athens and Corinth between about 600 and 300 BC.

Styles

Early wares include the geometric style of the 9th and 8th centuries BC, with bands of meanders and stylised figures, and the orientalising style influenced by Near Eastern motifs. Athens then developed black-figure painting, in which figures were silhouetted in black slip with incised detail, followed around 530 BC by the red-figure technique, which reversed the scheme and allowed finer brush-drawn detail.

Shapes and uses

Greek vase shapes were adapted to functions: the amphora for storage and transport, the krater for mixing wine and water, the kylix and skyphos for drinking, the hydria for fetching water, and the lekythos for oil, often used in funerary ritual. Many painted scenes depict mythology, athletics, symposia, warfare and women's domestic life.

Production and trade

Potters and painters worked in districts such as the Kerameikos of Athens. Some painters signed their work, and modern scholarship, notably by Sir John Beazley, has attributed thousands of unsigned vases to individual hands. Greek pottery was exported throughout the Mediterranean and is found in quantity in Etruscan tombs in Italy.
