Canadian citizenship

Canadian citizenship is the legal status held by nationals of Canada. It was created by the Canadian Citizenship Act of 1947, which for the first time established a citizenship distinct from British subject status. The modern law is the Citizenship Act of 1977 and its amendments.

Acquisition

Citizenship is generally acquired by birth in Canada (with limited exceptions, such as children of foreign diplomats) or by descent from a Canadian parent, subject to a first-generation limit introduced in 2009 for those born abroad. Immigrants may naturalise after becoming permanent residents, residing in Canada for at least three of the previous five years, filing taxes, passing a citizenship test on Canadian history, values and institutions, and demonstrating proficiency in English or French.

Rights and duties

Citizens may vote, run for office, hold a Canadian passport and enter and remain in Canada without restriction. New citizens swear an oath of citizenship that, since 2021, includes a commitment to respect the rights and treaties of Indigenous peoples. Canada permits dual citizenship.

Loss and revocation

Citizenship may be renounced voluntarily, and may be revoked in cases of fraud or misrepresentation in obtaining it. A controversial 2014 provision permitting revocation for certain crimes by dual citizens was repealed in 2017.
