Commutator

In mathematics, the commutator gives an indication of the extent to which a certain binary operation fails to be commutative. There are different definitions used in group theory and ring theory. The term is also used in electrical engineering for the rotating switch in a DC motor that reverses the current direction in the rotor windings each half turn, allowing the motor to keep turning in one direction; this article concerns the mathematical concept.

Group theory

The commutator of two elements g and h of a group G is the element [g, h] = g−1h−1gh. It is equal to the identity element if and only if g and h commute, that is, if gh = hg. The subgroup generated by all commutators of a group is called its derived subgroup or commutator subgroup. A group is abelian precisely when its commutator subgroup is trivial.

Ring and algebra theory

In a ring or associative algebra, the commutator of two elements a and b is defined by [a, b] = ab − ba. It is zero if and only if a and b commute. This operation turns any associative algebra into a Lie algebra, satisfying antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity. The commutator plays a central role in quantum mechanics, where the observables' commutators determine the uncertainty relations — for instance, position and momentum satisfy [x, p] = iħ.

Identities

Commutators satisfy a number of important identities, including the product rules [A, BC] = [A, B]C + B[A, C] (in ring theory) and the Jacobi identity [A, [B, C]] + [B, [C, A]] + [C, [A, B]] = 0. Graded commutators and anticommutators extend the notion to graded algebras and fermionic operators in physics.
