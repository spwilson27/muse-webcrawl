Glia

Glia
LocationCentral and peripheral nervous systems
FunctionSupport, insulation, immune defence, homeostasis
Types (CNS)Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, ependymal cells
Types (PNS)Schwann cells, satellite cells

Glia, also called glial cells or neuroglia, are non-neuronal cells of the nervous system that maintain homeostasis, form myelin, and provide support and protection for neurons. The name derives from the Greek word for "glue", reflecting the early belief that they merely held neurons together; modern research shows they play active roles in signalling, immunity and synaptic function.

Types

In the central nervous system, astrocytes regulate the blood–brain barrier, recycle neurotransmitters and buffer ions; oligodendrocytes produce the myelin sheaths that insulate axons; microglia act as the brain's resident immune cells, clearing debris and pathogens; and ependymal cells line the ventricles and produce cerebrospinal fluid. In the peripheral nervous system, Schwann cells form myelin and satellite cells surround neuronal cell bodies in ganglia.

Numbers and roles

The long-repeated claim that glia outnumber neurons ten to one is a myth; in the human brain the ratio is close to 1:1, with roughly 86 billion glia and 86 billion neurons. Glia are implicated in most brain diseases, from gliomas (tumours of glial origin) to the demyelination of multiple sclerosis and neuroinflammation in Alzheimer's disease.
