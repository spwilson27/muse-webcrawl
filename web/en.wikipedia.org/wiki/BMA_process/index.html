BMA process

The BMA process produces hydrogen cyanide in externally heated platinum-coated reactor tubes.

The BMA process (from the German Blausäure-Methan-Ammoniak, "hydrocyanic acid–methane–ammonia") is an industrial chemical process for the production of hydrogen cyanide (HCN). Developed in Germany by Degussa in the 1950s, it is one of the three main commercial routes to hydrogen cyanide, alongside the Andrussow process and the Shawinigan process.

Chemistry

In the BMA process, a mixture of methane and ammonia is reacted, without added oxygen, over a platinum-based catalyst at temperatures around 1,200–1,300 °C:

CH4 + NH3 → HCN + 3 H2

The reaction is strongly endothermic, so heat must be supplied continuously. Industrially it is carried out inside tubes of sintered alumina coated on the inside with platinum, suspended in a furnace heated by burning gas. Unlike the Andrussow oxidation, which burns part of the feed with air, the BMA reaction takes place in the absence of oxygen, and the hydrogen formed is a valuable co-product. Conversion is high and the process gives a concentrated hydrogen cyanide stream, though its energy consumption and the cost of the externally heated reactor tubes are higher than for the Andrussow route, so BMA plants are generally smaller and have been operated mainly in Europe.

Uses of the product

Hydrogen cyanide is a platform chemical used to make methyl methacrylate (for acrylic plastics), adiponitrile (for nylon), sodium cyanide (for gold mining) and various pesticides and chelating agents.
