Limnology (from Greek λίμνη, limne, "lake" and λόγος, logos, "knowledge") is the study of inland aquatic ecosystems. The study of limnology includes aspects of the biological, chemical, physical and geological characteristics and functions of inland waters — running (rivers and streams) and standing (lakes, reservoirs and ponds) — including both fresh and saline waters.

Limnologists study how organisms interact with their environment and each other, the cycling of nutrients such as phosphorus and nitrogen, thermal stratification and mixing of lakes, food webs, and the effects of human activity, including eutrophication, acidification and climate change.

History

The discipline began with François-Alphonse Forel's monographs on Lake Geneva (1892–1904), which coined the term limnology. August Thienemann and Einar Naumann developed lake typology (oligotrophic, eutrophic, dystrophic) in the 1920s; G. Evelyn Hutchinson's Treatise on Limnology (from 1957) made the field a rigorous quantitative science. Canada's Experimental Lakes Area, where whole lakes were deliberately fertilised, proved phosphorus control was the key to fighting algal blooms.

Lake physics and chemistry

Because water is densest at 4 °C, temperate lakes stratify in summer into a warm epilimnion, a sharp thermocline (metalimnion) and a cold hypolimnion, mixing in spring and autumn (dimictic lakes). Oxygen, pH, dissolved carbon and nutrient concentrations vary with depth and season, structuring the habitat.

Lake biology

Phytoplankton, zooplankton, benthic invertebrates, macrophytes and fish form the food web; energy flows from littoral (shore) and pelagic (open-water) primary production. Limnology contributed the concept of the trophic cascade, demonstrated when predator manipulations changed algal abundance.

Applied limnology

Limnological knowledge underpins drinking-water management, wastewater treatment, fisheries, flood control and the restoration of degraded lakes, and is essential for assessing how inland waters — which emit significant methane and CO2 — figure in the global carbon cycle.
