Optical rotation

Optical rotation, also known as polarization rotation or circular birefringence, is the rotation of the orientation of the plane of polarization of a beam of linearly polarized light as it travels through certain materials. It occurs in chiral molecules — those that lack mirror symmetry — and in some crystals such as quartz. A substance that rotates polarization to the left (counterclockwise, viewed facing the beam) is called levorotatory (denoted (−) or l), and one that rotates it to the right is dextrorotatory ((+) or d).

History

The phenomenon was studied by François Arago and Jean-Baptiste Biot in the early 19th century. In 1848 Louis Pasteur showed that crystals of sodium ammonium tartrate occurred in two mirror-image forms that rotated light in opposite directions, and he traced the asymmetry to the molecules themselves — the founding experiment of stereochemistry.

Measurement and specific rotation

Optical rotation is measured with a polarimeter. The observed rotation α depends on the path length, concentration, temperature and the wavelength of light (often the sodium D line at 589 nm). The specific rotation [α] normalizes these variables: for a solution, [α] = α / (l·c), where l is the path length in decimetres and c is the concentration in g/mL. Enantiomers have equal and opposite specific rotations, and a 50:50 mixture (a racemate) shows no rotation.

Explanation

Linearly polarized light can be decomposed into equal left- and right-circularly polarized components. In a chiral medium the two components propagate at different speeds (different refractive indices), so their recombination produces linear polarization rotated by an angle proportional to the difference and to the path length. The rotation generally varies with wavelength — a phenomenon called optical rotatory dispersion — and near an absorption band the related circular dichroism appears (the Cotton effect).

Applications

Polarimetry is used in the sugar industry (saccharimetry) to assay sucrose, in pharmaceuticals to verify the enantiomeric purity of chiral drugs, and in chemistry to characterize newly synthesized chiral compounds.
