Roman Republic (1849)

The Roman Republic (Italian: Repubblica Romana) was a short-lived revolutionary state proclaimed in Rome on 9 February 1849, during the revolutions of 1848 in the Italian states. It replaced the temporal rule of the Papal States after Pope Pius IX fled to Gaeta, and was crushed by French troops five months later.

Background

The assassination of the papal minister Pellegrino Rossi in November 1848 and the pope's flight forced a Constituent Assembly, which voted to end the temporal power of the papacy and proclaim a republic. Giuseppe Mazzini, the theorist of Italian nationalism, arrived in March and became the republic's leading figure as head of a ruling triumvirate.

Government and reforms

The republic abolished the death penalty, proclaimed freedom of religion and the press, confiscated church property to fund social housing, and drafted one of the most democratic constitutions of its era, granting universal male suffrage. Its motto was Dio e Popolo ("God and the People").

Defence and fall

France, seeking to restore the pope and counter Austrian influence, sent an expedition under General Oudinot. The republic's defence was organized by Giuseppe Garibaldi, whose volunteers repelled the first French attack on 30 April 1849. After reinforcements arrived, French forces besieged the city; the Janiculum Hill saw fierce fighting in which figures such as Goffredo Mameli were killed. Rome fell on 3 July 1849. Garibaldi led a retreat northward, during which his wife Anita died.

Legacy

Though it lasted less than five months, the Roman Republic became a founding symbol of the Italian Risorgimento. Rome was finally taken from the papacy in 1870 and became the capital of the Kingdom of Italy.

Not to be confused with the ancient Roman Republic.
