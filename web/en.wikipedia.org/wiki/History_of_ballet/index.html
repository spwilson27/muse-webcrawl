History of ballet

The history of ballet spans five centuries, from the court spectacles of Renaissance Italy and France to the Romantic ballet, the Imperial Russian classics, and the neoclassical and contemporary ballet of the 20th and 21st centuries.

Origins in the courts

Ballet began in the Italian Renaissance courts, where dancing masters staged allegorical entertainments. Catherine de' Medici brought the form to France; the Ballet Comique de la Reine (1581) is often considered the first ballet. Louis XIV, himself a dancer, founded the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661 and the Paris Opera Ballet in 1669; his dancing master Pierre Beauchamp is credited with codifying the five positions of the feet.

The 18th century

Jean-Georges Noverre's Lettres sur la danse (1760) championed the ballet d'action — dance as drama rather than ornament. Professional female dancers rose to prominence at the Paris Opera, and technique advanced as heels and restrictive costumes gave way to lighter dress.

Romantic ballet

The Romantic era (c. 1830–1850) glorified the ethereal ballerina: Marie Taglioni in La Sylphide (1832) danced en pointe in a bell-shaped white tutu, and Giselle (1841) became the era's masterpiece. Centres shifted from Paris toward Denmark (August Bournonville) and Russia.

Imperial Russia and the classics

In St Petersburg, Marius Petipa choreographed the great full-evening classics with Tchaikovsky — The Sleeping Beauty (1890), The Nutcracker (1892) and Swan Lake (1895 revision). Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes (1909–1929) then revolutionised ballet in the West with Fokine, Nijinsky, Stravinsky and The Rite of Spring (1913).

20th century to today

George Balanchine co-founded the New York City Ballet and defined neoclassicism; Britain developed companies under Ninette de Valois and Frederick Ashton; the Soviet Union maintained the Bolshoi and Kirov traditions. Contemporary ballet now blends classical technique with modern dance, and choreographers worldwide continue to expand the form.
