Ivor Armstrong Richards (1893–1979).

Ivor Armstrong Richards (26 February 1893 – 7 September 1979) was an English literary critic and rhetorician. His books Principles of Literary Criticism (1924), Practical Criticism (1929), and The Philosophy of Rhetoric (1936) were foundational for the New Criticism, the dominant mode of Anglo-American literary study in the mid-twentieth century. He was one of the founders of the contemporary study of literature in English at Cambridge and a pioneer of close reading as a classroom method.

Life and career

Richards was educated at Clifton College and Magdalene College, Cambridge, where he read moral sciences. After the First World War he began lecturing in English, then a young subject at Cambridge. With C. K. Ogden he wrote The Meaning of Meaning (1923), a pioneering work in semantics that introduced the semiotic triangle of symbol, thought, and referent. In the 1930s he worked with Ogden on Basic English, a controlled 850-word auxiliary international language. After visiting China in the late 1920s, recorded in Mencius on the Mind (1932), he moved to Harvard University in 1939, where he spent the rest of his career working on education, language learning, and the design of reading materials. He was also a published poet and a keen mountaineer.

Practical criticism

In Practical Criticism Richards reported an experiment in which he gave his Cambridge students poems stripped of authors' names and dates and collected their written interpretations. The often wildly divergent and inept "protocols" led him to argue for a disciplined method of reading attentive to the words on the page — tone, sense, feeling, and intention — rather than to reputation or biography. The experiment transformed the teaching of literature and remains the ancestor of the anonymous-poem exercises still used in schools and examinations.

Key ideas

Richards distinguished the referential (scientific) and emotive uses of language, attempted a psychological theory of value grounded in the organisation of impulses, and analysed metaphor as an interaction of "tenor" and "vehicle" — a framework still standard in rhetoric. His emphasis on ambiguity, irony, and the contextual determination of meaning influenced critics including William Empson, his student, as well as F. R. Leavis and the American New Critics.
