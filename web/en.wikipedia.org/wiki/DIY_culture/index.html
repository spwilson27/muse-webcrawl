DIY culture is a broad social and cultural movement centred on the ethic of do it yourself: making, repairing and distributing things without relying on professionals, corporations or established institutions. It spans home improvement and crafts, independent music and publishing, open-source technology and the maker movement.

Origins

Self-reliant making is ancient, but the modern DIY ethos took shape in the post-war home-improvement boom and the counterculture of the 1960s and 1970s, epitomised by Stewart Brand's Whole Earth Catalog (from 1968), which spread tools and practical knowledge outside commercial channels.

Punk and independent media

DIY became a defining ethic of punk rock from the mid-1970s: bands self-recorded, self-released records on independent labels, booked their own tours and promoted shows with photocopied flyers. The same impulse drove the zine scene — cheap, self-published magazines — and later indie music, riot grrrl and hardcore networks that built durable alternatives to the mainstream culture industry.

Digital era and maker movement

The internet amplified DIY culture through how-to videos, forums, open-source software and hardware, 3D printing and crowdfunding. The maker movement, hackerspaces and repair cafés promote hands-on creation and repair, partly as a response to disposable consumer goods and partly as a creative practice in its own right.

Criticism

Commentators note that DIY can also be commercialised — sold back as lifestyle branding — and that self-reliance presumes time, tools and skills not equally available to everyone. Nonetheless, it remains a persistent strand in debates about consumption, sustainability and cultural participation.
