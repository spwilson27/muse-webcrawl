First Crusade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Medieval illumination of the siege and capture of Jerusalem in 1099]

The First Crusade (1096–1099) was the first of a series of religious wars initiated by the Latin Church to recover the Holy Land from Muslim rule. Proclaimed by Pope Urban II at the Council of Clermont in November 1095, in response to a Byzantine appeal for help against the Seljuk Turks, it drew tens of thousands of western knights, clergy and commoners across Europe and Anatolia. Against expectation it succeeded: after victories at Nicaea, Dorylaeum and Antioch, the crusaders stormed Jerusalem on 15 July 1099, massacring much of its population, and founded the Crusader states of Edessa, Antioch, Jerusalem and Tripoli.

The road to Jerusalem

A disorderly 'People's Crusade' under Peter the Hermit was annihilated in Anatolia in 1096, after its German contingents had perpetrated the Rhineland massacres of Jewish communities. The main armies, led by Godfrey of Bouillon, Bohemond of Taranto, Raymond of Toulouse, Robert of Normandy and others, took Nicaea (1097), survived the brutal siege of Antioch (1097–98) — where a timely 'discovery' of the Holy Lance revived morale — and pressed on to Jerusalem, held by the Fatimids.

Consequences

The Kingdom of Jerusalem and its sister states endured, with difficulty, for nearly two centuries, sustained by later crusades, Italian sea power and military orders such as the Templars and Hospitallers. The crusade transformed relations between Latin Christians, Byzantines (whose capital the Fourth Crusade would sack in 1204), Jews and Muslims, and left a long legacy in European and Middle Eastern memory.
