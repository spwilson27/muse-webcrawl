The Messerschmitt Me 262, nicknamed Schwalbe ("Swallow") in fighter versions and Sturmvogel ("Storm Bird") in fighter-bomber versions, was the world's first operational jet-powered fighter aircraft. Designed by a team led by Wilhelm Messerschmitt, it was produced in Germany during the closing stages of World War II and entered service with the Luftwaffe in 1944.

Development and service

Development began before the war, but chronic problems with the Junkers Jumo 004 turbojet engines — and a shortage of strategic metals that limited engine life to as little as 10–25 hours — delayed operational use until mid-1944. The Me 262 was roughly 170 km/h faster than the best Allied piston-engine fighters, and its four 30 mm cannon were devastating against bomber formations; Allied pilots learned to attack the jets at their most vulnerable moments, during take-off and landing. However, the type arrived too late and in too small numbers to alter the air war, and disputes over whether it should be used as a bomber further delayed deployment. Czechoslovakia built a small post-war derivative, the Avia S-92.

Legacy

The Me 262's swept wing and podded underwing engines influenced a generation of post-war jet fighters on both sides of the Cold War. Surviving aircraft are displayed in museums in Germany, the United States, Britain, Australia and South Africa, and modern reproductions powered by General Electric J85 engines have flown since the 2000s.
