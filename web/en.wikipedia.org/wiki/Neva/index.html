The Neva (Russian: Нева) is a river in northwestern Russia flowing from Lake Ladoga through the Leningrad Oblast to the Neva Bay of the Gulf of Finland. Despite its modest length of 74 km, it is the third largest river in Europe by discharge, because it drains the vast basin of Lake Ladoga, itself fed by Lakes Onega and Ilmen and thousands of smaller lakes. The city of Saint Petersburg, founded by Peter the Great in 1703, stands in its delta, and the river is central to the city's identity and history.

Geography

The Neva begins at Shlisselburg, where it leaves Lake Ladoga, and flows southwest to the Gulf of Finland. Near its mouth it divides into a delta of several arms, the largest being the Great Neva and Little Neva, among the islands on which central Saint Petersburg is built. The river is wide and deep — up to 1,200 metres across and 24 metres deep — and is navigable throughout, forming the final link of the Volga–Baltic Waterway and the White Sea–Baltic Canal.

History

In the Middle Ages the river was a vital segment of the trade route from the Varangians to the Greeks and of Novgorod's commerce with the Baltic. In 1240 Alexander Nevsky won his name-defining victory over Swedish forces at the Battle of the Neva. Control of the river mouth was contested between Sweden and Russia until Peter the Great seized it in the Great Northern War and founded Saint Petersburg. The Neva is notorious for autumn floods, when westerly winds drive Baltic water up the river; the catastrophic flood of 1824, described by Pushkin in The Bronze Horseman, killed hundreds. A flood barrier across the Gulf, completed in 2011, now protects the city. During the Siege of Leningrad (1941–44) the frozen river formed part of the supply corridor across Lake Ladoga known as the Road of Life.
