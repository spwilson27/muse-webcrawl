Bartender


A bartender at work

A bartender (also known as a barkeep or barman) is a person who formulates and serves alcoholic — and often non-alcoholic — beverages from behind the bar in a licensed establishment. Beyond mixing drinks, bartenders typically take orders, handle payment, maintain stock and glassware, check patrons' identification where required, and are expected to stop service to intoxicated customers.

History

The profession developed with the rise of taverns and public houses. In the 19th-century United States, bartending became a respected craft: Jerry Thomas, author of the first bartender's guide, How to Mix Drinks (1862), became famous for elaborate showmanship. Harry Craddock of the Savoy Hotel in London codified hundreds of recipes in The Savoy Cocktail Book (1930). Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933) depressed the trade domestically while sending many American bartenders abroad. A craft-cocktail revival since the 2000s has restored pre-Prohibition techniques and raised the profile of the occupation.

Skills and styles

Working bartenders concentrate on speed, accuracy and service; "mixologists" emphasise recipe creation. Flair bartending adds performance — juggling and flipping bottles and tools — popularised by the 1988 film Cocktail. In many jurisdictions bartenders must complete responsible-service training, and in the United States their income derives largely from tips.
