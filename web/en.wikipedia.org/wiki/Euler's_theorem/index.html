Euler's theorem

Euler's theorem generalises Fermat's little theorem to composite moduli.

In number theory, Euler's theorem (also known as the Fermat–Euler theorem) states that if n and a are coprime positive integers, then a raised to the power of Euler's totient function of n is congruent to 1 modulo n:

aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n),   whenever gcd(a, n) = 1.

The theorem was published by Leonhard Euler in 1763, generalising Fermat's little theorem, which is the special case where n is prime and φ(n) = n − 1.

Proof outline

The standard proof observes that the residues coprime to n form a multiplicative group of order φ(n). Multiplying all these residues by a merely permutes them, so the product of the residues equals the product of the residues multiplied by aφ(n); cancelling the (invertible) product yields the result. In group-theoretic terms the theorem is an immediate corollary of Lagrange's theorem.

Applications

Euler's theorem underlies the RSA cryptosystem, whose decryption works because messages raised to the totient power return to themselves modulo the product of two primes. It is also used to compute modular inverses and to reduce enormous exponents in modular arithmetic; Carmichael's theorem provides a sharper exponent using the least common multiple of totients of prime-power factors.

Generalisations

The theorem extends to the statement that ak+φ(n) ≡ ak (mod n) for sufficiently large k even when a and n are not coprime, and has analogues in abstract algebra for finite groups and in the theory of modular forms.
