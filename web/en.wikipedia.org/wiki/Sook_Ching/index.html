Sook Ching

Sook Ching (Chinese: 肅清; pinyin: Sùqīng, meaning "purge through cleansing") was a mass killing that occurred from 18 February to 4 March 1942 in Singapore after its fall to the Empire of Japan at the end of the Malayan campaign of the Second World War. It was a systematic purge and massacre of perceived hostile elements among the Chinese population of Singapore by the Japanese military during the Japanese occupation of Singapore.

Screening of Chinese men during the Sook Ching, February 1942.

The Japanese authorities believed that elements of the ethnic Chinese community had supported the resistance in China and the Allied war effort, and sought to eliminate those considered anti-Japanese, including former volunteers of Dalforce and other local defence units, communists, criminals, and men who simply fell under suspicion at the screening points. Estimates of the death toll vary widely: Japanese sources have cited figures in the thousands, while Singaporean Chinese sources and post-war accounts put the number of victims between 25,000 and 50,000.

Background

Singapore surrendered on 15 February 1942 after the Battle of Singapore. The island's Chinese community had, before the war, raised funds for China's resistance to Japanese invasion following the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, and many local Chinese had volunteered for service against the Japanese in units such as Dalforce. General Tomoyuki Yamashita's Twenty-Fifth Army, and in particular the Kempeitai (military police), moved quickly to register and screen the Chinese population.

The screening and massacres

On 18 February 1942, all Chinese males aged between 18 and 50 were ordered to report to screening centres across the island. The screening was conducted arbitrarily: hooded informants pointed out supposed anti-Japanese elements, and criteria reportedly included such arbitrary markers as having spectacles, soft hands suggesting an intellectual, or tattoos suggesting membership in a triad. Those selected were loaded onto trucks, driven to remote beaches and killing sites — including Changi Beach, Punggol Point, and Siglap — and executed, their bodies disposed of in the sea or in mass graves. Women were not generally screened, though some were killed along with their families.

The operation was planned by the Kempeitai under officers including Masanobu Tsuji, and was later extended to Chinese communities elsewhere in Malaya, notably in Penang and Johor.

Aftermath

After the war, several Japanese officers were tried by a British military court in Singapore in 1947 for their roles in the massacre; two were executed and others imprisoned. In the 1960s, mass graves of victims were discovered at Siglap — in an area that came to be known as the "Valley of Tears" — prompting public demands for compensation. In 1963 the Japanese government paid compensation to the Chinese community of Singapore through a settlement connected with broader reparations arrangements, and funds were used in part for memorials and bursaries.

The victims are commemorated by the Civilian War Memorial in Singapore, unveiled in 1967, whose four pillars represent the Chinese, Malay, Indian, and Eurasian communities, and by memorials at the former killing sites, including Changi Beach and Punggol Point. The Sook Ching remains one of the most traumatic episodes of the Japanese occupation in Singapore's national memory and is taught as part of the country's history curriculum.
