Magnetic stripe card

A magnetic stripe card is a type of card capable of storing data by modifying the magnetism of tiny iron-based magnetic particles on a band of magnetic material on the card. The magnetic stripe, sometimes called a swipe card or magstripe, is read by swiping it past a magnetic reading head.

Standards

The physical characteristics and data format of magnetic stripes on payment and ID cards are defined by the ISO/IEC 7810, 7811, and 7813 standards. A standard stripe has three tracks. Track 1, developed by the airline industry (IATA), holds up to 79 alphanumeric characters including the cardholder's name. Track 2, developed by the banking industry (ABA), holds 40 numeric characters including the primary account number. Track 3, designed for financial data interchange, is less commonly used. Data is written using flux transitions in the F2F (Aiken biphase) encoding.

History

Magnetic stripes derive from magnetic wire and tape recording, adapted to cards in the 1960s; IBM engineer Forrest Parry is credited with attaching a strip of magnetic tape to a plastic card after his wife suggested ironing it on. American Airlines and Citibank pioneered stripe-equipped cards in the late 1960s, and the technology became universal on credit cards, debit cards, ATM cards, hotel key cards, transit passes, and gift cards.

Security and decline

Stripe data is static and trivially cloned with inexpensive skimming equipment, making the technology vulnerable to fraud. Since the 2010s, payment systems in most of the world have migrated to EMV chip cards and contactless payments, and some issuers have announced plans to phase out the stripe entirely, though it remains a common fallback.
