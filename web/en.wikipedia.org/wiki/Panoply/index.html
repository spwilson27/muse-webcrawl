Panoply

A panoply is a complete suit of armour. The word originates in the Ancient Greek panoplía (πανοπλία), from pan ("all") and hópla ("arms"), and originally denoted the full equipment of a hoplite of the classical Greek world: helmet, cuirass, greaves, shield (aspis or hoplon), spear and sword.

The hoplite panoply

Classical hoplites supplied their own equipment, so the panoply was both a military necessity and a marker of citizen status. Its principal elements included:

  • Helmet — usually of bronze, in styles such as the Corinthian, Chalcidian, or later Thracian types.
  • Cuirass — of bronze (bell or muscled cuirasses) or of glued linen layers (linothorax), protecting the torso.
  • Greaves (knemides) — shaped bronze plates protecting the shins.
  • Shield — the large, round, bronze-faced wooden aspis, about 80–100 cm across, carried on the left arm with a double grip.
  • Spear (dory) — the main weapon, roughly 2–3 metres long, with a butt-spike.
  • Sword (xiphos or kopis) — a short sidearm for close combat.

The cost of a full panoply — perhaps the equivalent of a year's wages for a skilled worker — restricted hoplite service to citizens of some property, and captured panoplies were prized trophies, dedicated in temples or set up as monuments.

Extended meanings

In later usage the word broadened beyond armour. From the early modern period, a "panoply" could denote a ceremonial display of arms and armour — for example the arrangements of weapons hung in halls and armouries — and by extension any splendid or complete array of things: a "panoply of stars", or the "whole panoply" of a legal or bureaucratic apparatus.
