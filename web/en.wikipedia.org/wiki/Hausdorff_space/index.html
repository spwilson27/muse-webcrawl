Hausdorff space

In topology and related branches of mathematics, a Hausdorff space (also called a separated space or T2 space) is a topological space where, given any two distinct points, there exist disjoint neighbourhoods of each. Intuitively, points can be "separated" from each other by open sets. The condition is named after Felix Hausdorff, who included it among the axioms in his foundational 1914 work Grundzüge der Mengenlehre. (For the measure concept also bearing his name, see Hausdorff measure.)

Definition

A topological space X is Hausdorff if for every pair of distinct points x, y ∈ X there exist open sets U and V with x ∈ U, y ∈ V, and U ∩ V = ∅.

The Hausdorff condition is the second of the classical separation axioms. It is stronger than the T1 condition (points are closed) and weaker than being a regular or normal space.

Examples

Most spaces encountered in analysis are Hausdorff: every metric space is Hausdorff (take balls of radius half the distance between the points), and hence so are ℝn, all manifolds, and all normed vector spaces in their standard topologies. Non-Hausdorff spaces arise naturally too: the Zariski topology on an algebraic variety is only T1; the particular point and indiscrete topologies are not Hausdorff; and quotients of Hausdorff spaces can fail to be Hausdorff, as in the "line with two origins."

Properties

In a Hausdorff space, limits of sequences (and, more generally, nets and filters) are unique when they exist — the analytic payoff of the axiom. Compact subsets are closed, continuous images of compact sets behave well, and the diagonal is closed in X × X, an equivalent characterisation. Subspaces of Hausdorff spaces are Hausdorff, and products of Hausdorff spaces are Hausdorff. Algebraic geometry's failure to satisfy the axiom motivates the alternative notion of separated morphisms for schemes.
