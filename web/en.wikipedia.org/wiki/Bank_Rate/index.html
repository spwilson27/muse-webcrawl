Bank Rate is the official interest rate set by the Bank of England: the rate at which the central bank lends to commercial banks and, in the current system of reserve remuneration, the rate it pays on the reserves that commercial banks hold with it. It is the principal instrument of UK monetary policy and is decided by the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee in pursuit of the government's 2 percent inflation target.

History

A rate known as Bank Rate has existed since the Bank of England's early history and for much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries was the publicly announced signal of policy, changed by the Bank's Court of Directors, at times under Treasury direction. It reached a famous peak of 17 percent in 1979–1981 under the "minimum lending rate" arrangements, and figured prominently in episodes such as the 1967 devaluation and the 1992 ERM crisis, when it was raised to 12 percent and a rise to 15 percent was announced (but never took effect) on Black Wednesday. From 1997, decisions passed to the Monetary Policy Committee.

Recent history

During the financial crisis Bank Rate was cut in steps to 0.5 percent in March 2009, then to 0.25 percent in August 2016 after the EU referendum, and finally to a historic low of 0.1 percent in March 2020 as the COVID-19 pandemic struck. As inflation surged in 2021–22, the Committee raised Bank Rate in fourteen consecutive decisions to a peak of 5.25 percent in August 2023, the highest since 2008. With inflation falling back, the Committee began a gradual easing cycle in August 2024, reducing the rate in a series of steps through 2025.

Transmission

Bank Rate influences the rates banks charge households and businesses for mortgages, loans and credit, and the rates they offer savers; market interest rates such as SONIA track it closely. Changes work through the economy with long lags — conventionally up to about two years — affecting spending, asset prices, the exchange rate of sterling, and ultimately inflation.
