Field-programmable gate array

A field-programmable gate array (FPGA) is a type of integrated circuit that can be programmed or reprogrammed after manufacturing by a customer or designer using a hardware description language (HDL). The circuit is implemented in an array of configurable logic blocks (CLBs) connected by programmable interconnects, allowing the device to realise arbitrary digital logic functions.

Architecture

A modern FPGA contains logic blocks built around look-up tables (LUTs), flip-flops and multiplexers; dedicated blocks for block RAM, digital signal processing (multipliers/DSP slices), clock management (PLLs), high-speed serial transceivers and sometimes hard processor cores. SRAM-based FPGAs load their configuration (bitstream) at power-up from external memory; flash- and antifuse-based devices retain configuration non-volatilely.

History

Xilinx, founded in 1984 by Ross Freeman and others, introduced the first commercially successful FPGA, the XC2064, in 1985, with 64 logic blocks. Altera (now part of Intel) became the other major vendor. The FPGA market grew from prototyping and "glue logic" into communications, automotive, aerospace and data-centre acceleration.

Applications and design flow

Designers describe circuits in Verilog or VHDL; synthesis, placement and routing tools convert the description into a bitstream. FPGAs are used for ASIC prototyping, telecommunications, video processing, high-frequency trading, and hardware acceleration of workloads such as machine-learning inference and network functions. Compared with ASICs, FPGAs offer flexibility at the cost of higher unit price, larger area and greater power consumption; compared with software on CPUs or GPUs, they offer fine-grained parallelism and deterministic latency.
