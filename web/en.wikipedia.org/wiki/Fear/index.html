Fear

Fear is an unpleasant emotion induced by the perception of danger or threat, which causes physiological changes and behavioural responses such as fleeing, hiding or freezing. It is one of the basic emotions recognised across cultures and species, and serves an adaptive function by motivating organisms to confront or escape threats (the fight-or-flight response).

Physiology

Fear processing centres on the amygdala, which detects threats and activates the sympathetic nervous system and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. The resulting cascade releases adrenaline and cortisol, increasing heart rate, blood pressure and vigilance. The American psychologist Walter Cannon described this mobilisation as the fight-or-flight response; later research added freezing and other defensive reactions.

Fear, anxiety and phobias

Fear is distinguished from anxiety, which is oriented toward anticipated or imagined future threats rather than present danger. When fear is excessive, persistent and attached to specific objects or situations, it may constitute a phobia; generalised dysfunction is treated through therapies including exposure therapy and cognitive behavioural therapy. Fear learning and extinction are studied through classical conditioning paradigms such as fear conditioning in rodents.

In society and culture

Fear has been used as a tool of persuasion and control in politics, advertising and religion, and is the stock-in-trade of horror fiction and film. Franklin D. Roosevelt's 1933 inaugural line — "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself" — remains a famous commentary on collective fear during the Great Depression.
