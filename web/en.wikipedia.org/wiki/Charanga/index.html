Charanga

Charanga is a traditional Cuban dance-music ensemble, also called a charanga francesa ("French charanga"), whose instrumentation combines European classical instruments — a French wooden flute and a string section of violins — with African-derived percussion: güiro, cata (a struck wooden idiophone), and later timbales and congas. Charangas are the classic orchestras of the danzón, Cuba's national dance, and of the chachachá and the mambo's danzón-based offshoots.

Instrumentation

A traditional charanga consists of a five-key (later Boehm-system) flute playing virtuosic improvised flourishes, violins playing rhythmic, syncopated figures called guajeos, piano, bass, and percussion. The absence of brass distinguishes the charanga's lighter, more refined sound from that of the Cuban conjunto or big band.

History

Charangas emerged in the early 1900s from the orchestras that played French contredanse-derived music in Cuba; early groups included Orquesta de Antonio Torroella and the orchestra of the Tata Alfonso family. In the 1930s and 1940s, Orquesta Aragón — founded in 1939 in Cienfuegos — became the definitive charanga, setting the standard with flautist Richard Egüés. Charanga musicians drove two major dance crazes: the danzón de nuevo ritmo of Arcaño y sus Maravillas, which led to the mambo, and Enrique Jorrín's chachachá in the 1950s. Charangas later flourished in New York salsa (Charanga Habanera in Cuba, and the bands of José Fajardo and Johnny Pacheco) and in West Africa, where charanga recordings were hugely influential.
