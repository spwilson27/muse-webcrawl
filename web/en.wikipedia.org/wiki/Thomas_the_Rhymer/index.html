Thomas the Rhymer

Thomas of Erceldoune, better known as Thomas the Rhymer (fl. c. 1220–1298), also called Thomas Learmont, was a Scottish laird and reputed prophet from Erceldoune (now Earlston) in the Scottish Borders. He is the hero of a medieval romance and a traditional ballad (Child ballad 37) that recount his encounter with the Queen of Elfland.

Historical Thomas

Documentary evidence records a Thomas of Erceldoune in the thirteenth century, son of Thomas de Rymor. Later Scots chroniclers attributed prophecies to him, and collections of "Thomas's prophecies" circulated for centuries, some applied retrospectively to events such as the death of Alexander III of Scotland (1286) and the Battle of Bannockburn.

The ballad and romance

In the ballad, Thomas, lying by the Eildon Hills, is carried off by the Queen of Elfland to her realm, where he serves her (for seven years in most versions) and is shown the roads to Heaven, Hell, and Elfland. Forbidden to speak while there, he returns with the gift of prophecy and "a tongue that cannot lie," in some versions after eating the fruit of a tree in the fairy queen's garden — a motif linking him to wider Celtic and European otherworld traditions. A medieval romance version, Thomas of Erceldoune, survives in several manuscripts. Sir Walter Scott included the ballad in his Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border and wrote a continuation, spreading Thomas's fame in the Romantic era.

Legacy

Thomas the Rhymer is a fixture of Borders folklore; the Rhymer's Tower at Earlston and sites under the Eildon Hills are associated with his story. He appears in literature and song up to the present day, and in Rudyard Kipling's poem "The Last Rhyme of True Thomas."
