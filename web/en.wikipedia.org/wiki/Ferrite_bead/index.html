Ferrite bead

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ferrite bead — also called a ferrite choke, ferrite clamp, or simply a choke — is a passive electronic component that suppresses high-frequency noise in electronic circuits. It consists of a ferrite ceramic core (a cylinder, bead, or clamp-on block) through which a conductor passes. The ferrite's impedance rises sharply with frequency, so the bead blocks or absorbs radio-frequency interference (RFI) while passing the desired DC or low-frequency signal more or less untouched. The familiar fat cylindrical bulges near the ends of many computer monitor, USB, and power-supply cables are clip-on ferrite chokes.

How it works

Ferrite is a ferromagnetic ceramic, typically made of iron oxide mixed with nickel, zinc, or manganese oxides. When a conductor passes through the ferrite, the pair form the equivalent of an inductor with a very lossy core. At high frequencies the ferrite's magnetic losses dominate: instead of merely storing and returning energy as an ideal inductor would, the bead converts the unwanted high-frequency energy into a tiny amount of heat. Unlike a discrete inductor, a ferrite bead is therefore specified by its impedance (in ohms at a stated frequency, often 100 MHz) rather than by inductance, and its impedance curve has distinct inductive, resonant, and resistive regions. Looping the cable through the bead two or more times multiplies the impedance approximately with the square of the number of turns.

Applications

  • Cable chokes — clamp-on ferrites on power leads, monitor cables, and audio leads suppress RF noise radiating from, or entering, equipment, helping devices pass electromagnetic-compatibility (EMC) regulations.
  • Chip beads — surface-mount ferrite beads filter power rails and signal lines on printed circuit boards, isolating noisy digital sections from sensitive analogue circuitry.
  • Power supplies — beads on the output of switched-mode power supplies attenuate switching ripple and spikes.
  • Amateur radio — ferrite chokes ("baluns" wound on ferrite toroids) prevent unwanted common-mode currents on antenna feedlines.

Design considerations

Because a ferrite bead behaves partly like an inductor, combining it with the capacitance of a load can form an LC resonant circuit that rings or even amplifies noise at some frequency; careful designers check impedance curves and damping. Large DC currents through a bead can also saturate the ferrite and reduce its effectiveness, so power-line beads must be rated for the intended bias current.
