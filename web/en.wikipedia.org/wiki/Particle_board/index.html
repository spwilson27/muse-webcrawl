Particle board

Particle board, showing its composition of wood chips bonded under pressure.

Particle board, also known as particleboard, chipboard or low-density fibreboard, is an engineered wood product manufactured from wood chips and a synthetic resin or other suitable binder, which is pressed and extruded. It is cheaper, denser and more uniform than conventional wood and plywood, and is often used when cost or stability matters more than strength and appearance.

Manufacture

Wood particles — often sawmill waste such as shavings, flakes and sawdust — are dried, blended with resin (commonly urea-formaldehyde) and wax, formed into a mat with finer particles at the surfaces, and hot-pressed into sheets at high temperature and pressure. The resulting panels are trimmed, cooled and sanded.

Properties and uses

Particle board has no grain, good dimensional stability and a smooth surface that takes veneer, laminate or paint well. It is widely used in flat-pack furniture, kitchen cabinets, shelving, flooring substrate and door cores. It is, however, weaker and heavier than plywood, is vulnerable to moisture damage and swelling, and can release formaldehyde from its resin binder, a concern that has led to emissions standards and low-emitting formulations in many countries.

History

The material was developed in Germany during the Second World War, when scarce timber was stretched by binding wood waste; the first commercial plant was established in Bremen in 1941. Production expanded worldwide in the postwar decades alongside the growth of the furniture industry.
