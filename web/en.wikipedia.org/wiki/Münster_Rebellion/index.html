Münster Rebellion

The Münster Rebellion (German: Täuferreich von Münster, "Anabaptist dominion of Münster") was an attempt by radical Anabaptists to establish a communal, theocratic government in the German city of Münster between February 1534 and June 1535. It ended when the city was retaken by the forces of its expelled prince-bishop, and its leaders were executed.

Background

Münster, a prince-bishopric in Westphalia, had been drawn into the upheavals of the Reformation. The preacher Bernhard Rothmann led the city's evangelical movement, and by 1533 Anabaptist ideas carried by Dutch and German refugees — followers of the executed prophet Melchior Hoffman — were gaining ground. In February 1534 Anabaptists won control of the city council; those who refused rebaptism were expelled, and thousands of the "godly" streamed in from the Low Countries and northern Germany.

The kingdom

The Dutch disciple Jan Matthys declared Münster the New Jerusalem and instituted a community of goods, seizing property and burning debt records. Matthys was killed in a rash sortie at Easter 1534, and leadership passed to John of Leiden (Jan van Leiden), a former tailor's apprentice who proclaimed himself king of the New Jerusalem in September 1534. He introduced polygamy — taking some sixteen wives — staged elaborate court ceremonies, minted coins, and governed through terror as the prince-bishop Franz von Waldeck besieged the city.

Fall and executions

Starvation tightened through the spring of 1535. On the night of 24–25 June 1535, aided by deserters, the bishop's troops entered Münster and slaughtered most of the defenders. John of Leiden and two associates were tortured and executed in the marketplace on 22 January 1536; their bodies were displayed in iron cages hung from the tower of St Lambert's Church, where the cages remain to this day.

Legacy

The rebellion discredited revolutionary Anabaptism and pushed surviving Anabaptists toward the pacifism of Menno Simons, founder of the Mennonites. The episode has been retold in novels such as Marguerite Yourcenar's The Abyss and Luther Blissett's Q, and in Meyerbeer's opera Le prophète. Historians continue to debate how far the Münster regime reflected Anabaptist theology and how far it was a product of siege radicalisation.
