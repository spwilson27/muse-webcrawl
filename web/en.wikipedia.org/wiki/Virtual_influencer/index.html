Virtual influencer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Also known asCGI influencer, digital influencer, virtual human
First major examplesLil Miquela (2016), Shudu Gram (2017), Imma
PlatformsInstagram, TikTok, YouTube, Weibo

Virtual influencers are computer-generated fictional personas who maintain social media accounts and promote brands, lifestyles, and causes in the manner of human social media influencers. They are designed—typically using CGI, motion capture, and art direction—by agencies, brands, or technology companies, and their posts mix rendered imagery with real-world photography. The most famous early example is Lil Miquela (Miquela Sousa), created by the Los Angeles company Brud in 2016, who amassed millions of Instagram followers and fronted campaigns for fashion houses including Prada and Calvin Klein.

Notable examples

Shudu Gram (2017), created by photographer Cameron-James Wilson and promoted as the "world's first digital supermodel," modelled for Fenty Beauty and Balmain. Imma, a pink-haired Japanese virtual model created by ModelingCafe, has appeared in advertising in Japan and China. Lu do Magalu, the virtual spokeswoman of Brazilian retailer Magazine Luiza, predates the CGI wave in origin (debuting in 2003 as a sales avatar) and is among the most-followed virtual characters in the world. Other examples include Bermuda and Blawko (also by Brud), FN Meka (a virtual rapper whose 2022 major-label deal was quickly dropped after criticism), China's Ayayi, and South Korea's Rozy.

Marketing use

Brands value virtual influencers for total control of image and messaging, absence of personal scandal risk, tireless availability, and novelty value; studies by marketing firms have claimed higher engagement rates than average human influencers, though comparisons are contested. Virtual influencers have appeared at fashion weeks, released singles, and been "interviewed" in character, and advances in generative AI in the 2020s have lowered the cost of creating them dramatically, producing a proliferation of AI personas—some openly disclosed as synthetic and others not.

Criticism

Virtual influencers have been criticized for promoting unrealistic beauty standards with literally impossible bodies, for brands profiting from simulated diversity (as in debates around Shudu, a Black-presenting model created by a white photographer), and for deceptive marketing when audiences do not realize a persona is fictional. Regulators in several countries have moved to require disclosure of advertising and of artificially generated content.
