Solar System

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Solar System is the gravitationally bound system of the Sun and the objects that orbit it. It formed about 4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of a giant molecular cloud. The vast majority of its mass is in the Sun, with much of the remainder in Jupiter. The four inner planets — Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars — are terrestrial planets of rock and metal; the four outer planets are giants, Jupiter and Saturn being gas giants and Uranus and Neptune ice giants.

Other objects include the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, the moons of the planets (Earth's Moon among them), and beyond Neptune the Kuiper belt, the scattered disc and the distant, spherical Oort cloud of comets. Six planets and many smaller bodies are orbited by natural satellites.

Formation and evolution

The Solar System formed from a rotating cloud of interstellar gas and dust — the solar nebula. As it collapsed under gravity it spun faster and flattened into a protoplanetary disc, from which the Sun grew at the centre and the planets accreted. This nebular hypothesis, in forms proposed by Kant and Laplace, is supported by observations of discs around young stars made by telescopes and by agencies and observatories such as NASA and ESA.

The Sun

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star ("yellow dwarf") containing about 99.86% of the system's mass. Its core fuses hydrogen into helium, releasing the light and heat that sustain life on Earth. In roughly five billion years it will exhaust its core hydrogen, expand into a red giant, and eventually leave a white dwarf.

Inner Solar System

The inner planets are small, dense and rocky. Venus is the hottest planet, thanks to a runaway greenhouse effect beneath thick clouds of carbon dioxide and sulphuric acid. Earth is the only known planet with liquid-water oceans and life. Mars, the "red planet", hosts the tallest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, and is the target of robotic missions searching for evidence of past habitability — several of them listed by NASA.

Outer Solar System

Jupiter, the largest planet, has a mass two and a half times that of all the other planets combined, and a centuries-old storm, the Great Red Spot. Saturn is famous for its bright rings of ice particles. Uranus rotates on its side, with an axial tilt of about 98 degrees, and Neptune, the most distant planet, has the fastest winds in the Solar System.

Trans-Neptunian region

Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper belt, a disc of icy bodies including the dwarf planet Pluto, which the International Astronomical Union reclassified from planet status in 2006 — a decision still popularly debated. The Oort cloud, far beyond, is thought to be the source of long-period comets and may extend a light-year or more from the Sun.

Exploration

Robotic probes have visited every planet: Mariner, Pioneer, Voyager, Galileo, Cassini–Huygens, New Horizons and many others. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is the most distant human-made object and has entered interstellar space. Human exploration has so far reached only the Moon, between 1969 and 1972 with NASA's Apollo programme.
