Brood parasite

Brood parasites are organisms that rely on others to raise their young. The strategy appears among birds, insects and fish: the parasite lays its eggs in the nest (or equivalent) of a host, which then incubates the eggs and feeds the offspring, often at great cost to its own young.

Birds

About 1% of bird species are obligate brood parasites, including many cuckoos, cowbirds, honeyguides, indigobirds and whydahs, and the black-headed duck. Classic examples are the common cuckoo, whose chick ejects the host's eggs, and the brown-headed cowbird of North America. Parasite eggs often mimic the host's eggs in colour and pattern, driving an evolutionary arms race of egg discrimination and mimicry. Hosts may reject eggs, desert nests, or mob adult parasites.

Other groups

Cuckoo bees and cuckoo wasps lay eggs in the provisioned nests of other insects; the cuckoo catfish of Lake Tanganyika parasitises mouthbrooding cichlids, its fry eating the host's eggs inside the parent's mouth. Some butterflies, such as large blues, have caterpillars adopted and fed by ants.

Costs and defences

Parasitism can sharply reduce host breeding success, selecting for defences; in turn parasites evolve counter-adaptations, a textbook case of coevolution.
