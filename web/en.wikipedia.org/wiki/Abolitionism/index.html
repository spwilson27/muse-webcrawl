Abolitionism

[Image: the famous 1788 diagram of the slave ship Brookes — rows of figures packed in the ship's hold in cross-section]

Abolitionism, or the abolitionist movement, was the movement to end slavery and the slave trade. It arose in the eighteenth-century Atlantic world, drawing on Enlightenment ideas of natural rights, evangelical Christianity and the testimony and resistance of enslaved people themselves, and it achieved the legal abolition of the transatlantic slave trade and then of slavery itself across the Americas and Europe during the nineteenth century — Brazil, in 1888, being the last Western country to abolish it.

In Britain, campaigners such as William Wilberforce, Thomas Clarkson and Olaudah Equiano built the first modern mass campaign — petitions, boycotts of slave-grown sugar, and the famous diagram of the slave ship Brookes — winning abolition of the slave trade in 1807 and of slavery in most of the British Empire in 1833.

In the United States

American abolitionism grew from Quaker and revolutionary-era roots into a mass movement in the 1830s: William Lloyd Garrison's newspaper The Liberator (1831), Frederick Douglass's Narrative (1845) and speeches, Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), and the Underground Railroad by which enslaved people escaped to free states and Canada.

The movement fractured and fuelled national politics: the Republican Party arose to stop slavery's spread, and the American Civil War turned emancipation into policy — the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the Thirteenth Amendment (1865) ended legal slavery in the United States.

Legacy

Legal abolition did not end forced labour: convict leasing, colonial forced labour, and modern trafficking and forced labour — estimated by international bodies to affect tens of millions today — are the movement's unfinished business, and abolitionist history is central to how societies remember slavery.
