Dragon

A dragon is a large magical legendary creature that appears in the folklore of multiple cultures worldwide. Beliefs about dragons vary considerably through time and region, but dragons in Western cultures since the High Middle Ages have often been depicted as winged, horned, and capable of breathing fire. Dragons in Eastern cultures are usually depicted as wingless, four-legged, serpentine creatures with above-average intelligence. Commonalities between dragons' traits include: hybrid features, possibly combining characteristics of reptiles, birds, and predatory mammals; reproduction by laying eggs; and, in some traditions, an association with water and weather.

Origins and etymology

The English word dragon comes via Old French from Latin draco and Greek drákōn, "serpent" or "giant seafish," possibly from a verb meaning "to see clearly." Dragon-like creatures appear among the earliest mythologies: the Babylonian Mušḫuššu, the multi-headed Hydra of Greek myth, the Norse world-serpent Jörmungandr, and the Chinese dragon (龍, lóng) attested from Neolithic carvings.

Dragons in Western tradition

Greco-Roman dragons guarded treasures and sacred places (Ladon and the golden apples). Medieval European dragons became emblems of evil and chaos to be slain by saints and heroes — Saint George, Sigurd, and Beowulf — and figures of heraldry, most famously the red dragon of Wales.

Dragons in Eastern tradition

Chinese dragons are benevolent symbols of imperial power, rain, and good fortune, governing waters through Dragon Kings; the motif spread throughout East and Southeast Asia (Japanese ryū, Korean yong, Vietnamese rồng).

Modern usage

Dragons are staples of modern fantasy literature and games, from Tolkien's Smaug in The Hobbit to Dungeons & Dragons and Game of Thrones. Hypotheses for the origin of dragon myths include fossil discoveries, exaggerated reports of real reptiles such as crocodiles and monitor lizards, and innate human fear of snakes.
