Second Boer War concentration camps

During the Second Boer War (1899–1902), British forces established a network of concentration camps in South Africa to intern Boer civilians — mainly women and children — as well as black Africans displaced by the war. The camps were created as part of a scorched-earth strategy intended to deny supplies and shelter to Boer guerrilla commandos.

Background

After the conventional phase of the war ended in 1900 with the occupation of the Boer republics' capitals, Boer commandos continued a guerrilla campaign. Under Lord Kitchener, British forces adopted a strategy of farm burning, the construction of blockhouse lines, and the systematic removal of the civilian population from the countryside into camps, where they could be controlled and prevented from aiding the commandos.

Conditions and mortality

Conditions in the camps were often appalling. Overcrowding, poor sanitation, inadequate clean water, and meagre rations led to epidemics of measles, typhoid, dysentery, and pneumonia. Mortality peaked in late 1901, when the death rate in some camps exceeded 300 per thousand per year. Around 26,000 to 28,000 Boer women and children died, the majority of them children under sixteen. Separate camps for black Africans, administered by a Native Refugee Department, held well over 100,000 people, and at least 14,000 deaths were recorded, with the true figure probably considerably higher.

Public reaction

The camps became a scandal in Britain after the campaigner Emily Hobhouse visited South Africa in early 1901 and published a damning report on conditions. Her findings led to the appointment of the Fawcett Commission, a committee of women chaired by Millicent Fawcett, whose recommendations prompted reforms that sharply reduced mortality by early 1902. The Liberal leader Henry Campbell-Bannerman famously denounced the policy as "methods of barbarism".

Legacy

The camps left a deep mark on Afrikaner collective memory and became a central theme of Afrikaner nationalism in the twentieth century. Memorials, such as the Women's Monument in Bloemfontein, commemorate the dead. The term "concentration camp" itself entered wide public usage through this conflict, having earlier been applied to Spanish camps in Cuba.
