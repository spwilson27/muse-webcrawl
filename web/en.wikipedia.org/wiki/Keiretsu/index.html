A keiretsu (Japanese: 系列, literally "system" or "series") is a set of companies with interlocking business relationships and shareholdings that dominated the Japanese economy in the second half of the 20th century. In the legal sense, it is a type of business group comprising a number of ostensibly independent firms organised around a main bank and cross-shareholdings.

History

The keiretsu system has its roots in the zaibatsu—family-controlled conglomerates such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi—which were dissolved by the Allied occupation after World War II. As the occupation reforms relaxed, the former zaibatsu firms reassembled around banks rather than family holding companies, forming the horizontal keiretsu that powered Japan's postwar economic miracle. Cross-shareholding insulated managers from takeover pressure and stock-market swings.

Types

  • Horizontal keiretsu (kinyū keiretsu): a group of large firms in different industries centred on a main bank and a trading company, meeting in presidents' councils (such as Mitsubishi's Kinyōkai). The "Big Six" were Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, Fuyo, Sanwa and Dai-Ichi Kangyō Bank (DKB).
  • Vertical keiretsu (seisan keiretsu): supply-chain networks in which a major manufacturer (such as Toyota or Matsushita/Panasonic) sits atop tiers of affiliated suppliers and distributors, enabling just-in-time production and close coordination.

Decline

From the 1990s, Japan's asset-bubble collapse, banking crises and bank mergers (which fused rival groups) weakened the main-bank system, and cross-shareholdings steadily unwound, though network ties remain influential in Japanese business.
