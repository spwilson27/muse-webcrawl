Ergotamine

Ergotamine
Class: Ergot alkaloid
Uses: acute migraine treatment
Source: Claviceps purpurea (ergot fungus)
Formula: C33H35N5O5

Ergotamine is an ergopeptine and part of the ergot family of alkaloids; it is produced by the fungus Claviceps purpurea, which grows on rye and related grasses. It is used medically, usually in combination with caffeine, for the treatment of acute migraine attacks. Structurally and biochemically, it is closely related to ergoline derivatives and acts as an agonist at serotonin, dopamine and adrenergic receptors.

Medical use

Ergotamine causes vasoconstriction of intracranial blood vessels, which was historically thought to be the basis of its anti-migraine effect. It was introduced for migraine treatment in the 1920s after its isolation by Arthur Stoll at Sandoz in 1918. Its use has declined with the availability of triptans, which are more selective and better tolerated, but it remains an option for some patients, including for prolonged attacks.

Adverse effects

Ergotamine has poor and erratic oral bioavailability and commonly causes nausea. Overuse can lead to medication-overuse headache. Excessive or prolonged use may cause ergotism, with intense peripheral vasoconstriction that can progress to gangrene — the poisoning historically associated with eating ergot-contaminated grain, known in the Middle Ages as "St Anthony's fire". The drug is contraindicated in pregnancy and in cardiovascular disease, and it interacts dangerously with certain antibiotics and other drugs that inhibit its metabolism.
