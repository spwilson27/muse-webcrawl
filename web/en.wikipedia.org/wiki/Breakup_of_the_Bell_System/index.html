The breakup of the Bell System was the dismantling, effective January 1, 1984, of the Bell System, the formerly state-sanctioned telecommunications monopoly operated by AT&T in the United States and Canada. Under a 1982 consent decree settling the antitrust case United States v. AT&T, AT&T agreed to divest its local telephone operations, which were reorganized into seven independent Regional Bell Operating Companies (RBOCs), commonly known as the "Baby Bells".

Background

For most of the 20th century, AT&T and its subsidiaries formed a vertically integrated monopoly: Western Electric manufactured the equipment, Bell Laboratories conducted research, and 22 local Bell operating companies provided telephone service to most Americans. The Kingsbury Commitment of 1913 and subsequent regulatory settlements had preserved this structure for decades.

In 1974, the U.S. Department of Justice filed an antitrust suit alleging that AT&T had monopolized telecommunications markets. MCI and other competitors had also brought private suits, and technological change — including microwave transmission and the Carterfone decision — had begun to erode the rationale for a single integrated monopoly.

The settlement

On January 8, 1982, AT&T and the Justice Department announced a settlement known as the Modification of Final Judgment (MFJ). AT&T would keep its long-distance services, Western Electric, and Bell Labs, but would divest the local exchange monopolies. Judge Harold H. Greene oversaw the decree, modifying aspects such as allowing the RBOCs to offer electronic publishing only after a delay.

The seven Baby Bells

  • Ameritech — Midwest (Illinois Bell, Michigan Bell, and others)
  • Bell Atlantic — Mid-Atlantic
  • BellSouth — Southeast
  • NYEx / NYNEX — New York and New England
  • Pacific Telesis — California and Nevada
  • Southwestern Bell — Southwest
  • US West — Mountain and Northwest states

Consequences and reconsolidation

The divestiture introduced competition into long-distance service and customer-premises equipment, and long-distance prices fell sharply through the 1980s and 1990s. Local service remained regulated. Beginning in the late 1990s, a wave of mergers substantially reconsolidated the industry: Southwestern Bell (renamed SBC) acquired Pacific Telesis and Ameritech, then AT&T itself in 2005, adopting the AT&T name; Bell Atlantic merged with NYNEX and GTE to form Verizon. Several Baby Bell successors thus reconstituted much of the old system's footprint in two national carriers.
