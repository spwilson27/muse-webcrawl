Exaptation

Feathers are a canonical example of exaptation: likely evolved for insulation or display, they were later co-opted for flight.

Exaptation is a term in evolutionary biology for a trait that evolved for one function — or for no function — but later came to serve a different function. The term was introduced by Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba in 1982 to fill a conceptual gap left by the word "preadaptation", which misleadingly implied foresight in evolution.

Definition

Gould and Vrba proposed a terminology distinguishing adaptations, features built by natural selection for their current role, from exaptations, features whose usefulness is not the reason they originally evolved. An exaptation may be a former adaptation co-opted for a new use, or a non-adaptive by-product ("spandrel") that happens to prove useful. Once co-opted, a trait may undergo further selection for its new role, becoming a secondary adaptation.

Examples

  • Feathers in birds: insulation and display likely preceded their aerodynamic use in flight.
  • Bird wings themselves may have begun as forelimbs used in display, balance, or prey handling ("wing-assisted incline running").
  • Bones: mineralised tissue may first have served mineral storage and ion balance before supporting bodies on land.
  • Swim bladders and lungs: the lungs of early fish, used for breathing air, were co-opted in many bony fish into buoyancy organs.
  • Human language and reading: reading recruits brain circuitry (such as the visual word form area) that evolved for other purposes of vision and language — a cultural exaptation.

Significance

The concept warns against assuming that a trait's current utility explains its origin — the "adaptationist" habit criticised by Gould and Richard Lewontin in their 1979 "spandrels" paper. Exaptation also helps explain evolutionary novelty: major transitions often proceed by co-opting structures already present rather than by building new ones from scratch, a process related to François Jacob's image of evolution as a "tinkerer".
