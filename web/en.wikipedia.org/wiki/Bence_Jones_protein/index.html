Bence Jones protein

Bence Jones protein is a monoclonal globulin protein found in the blood or urine of many patients with multiple myeloma and related plasma cell disorders. It consists of free immunoglobulin light chains — either kappa (κ) or lambda (λ) — produced in excess by a clonal population of plasma cells, unaccompanied by the heavy chains of a complete antibody molecule. Because of their small size (about 22–44 kDa), the light chains are filtered by the kidneys and appear in the urine.

History

The protein is named after the English physician Henry Bence Jones, who described it in 1847 in the urine of a patient with mollities ossium (softening of the bones — in retrospect, multiple myeloma). Bence Jones noted the protein's curious thermal behaviour: it precipitated when urine was heated to about 50–60 °C, redissolved as the temperature approached boiling, and reprecipitated on cooling. This heat test was used diagnostically for more than a century.

Clinical significance

Bence Jones proteinuria is a hallmark of multiple myeloma, present in a majority of cases, and may also occur in Waldenström's macroglobulinaemia, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, amyloidosis and monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS). In "light chain myeloma", the clone secretes only light chains and no intact immunoglobulin.

Excess light chains can damage the kidneys — a major cause of morbidity in myeloma — through cast nephropathy ("myeloma kidney"), in which the chains precipitate with Tamm–Horsfall protein in the renal tubules, and through deposition diseases and AL amyloidosis, in which light chains form amyloid fibrils in organs including the heart, kidneys and nerves.

Detection

The historical heat test has been replaced by urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, which identifies the light chain type, and by the serum free light chain assay, which measures kappa and lambda chains in blood with great sensitivity and is now standard in the diagnosis and monitoring of plasma cell disorders.
