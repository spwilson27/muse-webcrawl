Anglo-Scottish Wars

The Anglo-Scottish Wars comprise the various battles which continued to be fought between the Kingdom of England and the Kingdom of Scotland from the early 14th century to the late 16th century. Although the Wars of Scottish Independence (1296–1357) usually mark their conventional beginning, border warfare, raiding, and full-scale invasions recurred for centuries, ending only with the Union of the Crowns in 1603.

Wars of Scottish Independence

When the Scottish succession was disputed after the death of Alexander III and the Maid of Norway, Edward I of England asserted overlordship, provoking the First War of Independence. William Wallace won at Stirling Bridge in 1297 but was defeated at Falkirk; Robert the Bruce's victory at Bannockburn in 1314 secured Scottish independence, recognised by the Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton in 1328. A Second War (1332–57) followed the interventions of Edward III and the "Disinherited".

Border warfare

Through the later Middle Ages, the Anglo-Scottish border was a militarised frontier of marcher lordships, bastle houses, and reiver families. Major invasions produced battles such as Otterburn (1388), Homildon Hill (1402), and Piperdean. Scotland's "Auld Alliance" with France repeatedly drew it into England's continental wars, most disastrously at Flodden in 1513, where James IV was killed.

Tudor period

Henry VIII's "Rough Wooing" (1543–51) sought to force a marriage between the infant Mary, Queen of Scots, and Edward VI, devastating the Scottish Lowlands. The Scottish Reformation and the Treaty of Edinburgh (1560) realigned Scotland toward England. Conflict effectively ended when James VI of Scotland inherited the English throne in 1603, and the border was pacified as the "Middle Shires".
