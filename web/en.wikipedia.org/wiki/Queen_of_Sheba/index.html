Queen of Sheba


Illustration placeholder: the Queen of Sheba before Solomon

The Queen of Sheba is a legendary figure first mentioned in the Hebrew Bible (1 Kings 10; 2 Chronicles 9) and the Qur'an, where she journeys to Jerusalem to test Solomon with "hard questions" and is overwhelmed by his wisdom and wealth, presenting him with spices, gold, and precious stones. In the Ethiopian national epic, the Kebra Nagast, she is called Makeda; in Islamic tradition she is usually called Bilqis.

Biblical account

According to the Hebrew Bible, "when the queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD, she came to prove him with hard questions" (1 Kings 10:1). Solomon answered all her questions; she then praised his wisdom, gave him 120 talents of gold, and received from him "all her desire, whatsoever she asked". The narrative presents the visit as diplomatic and commercial recognition of Solomon's kingdom.

Islamic tradition

In the Qur'an (Surah An-Naml), the queen — called Bilqis in later tradition — receives a letter from Solomon summoning her to submit; after consultations with her council and an exchange of gifts, she comes to him. The story includes Solomon's throne, the hoopoe messenger, and the glass pavement the queen mistakes for water. Commentators describe her as ruler of Saba (Yemen) who embraced Solomon's faith.

Ethiopian tradition

The Kebra Nagast ("Glory of the Kings"), Ethiopia's national epic, makes Makeda queen of Ethiopia, her union with Solomon the origin of the Solomonic dynasty, and their son Menelik I the founder of the imperial line — a genealogy claimed by Ethiopian emperors down to Haile Selassie.

Historicity and identification

Modern scholarship does not confirm a historical queen of Sheba. Some identify her realm with Saba in southern Arabia (Yemen), others with the kingdom of Axum. A 10th-century BCE south Arabian inscription mentioning a female ruler, and Assyrian records of Arabian queens such as Zabibe, are sometimes cited as possible background. She remains a figure of legend rather than of the archaeological record.

In art and culture

The queen appears in Handel's oratorio Solomon (famous for "The Arrival of the Queen of Sheba"), in operas, films, and countless paintings from the Renaissance onward.
