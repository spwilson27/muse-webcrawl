A fly line is a specialized fishing line used in fly fishing. Unlike conventional fishing lines, whose weight is supplied by the lure, the fly line itself is heavy and thick: the caster propels the line, and the nearly weightless artificial fly is carried along by it. Casting works by forming a loop of line that unrolls toward the target.

Construction

Modern fly lines consist of a braided or monofilament core coated with a plasticized polyvinyl chloride or polyurethane coating. The coating contains additives—hollow glass microspheres for floating lines, tungsten powder for fast-sinking lines—that set the line's density and slickness. Line diameters run about 0.5 to 2 mm over lengths of typically 27 to 35 m (90–110 ft).

Weights and tapers

Fly lines are classified by the weight, in grains, of the first 30 feet (9.14 m) of line under the AFTMA/AFFTA standard: a 5-weight line weighs about 140 grains (9.1 g), and classes run from 1 (light trout) to 15 (billfish). Tapers describe how the diameter varies along the line. In a weight-forward taper the mass concentrates near the front, aiding distance casting and turning over large flies; a double taper is symmetrical and economical; a level line is uniform. Specialized profiles include shooting heads and Spey lines.

Density types

Lines are sold as floating (F), sinking (S), and intermediate (I), with sink rates graded from about 1 to 8 inches per second, plus sink-tip designs whose front section sinks while the running line floats. The angler matches line weight to the rod's rating and chooses taper and density according to the water, fly size, and target species.
