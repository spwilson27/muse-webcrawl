Arguin

Arguin Island, off the coast of Mauritania in the Banc d'Arguin.

Arguin (Portuguese: Arguim) is an island off the western coast of Mauritania in the Bay of Arguin. It is approximately 6 km long and 2 km wide and lies within the Banc d'Arguin National Park, a major breeding ground for migratory birds. Historically the island was one of the earliest European trading posts on the West African coast and changed hands repeatedly among Portugal, the Netherlands, Brandenburg-Prussia, France and Britain.

History

Portuguese navigators reached Arguin in the 1440s; a fort and trading factory were established there by 1448 under the auspices of Henry the Navigator, trading for gum arabic, gold and enslaved people. The Dutch seized the island in 1633, lost it to the English in 1665, and the Brandenburg African Company held a fort there from 1685 to 1721. France then took control, and Arguin became part of French Mauritania. Near the island, in July 1816, the French frigate Méduse ran aground on the Arguin Bank — the shipwreck made infamous by the raft of the Medusa and Géricault's painting.

Geography and ecology

The island's surrounding shallows, the Banc d'Arguin, are among the richest fishing grounds in West Africa and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, hosting millions of wintering waders and breeding colonies of pelicans, flamingos and terns, as well as endangered monk seals nearby. The Imraguen people traditionally fish these waters from sailing lanchas.
