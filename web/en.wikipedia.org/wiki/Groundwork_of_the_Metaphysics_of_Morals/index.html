The Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785; German: Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten) is the first of Immanuel Kant's mature works on moral philosophy, and remains one of the most influential in the field. In it Kant sets out to identify and establish the supreme principle of morality, which he names the categorical imperative.

Structure and argument

The work is divided into a preface and three sections. The first section moves from common rational knowledge of morality to philosophical knowledge; the second from popular moral philosophy to a metaphysics of morals; the third from a metaphysics of morals to a critique of pure practical reason.

Kant begins from the claim that the only thing good without qualification is a good will — a will that acts from duty rather than inclination. He then formulates the categorical imperative in several ways, including the formula of universal law ("Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it become a universal law"), the formula of humanity ("Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means"), and the formula of autonomy, associated with the idea of a "kingdom of ends".

Influence

The Groundwork is the foundational text of Kantian deontological ethics and a standard text in university moral philosophy. Its arguments have been developed and criticised by, among others, Hegel, John Stuart Mill, and twentieth-century Kantians such as John Rawls and Christine Korsgaard.
