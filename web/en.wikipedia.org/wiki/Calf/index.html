A calf (plural calves) is a young domestic cow or bull. Calves are reared to become adult cattle or are slaughtered for their meat, called veal, and their hides. The term calf is also used for the young of some other large mammals, including elephants, whales, dolphins, bison, moose, deer and hippos.

Rearing

A newborn calf typically weighs 25–45 kg. In dairy farming, calves are usually separated from their mothers within a day or two and fed milk or milk replacer, first receiving colostrum, which is rich in antibodies. In beef production, calves generally stay with their mothers on pasture until weaning at around six to nine months.

Young calves are vulnerable to scours (diarrhoea), pneumonia and navel infections; their immature immune systems and single-chambered stomach function (calves are born with a functioning abomasum and develop the rumen as they begin eating solid feed) shape how they are managed.

Terminology

A female calf that has not yet had a calf of her own is called a heifer. A young male is a bull calf; a castrated male is a steer calf. Calves raised for veal are usually slaughtered at a few months of age.

Other uses of the word

By extension, "calf" also names the back portion of the lower human leg (the calf muscles, gastrocnemius and soleus), a detached piece of floating ice smaller than an iceberg ("calving" also describes the breaking off of ice from a glacier), and a type of fine leather made from calfskin, used for bookbinding.
