Zeitgeber

A zeitgeber (from German, "time giver") is any external or environmental cue that entrains an organism's internal biological clock to the Earth's 24-hour light/dark cycle. The term was introduced by Jürgen Aschoff, one of the founders of chronobiology, in the 1960s.

Light as the primary zeitgeber

Light is the most powerful zeitgeber for most organisms. In mammals, light information reaches the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus — the body's master clock — through the retinohypothalamic tract, using intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells containing the photopigment melanopsin. The SCN adjusts the phase of the circadian clock so that internal rhythms of sleep, hormone release and body temperature align with the external day.

Other zeitgebers

  • Temperature cycles, which can entrain clocks especially in plants and ectotherms.
  • Feeding and fasting, which strongly affect peripheral clocks in organs such as the liver.
  • Social cues and activity schedules, important in humans.
  • Pharmacological agents, such as melatonin, which can shift clock phase.

Clinical relevance

Because endogenous circadian periods are usually slightly longer or shorter than 24 hours, daily zeitgeber input is necessary to prevent rhythms from drifting. Disruption of zeitgebers contributes to jet lag, shift-work sleep disorder, and the circadian misalignment experienced by blind people whose clocks cannot respond to light. Timed light exposure is a standard therapy for realigning the clock.
