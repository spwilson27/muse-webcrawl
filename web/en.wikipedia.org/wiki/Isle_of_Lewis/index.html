The Isle of Lewis (Scottish Gaelic: Eilean Leòdhais) is the northern part of Lewis and Harris, the largest island in the Outer Hebrides of Scotland. Though Lewis and Harris form a single island, the mountainous, historically separate districts are treated as different "isles". Lewis is comparatively low-lying and peat-covered, and its principal town, Stornoway, is the largest settlement in the Hebrides.

History

Lewis is rich in prehistoric monuments, most famously the Callanish (Calanais) standing stones, erected in the late Neolithic, and the Iron Age broch at Dun Carloway. Norse settlement from the ninth century left a deep imprint in place names; the walrus-ivory Lewis chessmen, found in a hoard at Uig in 1831, date from the twelfth century, when the island belonged to the Norse Kingdom of the Isles. It passed to Scotland by the Treaty of Perth (1266) and later came under the MacLeods and, from 1610, the Mackenzies of Seaforth. Stornoway and the island were sold to Sir James Matheson in 1844 and later to Lord Leverhulme, whose ambitious schemes foundered amid land raids by returning servicemen after the First World War.

Culture and economy

Lewis has one of the highest proportions of Scottish Gaelic speakers in Scotland, and the language is prominent in education, media and the churches. The island is noted for strict Presbyterian Sabbath observance, for crofting townships, and for the production of Harris tweed, much of it woven in Lewis. Fishing, fish farming, public services, tourism and renewable energy are mainstays of the economy. Ferries connect Stornoway with Ullapool on the mainland, and there are flights to Glasgow, Edinburgh, Aberdeen and Inverness.
