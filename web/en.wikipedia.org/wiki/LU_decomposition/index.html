LU decomposition

In numerical analysis and linear algebra, lower–upper (LU) decomposition or factorization factors a matrix as the product of a lower triangular matrix and an upper triangular matrix (see matrix decomposition). The product sometimes includes a permutation matrix as well. LU decomposition can be viewed as the matrix form of Gaussian elimination. Computers usually solve square systems of linear equations using LU decomposition, and it is also a key step when inverting a matrix or computing the determinant of a matrix.

Definition

Let A be a square matrix. An LU factorization refers to the factorization of A, with proper row and/or column orderings or permutations, into two factors: a lower triangular matrix L and an upper triangular matrix U:

A = LU

In the lower triangular matrix, all elements above the diagonal are zero; in the upper triangular matrix, all elements below the diagonal are zero. For a 3×3 matrix this takes the explicit form of products of matrices with unit or non-unit diagonals. Variants include the Doolittle algorithm (unit diagonal in L) and the Crout algorithm (unit diagonal in U).

LU factorization with partial pivoting

Not every square matrix admits an LU decomposition in the simple form above — a zero on the diagonal can make the process break down even for invertible matrices. With row permutations the factorization becomes PA = LU, where P is a permutation matrix. Partial pivoting — swapping the largest-magnitude available pivot into place at each step — also greatly improves numerical stability and is the form used in practice by libraries such as LAPACK.

Applications

Solving linear equations

Given Ax = b, once the factorization is known the system splits into two triangular systems, Ly = Pb and Ux = y, each solvable in O(n²) operations by forward and back substitution. The factorization itself costs about 2n³/3 floating-point operations, so it is especially efficient when many right-hand sides share the same coefficient matrix.

Inverting matrices and determinants

The inverse of A can be obtained by solving for each unit vector in turn. The determinant follows from det(A) = det(P)⁻¹ det(L) det(U), and since the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries, this is inexpensive once the factorization is available.

History

The method is commonly attributed to the Polish astronomer and mathematician Tadeusz Banachiewicz (1938), with related work by Prescott Durand Crout and others in the late 1930s and 1940s; the underlying elimination procedure descends from Gauss.
