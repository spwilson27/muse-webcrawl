Science wars

The science wars were a series of intellectual exchanges, primarily in North America in the 1990s, between scientific realists and postmodernist critics about the nature of scientific theory and intellectual inquiry more broadly. Realists accused postmodernists and adherents of the strong programme in the sociology of scientific knowledge of denying scientific objectivity and promoting relativism; critics replied that realists misunderstood their work and caricatured it.

The science wars pitted natural scientists against postmodern critics of science.

Background

The dispute had roots in Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) and in science and technology studies, which examined how social factors shape scientific knowledge. Tensions rose with books such as Paul Gross and Norman Levitt's Higher Superstition (1994), which attacked academic leftists and postmodernists for hostility to science.

The Sokal affair

The affair reached public prominence in 1996, when physicist Alan Sokal published a deliberately nonsensical parody article, "Transgressing the Boundaries: Towards a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity", in the cultural studies journal Social Text, and then revealed the hoax. The episode — often called the Sokal affair — became the emblematic event of the science wars.

Aftermath

By the early 2000s the exchanges had largely subsided. Scholars such as Bruno Latour later expressed concern that the critical tools of science studies had been taken up by climate-change deniers and other opponents of scientific consensus.
