Radif (music)

Placeholder image: a tar player performing Persian classical music.

Radif (Persian: ردیف, meaning "row" or "series") is a collection of many old melodic figures preserved through generations by oral tradition in Persian classical music. It organises the melodies, called gushehs, within a number of different tonal spaces, called dastgahs. It is the central repertoire and pedagogical foundation of Persian music: students memorise the radif of a master over years of study, internalising the melodic models from which improvisation and composition are built.

Structure

The traditional radif is organised into twelve systems: seven principal dastgahs (Shur, Mahur, Homayun, Chahargah, Segah, Nava and Rast-Panjgah) and five secondary systems called avaz. Within each system are dozens of gushehs — short melodic movements, from a few seconds to several minutes long — many named after places, peoples, or poetic and narrative references. A complete radif contains several hundred gushehs and takes seven to ten hours to perform in full.

Transmission

The radif was historically transmitted orally, from master (ostad) to student, and differs somewhat between lineages. The most authoritative version is the instrumental radif of Mirza Abdollah (1845–1918) for tar and setar, transcribed by his student Alinaghi Vaziri and later published in notation by Mousa Maroufi; the vocal radif of Abdollah Davami is the standard for singers. In 2009, UNESCO inscribed the Radif of Iranian music on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Performance practice

Mastery of the radif is not an end in itself: it equips the musician to improvise within the modal framework of a dastgah, sequencing gushehs in a performance that moves from introductory sections through modulations to a climax and resolution, often concluding with a composed metric piece (tasnif or reng).
