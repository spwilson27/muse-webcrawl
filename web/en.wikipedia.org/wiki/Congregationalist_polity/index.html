Congregationalist polity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Congregationalist polity, often called congregational polity, is a system of church governance in which every local church congregation is independent and ecclesiastically sovereign — autonomous in its own affairs — rather than being governed by a hierarchy of bishops or presbyteries. It is one of the three historic forms of church polity, alongside episcopal polity and presbyterian polity.

Principles

Under congregational polity, church government is sometimes said to be a direct "democracy", though adherents prefer to speak of the lordship of Christ exercised through the whole congregation. The local church calls and ordains its own ministers, admits and disciplines its own members, owns its property, and decides its own doctrine and practice. Congregations may voluntarily associate in fellowships, associations, or denominations for mission and mutual support, but such bodies are advisory and cannot compel a local church.

The theological foundation is typically the priesthood of all believers and the belief that Christ promised his presence to even the smallest gathered congregation (Matthew 18:20). Historic statements include the Savoy Declaration (1658), the English Congregationalists' adaptation of the Westminster Confession, and the Cambridge Platform (1648) of the New England churches.

Churches practising congregational polity

Congregational polity is characteristic of Congregational churches (such as the United Church of Christ and the National Association of Congregational Christian Churches in the United States), most Baptist churches (whose commitment to local autonomy reflects the same principle), the Disciples of Christ, many independent and non-denominational evangelical churches, and Plymouth Brethren assemblies. Unitarian and Universalist congregations also govern themselves congregationally.

History

The model emerged from the English Separatists of the late 16th century, including Robert Browne and the congregation led by John Robinson that produced the Pilgrims of Plymouth Colony. It was carried to New England, where it shaped the "standing order" of colonial Massachusetts and Connecticut, and was defended by theologians such as John Cotton. From churches it influenced broader democratic practice: town-meeting self-government in New England is often traced in part to congregational habits of covenant and consent.
