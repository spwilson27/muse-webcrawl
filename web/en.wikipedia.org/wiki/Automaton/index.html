Automaton

An automaton (plural: automata) is a relatively self-operating machine, or control mechanism designed to automatically follow a sequence of operations, or respond to predetermined instructions. Some automata, such as bell strikers in mechanical clocks, are designed to give the illusion to the casual observer that they are operating under their own power or will. The term has long been commonly associated with automated puppets that resemble moving humans or animals, built to impress and/or to entertain people.

History

Ingenious mechanisms were described in antiquity: the Antikythera mechanism computed astronomical cycles, and Hero of Alexandria wrote treatises on pneumatics and self-moving devices for temples and theatres. Medieval and early modern clockwork produced striking jacks and astronomical clocks; al-Jazari's thirteenth-century Book of Ingenious Devices described programmable humanoid automata. In the eighteenth century, master craftsmen such as Jacques de Vaucanson — whose Digesting Duck (1739) appeared to eat and defecate — and the Jaquet-Droz family produced programmable automata including a boy who writes custom text, works that anticipate stored-program machinery.

Fakes and philosophy

Not all celebrated automata were genuine: the Mechanical Turk (1770), which appeared to play chess, concealed a human operator — an "automaton" as illusion rather than mechanism. Philosophers from Descartes onward used automata to probe the boundaries between mechanism and mind, a debate inherited by modern automata theory, in which "automaton" denotes an abstract model of computation (finite automata, pushdown automata, Turing machines).
