Biological pest control

Biological control or biocontrol is a method of controlling pests such as insects, mites, weeds and plant diseases using other organisms. It relies on predation, parasitism, herbivory or other natural mechanisms, and is a key component of integrated pest management (IPM) programmes. Control agents include predators (ladybirds, lacewings), parasitoid wasps, pathogens such as the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), and herbivorous insects used against invasive weeds.

Types

Importation (classical biocontrol) introduces a pest's natural enemy from its native range; a famous success was the vedalia beetle, imported from Australia to California in 1888–89 to save the citrus industry from cottony cushion scale. Augmentation supplements existing enemies by mass-releasing agents such as Trichogramma wasps or predatory mites in greenhouses. Conservation modifies the environment — hedgerows, refuges, reduced spraying — to favour enemies already present.

Risks

Poorly screened introductions have caused harm: the cane toad in Australia and the rosy wolf snail are notorious examples, and the tachinid fly Compsilura concinnata attacks native North American silk moths. Modern programmes therefore require host-specificity testing and regulatory approval. When well chosen, biocontrol offers durable, chemical-free suppression that does not select for pesticide resistance.
