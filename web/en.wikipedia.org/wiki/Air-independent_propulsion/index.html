Air-independent propulsion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Type 212A submarine, an AIP-equipped design

Air-independent propulsion (AIP), or air-independent power, is any marine propulsion technology that allows a non-nuclear submarine to operate without access to atmospheric oxygen — that is, without surfacing or using a snorkel. AIP can augment or replace the diesel-electric propulsion systems of conventional submarines, extending submerged endurance from a few days to several weeks, although the submarine remains far slower and less capable submerged than a nuclear-powered vessel.

Technologies

Closed-cycle diesel engines

Closed-cycle systems run a conventional diesel engine submerged using stored oxygen (often liquid oxygen), recirculating exhaust gas scrubbed of carbon dioxide. The Soviet Union fielded such boats (the Quebec class) after the Second World War, but stored oxygen proved hazardous.

Closed-cycle steam turbines

The French MESMA system (Module d'Energie Sous-Marine Autonome) burns ethanol and liquid oxygen to raise steam for a turbo-electric plant; it has been fitted to Pakistan's Agosta 90B submarines and offered for export designs.

Stirling engines

The Swedish Stirling engine AIP system, burning diesel fuel and liquid oxygen in an external-combustion engine, powers Sweden's Gotland class, Japan's later Sōryū-class boats (before the switch to lithium-ion batteries), and China's Type 039A/041 (Yuan class).

Fuel cells

Germany's Type 212A and Type 209-1400mod submarines and the Dolphin-class boats use hydrogen fuel cells (Siemens PEM cells) fed by hydrogen stored in tanks outside the pressure hull and liquid oxygen. Fuel-cell AIP is exceptionally quiet, having almost no moving parts, and is also used in Spain's S-80 class design.

History

The concept dates to the nineteenth century — the Spanish submarine Ictíneo II (1867) used a chemical air-independent drive — and the German Walter turbine of the Second World War, using high-test peroxide, influenced postwar Soviet and British experiments. Modern AIP entered wide service from the 1990s, and more recently large lithium-ion battery banks have been offered as a partial alternative, allowing long submerged endurance without a dedicated AIP plant.
