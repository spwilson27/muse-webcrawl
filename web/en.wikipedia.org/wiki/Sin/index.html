Sin

In a religious context, sin is a transgression against divine law or a deity. Each culture has its own interpretation of what it means to commit a sin. Sins are generally considered actions, any thought, word, or act considered immoral, selfish, shameful, harmful, or alienating might be termed "sinful."

In Abrahamic religions

In Judaism, sin is a violation of the commandments (mitzvot) and the covenant with God; repentance (teshuvah) and atonement restore the relationship. In Christianity, sin is understood as an offence against God that separates humanity from him; the doctrine of original sin holds that humans inherit a fallen nature from Adam's disobedience, and salvation is offered through the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ. Christian traditions distinguish mortal and venial sins (in Catholic teaching) and catalogue the seven deadly sins: pride, greed, wrath, envy, lust, gluttony, and sloth. In Islam, sin (dhanb, khati'ah) is disobedience to God's commands; sins are classified as major (kabira) or minor, and sincere repentance (tawba) is always open to the believer.

In other traditions

In Hinduism and Buddhism, harmful action is framed more through karma — the law of cause and effect — than through offence against a creator god; acts rooted in ignorance, attachment, and aversion bind one to the cycle of rebirth (samsara). In Shinto, tsumi denotes impurity or transgression that can be removed through ritual purification.
