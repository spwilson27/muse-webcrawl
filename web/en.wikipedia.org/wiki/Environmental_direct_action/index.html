Environmental direct action

Environmental direct action refers to protest tactics used by environmental movements that aim to intervene immediately in a harmful activity rather than relying solely on lobbying, litigation or electoral politics. Tactics range from peaceful blockades, occupations and lock-ons to property damage and sabotage ("ecotage" or "monkeywrenching"), and from the actions of large organisations such as Greenpeace to decentralised networks such as Earth First! and, more recently, climate movements like Extinction Rebellion and Just Stop Oil.

History

Greenpeace, founded in 1971, popularised non-violent direct action — placing small boats and bodies between whalers or nuclear tests and their targets — as "bearing witness". The late 1970s saw anti-nuclear occupations of reactor sites (Seabrook, Wyhl) and the rise of Earth First! (founded 1980), which defended tree-sits and road blockades to protect old-growth forests in the United States. Anti-roads protests in Britain in the 1990s, including the Newbury bypass campaign, developed tunnelling and tree-house occupations.

Debates

Supporters argue direct action is necessary where institutional channels have failed and that it has stopped or delayed destructive projects and shifted public debate. Critics, including some within the environmental movement, argue that disruptive or destructive tactics alienate the public and invite repression; governments have responded with protest-specific legislation and, in the United States, "ecoterrorism" statutes directed at groups such as the Earth Liberation Front, which committed arson attacks in the 1990s and 2000s.
