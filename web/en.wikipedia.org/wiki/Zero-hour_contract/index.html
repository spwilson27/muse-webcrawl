Zero-hour contract

A zero-hour contract is a type of employment contract in which the employer is not obliged to provide any minimum number of working hours, while the worker is usually not obliged to accept any work offered, although in practice many such workers feel compelled to accept shifts. The term is most commonly used in the United Kingdom, where zero-hour contracts became a prominent subject of political debate in the 2010s. Similar arrangements exist in other countries under names such as "on-call" or "casual" contracts.

Definition

Under a zero-hour contract, the employer does not guarantee the individual any hours of work. The individual may sign an agreement to be available for work as and when required, so that no particular number of hours or times of work are specified. Workers on such contracts are typically paid only for the hours they actually work. In the UK, people on zero-hour contracts are generally entitled to statutory employment rights that depend on their employment status, including the national minimum wage, paid annual leave, and rest breaks.

History in the United Kingdom

Zero-hour contracts were used for many years in sectors such as hospitality, retail, healthcare, and agriculture, where demand for labour fluctuates. Their use expanded rapidly in the early 2010s; estimates by the Office for National Statistics suggested that the number of people reporting a zero-hour contract as their main job rose from around 168,000 in 2010 to over 900,000 by the late 2010s. The growth drew criticism from trade unions and some politicians, who argued the contracts transferred excessive risk to workers and made household budgeting difficult.

Legal reforms

The Small Business, Enterprise and Employment Act 2015 banned exclusivity clauses in zero-hour contracts in Great Britain, making it unenforceable for an employer to prohibit a zero-hour worker from working for another employer. Subsequent debates have considered proposals for a right to request a contract reflecting actual hours worked, advance notice of shifts, and compensation for cancelled shifts.

Criticism and support

Critics argue that zero-hour contracts are associated with income insecurity, underemployment, and difficulty accessing credit or housing. Supporters, including some business groups, contend that the contracts provide flexibility valued by students, carers, and semi-retired people, and allow employers to respond to fluctuating demand. Research findings differ on how many workers on such contracts would prefer guaranteed hours.
