Isocrates (Greek: Ἰσοκράτης; 436–338 BC) was an ancient Greek rhetorician, one of the ten Attic orators listed in the Alexandrian canon compiled by Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus of Samothrace. He was a prolific writer and an influential teacher whose school rivalled Plato's Academy.

Life

Isocrates was born into a wealthy Athenian family whose fortune was ruined by the Peloponnesian War. A weak voice and natural shyness kept him from a career speaking in the assembly or law courts, so he worked first as a logographer (professional speechwriter for litigants) and then, around 392 BC, opened his own school of rhetoric in Athens. His pupils included the historians Ephorus and Theopompus and the orator Hyperides.

Writings

Twenty-one of Isocrates' speeches survive, together with nine letters. His Against the Sophists set out his educational programme, emphasising practical wisdom and civic virtue over abstract philosophy. In the Panegyricus (380 BC) he urged the Greek cities to unite under Athenian leadership against Persia; in the famous Antidosis (354 BC) he defended his career in the form of a fictitious court speech. Late in life, in To Philip, he appealed to Philip II of Macedon to lead the Greeks in a crusade against Persia.

Influence

Isocrates' model of a broad, humanistic education grounded in rhetoric shaped the later Roman tradition through Cicero and Quintilian, and ultimately the liberal arts curriculum of the West. He died in 338 BC, shortly after the Battle of Chaeronea.
