Luteinizing hormone (LH, also known as lutropin and sometimes lutrophin) is a hormone produced by gonadotropic cells in the anterior pituitary gland. The production of LH is regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus stimulating its release. In females, an acute rise of LH ("LH surge") triggers ovulation and development of the corpus luteum. In males, where LH had also been called interstitial cell–stimulating hormone (ICSH), it stimulates Leydig cell production of testosterone.

Structure

LH is a heterodimeric glycoprotein. Each monomeric unit is a glycoprotein molecule; one alpha and one beta subunit make the full, functional protein. Its structure is similar to that of the other glycoprotein hormones, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). The alpha subunits of these four hormones are identical, containing 92 amino acids in humans; the beta subunits vary. The beta subunit of LH has 120 amino acids and confers its specific biological action, one reason LH can be distinguished from FSH despite their overlapping roles.

Genes

The gene for the alpha subunit is located on chromosome 6q14.3 and is shared among the glycoprotein hormones. The LH beta subunit gene (LHB) is located on chromosome 19q13.32 and is expressed in pituitary gonadotrope cells under the control of GnRH and modulated by sex steroids and inhibin.

Function

In both males and females, LH works upon endocrine cells in the gonads to produce androgens. In females, LH supports theca cells in the ovaries that provide androgens and hormonal precursors for estradiol production. At the time of menstruation, FSH initiates follicular growth; as the follicle matures and estrogen rises, a sustained high estrogen level triggers the mid-cycle LH surge, which causes the release of the mature egg (ovulation) and transforms the ruptured follicle into the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone. In males, LH binds to receptors on Leydig cells in the testes, stimulating synthesis and secretion of testosterone.

Normal levels and measurement

LH levels are normally low during childhood and, in women, high after menopause. During the reproductive years typical levels are roughly 1–20 IU/L, rising sharply during the mid-cycle surge. LH is measured in fertility workups, in evaluating precocious or delayed puberty, and in diagnosing pituitary and gonadal disorders. Home ovulation predictor kits detect the urinary LH surge. Excess LH relative to FSH is a commonly noted feature of polycystic ovary syndrome, though it is not a diagnostic criterion.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of LH can result in hypogonadism, while excessive secretion is associated with conditions such as premature menopause and certain pituitary adenomas. Recombinant LH (lutropin alfa) and urinary preparations are used in assisted reproduction, and LH activity is also supplied by hCG and human menopausal gonadotropin (hMG) preparations.
