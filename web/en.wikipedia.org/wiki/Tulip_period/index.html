Tulip period

Period1718–1730
EmpireOttoman Empire
SultanAhmed III
Grand VizierNevşehirli Damat Ibrahim Pasha

The Tulip period (Turkish: Lâle Devri) was a phase in Ottoman history, conventionally dated from the Treaty of Passarowitz in 1718 to the Patrona Halil revolt in 1730, during the reign of Sultan Ahmed III. Named by later historians after the Ottoman elite's fashion for cultivating, displaying and celebrating tulips, the period is noted for peace abroad after costly wars, a flourishing court culture, conspicuous consumption, and the Empire's first systematic openness to European influences in art, architecture, technology and diplomacy.

Characteristics

Under Grand Vizier Nevşehirli Damat Ibrahim Pasha, the court pursued peace with Austria and Persia while investing in palaces, gardens, fountains and public works in Istanbul. The summer palace of Sa'dabad, built in 1722 along the Kağıthane stream in imitation of European — especially French — models, became the emblem of the era's lavish entertainments, which included night-time "tulip illuminations" in palace gardens lit by lamps and lantern-bearing tortoises.

Cultural and technological developments

The period saw notable openings toward Europe: the first Ottoman embassies to Paris and Vienna, the introduction of European-style military missions, and, most consequentially, the establishment of the first Ottoman Turkish printing press by İbrahim Müteferrika in 1727, which began publishing books in Turkish in Arabic script in 1729. Tulip cultivation became a passion of the court and wealthy households; rare bulbs commanded high prices, and the flower gave its name to the age.

End

The era's extravagance, combined with military setbacks against Persia and resentment of rising prices and taxes, fuelled popular discontent. In September 1730 the Patrona Halil revolt toppled Ahmed III and executed Ibrahim Pasha, ending the Tulip period. The Sa'dabad palace was largely destroyed by the rebels.
