Well-being

Well-being, or wellbeing, is broadly what is good for a person: the state of being comfortable, healthy, happy and able to flourish. It is a central concept in philosophy (where it overlaps with welfare and the good life), in psychology, in public health, and increasingly in economics and public policy, where governments measure population well-being alongside income and GDP.

Theories

  • Hedonism – well-being consists in pleasure and the absence of pain.
  • Desire-satisfaction theories – a person's life goes well when their informed desires are fulfilled.
  • Objective list theories – certain goods (health, knowledge, friendship, achievement, autonomy) contribute to well-being whether or not they are desired or pleasurable.
  • Eudaimonic theories – following Aristotle, well-being is flourishing: living and doing well through the exercise of one's capacities in accordance with virtue.

Measurement

Psychologists distinguish subjective well-being — typically measured by self-reported life satisfaction, positive affect and low negative affect, in the tradition of Ed Diener's research — from broader indicators including physical health, social relationships, economic security and meaning. Martin Seligman's PERMA model (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment) is an influential account of flourishing. At the national level, measures such as the World Happiness Report, the OECD Better Life Index and Bhutan's Gross National Happiness attempt to track well-being across populations.

Determinants

Research associates well-being with health, income (with diminishing returns), employment, social connection, autonomy, and factors such as exercise, sleep and time in nature. Well-being is also studied in specific settings, including workplace well-being and student well-being, and is a target of positive psychology interventions.
