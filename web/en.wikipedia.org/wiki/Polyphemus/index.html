Polyphemus

In Greek mythology, Polyphemus (Greek: Πολύφημος, "much spoken of") is the one-eyed giant son of Poseidon and Thoosa. He is a Cyclops who figures most famously in Homer's Odyssey, where he imprisons Odysseus and his men in his cave and devours several of them before Odysseus blinds him and escapes.

In the Odyssey

Odysseus and twelve of his crew enter Polyphemus' cave seeking hospitality. The Cyclops traps them by rolling a huge boulder across the entrance and eats two men that evening and two more the next day. Odysseus plies the giant with strong unmixed wine, and when asked his name replies "Outis" — "Nobody." After Polyphemus falls asleep, the men drive a heated olive-wood stake into his single eye. When the other Cyclopes hear his cries, Polyphemus shouts that "Nobody" is hurting him, so they go away. The Greeks escape by clinging beneath the bellies of the giant's sheep as he lets them out to pasture, feeling their backs to ensure no man rides on top. As he sails away, Odysseus taunts the giant and reveals his true name — allowing Polyphemus to pray to his father Poseidon, who curses Odysseus' homeward voyage.

Other traditions

In later literature Polyphemus became a lovesick pastoral figure, pining for the sea-nymph Galatea; in Theocritus' Idylls he sings of his unrequited love, and in Ovid's Metamorphoses he crushes Galatea's lover Acis with a rock — the story set by Handel in Acis and Galatea. Euripides' satyr play Cyclops retells the Odyssey episode comically.
