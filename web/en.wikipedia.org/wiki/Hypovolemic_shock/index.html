Hypovolemic shock

Hypovolemic shock
DefinitionShock caused by inadequate circulating blood volume
SpecialtyEmergency medicine, trauma surgery
SymptomsRapid weak pulse, low blood pressure, cool clammy skin, confusion
CausesHaemorrhage, severe dehydration, burns

Volume resuscitation with intravenous fluids and blood products is the mainstay of treatment.

Hypovolemic shock is a form of shock caused by an inadequate volume of blood or fluid in the circulatory system, most commonly from severe bleeding (haemorrhagic shock). Loss of circulating volume reduces venous return and cardiac output, so tissues receive insufficient oxygen; untreated, it progresses to organ failure and death. It is one of the four classic categories of shock, alongside cardiogenic, obstructive and distributive shock.

Causes

Haemorrhage — from trauma, gastrointestinal bleeding, ruptured aneurysm or obstetric emergencies — is the leading cause. Non-haemorrhagic causes include severe dehydration from vomiting, diarrhoea or heat illness; fluid sequestration in burns, pancreatitis or bowel obstruction; and excessive diuresis.

Signs and stages

Haemorrhage is conventionally divided into four classes by the fraction of blood volume lost: Class I (up to 15%) with minimal signs; Class II (15–30%) with tachycardia and narrowed pulse pressure; Class III (30–40%) with hypotension, marked tachycardia and confusion; and Class IV (over 40%), immediately life-threatening. Typical signs include a fast, weak pulse, rapid breathing, cool pale or mottled skin, delayed capillary refill, reduced urine output and altered mental state.

Treatment

The priorities are to stop the bleeding (direct pressure, tourniquets, surgery or embolisation) and to restore circulating volume with intravenous crystalloids and, for blood loss, transfusion of packed red cells, plasma and platelets in balanced ratios. The antifibrinolytic tranexamic acid improves survival if given within three hours of injury. In uncontrolled haemorrhage, permissive hypotension — accepting a lower blood pressure until bleeding is controlled — may limit further loss. Definitive care follows the ABCDE approach of advanced trauma life support.
