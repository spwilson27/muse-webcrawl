Omayra Sánchez Garzón (28 August 1972 – 16 November 1985) was a Colombian girl trapped in water and concrete rubble by a mudslide following the eruption of the Nevado del Ruiz volcano in Tolima, Colombia. After a rescue attempt failed to free her, she died, likely of gangrene or hypothermia, after having been trapped for about 60 hours.

The Armero tragedy

On 13 November 1985, the eruption of Nevado del Ruiz melted part of the volcano's glacier and sent lahars — volcanic mudflows — down its slopes. The flows destroyed the town of Armero almost entirely, killing roughly 25,000 people in what became known as the Armero tragedy, the deadliest volcanic disaster of the twentieth century in the Americas.

Omayra Sánchez was trapped up to her neck in water, concrete, and debris from her family's destroyed home, with the bodies of her father and aunt beneath her. Rescue workers were unable to free her legs, which were pinned under a brick door, and lacked the pump needed to lower the water around her without the risk of her legs being crushed. Her calm and dignity in conversation with rescuers and journalists were broadcast around the world.

The photograph

Shortly before her death, photojournalist Frank Fournier took a picture of Sánchez in her ordeal, titled The Agony of Omayra Sánchez. The image was published worldwide six months later and won the World Press Photo of the Year for 1985. It remains the subject of an ongoing debate about the role of the photojournalist and the limits of documenting suffering without being able to intervene.

Legacy

Sánchez's death became a symbol of the Colombian government's failure to act on warnings about the volcano's danger. The writer Isabel Allende honoured her in the short story "And of Clay Are We Created", based on the events. A species of pseudoscorpion, Parobisium omayrae, was named in her memory.
