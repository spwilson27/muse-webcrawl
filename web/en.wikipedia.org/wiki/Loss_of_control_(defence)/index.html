Loss of control (defence)

Loss of control is a partial defence to murder in the law of England and Wales, introduced by sections 54 and 55 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009. It replaced the old defence of provocation and reduces a murder conviction to voluntary manslaughter where the defendant killed as a result of a loss of self-control attributable to a qualifying trigger, and a person of the defendant's sex and age with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint might have reacted similarly.

Requirements

Three elements must be shown: (1) the defendant's acts resulted from a loss of self-control — which need not be sudden, though delay and evidence of planning or revenge weigh against it; (2) the loss of control had a qualifying trigger; and (3) the objective standard in section 54(1)(c) is met. The judge decides whether sufficient evidence exists for the defence to go to the jury; the prosecution must then disprove it beyond reasonable doubt.

Qualifying triggers

A qualifying trigger is either the defendant's fear of serious violence from the victim against the defendant or another identified person, or things said or done (or both) which constituted circumstances of an extremely grave character and caused the defendant to have a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged. Sexual infidelity is expressly disregarded as a trigger, though in R v Clinton (2012) the Court of Appeal held it may still form part of the context where other triggers exist. Incitement by the defendant, and acting in a considered desire for revenge, exclude the defence.

Comparison with provocation

The reform followed Law Commission criticism of provocation — in particular its suddenness requirement and the "reasonable man" test — and cases such as R v Ahluwalia. Loss of control is deliberately narrower in some respects (revenge and sexual infidelity exclusions) and broader in others (fear trigger; no suddenness requirement).
