Strike action

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Strike action, often simply a strike, is a work stoppage caused by the mass refusal of employees to work, used as leverage in disputes with employers (over pay, conditions, staffing, dismissals) and, in general and political strikes, against governments. Variants include the picket, the sit-down strike (Flint, 1936–37), the wildcat strike (unofficial), the sympathy strike, the work-to-rule and the general strike (Britain 1926; France 1968 and 1995; India's recurrent bandhs sit at the strike–protest boundary).

Strike rights sit in international law (ILO Conventions 87 and 98 imply a right to strike in the ILO's reading; ICESCR Article 8 guarantees one subject to limits) and national law varies sharply: France protects striking as a constitutional individual right; Britain regulates it through ballots and notice (and bans secondary action), with minimum-service-levels legislation added in 2023 amid a large strike wave; the US NLRA (1935) protects concerted activity but permits permanent replacement of economic strikers (Mackay Radio, 1938) — a doctrine blamed by unions for the long decline of US strike frequency. Strike statistics have fallen across the OECD since the 1970s even as episodic waves (public-sector, logistics, platform work) recur; economists study strikes as costly signalling that resolves bargaining under private information.
