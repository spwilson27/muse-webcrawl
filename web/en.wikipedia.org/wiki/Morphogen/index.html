Morphogen

Morphogen
FieldDevelopmental biology
FunctionPositional information; pattern formation
ExamplesBicoid; Sonic hedgehog; retinoic acid; BMP; Wnt

A morphogen is a signalling molecule that acts directly on cells to produce specific responses depending on its local concentration. Morphogens spread from a localised source to form a concentration gradient across a developing tissue, and cells interpret their position within the gradient by switching on different genes at different threshold concentrations — the basis of the "French flag model" proposed by Lewis Wolpert in 1969.

The French flag model

In Wolpert's model, a field of cells is patterned by a gradient declining from one edge: cells exposed to high concentration adopt one fate (blue), intermediate concentration a second (white), and low concentration a third (red) — like the stripes of the French flag. The model requires that the molecule act directly, at a distance, and evoke at least two distinct responses besides the "off" state.

Classic examples

  • Bicoid in the fruit fly Drosophila: a transcription factor gradient from the anterior of the embryo that patterns head and thorax structures; its discovery by Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard's group was a landmark demonstration of a real morphogen.
  • Sonic hedgehog (Shh): patterns the vertebrate neural tube ventrally and the limb's anterior–posterior axis from the zone of polarizing activity.
  • Retinoic acid, BMP and Wnt family signals, Activin, and Decapentaplegic (Dpp) in Drosophila also meet the morphogen criteria in various contexts.

Mechanisms and debates

How gradients form — free diffusion, restricted diffusion, transcytosis, or transport along cellular extensions (cytonemes) — and how cells read concentration robustly despite noise, remain active research areas. Reaction–diffusion systems of the kind Alan Turing proposed in 1952 can also generate periodic patterns without a pre-existing source, and interact with morphogen gradients in real embryos.
