Commissioner of Baseball

The Commissioner of Baseball is the chief executive of Major League Baseball (MLB) and the associated Minor League Baseball organisation. The commissioner is elected by the owners of the 30 MLB clubs and is empowered to act "in the best interests of baseball" — a broad grant of authority dating to the office's creation after the Black Sox Scandal. The incumbent is Rob Manfred, who took office in January 2015.

The commissioner is Major League Baseball's chief executive.

Origin of the office

Before 1920, baseball was governed by a three-man National Commission. The fixing of the 1919 World Series (the Black Sox Scandal) destroyed public confidence, and the owners created a single commissioner with near-absolute power, appointing federal judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis. Landis expelled the eight accused Chicago White Sox players for life despite their acquittal in court, setting the precedent for the office's extraordinary disciplinary authority.

Powers and notable actions

The commissioner negotiates labour agreements with the players' union (in conjunction with club owners), oversees broadcasting contracts, disciplines players and clubs, and rules on matters from expansion to the game's rules. Landmark actions include Landis's Black Sox bans; Kuhn's voiding of player sales; Giamatti's lifetime ban of Pete Rose for gambling (1989); Selig's oversight of the steroid era, the introduction of the wild card, interleague play, and instant replay; and Manfred's institution of the pitch clock and other pace-of-play rules in 2023.
