Grey matter (or gray matter) is a major component of the central nervous system, consisting of neuronal cell bodies, neuropil (dendrites and unmyelinated axons), glial cells, synapses and capillaries. It takes its name from its pinkish-grey colour in fresh tissue, in contrast to white matter, whose whiteness comes from the fatty myelin sheathing its axons. Grey matter is where most information processing in the nervous system occurs: it contains the synapses at which neurons communicate.

Distribution

In the brain, grey matter forms the cerebral cortex — the folded outer sheet responsible for perception, voluntary movement, language and cognition — and the cerebellar cortex, as well as deep structures embedded in white matter such as the thalamus, hypothalamus, basal ganglia and brainstem nuclei. In the spinal cord the arrangement is inverted: a butterfly- or H-shaped core of grey matter, organised into dorsal, lateral and ventral horns, is surrounded by white matter tracts. Grey matter makes up roughly 40% of the human brain by volume but consumes a disproportionate share of its energy.

Development and change

Cortical grey matter volume increases through childhood as synapses proliferate, then declines during adolescence through synaptic pruning, a process linked to learning and maturation. Further gradual loss with ageing, and accelerated loss in diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and schizophrenia, can be measured with MRI using voxel-based morphometry. Experience and training can measurably alter grey matter density in relevant regions, a form of neuroplasticity.
