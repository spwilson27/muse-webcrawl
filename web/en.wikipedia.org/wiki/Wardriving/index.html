Wardriving

Wardriving is the act of searching for Wi-Fi wireless networks, usually from a moving vehicle, using a laptop or smartphone. It involves driving around an area and mapping the locations of wireless access points.

Typical toolsLaptop or phone, Wi-Fi adapter, GPS receiver, software such as Kismet or NetStumbler
Named afterWardialing

Etymology

The term was coined by computer security researcher Peter Shipley around 2000, by analogy with wardialing — the older practice of dialling ranges of telephone numbers to find modems, popularised by the 1983 film WarGames.

Practice

Wardrivers typically log each access point's SSID, MAC address, signal strength, encryption status and GPS coordinates, and may upload the results to public databases such as WiGLE, producing searchable maps of wireless coverage. Passive scanning merely receives broadcast beacon frames and is generally legal in many jurisdictions, though connecting to or using a network without authorisation is not. Related practices include warwalking, warcycling, and warflying with drones.

Ethics and law

Debate over wardriving has centred on whether detecting broadcast networks constitutes intrusion. Most legal analyses distinguish detection — receiving signals a network publicly transmits — from unauthorised access, which is prohibited under computer-misuse laws in most countries.
