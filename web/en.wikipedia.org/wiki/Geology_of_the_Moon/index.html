Geology of the Moon

The geology of the Moon (sometimes called selenology, from Selene, the Greek goddess of the Moon) is the study of the structure, composition and history of Earth's Moon. It is based on telescopic observation, orbital spacecraft measurements, and laboratory analysis of lunar rocks and soil (regolith) returned by the Apollo and Luna programmes and of lunar meteorites.

Surface features

The lunar surface consists of two principal terrains. The bright highlands are rugged, heavily cratered and composed mainly of anorthosite, a plagioclase-rich rock that crystallised from a global magma ocean early in lunar history. The dark maria (singular mare) are vast plains of basaltic lava that flooded giant impact basins, mostly on the near side, between about 3.9 and 3.0 billion years ago. The entire surface is mantled by regolith, a layer of fragmental debris produced by billions of years of impact cratering. Notable features include impact basins such as the South Pole–Aitken basin — one of the largest in the Solar System — and mountain ranges forming the rims of basins such as Imbrium.

Interior

Seismic data from Apollo instruments show a differentiated body: a crust averaging roughly 40–50 km thick (thicker on the far side), a mantle extending to about 1,000 km depth, and a small, partially molten iron-rich core a few hundred kilometres in radius. Moonquakes are much weaker than terrestrial earthquakes and are triggered largely by tidal stresses.

History

The Moon formed about 4.5 billion years ago, most likely from debris of a giant impact between the early Earth and a Mars-sized body (Theia). After crystallisation of the magma ocean, intense bombardment (the Late Heavy Bombardment) cratered the crust; mare volcanism followed and largely ceased by about 1 billion years ago, leaving the Moon geologically quiet, though permanently shadowed polar craters harbour water ice discovered by orbiters such as Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter and India's Chandrayaan-1.
