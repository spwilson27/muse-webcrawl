Reflection seismology


Marine seismic survey: a vessel tows air guns and hydrophone streamers.

Reflection seismology (or seismic reflection) is a method of exploration geophysics that uses the principles of seismology to estimate the properties of the Earth's subsurface from reflected seismic waves. The method is analogous to sonar and echolocation, and is the principal tool used in the search for hydrocarbons.

Method

A controlled source—vibroseis trucks or dynamite on land, air guns at sea—emits acoustic energy into the ground. Waves reflect at boundaries between rock layers with contrasting acoustic impedance; the contrasts are governed by each layer's reflection coefficients. Receivers (geophones on land, hydrophones in marine streamers) record the arrival times and amplitudes of the reflections. Processing the traces—stacking, migration, and velocity analysis—produces images of subsurface structures such as folds, faults, and stratigraphic traps.

Survey geometries

In a 2D survey, sources and receivers lie along a single line, producing a cross-section. 3D seismic surveys acquire a dense grid of data, yielding a volume image; repeated 3D surveys over time (4D seismic) monitor fluid movement in producing reservoirs.

Applications and limits

Beyond oil and gas, reflection seismology images groundwater, mineral deposits, geothermal systems, and crustal structure. Its resolution decreases with depth, and interpretation is non-unique: seismic images must be calibrated with well logs and geological knowledge.
