Andrew Smith Hallidie

Andrew Smith Hallidie (March 16, 1836 – April 24, 1900) was an English-born American inventor and engineer who promoted the first successful cable car system, the San Francisco cable car system, which began operating in 1873. A manufacturer of wire rope, he is often called the "father of the cable car." Hallidie Plaza in San Francisco is named after him.

Early life

Hallidie was born in London in 1836, the son of Andrew Smith, a Scottish inventor and manufacturer (he later added "Hallidie" to his name, after his godfather, a physician). Apprenticed in his father's machine shop, he emigrated to California in 1852 during the Gold Rush. After trying mining, he turned to bridge building and engineering, constructing suspension bridges across rivers in the Sierra Nevada mining country.

Wire rope and mining engineering

In 1857 Hallidie established a wire rope factory in San Francisco, manufacturing rope used for suspension bridges, aerial tramways carrying ore in the mines, and the "endless wire ropeway" used in mining. His cable systems for transporting ore buckets across gullies helped inspire his idea for moving streetcars by an underground cable.

Cable cars

San Francisco's steep hills made horse-drawn streetcars slow, dangerous and cruel to the animals; Hallidie later said witnessing horses struggle up Jackson Street helped prompt his invention. With partners, he formed the Clay Street Hill Railroad, and on August 2, 1873, he personally tested the first cable car on Clay Street. The line opened to the public on September 1, 1873, and its success — despite skepticism and the 1873 financial panic — led to cable lines throughout San Francisco and other hilly cities. Hallidie's patents and his presidency of the company made him the central figure of early cable traction.

Later life and legacy

Hallidie served as a regent of the University of California, promoted technical education, and remained active in San Francisco business until his death in 1900. The world's last surviving manually operated cable car system still runs in San Francisco, and Hallidie Plaza, at the Powell Street cable car turntable, commemorates him.
