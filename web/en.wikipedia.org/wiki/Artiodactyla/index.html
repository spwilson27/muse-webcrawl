Artiodactyla is an order of placental mammals commonly known as the even-toed ungulates. Unlike the odd-toed perissodactyls (horses, tapirs, rhinoceroses), artiodactyls bear their weight equally on the third and fourth toes of each foot. The order includes many of the world's most economically important and ecologically prominent mammals: pigs, hippopotamuses, camels, deer, giraffes, antelopes, sheep, goats, and cattle.

Description

The defining feature of the group is the double-pulley astragalus (ankle bone), which gives the foot great flexibility and spring. Most species walk on two functional toes (digits III and IV) whose fused or paired bones may form a cannon bone, while digits II and V are reduced or absent; hippos retain four toes. Many artiodactyls bear horns or antlers, and a large number are ruminants, fermenting plant food in a multi-chambered stomach with the help of microorganisms—a strategy that allows efficient use of coarse vegetation. Sizes range from mouse deer weighing about 2 kg to the hippopotamus and giraffe, exceeding a tonne.

Evolution and classification

Artiodactyls appear in the early Eocene, about 55 million years ago, with small rabbit-sized forms such as Diacodexis. They diversified extensively as grasslands spread during the Miocene. Molecular evidence shows that cetaceans (whales and dolphins) evolved from within the artiodactyls, with hippopotamuses as their closest living relatives; many modern classifications therefore merge the groups as Cetartiodactyla, though Artiodactyla remains widely used for the traditional terrestrial order.

Major living groups include:

  • Tylopoda — camels and llamas.
  • Suina — pigs and peccaries.
  • Cetruminantia — ruminants (chevrotains, deer, giraffes, pronghorn, bovids), hippopotamuses, and cetaceans.

Relationship with humans

Artiodactyls include most domesticated livestock—cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, domestic camels, llamas, alpacas, and domestic water buffalo—central to agriculture for milk, meat, wool, leather, and traction. Wild species are hunted for food and sport, and several, such as the saiga and various deer, are subjects of major conservation concern.
