Song poetry

Song poetry may refer in Chinese literary history to the lyric poetry of the Song dynasty (960–1279), written predominantly in the ci form — verse written to pre-existing musical tunes with lines of irregular length. Together with Tang dynasty shi poetry, Song ci (宋詞, Sòngcí) represents one of the twin peaks of classical Chinese lyric art.

An edition of Song dynasty ci lyrics

The ci form

Each ci is written to a named tune pattern (cipai) dictating line lengths, tonal patterns and rhyme. Over a thousand tune patterns are recorded. Originally songs performed by courtesans in entertainment quarters, ci were elevated into serious literature by poets such as Liu Yong, who popularised the slow, long manci form.

Major poets

  • Su Shi (1037–1101) broadened ci beyond love themes to philosophy, history and landscape, founding the bold and unconstrained haofang style.
  • Li Qingzhao (1084–c. 1155), the most celebrated female poet of China, master of the delicate wanyue ("graceful restraint") manner.
  • Xin Qiji (1140–1207) fused patriotic, martial themes with the haofang manner after the fall of the north to the Jurchen Jin.
  • Zhou Bangyan and Jiang Kui refined musical craftsmanship and tonal artistry.

Styles

Critics conventionally divide Song ci between the wanyue school — subtle, restrained, often romantic — and the haofang school — heroic, expansive and free. Collected editions such as the Huajian ji (for the earlier Five Dynasties) and the comprehensive Quan Song Ci preserve more than 20,000 lyrics by some 1,300 poets.

Legacy

Song lyrics influenced later drama (Yuan qu) and remain widely read, memorised and set to music in China today. In English translation, the term also overlaps with "song poetry" as a general label for sung verse.
