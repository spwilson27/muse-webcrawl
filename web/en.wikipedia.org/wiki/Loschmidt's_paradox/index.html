Loschmidt's paradox

Loschmidt's paradox, also known as the reversibility paradox, irreversibility paradox, or Umkehreinwand, is the objection that it should not be possible to deduce an irreversible process from time-symmetric dynamics. This puts the time reversal symmetry of (almost) all known low-level fundamental physical processes at odds with any attempt to infer from them the second law of thermodynamics, which describes the macroscopic behaviour of many systems. Both of these are well-accepted principles in physics, with sound observational support, but they seem to be in conflict; hence the paradox.

Origin

The paradox is named after Austrian physicist Johann Josef Loschmidt, who formulated the objection in 1876 in discussions with Ludwig Boltzmann. Loschmidt argued that if a system evolves from a low-entropy state to a high-entropy state, then by reversing all the velocities of the particles one could obtain a system that evolves from high entropy back to low entropy — apparently violating the second law. Boltzmann's statistical interpretation of entropy, expressed in the formula S = k log W, was developed partly in response: the second law holds statistically, not as an absolute dynamical necessity.

The arrow of time

Any process that happens regularly in one direction but never in the reverse is said to have an arrow of time. Because the microscopic laws of motion — Newton's equations as well as the Schrödinger equation — are invariant under time reversal, the thermodynamic arrow cannot follow from the equations of motion alone. Most modern resolutions appeal to special initial conditions: the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state (the "Past Hypothesis"), so entropy increases simply because there is far more room above than below.

Dynamical systems

Current research in nonequilibrium statistical mechanics considers two main approaches. One, associated with Boltzmann himself, treats entropy as a measure of the phase-space volume (or number of microstates) compatible with a macrostate; reversed trajectories exist but are overwhelmingly improbable and unstable to perturbation. The other approach, developed through the work of Sinai, Ruelle, Gallavotti and Cohen on chaotic dynamical systems, seeks rigorous derivations of transport laws from reversible microscopic dynamics with chaotic mixing.

Fluctuation theorem

The fluctuation theorem, proved in various forms by Evans, Cohen, Morris, Gallavotti and others in the 1990s, quantifies how entropy-consuming trajectories become exponentially unlikely as system size or duration grows. It shows precisely how macroscopic irreversibility emerges from reversible dynamics for large systems over long times, turning Loschmidt's objection into a calculable statement about probabilities rather than an impossibility.
