Nootropic

Nootropics (from Greek nóos, "mind", and tropḗ, "turning"), colloquially called smart drugs or cognitive enhancers, are substances claimed to improve cognitive function — particularly memory, creativity, motivation and attention — in healthy individuals. The term was coined in 1972 by Romanian psychologist and chemist Corneliu E. Giurgea for the compound piracetam.

Giurgea's criteria

Giurgea proposed that a true nootropic should enhance learning and memory, protect the brain against various injuries, facilitate interhemispheric information transfer, and have few side effects and extremely low toxicity.

Commonly cited substances

The category popularly includes racetams (piracetam, aniracetam), stimulants used off-label such as modafinil and methylphenidate, ampakines, cholinergics, and various herbal products and supplements such as caffeine, L-theanine, Bacopa monnieri and ginkgo biloba.

Evidence and regulation

Scientific evidence for meaningful cognitive enhancement in healthy people is limited and mixed for most compounds; prescription stimulants show modest effects on some measures, mainly under sleep deprivation. Regulatory status varies widely by country, and many products are sold as dietary supplements without rigorous approval. Medical bodies caution about side effects, interactions and the ethical issues of enhancement use in competitive settings.
