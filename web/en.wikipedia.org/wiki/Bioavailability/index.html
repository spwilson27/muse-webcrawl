Bioavailability

FieldPharmacology / pharmacokinetics
SymbolF
DefinitionFraction of an administered dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged
IV reference100% by definition

In pharmacology, bioavailability is a subcategory of absorption and is the fraction (%) of an administered drug that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. By definition, a drug given intravenously has a bioavailability of 100%; drugs given by other routes, such as orally, usually have lower bioavailability because of incomplete absorption and first-pass metabolism in the liver.

Measurement

Absolute bioavailability is determined by comparing the area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC) after a non-intravenous dose with the AUC after an intravenous dose of the same drug, corrected for dose. Relative bioavailability compares one formulation with another (for example, a generic tablet with a reference product), a key measure in bioequivalence studies.

Factors affecting bioavailability

Oral bioavailability depends on dissolution of the dosage form, stability in gastrointestinal fluids, transport across the intestinal wall, efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein, and metabolism by gut-wall and hepatic enzymes (first-pass effect). Food, gastric pH, disease states, and interacting drugs can all alter it. Prodrugs — compounds converted to the active drug in the body — are sometimes designed specifically to improve bioavailability.

Nutritional usage

In nutrition science, bioavailability refers more loosely to the proportion of a nutrient that is absorbed and available for use or storage, which for dietary supplements and foods is rarely quantified with the precision used for drugs.
