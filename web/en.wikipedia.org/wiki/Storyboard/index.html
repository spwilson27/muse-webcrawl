Storyboard

A storyboard is a graphic organiser consisting of a sequence of drawings or images displayed in order for the purpose of pre-visualising a motion picture, animation, interactive media sequence or television production. Like a comic strip, each panel represents a shot or scene, usually accompanied by notes about dialogue, camera movement and action.

The storyboarding process was developed at the Walt Disney studio in the early 1930s, where artists such as Webb Smith pinned sequential sketches to boards to plan animated shorts. The practice spread to live-action filmmaking — David Lean, Alfred Hitchcock and Orson Welles were notable users — and Hitchcock's films were storyboarded so thoroughly that he claimed the finished film was already complete in his head before shooting.

Storyboards help directors, producers and clients agree on a production's visual plan before expensive shooting begins, revealing problems in staging or continuity early. Today storyboarding is also used in advertising, video game design, software user-experience design and business presentations, and is often produced digitally as "animatics" combining panels with sound and rough timing.
