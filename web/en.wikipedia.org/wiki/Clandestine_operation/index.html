Clandestine operation

A clandestine operation is an intelligence or military operation carried out in such a way that the operation itself goes unnoticed by the target population and general public. It differs from a covert operation, in which the operation is known to occur but the identity of the sponsor is concealed or plausibly deniable. Clandestine operations emphasise concealment of the activity itself; the United States intelligence community formally distinguishes "clandestine" services, such as the CIA's former National Clandestine Service, from covert action authorised under statute.

In a clandestine operation, the activity itself — not merely its sponsor — is hidden.

Meaning and usage

The terms clandestine and covert are often used interchangeably in popular writing, but intelligence doctrine historically separates them: a clandestine operation hides the act, while a covert operation hides the actor. An espionage network collecting information in secret is clandestine; a propaganda campaign of unattributed content is covert. Operations may be both, and exposure can turn a clandestine activity into an overt scandal, as with the exposure of spy networks or secret detention sites.

Examples

Historical examples of clandestine activity include the clandestine HUMINT networks run by intelligence services during the Second World War and the Cold War, secret reconnaissance flights, and clandestine stay-behind networks organised in Western Europe after 1945. Clandestine cell systems are also used by insurgent, terrorist and organised-crime groups to compartmentalise members and protect the wider group when a cell is compromised.

Law

Under international humanitarian law, espionage is not prohibited but spies captured while engaged in espionage may be denied prisoner-of-war status; domestic law in most states criminalises espionage by foreign agents while authorising their own services under oversight frameworks.
