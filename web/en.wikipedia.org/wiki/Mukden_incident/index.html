Mukden incident
Date18 September 1931
LocationNear Mukden (Shenyang), Manchuria, China
PerpetratorsOfficers of the Japanese Kwantung Army
OutcomeJapanese invasion of Manchuria; creation of Manchukuo

The Mukden incident, also known as the Manchurian incident, was a false flag event staged by Japanese military personnel on 18 September 1931 as a pretext for the invasion of Manchuria. A small quantity of dynamite was detonated near a section of the Japanese-owned South Manchuria Railway outside Mukden (now Shenyang); the explosion was so weak that a train passed over the track minutes later, but the Imperial Japanese Army accused Chinese dissidents of the act and responded with a full invasion.

Background

Japan had held extensive rights in Manchuria since the Russo-Japanese War, guarded by the Kwantung Army. Amid economic crisis and rising militarism, Kwantung Army officers — notably Colonel Seishirō Itagaki and Lieutenant Colonel Kanji Ishiwara — plotted to provoke a conflict that would let Japan seize Manchuria outright, acting without orders from Tokyo.

Events and aftermath

Within hours of the explosion, Japanese forces attacked the Chinese barracks at Beidaying and occupied Mukden; within five months the whole of Manchuria was under Japanese control, despite the civilian government's attempts to limit the escalation. In February 1932 Japan installed the puppet state of Manchukuo under Puyi, the last Qing emperor.

The League of Nations' Lytton Report (1932) concluded that Japan was the aggressor; Japan responded by withdrawing from the League in 1933. The incident is widely regarded as a milestone on the road to the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Pacific War. In China, 18 September is commemorated as a day of national humiliation, and air-raid sirens sound annually in Shenyang and other cities.
