Eighty Years' War

The Eighty Years' War (1566/1568–1648), also known as the Dutch Revolt or the Dutch War of Independence, was an armed conflict in the Low Countries between rebellious provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands and the Spanish Empire. It ended with the Peace of Münster in 1648, in which Spain recognised the independence of the Dutch Republic.

Origins

Discontent grew under Philip II of Spain over centralisation, taxation, and the persecution of Protestants. In 1566 unrest culminated in the Beeldenstorm, a wave of iconoclasm. Philip sent the Duke of Alba with an army in 1567; Alba's repression, through the "Council of Troubles," drove many into revolt under William of Orange.

Course of the war

The capture of Brielle by the Sea Beggars in 1572 turned revolt into open war. Spanish victories in the south were balanced by Dutch resistance in the waterlogged provinces of Holland and Zeeland, marked by sieges such as those of Leiden (1574) and Antwerp (1584–85). In 1579 the southern provinces formed the Union of Arras and the northern ones the Union of Utrecht; in 1581 the north issued the Act of Abjuration, declaring Philip deposed. The war was interrupted by the Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621), after which fighting resumed as part of the wider Thirty Years' War. Under stadtholders Maurice and Frederick Henry of Orange, the Republic expanded and prospered, entering its Golden Age.

Outcome

The Peace of Münster, part of the Peace of Westphalia, confirmed the Dutch Republic as an independent state, while the Southern Netherlands remained Spanish. The division of the Low Countries laid the basis for the modern states of the Netherlands and Belgium.
