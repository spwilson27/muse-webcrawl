Clarence Seward Darrow (April 18, 1857 – March 13, 1938) was an American lawyer who became famous in the 19th century for high-profile representations of trade unions, and in the 20th century for several renowned cases, including the Leopold and Loeb murder trial, the Scopes "Monkey" Trial, and the Ossian Sweet defense. He was a leading member of the American Civil Liberties Union and a prominent advocate against the death penalty and for labor rights, economic justice, and agnosticism.

Early life and career

Darrow was born in the small town of Farmdale, Ohio, the fifth of eight children. He attended Allegheny College and the University of Michigan Law School, though he left law school after a year to read law on his own, and was admitted to the Ohio bar in 1878. He moved to Chicago in 1887 and rose to prominence as a corporate lawyer for the Chicago and North Western Railway before resigning in 1894 to defend Eugene V. Debs, leader of the American Railway Union, in the aftermath of the Pullman Strike.

Labor lawyer

Darrow built his reputation defending labor unions and their members, including the Western Federation of Miners in the 1907 trial of Bill Haywood, Harry Orchard, and George Pettibone, who were accused of murdering former Idaho governor Frank Steunenberg; all were acquitted. In 1911 he defended the McNamara brothers in the Los Angeles Times bombing case, pleading them guilty to avoid death sentences, and was himself twice tried for jury bribery; the first trial ended in acquittal and the second in a hung jury.

Famous criminal cases

In 1924, Darrow defended teenage thrill killers Nathan Leopold and Richard Loeb in Chicago, delivering a celebrated twelve-hour closing argument that persuaded the judge to sentence them to life imprisonment rather than death. In 1925, he defended schoolteacher John T. Scopes in Dayton, Tennessee, prosecuted for teaching evolution; the trial became a national spectacle pitting Darrow against prosecutor William Jennings Bryan, whom Darrow cross-examined on the Bible. Scopes was convicted, though the verdict was later overturned on a technicality. Later that year and in 1926, Darrow defended Dr. Ossian Sweet and his family in Detroit on murder charges arising from self-defense against a white mob, winning acquittals.

Later years and legacy

A noted orator and debater, Darrow wrote an autobiography, The Story of My Life (1932). He died in Chicago in 1938. His life inspired the play and film Inherit the Wind. Darrow is remembered as one of the most famous American trial lawyers and civil libertarians.
