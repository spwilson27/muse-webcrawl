The Leluh ruins are the remains of a prehistoric and early historic stone city on Lelu Island, off the island of Kosrae in the Federated States of Micronesia. Built on some 30 artificial islets in the lagoon, the complex of walled compounds once housed the paramount chiefs and nobility of Kosrae, and forms — together with Nan Madol on Pohnpei — one of the two great stone cities of Micronesia.

Description

The city occupies about 27 hectares (67 acres) of reef flat, connected by canals that served as streets for canoes. Its compounds are enclosed by walls of stacked basalt, often in double courses filled with coral rubble, in places rising 4–5 metres. Within the walls stood dwellings, royal tombs, meeting houses, and sacred precincts; the royal compound known as Insru included feast houses and burial platforms. At its peak around the 17th century, Leluh probably housed about 1,500 people, the elite of an island population many times larger.

History and abandonment

Construction in stone began around 1250 CE and intensified over the following centuries as Kosraean society became one of the most stratified in the Pacific, ruled by a single king (Tokosra) believed to be divine. European contact from the 1820s brought trade but also devastating disease; by the 1850s the population collapsed and the city was abandoned, its surviving inhabitants concentrating in other settlements. Today the ruins lie within Lelu village, partly overgrown and partly incorporated into gardens, and are managed as an archaeological site by the Kosrae State Historic Preservation Office.
