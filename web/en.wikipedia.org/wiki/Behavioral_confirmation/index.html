Behavioral confirmation is a type of self-fulfilling prophecy in which a person's expectations about another person lead them to act in ways that cause the target to behave in expectation-confirming ways. The perceiver's initially false belief thus becomes true through the social interaction itself.

The process

Researchers typically describe four steps: (1) the perceiver forms an impression or expectation of the target; (2) the perceiver treats the target on the basis of that expectation; (3) the target adjusts their behaviour in response to that treatment; and (4) the perceiver interprets the target's behaviour as confirmation of the original expectation, overlooking its situational cause.

Classic research

In the best-known demonstration, Snyder, Tanke and Berscheid (1977) had male participants talk by telephone with women they believed — based on a photograph — to be physically attractive or unattractive. Independent raters, hearing only the women's side of the conversations, judged the women believed to be attractive as warmer, more sociable and more poised: the men's expectations had elicited confirming behaviour. Related work covers teacher expectations (the Pygmalion effect, Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968), expectations about race, gender, mental illness and social class, and confirmation in job interviews and clinical settings.

Moderators and accuracy

Behavioral confirmation is not inevitable. It is weaker when perceivers are motivated to be accurate, when targets have strong self-conceptions they assert in the interaction, and when interactions are long or repeated. Jussim and others note that social expectations are often more accurate than biasing, and that expectancy effects in natural settings are usually modest — a point of ongoing debate in social psychology.
