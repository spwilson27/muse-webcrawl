Humorism

Humorism, or humoralism, was a system of medicine in which health and disease were explained by the balance of four bodily fluids, or humours: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Developed in ancient Greek medicine and systematised by Hippocrates and Galen, it dominated Western medical thought for more than two thousand years and also shaped Ayurvedic and Unani traditions.

Theory

Each humour was associated with a temperament (sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, melancholic), an element, a season and qualities of hot, cold, wet and dry. Illness resulted from an excess or deficiency of one humour, and treatments — bloodletting, purging, emetics and dietary regimens — aimed to restore balance.

Decline

Humorism was gradually displaced from the sixteenth century onward by anatomical and experimental medicine, and finally abandoned in the nineteenth century with the rise of germ theory and cellular pathology, though its vocabulary survives in words like "melancholy" and "good humour".
