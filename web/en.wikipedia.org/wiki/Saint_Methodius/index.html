Saint Methodius

Saint Methodius of Thessalonica (c. 815 – 6 April 885) was a Byzantine Greek monk and missionary, the elder brother of Saint Cyril. Together, the brothers — honoured as the "Apostles to the Slavs" — evangelised the Slavic peoples of Central and Eastern Europe, devised the Glagolitic alphabet to write the Slavonic language, and translated the Bible and liturgy into what became Old Church Slavonic.

Early life

Methodius was born in Thessalonica, in present-day Greece, the son of a Byzantine military officer. Trained in law and administration, he became governor (archon) of a Slav-inhabited district of the empire, gaining the fluent knowledge of Slavic that later proved decisive. He subsequently withdrew to a monastery on Mount Olympus in Bithynia, where his younger brother Constantine (Cyril) also spent time.

Missions

Around 860 the brothers were sent on a mission to the Khazars. In 862, Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia asked Constantinople for teachers who could instruct his people in their own language; the emperor and the patriarch Photios chose Cyril and Methodius, who prepared the Glagolitic script and a Slavonic translation of the Gospels and liturgy. Their use of the vernacular brought them into conflict with Frankish clergy who insisted on Latin, and the brothers travelled to Rome, where Pope Adrian II approved the Slavonic liturgy. Cyril died in Rome in 869. Methodius was consecrated archbishop of Moravia and Pannonia and returned north, enduring imprisonment by his Frankish rivals (870–873) before resuming his work. After his death in 885, his disciples were expelled from Moravia and carried the Slavonic tradition to Bulgaria, Serbia and Rus'.

Legacy

Pope John Paul II named Cyril and Methodius co-patrons of Europe in 1980. Their feast is a public holiday in several Slavic countries, and the Cyrillic script — developed by their disciples in Bulgaria — is named in Cyril's honour.
