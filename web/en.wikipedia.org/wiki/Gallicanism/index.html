Gallicanism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gallicanism is the belief that popular civil authority — often represented by the monarch's or the state's authority over the Catholic Church — is comparable to that of the Pope. Gallicanism is a rejection of ultramontanism; it has parallels in Anglicanism and is a form of conciliarism. The idea originated in France (the former Gaul, or Gallia, from which the term derives) and spread to the Low Countries, especially in the Catholic University of Leuven debates about Jansenism.

Principles

Its classic statement is the Declaration of the Clergy of France of 1682, drawn up under Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, which set out four Gallican Articles: (1) popes have no authority over temporal matters or kings; (2) general councils are above the pope, per the Council of Constance; (3) papal authority is limited by the ancient canons and the customs of the Gallican Church; (4) papal judgments in matters of faith are not irreformable without the consent of the Church.

History

Gallican claims rested on older precedents: the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges (1438), the "liberties of the Gallican Church", and the king's right of régale — collecting revenues of vacant sees and nominating to benefices. The 1682 Declaration under Louis XIV was later formally revoked for diplomatic reasons with Rome (1693) but remained influential teaching in French seminaries. The First Vatican Council's definition of papal infallibility (1870) dealt Gallicanism a decisive blow, though its conciliar and national-church legacy fed into the Old Catholic schism and later conciliar theology.
