Motif (music)

In music, a motif (also motive) is a short musical idea — a distinctive rhythmic, melodic or harmonic figure — that recurs in a composition and serves as a building block for its thematic development. A motif is typically only a few notes long, shorter than a full melody or theme, but it carries a recognizable identity that allows it to be repeated, varied and developed across a work.

Characteristics

A motif is defined by some combination of interval, rhythm and contour that makes it identifiable even when transposed, inverted, augmented or otherwise transformed. Composers exploit these transformations to generate large-scale musical structures from very small amounts of material, a technique central to the classical sonata form and to the idea of "developing variation" described by Arnold Schoenberg in his writing on Johannes Brahms.

Famous examples

The most famous motif in Western music is arguably the four-note opening of Ludwig van Beethoven's Fifth Symphony (short–short–short–long), which pervades the entire first movement and returns throughout the work. In opera, Richard Wagner's system of leitmotifs attached short motifs to particular characters, places and ideas, weaving them into a continuous symphonic fabric. Motifs are equally fundamental in other traditions and genres, from the short repeated riffs of rock and jazz to the ostinato patterns of minimalist music.

Motif and theme

A theme is a more complete, self-contained musical statement, often a full melody, whereas a motif is a fragment — a "seed" from which themes can be grown. A single theme may be built from one motif or from several combined; conversely, analysis frequently reduces complex themes to the small set of motifs from which they derive.
