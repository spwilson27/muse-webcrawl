Diplomatic corps

The diplomatic corps (French: corps diplomatique) is the collective body of foreign diplomats accredited to a particular country or body. It is understood mainly as the group of heads of diplomatic missions — ambassadors, high commissioners, nuncios and chargés d'affaires — resident in a capital city, although the term may extend to all diplomatic staff of the embassies there. The diplomatic corps functions collectively on ceremonial occasions and may act together, through its dean, on matters affecting the status and privileges of all missions.

Dean of the diplomatic corps

The senior member of the corps — the longest-serving head of mission in the capital — is its dean or doyen, who represents the corps on ceremonial occasions, speaks for it on questions of privilege and protocol, and traditionally greets the head of state on the corps' behalf, for example at New Year receptions. In many Catholic countries the papal nuncio is automatically recognised as dean regardless of seniority, a custom acknowledged in the Vienna Convention's rules on precedence. In the United Kingdom the dean is the longest-serving ambassador or high commissioner in London.

Functions

Although diplomats individually represent their own governments, the corps has collective functions: it attends state ceremonies as a body, ranks by precedence in official processions, and may make joint representations to the host foreign ministry about security, taxation or treatment of missions. The host state's protocol department maintains the official diplomatic list recording all accredited members and their order of precedence.

History

The concept emerged with the system of resident ambassadors that developed in Renaissance Italy and spread through Europe; the Congress of Vienna (1815) first codified the classes and precedence of diplomatic agents, rules later superseded by the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations of 1961, which remains the governing treaty for the corps in nearly every country.
