Cannula

A cannula (from Latin cannula, "little reed") is a thin tube inserted into a vein, artery or body cavity to administer medication or fluids, withdraw fluid, or keep a passage open. Cannulas are among the most commonly used medical devices in hospitals worldwide.

Intravenous cannulas

The peripheral intravenous (IV) cannula consists of a short flexible plastic tube mounted over a needle. The needle is used to puncture a vein, the tube is advanced into the vessel, and the needle is withdrawn, leaving the tube in place for infusions or injections. Sizes are measured by gauge and colour-coded internationally — for example, 14G (orange), 18G (green), 20G (pink) and 22G (blue) — with smaller gauge numbers indicating wider bores for faster flow.

Other types

  • Nasal cannula — prongs resting in the nostrils that deliver supplemental oxygen.
  • Tracheostomy cannula — the tube maintaining an airway through a tracheostomy.
  • Surgical cannulas — used in liposuction, arthroscopy and laparoscopic ports.
  • Arterial cannula — for blood pressure monitoring and sampling.

Complications

Insertion can cause pain, bruising and infection; cannulas left in place may lead to phlebitis, infiltration of fluid into surrounding tissue, or blockage. Modern practice emphasises aseptic insertion and timely removal or replacement.
