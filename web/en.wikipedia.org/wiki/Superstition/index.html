Superstition

A superstition is any belief or practice considered irrational or supernatural by the surrounding society, typically involving attributions of events to luck, fate, magical influences, or omens, without a plausible causal mechanism. Common examples include lucky charms, the belief that Friday the 13th is unlucky, avoiding walking under ladders, knocking on wood, and interpreting the appearance of certain animals as omens.

Definitions and boundaries

The label is relative and often contested: what one culture calls superstition another may regard as religion, tradition, or folk wisdom. Historically, religious authorities applied the term (from the Latin superstitio) to rival or excessive religious practices, and Enlightenment thinkers extended it to beliefs they considered contrary to reason and science. Modern psychologists generally define superstition functionally, as beliefs in causal relationships that are not supported by evidence.

Psychological explanations

Research in psychology links superstitious behaviour to the human tendency to detect patterns and agency, to illusions of control, and to reinforcement learning from coincidental outcomes — classically demonstrated by B. F. Skinner's 1948 experiments in which pigeons developed ritual behaviours "reinforced" by food delivered on a fixed schedule regardless of their actions. Anxiety, uncertainty, and high-stakes situations increase superstitious behaviour, which is why athletes, gamblers, sailors, soldiers, and performers are noted for elaborate personal rituals. Some experiments suggest lucky charms and rituals can genuinely improve performance by raising confidence and self-efficacy.

Cultural variation

Superstitions differ sharply across cultures: the number 4 is avoided in much of East Asia (tetraphobia) while 13 is feared in the West; black cats are unlucky in some countries and lucky in others; and practices such as throwing salt over the shoulder, reading omens, or consulting horoscopes persist alongside modern science in most societies.
