Smartglasses are wearable computing devices worn like eyeglasses that add information to, or capture data from, the wearer's field of view. Most models combine some subset of an optical head-mounted display (OHMD), camera, microphones, speakers, wireless connectivity and sensors, and are controlled by voice, touchpads or companion smartphones. They are a major form factor for augmented reality (AR).

History

Research devices date to Ivan Sutherland's 1968 head-mounted display and Steve Mann's wearable-computing rigs of the 1980s–90s. The category entered public consciousness with Google Glass, unveiled in 2012 and sold to "Explorers" from 2013: its small prism display and always-available camera provoked a privacy backlash (wearers were dubbed "glassholes") and bans in some venues; Google ended consumer sales in 2015, refocused on an Enterprise Edition (2017–2023), and discontinued the line.

Microsoft's HoloLens (2016) and HoloLens 2 (2019) targeted industry and medicine with full spatial AR. Camera-first glasses without displays — Snap Spectacles (2016) and Ray-Ban Stories (2021), succeeded by the Ray-Ban Meta line (2023) with an onboard AI assistant — achieved wider consumer acceptance. Display-equipped AR glasses from firms such as Xreal and Rokid, and prototypes like Meta's Orion (2024), continue the push toward everyday AR eyewear.

Uses and concerns

In enterprise settings smartglasses support warehouse picking, remote expert assistance, surgical visualisation and maintenance checklists. Consumer uses include hands-free photos and video, navigation, live translation and AI assistants. Persistent concerns include bystander privacy, recording consent laws, distraction and driving safety, and social acceptability — issues that have shaped both product design (recording indicator lights) and regulation.
