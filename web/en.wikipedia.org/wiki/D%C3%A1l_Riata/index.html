Dál Riata (also Dalriada or Dalriata) was a Gaelic kingdom that encompassed the western seaboard of Scotland and north-eastern Ireland in the early medieval period, roughly the 6th to 8th centuries. Its people, the Scoti, gave their name eventually to Scotland itself.

Geography and structure

The kingdom straddled the North Channel of the Irish Sea: in Ireland it held territory in County Antrim, and in Britain it covered Argyll, the Isle of Bute and the southern Inner Hebrides. The Scottish royal centre was the hillfort of Dunadd near the Crinan Canal. The kingdom was divided among kindreds (cenéla), including the Cenél nGabráin and Cenél Loairn, which competed for the overkingship.

History

Later tradition held that Fergus Mór led a migration from Ireland around 500 CE, though historians now debate how much was migration versus cultural diffusion. In 563, the Irish monk Columba founded the monastery of Iona under Dál Riata's protection, which became a great centre of learning and missionary activity. King Áedán mac Gabráin (r. c. 574–608) campaigned widely but was defeated at Degsastan in 603. The kingdom's power waned under pressure from the Picts and Vikings, and in the 9th century its king Kenneth MacAlpin is traditionally credited with uniting Scots and Picts to form the Kingdom of Alba.

Sources

Knowledge of Dál Riata derives from sources such as Bede's Ecclesiastical History, the Irish annals, Adomnán's Life of Columba, and the Senchus fer n-Alban, a census and genealogy of the kingdom.
