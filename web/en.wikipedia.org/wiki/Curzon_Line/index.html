The Curzon Line was a demarcation line proposed in 1919–1920 as the eastern border of the newly re-established Poland. It was put forward during the Polish–Soviet War in a note of 11 July 1920 from the British Foreign Secretary, Lord Curzon, to the Soviet government, proposing an armistice line roughly following the ethnic boundary between Polish-speaking populations to the west and Ukrainian and Belarusian populations to the east. The line ran from the Baltic Sea southward along the Bug River toward the Carpathians, with two variants (A and B) differing around Lwów (Lviv) and the Galician oilfields.

History

The line had first been sketched by the Allied Supreme Council in December 1919 as a provisional eastern frontier for Poland. The Soviet side ultimately rejected the armistice proposal, and the Treaty of Riga (1921) fixed Poland's border some 250 km east of the Curzon Line, incorporating large Ukrainian and Belarusian populations into interwar Poland.

Second World War

The line acquired lasting significance during the Second World War. The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact's secret protocol (1939) divided Poland roughly along the line, and at the Tehran (1943) and Yalta (1945) conferences the Allies agreed that Poland's eastern border should follow the Curzon Line, with minor deviations in Poland's favour, while Poland was compensated with German territory in the west (the Oder–Neisse line). Millions of Poles were expelled from the eastern territories (Kresy) annexed by the USSR, and Ukrainians and others were moved in the opposite direction. Modern Poland's borders with Lithuania, Belarus and Ukraine still substantially follow the Curzon Line.
