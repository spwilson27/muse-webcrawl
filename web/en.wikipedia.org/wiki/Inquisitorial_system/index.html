Inquisitorial system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An inquisitorial system is a legal system in which the court, or a part of the court, is actively involved in investigating the facts of the case. It is the standard criminal procedure of civil-law countries, descended from Roman-canon procedure and codified after the French Revolution in instruments such as the Code of Criminal Instruction of 1808. A judge or examining magistrate supervises the gathering of evidence into a dossier, questions witnesses and the accused, and (in bench trials) decides guilt; the trial is conceived as an official inquiry into the truth rather than a contest between parties.

Defenders say inquisitorial procedure is better at finding facts, less dependent on the parties' wealth and forensic skill, and less prone to theatrical advocacy; critics in the common-law world reply that it risks confirmation bias when investigator and judge coincide, weakens the defence, and relies on written dossiers over live testimony. Modern systems are hybrids: France retains the juge d'instruction only for serious cases, Germany abolished the examining magistrate in 1975 in favour of prosecutorial investigation with judicial controls, and Italy moved in 1988 toward an adversarial model.
