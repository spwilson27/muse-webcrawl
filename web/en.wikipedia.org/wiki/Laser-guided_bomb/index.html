A laser-guided bomb (LGB) is a guided bomb that uses semi-active laser guidance to strike a designated target with greater accuracy than an unguided bomb. First developed by the United States during the Vietnam War, laser-guided bombs quickly proved their value: missions flown with LGBs achieved a far higher probability of destroying hard targets such as bridges than the same aircraft dropping unguided bombs.

Operation

An LGB is typically a standard general-purpose bomb fitted with a guidance kit: a laser seeker mounted on the nose and movable control canards, plus tail fins for stability. A designator — on the launching aircraft, another aircraft, or a ground team — illuminates the target with a coded laser pulse train. The seeker detects the reflected laser energy and commands the control surfaces to steer the bomb's glide toward the spot of reflected light. The bomb is unpowered and follows a ballistic-glide trajectory.

History

The first successful combat use came in 1968 with the BOLT-117, and the Paveway series followed in Vietnam, famously destroying the Thanh Hóa Bridge in 1972 after years of failed attempts with conventional weapons. LGBs were used extensively by coalition forces in the 1991 Gulf War, where they formed a small fraction of munitions dropped but struck a disproportionate number of strategic targets. Successive Paveway generations added improved seekers, and later weapons combined laser guidance with GPS/INS in dual-mode packages.

Limitations

Laser guidance requires a clear line of sight between the designator and target throughout the bomb's fall; cloud, smoke, dust, and rain can absorb or scatter the laser energy and degrade accuracy. The designating aircraft or ground team must also maintain the designation, which may expose it to enemy fire.
