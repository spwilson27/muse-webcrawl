Fundamental group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In algebraic topology, the fundamental group of a topological space X, written π1(X), is the group of equivalence classes of loops in X — paths that start and end at a chosen base point — where two loops are equivalent if one can be continuously deformed into the other (a homotopy). The group operation is concatenation of loops. Introduced by Henri Poincaré in 1895, it was the first and remains the most basic invariant of algebraic topology: it detects "holes" around which loops can wind.

Loops on a torus: a loop encircling the hole cannot be shrunk to a point, and π1 of the torus is ℤ × ℤ.

Examples

A space in which every loop can be contracted to a point is called simply connected and has trivial fundamental group; Euclidean space and the 2-sphere are examples. The fundamental group of the circle is the integers ℤ, each loop being classified by its winding number. The torus has π1 = ℤ × ℤ, while a figure-eight (wedge of two circles) has the free group on two generators. The real projective plane has π1 = ℤ/2ℤ, and knot complements in 3-space have rich, non-abelian fundamental groups whose study forms a large part of knot theory.

Properties

The fundamental group is a topological invariant: homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic fundamental groups, and continuous maps between spaces induce homomorphisms between their groups, making π1 a functor. The Seifert–van Kampen theorem computes it for spaces built from simpler pieces. Abelianising the fundamental group yields the first homology group H1. Covering spaces of a space correspond precisely to subgroups of its fundamental group — a correspondence at the heart of the theory — and a space has a simply connected universal cover exactly when it is reasonably well behaved locally.
