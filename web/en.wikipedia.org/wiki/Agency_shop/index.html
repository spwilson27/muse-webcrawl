Agency shop

In an agency shop, non-members pay fees for the representation they receive (image placeholder)

An agency shop is a form of union security agreement under which employees in a bargaining unit need not join the trade union, but must pay the union a fee — often called an agency fee or fair-share fee — to cover the cost of collective bargaining and representation from which they benefit. The arrangement aims to solve the "free rider" problem: where a union is legally obliged to represent all workers in a unit, non-members would otherwise receive union-negotiated wages and grievance handling without contributing.

Comparison with other arrangements

The agency shop sits between the closed shop (only union members may be hired; illegal in the US since 1947) and the union shop (workers must join after hiring), and the open shop, where no payments are required. In the United States, "right-to-work" laws in many states prohibit agency fees in the private sector.

Legal history

US courts long upheld agency fees while restricting their use to chargeable representational activities rather than political spending (Abood v. Detroit Board of Education, 1977). In Janus v. American Federation of State, County, and Municipal Employees (2018), the Supreme Court overruled Abood, holding that compulsory agency fees for public-sector employees violate the First Amendment. Canada follows a different model: the Rand formula (1946) makes dues check-off for all bargaining-unit members a standard of labour law.
