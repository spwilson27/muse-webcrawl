Amakusa Shirō

Amakusa Shirō (天草 四郎, c. 1621 – 12 April 1638) was the charismatic teenage leader of the Shimabara Rebellion, an uprising of Japanese peasants and rōnin — many of them Christian converts — against the Tokugawa shogunate in 1637–38. Born Masuda Shirō, the son of a former retainer of the Christian daimyō Konishi Yukinaga, he was regarded by followers as a prophesied saviour figure. After the rebellion's defeat he was executed at the age of about sixteen or seventeen.

Background

The Shimabara Peninsula and the Amakusa Islands had large populations of Kirishitan (Christian) peasants, who after the expulsion of their Christian lords were subjected to heavy taxation and persecution under Matsukura Katsuie and Terazawa Katataka. Famine and brutal tax collection in 1637 sparked revolt.

Rebellion

Shirō, famed locally for his learning, eloquence, and reputed holiness, was proclaimed the rebels' leader. The rebels seized the ruined Hara Castle and withstood a siege by a shogunal army of over 100,000 men for months. Starved into weakness, the castle fell in April 1638; Shirō was captured and beheaded, his head displayed in Nagasaki. Some 27,000–37,000 rebels and sympathisers were killed.

Legacy

The rebellion was the last major internal conflict of the Edo period and helped prompt the shogunate's final enforcement of national seclusion (sakoku) and the expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639. Shirō remains a popular figure in Japanese fiction, manga, and film, often portrayed as a tragic, saintly youth.
