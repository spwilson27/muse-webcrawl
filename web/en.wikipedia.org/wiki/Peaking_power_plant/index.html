Peaking power plants, also known as peaker plants, are power plants that generally run only when there is a high demand, known as peak demand, for electricity. Because they supply power only occasionally, the power supplied commands a much higher price per kilowatt hour than base load power. Peak load periods may coincide with heat waves, cold snaps, or the early-evening ramp when solar generation fades.

Characteristics

Peaker plants are designed to start quickly, to ramp output rapidly, and to be cheap to build rather than efficient to run. Their capacity factors are very low — often below 10 per cent — and because they run so few hours, fuel efficiency is less economically important than in base load plants. Typical peakers are simple-cycle combustion turbines fuelled by natural gas or oil, which can reach full output within about ten minutes, reciprocating engine plants, and conventional hydroelectric and pumped-storage stations that can dispatch stored water on demand.

Economics and criticism

In energy-only electricity markets, peakers recover their fixed costs from the very high prices of scarcity hours, or receive explicit capacity payments. Peakers are frequently criticised on environmental grounds: they are among the least efficient and most carbon-intensive generators, and older gas and oil peakers, often located in or near low-income urban communities, have become focal points of environmental justice campaigns. Grid-scale batteries, which can discharge instantaneously at peak times and charge during off-peak or solar-surplus hours, are increasingly displacing new gas peakers, particularly in California, Australia, and other solar-rich systems.

Contrast with other plants

Peakers sit at the top of the dispatch order: base load plants (nuclear, large coal) run almost constantly; load-following or intermediate plants (combined-cycle gas, hydro) adjust output daily; and peakers operate only at the extremes of demand.
