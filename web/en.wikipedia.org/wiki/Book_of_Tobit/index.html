The Book of Tobit is a deuterocanonical book of Jewish religious fiction, included in the canons of the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches and classed as Apocrypha in Protestant tradition. Written probably in the 3rd or 2nd century BCE, it tells the story of Tobit, a righteous Israelite exiled in Nineveh who is blinded, and of his son Tobias, who journeys to Media accompanied — unknowingly — by the archangel Raphael. Along the way Tobias marries Sarah, whose seven previous husbands were killed by the demon Asmodeus, and returns home with a cure for his father's eyes.

Fragments of the book in Aramaic and Hebrew found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran confirmed its Semitic origin and strengthened its standing in Catholic and Orthodox tradition. The narrative combines folktale motifs — the grateful dead, the monstrous fish, the disguised divine guide — with sustained instruction on piety, almsgiving, prayer, burial of the dead, and endogamous marriage.

Summary

Tobit, a devout man of the tribe of Naphtali deported to Nineveh, loses his property for burying executed Jews and is blinded when sparrow droppings fall into his eyes. In despair he prays for death; at the same moment, in Ecbatana, Sarah prays likewise after her seventh husband dies on the wedding night, slain by the demon Asmodeus. Tobit sends his son Tobias to recover a deposit of silver left with Gabael in Rages, and Tobias hires a guide — Raphael in human form.

On the journey a great fish leaps from the Tigris; Raphael instructs Tobias to preserve its heart, liver, and gall. The fumes of the burning heart and liver drive Asmodeus away on Tobias's wedding night, and the gall, applied to Tobit's eyes, restores his sight. Raphael then reveals his identity — "I am Raphael, one of the seven angels who stand ready and enter before the glory of the Lord" (12:15) — and Tobit sings a hymn prophesying Jerusalem's restoration.

Canon and texts

The book survives in two Greek recensions, a shorter one (Codex Vaticanus) and a longer one (Codex Sinaiticus) now generally regarded as closer to the original. Jerome translated it into Latin for the Vulgate, reportedly in a single day, while noting that it was not in the Hebrew canon. The Council of Trent (1546) affirmed it as canonical for Catholics; the Orthodox churches likewise receive it. The Qumran fragments (4Q196–200) preserve portions in Aramaic and Hebrew.

Themes and teachings

Tobit's farewell discourse (chapter 4) is among the earliest statements of the Golden Rule in negative form: "What you hate, do to no one." The book strongly commends almsgiving ("almsgiving delivers from death," 12:9), reverent burial, fasting, and prayer, and presents angels as mediators of human petitions. Its happy ending contrasts with more sombre wisdom books: here, righteousness is vindicated within the characters' lifetimes.

In art

Tobias and the Angel was a much-loved subject in Renaissance painting — treated by Filippino Lippi, Botticini, Verrocchio's workshop, and Rembrandt — often showing the youthful Tobias holding the fish beside his tall companion. Scenes of the healing of Tobit's blindness also appear frequently in Dutch Golden Age art.
