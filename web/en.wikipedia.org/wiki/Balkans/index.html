Balkans


The Balkan Peninsula in southeastern Europe.

The Balkans (also the Balkan Peninsula) is a geographical area in southeastern Europe with various geographical and historical definitions. The region takes its name from the Balkan Mountains (Stara Planina) that stretch throughout the whole of Bulgaria. The peninsula is bordered by the Adriatic Sea in the northwest, the Ionian Sea in the southwest, the Aegean Sea in the south, the Turkish straits in the east, and the Black Sea in the northeast. Its northern boundary is often given as the Danube, Sava, and Kupa rivers.

The Balkan Peninsula covers roughly 466,877 square kilometres and is home to some 55–60 million people, depending on the definition used. The highest point is Musala in the Rila Mountains of Bulgaria, at 2,925 metres.

Countries

Countries wholly or predominantly within the Balkans include Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Greece, Kosovo, Montenegro, North Macedonia, and Serbia; Croatia, Romania, Slovenia, Moldova, and Turkey (its European part, East Thrace) are included wholly or partly under broader definitions.

History

The region was one of the earliest in Europe to be settled by farming peoples in the Neolithic. In antiquity it was home to Illyrians, Thracians, Dacians, and the Greek city-states, and was absorbed by Macedon and then by the Roman Empire. Slavic peoples settled from the sixth century AD. For centuries the peninsula was divided between the Byzantine Empire, successive Balkan states, and Venice, before most of it came under Ottoman rule from the fourteenth to the nineteenth century.

The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw the emergence of independent nation-states through wars against the Ottomans and each other, including the Balkan Wars of 1912–13. The assassination in Sarajevo in 1914 triggered the First World War. After 1945 most of the region fell within the communist bloc, though Yugoslavia pursued an independent, non-aligned course and Greece remained in the Western camp. The 1990s brought the violent break-up of Yugoslavia. Since then, several Balkan states have joined the European Union and NATO, while others remain candidates.

Geography and nature

The terrain is predominantly mountainous: the Dinaric Alps run along the Adriatic coast, the Pindus range through Greece, the Balkan and Rhodope mountains through Bulgaria, and the Carpathians along the north. Major rivers include the Danube, Sava, Morava, Vardar, Struma, and Maritsa. The region has significant biodiversity and large surviving populations of brown bears, wolves, and lynx.
