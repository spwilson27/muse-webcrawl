Wireless sensor network

Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) refer to networks of spatially dispersed and dedicated sensors that monitor and record the physical conditions of the environment and forward the collected data to a central location. WSNs can measure environmental conditions such as temperature, sound, pollution levels, humidity, and wind.

These networks consist of many small sensor nodes, each equipped with sensing, processing, and radio communication capability and typically powered by a battery. The nodes form a network — often a multi-hop ad hoc network — that routes observations toward one or more sink nodes or gateways.

Typical multi-hop wireless sensor network architecture

Characteristics

Sensor nodes are constrained in energy, memory, and computational power, which drives research into energy-efficient communication protocols, data aggregation, duty cycling, and routing algorithms such as LEACH. Because nodes may be deployed in inaccessible or hostile environments and cannot easily be recharged, network lifetime is a primary design concern; standards such as IEEE 802.15.4 and protocols such as Zigbee and 6LoWPAN were developed for low-power operation.

Applications

Applications include environmental and habitat monitoring, precision agriculture, industrial process monitoring, structural health monitoring of buildings and bridges, healthcare monitoring, traffic control, and military surveillance and battlefield reconnaissance. Wireless sensor networks are considered a key enabling technology of the Internet of things.

History

The field traces its origins to military programs such as DARPA's Distributed Sensor Networks program beginning around 1980, and gained momentum in the late 1990s with research platforms such as the Berkeley motes and TinyOS, which demonstrated inexpensive, low-power sensor nodes built from commercial off-the-shelf components.
