Gutmann method

The Gutmann method is an algorithm for securely erasing the contents of computer hard disk drives, such as files or free space. Devised by Peter Gutmann and Colin Plumb and presented at the USENIX Security Symposium in 1996 in the paper "Secure Deletion of Data from Magnetic and Solid-State Memory", it consists of 35 passes of overwriting patterns chosen to target the data encoding methods used by different generations of magnetic storage media.

The 35-pass scheme

The method begins with four passes of random data, followed by 27 passes of specific patterns designed for older encoding schemes such as MFM and RLL, and ends with four more random passes. The intent was that even techniques such as magnetic force microscopy would be unable to recover the original data because residual traces of every encoding type would be swamped by mismatched overwrite patterns.

Criticism and relevance

Gutmann himself later noted that most of the passes are unnecessary for modern drives, which use PRML and EPRML encoding; only a few random overwriting passes are needed. He also emphasised that the method was aimed at magnetic media and that flash-based solid-state drives behave differently, because wear levelling and over-provisioning mean overwrites may not reach the physical cells holding the data. Modern standards generally regard a single overwrite pass as sufficient for modern magnetic drives, while physical destruction or cryptographic erasure is preferred for high-assurance sanitisation and SSDs.

Implementations

The Gutmann method has been implemented in many file-shredding tools, including DBAN, Eraser, the shred utility (with multiple passes), and various disk-wiping utilities, though many have since reduced the default number of passes.
