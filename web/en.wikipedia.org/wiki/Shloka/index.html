Shloka (Sanskrit: श्लोक, śloka) is a poetic form used in Sanskrit, the archetype of which is the verse of the epics. In a wider sense the word simply means a verse or stanza. The shloka is the most common metre of classical Sanskrit poetry: a couplet of two lines (hemistichs) of sixteen syllables each, each line divided into two pādas (quarters) of eight syllables, so that the whole verse comprises 32 syllables.

Origins

Tradition attributes the invention of the shloka to the sage Vālmīki, composer of the Rāmāyaṇa. In the famous legend, Vālmīki, witnessing a hunter kill one of a pair of mating krauñca birds, uttered a curse that spontaneously came out in perfect metre; he resolved to compose his epic in the metre thus revealed. The word śloka itself is connected in that story with śoka, "grief".

Metrical form

The classical shloka is a form of the anuṣṭubh metre. Each pāda of eight syllables follows rules governing the pattern of light (laghu) and heavy (guru) syllables, with the cadence of the second and fourth pādas being the most strictly regulated (typically the fifth syllable light, sixth heavy, seventh light). Four standard cadence types (vipulā) allow controlled variation. Because the metre is relatively simple and flexible, it became the default vehicle for narrative, didactic and philosophical composition: the Mahābhārata, the Bhagavad Gītā, the Purāṇas and countless later works are written predominantly in shlokas.

Usage

Beyond literature, shlokas are the unit of daily recitation: verses from scripture, prayers, and mnemonic and technical treatises (on medicine, astronomy, grammar and law) were cast in shloka form so they could be memorised and transmitted orally. In modern usage, "shloka" commonly refers to any short scriptural verse quoted for instruction or devotion.
