Edam (Dutch: Edammer) is a semi-hard cheese that originated in the Netherlands, and is named after the town of Edam in the province of North Holland. Edam is traditionally sold in flat-ended spheres with a pale yellow interior and a coat, or rind, of red paraffin wax. Edam ages and travels well, and does not spoil; it only hardens. These qualities made it the most popular cheese in the world between the 14th and 18th centuries, both at sea and in remote colonies.

Production

The cheese is made from partly skimmed cow's milk, curdled, pressed into round moulds, brined, and matured. Mild Edam is sold young, after about four weeks; aged varieties develop a sharper, nuttier flavour. For the domestic Dutch market the wax is usually yellow or uncoloured, while the iconic red coating is used for export; black wax denotes extra-aged cheese.

Flavour and use

Young Edam is mild, slightly salty, and nutty, softening in flavour description to a firm, sliceable cheese that pairs well with fruits such as pears, apples, and cherries, and with wines including Riesling and Pinot Noir. Aged Edam is drier and crumblier and is often grated.

In culture

Edam's durability made it a staple of ships' stores and colonial trade, and it remains a common sight in markets from the Caribbean to the Philippines. Together with Gouda, it accounts for a large share of Dutch cheese production.
