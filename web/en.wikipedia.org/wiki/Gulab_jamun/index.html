Gulab jamun (also spelled gulaab jamun) is a sweet confectionery or dessert, originating in the Indian subcontinent and a type of mithai popular in India, Pakistan, Nepal, the Maldives, and Bangladesh, as well as Myanmar. It is also common in nations with substantial populations of people with South Asian heritage, such as Mauritius, Fiji, Gulf states, and the Caribbean countries of Jamaica, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, and Suriname.

Preparation

Gulab jamun is made mainly from milk solids, traditionally from khoya — milk reduced to the consistency of soft dough. In modern recipes milk powder is commonly used instead. The dough, combined with a little flour and a leavening agent, is kneaded, shaped into small balls, and deep-fried at low temperature in oil or ghee until golden brown. The hot balls are then soaked in a light sugar syrup flavoured with green cardamom, rose water, or saffron.

Etymology and history

The word gulab is derived from the Persian gol (flower) and āb (water), referring to the rose water-scented syrup; jamun is the Hindi word for the Indian fruit java plum (Syzygium cumini), likened to the dessert in size, shape, and colour. The dish is thought to have been adapted during the Mughal period from Persian and Central Asian fritter traditions such as bamiyeh, though similar recipes may predate it.

Variations

A darker, caramelised version known as kala jamun is made by frying the balls longer. In West Bengal, related sweets include pantua and ledikeni, the latter created by the confectioner Bhim Chandra Nag in honour of Lady Canning in the 19th century. Gulab jamun is traditionally served at weddings, festivals such as Diwali and Eid, and as a warm dessert, sometimes with vanilla ice cream.
