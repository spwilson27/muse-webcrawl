Curtain wall

Placeholder image: Curtain wall

A curtain wall is an exterior covering of a building in which the outer walls are non-structural, designed only to keep the weather out and occupants in. Because the curtain wall is not structural, it can be made of lightweight materials, such as glass, aluminium, or thin stone panels, reducing construction costs and weight. It transfers its own dead load and wind loads back to the building's structural frame through connections at floors or columns.

History

The development of curtain walls followed the introduction of steel and reinforced-concrete frame construction in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which freed the facade from load-bearing duty. Early examples include the Hallidie Building in San Francisco (1918), with its glass curtain facade, and postwar skyscrapers such as Lever House (1952) and the Seagram Building (1958) in New York City, which popularized the glass-and-metal grid of the International Style.

Systems

Curtain walls are commonly classified as:

  • Stick systems, assembled piece by piece on site from mullions, rails, and infill panels.
  • Unitized systems, made of factory-assembled panels that are hung from the building frame, speeding installation.

Infill materials include vision glass, spandrel glass, metal panels, stone veneer, and louvers. Systems must manage water infiltration (often via rain-screen or pressure-equalization principles), air leakage, thermal expansion, and seismic movement.

Performance requirements

Curtain wall design addresses structural integrity under wind loads, thermal efficiency, condensation resistance, fire safety (including firestopping at slab edges), sound attenuation, and maintenance access. Failures such as water leaks and glass breakage — notably the falling panes of Boston's John Hancock Tower in the 1970s — have driven advances in testing standards, such as those of ASTM and AAMA.
