The Worshipful Company of Goldsmiths, commonly known as the Goldsmiths' Company, is one of the Great Twelve livery companies of the City of London, ranking fifth in order of precedence. It received its first royal charter in 1327 and has been based at Goldsmiths' Hall in Foster Lane since 1339; the present hall, designed by Philip Hardwick and opened in 1835, is the third on the site. The company's responsibilities historically included regulating the goldsmiths' trade, and it gave its name to the hallmark: the mark struck at the Hall to certify the standard of gold and silver wares.

Hallmarking and the assay office

Since 1300, when a statute of Edward I prescribed a sterling standard for silver, the company has supervised the testing (assaying) and marking of precious-metal articles. The London Assay Office, operated by the company, remains one of the four assay offices in the United Kingdom, and its leopard's head mark has been used since the 14th century. The company also conducts the annual Trial of the Pyx, the ceremony at which newly minted coins of the Royal Mint are tested for fineness and weight.

Modern activities

Today the company supports the modern goldsmithing and silversmithing trades through the Goldsmiths' Centre in Clerkenwell, exhibitions, competitions and apprenticeships, and it is a significant charitable grant-maker. Its educational benefactions include the foundation of Goldsmiths, University of London, which grew from the Goldsmiths' Technical and Recreative Institute established in 1891. Membership is by patrimony, servitude (apprenticeship), redemption (purchase) or invitation, and the company is governed by a Prime Warden and Court of Assistants.
