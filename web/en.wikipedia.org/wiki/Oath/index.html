Oath

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An oath is a solemn promise, often invoking a divine witness, regarding one's future action or one's testimony that a statement is true. A person who takes an oath makes a sacred pledge; a person who gives evidence without religious commitment makes an affirmation instead, which carries the same legal effect. Making a false statement under oath is the crime of perjury.

Legal oaths

Oaths are used in courts, where witnesses swear or affirm to tell "the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth"; in oaths of office taken by officials, judges, legislators and heads of state; and in oaths of allegiance or citizenship. The oath of office of the President of the United States is prescribed in Article II of the U.S. Constitution, requiring the president to swear to "faithfully execute the Office of President of the United States" and to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution." Members of the armed forces and jurors also take oaths in many jurisdictions.

History

Oaths are ancient: they appear in the religious and legal practices of ancient Near Eastern societies, Greece and Rome, where calling the gods to witness a promise made breaking it an act of impiety punished by divine retribution. In medieval Europe, compurgation (oath-helping) allowed a party to clear themselves by sworn statements supported by neighbours. Religious minorities who objected to swearing on theological grounds — notably Quakers — pressed for the right to affirm, which was gradually conceded in Britain and America; general affirmation statutes now typically give every witness the choice.

Notable oaths

  • Hippocratic Oath — an oath of ethics historically taken by physicians.
  • Oath of allegiance — a pledge of loyalty to a sovereign or country, often required of new citizens and officeholders.
  • Judicial oath — taken by judges to administer justice impartially.
  • Tennis Court Oath (1789) — sworn by deputies of the French Third Estate not to disperse until France had a constitution.

Perjury and related offences

Because an oath binds the conscience and the law, false swearing is criminalized as perjury, and inducing another to commit it is the offence of subornation of perjury (see subornation of perjury). Refusing to take an oath or give evidence when lawfully required may constitute contempt of court (see civil contempt).
