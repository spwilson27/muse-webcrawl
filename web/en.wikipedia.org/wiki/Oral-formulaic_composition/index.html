Oral-formulaic composition

Oral-formulaic composition is a theory in literary studies describing how oral poets compose long narrative poems in performance using a stock of formulas — repeated phrases fitted to metrical patterns — together with typical scenes and story patterns. It was developed by Milman Parry in the 1920s and 1930s and extended by his student Albert Lord.

Development

Parry analysed the Homeric epics and showed that their dense system of noun-epithet formulas ("swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn") made sense as the product of an oral tradition. To test the theory on a living tradition, Parry and Lord recorded South Slavic epic singers (guslari) in Yugoslavia, including Avdo Međedović, who could sing epics of Homeric length. Lord's The Singer of Tales (1960) set out the mature theory.

Key ideas

A formula is "a group of words regularly employed under the same metrical conditions to express a given essential idea". Skilled singers do not memorise fixed texts but recompose the song at each performance, so no single definitive original exists. The theory reshaped Homeric scholarship and has been applied to Old English poetry such as Beowulf, medieval epics and other oral traditions, while its strict claims have been debated and refined by later scholars.
