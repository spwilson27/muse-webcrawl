Overview

Alcoholism, in modern clinical usage alcohol use disorder (AUD), is a chronic condition characterised by an impaired ability to stop or control alcohol use despite adverse social, occupational or health consequences. DSM-5 grades AUD as mild, moderate or severe by criteria count; ICD-11 distinguishes harmful use and dependence.

Health effects and burden

Long-term heavy drinking causes liver disease (fatty liver, hepatitis, cirrhosis), pancreatitis, cardiomyopathy, several cancers and fetal alcohol spectrum disorders; withdrawal can be life-threatening (delirium tremens). The WHO estimates alcohol causes about 2.6 million deaths a year worldwide and is a leading risk factor for premature death and disability in people aged 15–49.

History and treatment

Alcohol problems have been described since antiquity; the disease concept was developed in the 19th–20th centuries (Magnus Huss coined "alcoholism" in 1849). Alcoholics Anonymous (founded 1935) popularised the twelve-step model; modern care adds cognitive-behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing and medications — naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram. Harm-reduction and abstinence-based approaches coexist, sometimes contentiously.
