On-base plus slugging

OPS combines a batter's ability to reach base and to hit for power

On-base plus slugging (OPS) is a sabermetric baseball statistic calculated as the sum of a player's on-base percentage (OBP) and slugging percentage (SLG):

OPS = OBP + SLG

It is intended as a quick measure of a hitter's overall offensive value, combining the two most important skills of a batter: getting on base and hitting for power. OPS became one of the most widely cited statistics of the sabermetric era and is now printed on scoreboards, broadcasts, and baseball cards.

History

The statistic was popularised in the 1980s, notably by writer John Thorn and statistician Pete Palmer, who featured it in The Hidden Game of Baseball (1984) under the name "production." Bill James later championed it as a simple and broadly accurate gauge of offensive contribution, and its use spread rapidly through front offices and the media during the "Moneyball" era.

Interpretation

Because OBP and SLG are measured on different scales, critics note that OPS weights them equally even though on-base percentage is worth somewhat more per point. Nevertheless, OPS correlates strongly with team run scoring. A rough scale for major league hitters:

  • .900 and above — elite, MVP-calibre
  • .800–.899 — very good, All-Star level
  • .710–.799 — around league average
  • below .700 — below average

The career OPS record is held by Babe Ruth at 1.164, followed by Ted Williams and Lou Gehrig. The single-season record is Barry Bonds's 1.422 in 2004. An adjusted version, OPS+, normalises the figure for league and ballpark and expresses it relative to 100 as league average.

Criticism and alternatives

Analysts criticise OPS for adding percentages with different denominators, for ignoring stolen bases and other contributions, and for underweighting on-base skills. More refined metrics such as wOBA (weighted on-base average) and wRC+ were developed partly in response, though OPS remains the most familiar single-number summary of hitting.
