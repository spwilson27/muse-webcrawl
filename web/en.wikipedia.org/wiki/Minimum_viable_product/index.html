Minimum viable product

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A minimum viable product (MVP) is a version of a product with just enough features to be usable by early customers, who can then provide feedback for future product development. The concept was coined and defined by Frank Robinson around 2001 and popularised by Steve Blank and Eric Ries as part of the Lean Startup methodology. Ries described an MVP as "that version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort."

A common illustration: build a usable skateboard first, not half a car.

Purpose

Gathering insights from an MVP is often cheaper than developing a full-featured product, reducing the risk of building something customers do not want. The MVP is the unit of the Lean Startup's "build–measure–learn" loop: a team forms a hypothesis, builds the smallest thing that can test it, measures actual user behaviour, and decides whether to persevere or "pivot".

Techniques

  • Landing page / explainer video MVP — a page describing the product and measuring sign-ups; Dropbox famously validated demand with a demonstration video before building its file-syncing technology.
  • "Wizard of Oz" MVP — a front-end that appears automated while humans perform the work behind the scenes.
  • Concierge MVP — a manual service delivered personally to a few customers to learn what should be automated.
  • Single-feature MVP — one core function released to early adopters.

Criticism

Critics argue the term is often misapplied to mean merely an early or stripped-down release, that releasing products perceived as low-quality can damage a brand, and that in some markets (enterprise, regulated or safety-critical products) an MVP may not be viable at all. Alternatives proposed include "minimum lovable product" and "minimum testable product".
