Cherry picking

This article is about the fallacy. For other uses, see Cherry picking (disambiguation).

Cherry picking, suppressing evidence, or the fallacy of incomplete evidence is the act of pointing to individual cases or data that seem to confirm a particular position while ignoring a significant portion of related and similar cases or data that may contradict that position. Cherry picking may be committed intentionally or unintentionally.

Selecting only favourable examples, like picking the best cherries, gives a misleading picture of the whole

The term is based on the perceived process of harvesting fruit, such as cherries. The picker is expected to select only the ripest and healthiest fruits, giving an observer the impression that all the fruit is of equally good quality when the majority may be under-ripe or damaged.

Description

Cherry picking is a fallacy of selective attention: the most common example is selecting evidence that confirms a hypothesis while ignoring evidence that disconfirms it. It is related to the cognitive bias known as confirmation bias. In science, the practice is sometimes called "cherry picking data", and it is one of the hallmarks distinguishing pseudoscience from science: a scientific method requires that all available evidence be considered, not only the parts favourable to a claim.

Examples

  • Climate change: selecting a short interval of years (for example, beginning in an unusually warm El Niño year) to claim that global temperatures are not rising, while ignoring the longer-term trend.
  • Medicine: citing individual anecdotes of patients who recovered after a treatment while ignoring controlled trials showing no effect.
  • Finance: advertising a fund's performance over a carefully chosen time window that excludes periods of loss.
  • Arguments about health risks: pointing to a single long-lived smoker as evidence that smoking is harmless, ignoring aggregate mortality statistics.

Related concepts

Cherry picking is closely related to selection bias and survivorship bias, in which the sample itself is unrepresentative. The "Texas sharpshooter fallacy" is a related error in which differences in data are ignored and similarities are overemphasised, as if a shooter fired at a barn wall and then painted the target around the tightest cluster of holes.
