Business model

A business model describes how an organization creates, delivers and captures value, in economic, social, cultural or other contexts. It is the rationale of how the organization delivers value to customers, induces customers to pay for value, and converts those payments to profit. Business model construction and modification is also called business model innovation and forms a part of business strategy.

Concepts and frameworks

The term spread widely during the internet boom of the 1990s, when start-ups were described by how they planned to make money online. Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur's Business Model Generation (2010) introduced the Business Model Canvas, nine blocks covering customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. Earlier, Joan Magretta distinguished business models (how a firm works) from strategy (how it competes), and Michael E. Porter emphasised competitive positioning.

Archetypes

Common patterns include the subscription model; freemium; the "razor and blades" model of cheap hardware and profitable consumables; franchising, as used by chains such as Burger King; two-sided platforms matching buyers and sellers; affiliate and advertising-supported models; and the long tail of niche digital products. MIT research by Weill and colleagues classified models by the type of rights sold and assets involved — creator, distributor, landlord and broker archetypes.

Innovation

Business model innovation — changing how value is created or captured rather than the product itself — is credited with the success of firms from Southwest Airlines and Dell to Netflix and Airbnb. Scholars caution that the concept is used loosely, overlapping with strategy, revenue models and operating models.
