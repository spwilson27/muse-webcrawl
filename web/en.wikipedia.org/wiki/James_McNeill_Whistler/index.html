James McNeill Whistler

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Whistler's Mother (1871) — Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1: the artist's mother seated in profile against a grey wall]

James Abbott McNeill Whistler (11 July 1834 – 17 July 1903) was an American artist active in Britain, a leading figure of Aestheticism who preached 'art for art's sake'. Famous for his tonal 'Nocturnes' of the Thames, his portrait known worldwide as Whistler's Mother, and his public feuds — above all the 1878 libel trial against John Ruskin — he was as celebrated for his wit as for his painting.

His major works include Symphony in White, No. 1: The White Girl (1862), Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1 (1871), Nocturne in Black and Gold — The Falling Rocket (c. 1875), and the Peacock Room (1876–77), now in the Freer Gallery, Washington.

The Ruskin trial

Whistler, born in Massachusetts and expelled from West Point (he joked that had silicon been a gas he would have been a general), settled in London in 1859. When the critic John Ruskin accused him of 'flinging a pot of paint in the public's face' and charging 200 guineas for it, Whistler sued for libel — winning a farthing's damages, no costs, and bankruptcy, but making his stand for the autonomy of art a public spectacle. His lectures and book The Gentle Art of Making Enemies (1890) fixed his legend.

Style and influence

Influenced by Japanese prints, Velázquez and the French avant-garde, Whistler arranged colour like music — 'symphonies', 'nocturnes', 'arrangements' — signing with a butterfly monogram. His austere harmonies and decorative interiors shaped British and American taste and the Aesthetic movement of Oscar Wilde's generation.
