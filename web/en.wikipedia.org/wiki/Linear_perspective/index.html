Linear perspective (also called geometric or central perspective) is a system of creating an illusion of depth on a flat surface. All parallel lines in the image that are not parallel to the picture plane converge at a single point — a vanishing point — on the scene's horizon line, and objects are rendered smaller as they recede, mimicking the way the visual world appears to a stationary viewer.

History

Artists in antiquity and the early medieval period used intuitive or "herringbone" convergence but no unified system. Around 1415 the Florentine architect Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated the first mathematically consistent construction in a famous experiment painting the Florence Baptistery as seen through a peephole and mirror. In 1435 Leon Battista Alberti codified the method in his treatise De pictura, describing the picture as an open window ("finestra aperta") through which the scene is viewed and giving the pavement construction for checking foreshortening. Painters such as Masaccio (in the Holy Trinity, c. 1427) and Piero della Francesca, who wrote a mathematical treatise on the subject, made perspective a foundation of Renaissance art, and it spread through Europe via treatises and prints.

The system

In one-point perspective, the picture plane is parallel to two axes of a rectilinear scene, and one set of parallel lines converges to a single vanishing point on the horizon. Two-point perspective (used, for example, for the corner of a building) has two vanishing points, and three-point perspective adds a third for vertical lines, used for views looking up or down. The geometry is that of the pinhole camera: linear perspective is exact for an ideal point of view, which is why extreme wide-angle views drawn in it can look distorted at the edges. Related graphical systems include curvilinear perspective, aerial perspective (a colour-and-contrast effect rather than a construction), and the parallel projections used in technical drawing.

Use in other fields

Beyond art, linear perspective underpins architectural drawing, stage design, photography, video games and 3D computer graphics, where the same projective geometry is computed by graphics pipelines; in mathematics it gave rise to projective geometry.
