The Spanish conquest of Guatemala was a protracted conflict during the Spanish colonization of the Americas in which Spanish colonizers gradually incorporated the territory that became the modern country of Guatemala into the colonial Viceroyalty of New Spain. Before the conquest, Guatemala contained a number of competing Mesoamerican kingdoms, predominantly Maya, including the K'iche', Kaqchikel, Mam, and Tz'utujil kingdoms in the highlands, as well as Pipil and other peoples in the Pacific lowlands.

The conquest began in 1524 with expeditions led by Pedro de Alvarado, who had served under Hernán Cortés in the conquest of Mexico. Alvarado's forces, allied with Kaqchikel warriors, defeated the K'iche' kingdom, capturing and executing its rulers and occupying its capital, Q'umarkaj. The first colonial capital, Santiago de los Caballeros de Guatemala, was founded in 1524 at Iximche, the Kaqchikel capital; after the Kaqchikel revolted, the capital was moved to Ciudad Vieja in 1527 and later to Antigua Guatemala in 1543.

Background

The highland Maya kingdoms had been weakened by inter-kingdom warfare and by epidemics of European diseases, such as smallpox, which arrived ahead of the Spanish themselves. The K'iche' kingdom of Q'umarkaj was the most powerful of the highland states, having expanded aggressively during the 15th century under kings such as K'iq'ab'.

Course of the conquest

Alvarado entered Guatemala from Soconusco in early 1524 and won a decisive battle against the K'iche' near Quetzaltenango, where the K'iche' lord Tecun Uman was killed according to legend. The Spanish then occupied Q'umarkaj and executed the K'iche' kings Oxib-Keh and Beleheb-Tzy. The Kaqchikel initially allied with the Spanish against their K'iche' enemies but rebelled in 1524 when Alvarado demanded large tributes of gold; their resistance in the mountains continued for years.

Other regions were subdued piecemeal: the Mam capital of Zaculeu fell in 1525 after a siege by Gonzalo de Alvarado; the Tz'utujil around Lake Atitlán were defeated in 1524; and the fortress of Mixco Viejo was taken in 1525. Campaigns into the lowlands of Petén and against the Lacandon and Manche Ch'ol peoples continued through the 16th and 17th centuries.

The fall of Nojpetén

The last independent Maya kingdom was the Itza kingdom of Petén, whose capital Nojpetén stood on an island in Lake Petén Itzá. Despite earlier contacts, including a visit by Cortés in 1525 and Franciscan missions in the 17th century, the Itza resisted incorporation until an expedition led by Martín de Ursúa y Arizmendi captured Nojpetén on 13 March 1697, completing the conquest.

Legacy

The conquest was devastating for the indigenous population, which collapsed through disease, warfare, enslavement, and forced labour under the encomienda system. Guatemala became a captaincy general within New Spain. Indigenous communities nonetheless preserved much of their culture and languages, and Maya peoples today form a large share of Guatemala's population.
