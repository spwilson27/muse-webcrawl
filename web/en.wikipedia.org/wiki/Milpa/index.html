Milpa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A milpa intercrops maize, beans, and squash — the "Three Sisters".

A milpa is a traditional Mesoamerican crop-growing system used throughout Mexico and Central America, based on the intercropping of maize with beans, squash, and a variety of other plants. The word comes from Nahuatl milpan, "in the field". Milpa agriculture historically combined shifting cultivation (clearing and burning a plot, farming it for a few years, then leaving it fallow to regenerate) with a sophisticated polyculture that supplied the nutritional core of the Mesoamerican diet.

The Three Sisters

The central combination of the milpa is the "Three Sisters": maize provides a stalk for climbing beans to grow on; the beans fix nitrogen in the soil, benefiting the maize; and squash spreads along the ground, its broad leaves shading out weeds and retaining soil moisture. Nutritionally, maize and beans complement one another's amino acids, forming a complete protein source. A milpa typically includes many additional species — chiles, tomatoes, tomatillos, avocados, and various herbs and quelites (edible greens) — and may harbour dozens of useful plants in a single field, including volunteer and semi-wild species that farmers tolerate and manage.

Ecology and significance

Milpa fields and their surrounding fallows form a mosaic landscape of high biodiversity, and the system has maintained an enormous diversity of maize landraces and other crop varieties in situ. Farming families rotate fields through a cycle of cultivation and forest fallow, a practice that stores carbon and supports wildlife when fallow periods are sufficiently long. Population pressure, land scarcity, and the promotion of monoculture have shortened fallows and eroded milpa practice in many areas, but the system remains widespread among Indigenous and smallholder farmers and is the subject of renewed interest in agroecology for its resilience and crop diversity.
