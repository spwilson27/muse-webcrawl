Marginal propensity to consume

In economics, the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is the proportion of an additional unit of income that a household or economy consumes rather than saves. Formally, MPC = ΔC / ΔY, the change in consumption divided by the change in disposable income. It is a central concept of Keynesian economics, introduced by John Maynard Keynes in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), where he posited a "fundamental psychological law" that consumption rises with income but by less than the increase in income — that is, MPC lies between 0 and 1.

Role in the multiplier

The MPC determines the size of the Keynesian spending multiplier: k = 1 / (1 − MPC). If the MPC is 0.8, an initial injection of spending generates five times as much total income in the simple model. The complementary fraction, 1 − MPC, is the marginal propensity to save (MPS).

Determinants and evidence

MPC tends to be higher for lower-income and liquidity-constrained households, which spend a larger share of windfalls — an empirical regularity invoked in designing fiscal stimulus, since transfers targeted at such households are spent more quickly. Studies of tax rebates and stimulus payments typically estimate MPCs in the range of roughly 0.2 to 0.6 within a few months, higher for durable-adjacent and necessity spending. Long-run theories such as the permanent income hypothesis and life-cycle hypothesis predict lower MPCs out of transitory income, as households smooth consumption.
