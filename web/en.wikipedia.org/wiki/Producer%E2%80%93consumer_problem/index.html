Producer–consumer problem

In computing, the producer–consumer problem (also known as the bounded-buffer problem) is a classic example of a multi-process synchronisation problem, first described by Edsger W. Dijkstra. The problem describes two processes, the producer and the consumer, who share a common, fixed-size buffer used as a queue. The producer generates data and puts it into the buffer; the consumer removes data from the buffer one item at a time.

Problem statement

The producer must not add data to a full buffer, and the consumer must not remove data from an empty buffer. A solution must also guarantee mutual exclusion so that the two processes do not access the buffer at the same time, which could corrupt its state. Without care, implementations can suffer from race conditions, deadlock, or busy waiting that wastes processor time.

Solutions

Dijkstra's solution uses two counting semaphores, empty (initially the buffer size) and full (initially zero), plus a binary semaphore for mutual exclusion. The producer waits on empty, acquires the lock, inserts the item, then signals full; the consumer does the converse. Equivalent solutions exist using monitors and condition variables, message passing, or lock-free ring buffers in modern concurrent programming.

Applications

The pattern underlies print spoolers, pipeline processing in shells, video streaming buffers, and producer–consumer queues throughout operating systems and middleware.
