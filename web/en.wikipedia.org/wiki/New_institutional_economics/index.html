New institutional economics

New institutional economics (NIE) is an economic perspective that attempts to extend economics by focusing on the institutions — the social and legal norms and rules — that underlie economic activity. It analyses how institutions structure incentives, reduce uncertainty, and affect economic performance, while analysing institutions themselves as outcomes of purposeful (if boundedly rational) human action.

History

The term was coined by Oliver Williamson in 1975, but its intellectual roots reach back to Ronald Coase's landmark papers "The Nature of the Firm" (1937) and "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960), which introduced the concepts of transaction costs and the role of property rights. Douglass North developed the framework into a theory of institutional change and long-run economic growth, and Elinor Ostrom applied it to the governance of common-pool resources. Coase (1991), North (1993) and Ostrom (2009) all received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

Main themes

  • Transaction costs: the costs of searching, bargaining, contracting and enforcing agreements explain why firms and non-market governance structures exist.
  • Property rights: well-defined and enforced rights are essential for investment and exchange.
  • Bounded rationality and opportunism: Williamson's behavioural assumptions underpin the theory of contracts and governance.
  • Path dependence: North showed how historical institutional trajectories constrain later development.
  • Commons governance: Ostrom's design principles for sustainable management of shared resources.

NIE differs from the older institutional economics of Thorstein Veblen and John R. Commons in retaining core neoclassical tools — scarcity and competition — while enriching them with institutional analysis. It has influenced development economics, economic history, law and economics, and political science.
