French horn

The modern double horn in F and B♭.

The French horn (since the 1930s known in professional circles simply as the horn) is a brass instrument made of tubing wrapped into a coil with a flared bell. It descends from the natural hunting horn used in France, and its sound is produced by the player's vibrating lips, with pitch controlled by valves, embouchure and the right hand placed in the bell.

Description

The modern instrument is usually a double horn combining an F horn and a shorter B♭ horn, switched by a thumb trigger, with about 3.7 metres (12 feet) of tubing in total. Three rotary valves operated by the left hand change the tube length to fill in the notes of the harmonic series. Because its harmonics lie unusually close together high in the series, the horn is regarded as one of the most difficult brass instruments to play accurately.

History

Early horns were natural instruments without valves, using crooks to change key and hand-stopping for chromatic notes, a technique refined in the 18th century and used extensively by Mozart in his four horn concertos. Valves were introduced in the early 19th century (patented by Heinrich Stölzel and Friedrich Blühmel in 1818), freeing composers such as Wagner, Brahms, Mahler and Richard Strauss to write the horn's rich Romantic repertoire.

Use

The horn is a standard member of the orchestra (usually in sections of two to eight), the wind quintet and the brass quintet, and features prominently in film scores. Notable soloists include Dennis Brain, Barry Tuckwell and Hermann Baumann.
