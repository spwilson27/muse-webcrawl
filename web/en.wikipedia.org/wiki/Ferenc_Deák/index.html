Ferenc Deák de Kehida (17 October 1803 – 28 January 1876) was a Hungarian statesman and Minister of Justice, known as "The Sage of the Country" (a haza bölcse). He was the architect of the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867, which created the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary and restored Hungary's constitutional position within it.

Early career

Deák was born into a landowning noble family in Zala County and trained in law. He entered national politics as a reformer in the diets of the 1830s and 1840s, advocating the abolition of serfdom, legal equality, and freedom of speech, and became a leading figure of the moderate reform opposition alongside Lajos Kossuth, though more cautious in method.

Revolution and passive resistance

In 1848 Deák served as Minister of Justice in the first responsible Hungarian government of Lajos Batthyány. When the Hungarian Revolution of 1848 turned into a war of independence under Kossuth, Deák withdrew from politics, opposing the dethronement of the Habsburgs. After the revolution's defeat in 1849, he became the symbol of "passive resistance"—refusing office and cooperation with the neo-absolutist regime of Vienna while insisting on Hungary's legal rights under the April Laws of 1848.

The Compromise of 1867

Deák's famous "Easter Article" of 1865 opened the path to negotiation with Emperor Franz Joseph I. Following Austria's defeat in the Austro-Prussian War (1866), the negotiations concluded in the Compromise (Ausgleich/Kiegyezés) of 1867: Hungary regained its parliament and government, sharing with Austria only the monarch and common ministries for foreign affairs, war, and joint finance. Deák declined high office in the new system, remaining an influential backbencher until his death in Budapest in 1876.
