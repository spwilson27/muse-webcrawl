Price of anarchy (networking)

Price of anarchy

Field: Algorithmic game theory, network theory

Introduced by: Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou (1999)

Related concept: Braess's paradox; price of stability

The price of anarchy in a routing or networking game is the ratio between the total cost of the worst-case Nash equilibrium — in which users selfishly choose their routes — and the total cost of a centrally coordinated optimal assignment. It quantifies the efficiency loss caused by decentralised, self-interested behaviour.

Definition

Given a network with latency functions on its edges and a set of users each travelling from a source to a destination, the price of anarchy equals the cost of the worst Nash equilibrium flow divided by the cost of the optimal flow. In an equilibrium, no individual user can reduce their own travel time by unilaterally switching routes.

Results

For networks with linear latency functions, the price of anarchy is at most 4/3, a result established by Roughgarden and Tardos; the classic example of Pigou shows this bound is tight. For polynomial latencies of degree p, the price of anarchy grows roughly as (p / ln p). The related Braess's paradox shows that adding capacity to a network can, counterintuitively, worsen equilibrium performance.

Beyond routing

The concept, introduced by Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou in 1999, has been extended to scheduling, resource allocation, and network formation games, and complements the price of stability, which compares the best equilibrium with the optimum.
