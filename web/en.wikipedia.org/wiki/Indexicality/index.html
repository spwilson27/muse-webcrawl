Indexicality

In the philosophy of language and linguistics, indexicality is the phenomenon by which an expression's meaning or reference depends on the context in which it is uttered. Indexicals include the pronouns I and you, demonstratives such as this and that, and adverbs such as here, now, today and tomorrow. The word "I", for instance, refers to whoever is speaking.

Semiotic background

The term derives from Charles Sanders Peirce's classification of signs, in which an index is a sign connected to its object by a real relation — as smoke is an index of fire, or a pointing finger of the thing pointed at. Linguists and anthropologists have extended the notion to the ways speech signals social identities, relationships and settings, so that ways of speaking "index" class, gender, formality and stance.

Philosophical treatment

David Kaplan's influential theory distinguishes an indexical's character (the rule fixing its reference given a context) from its content (the reference itself, constant across circumstances). Indexicality is central to debates about direct reference, essential indexicals (John Perry's "essential indexical"), and the semantics of tense and personal pronouns.
