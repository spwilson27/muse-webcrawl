Series (mathematics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Bars showing partial sums of a series levelling off toward a limit]

In mathematics, a series is the sum of the terms of an infinite sequence. A series converges when its sequence of partial sums approaches a limit; otherwise it diverges, as the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … famously does, though ever more slowly.

Convergent series are the engine of computation in analysis: functions such as eˣ, sine and cosine can be written as power series (Taylor series), letting calculators evaluate them to any accuracy. Geometric series, with a constant ratio between terms, are the simplest case and were summed already in antiquity.

Tests and surprises

A battery of convergence tests — ratio, root, comparison, integral — decides most series encountered in practice. Conditionally convergent series hold a trap: rearranging their terms can change their sum, as Riemann proved.

Series that diverge can still be assigned values by summation methods (Cesàro, Abel, and Ramanujan summation), a subtle idea used in parts of physics.
