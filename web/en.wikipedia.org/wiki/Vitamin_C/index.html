Vitamin C (ascorbic acid)
Chemical nameL-ascorbic acid
FormulaC₆H₈O₆
TypeWater-soluble vitamin
FunctionAntioxidant; cofactor for collagen synthesis and other enzymes

Good sourcesCitrus fruits, peppers, kiwifruit, broccoli, blackcurrants, guava

Vitamin C (also known as ascorbic acid and ascorbate) is a water-soluble vitamin found in citrus and other fruits, berries, and vegetables. It is an essential nutrient for humans — most animals synthesize it, but humans, other primates, guinea pigs, and a few other species lack the enzyme L-gulonolactone oxidase and must obtain it from the diet. Vitamin C is required for the synthesis of collagen, L-carnitine, and certain neurotransmitters; it is involved in protein metabolism and acts as an antioxidant, regenerating other antioxidants such as vitamin E and supporting immune function.

Deficiency — scurvy

Prolonged deficiency causes scurvy, characterized by fatigue, swollen bleeding gums, joint pain, anaemia, and impaired wound healing, as defective collagen undermines connective tissues and blood vessels. Scurvy was a scourge of long sea voyages; in 1747 James Lind's shipboard trial showed citrus cured it — one of the earliest controlled clinical trials — and the British Navy's adoption of lemon and later lime juice gave sailors the nickname "limeys". Vitamin C was isolated and chemically identified in 1928–1932 by Albert Szent-Györgyi, Walter Norman Haworth, and others ("ascorbic" = anti-scurvy), earning Nobel Prizes.

Health claims

Adequate intake supports normal immune function and skin health. Popular claims popularised by Linus Pauling that megadoses prevent or cure the common cold or cancer are not supported by clinical evidence: regular supplementation modestly shortens cold duration but does not reduce incidence in the general population, and high-dose intravenous vitamin C for cancer remains unproven. Being water-soluble, excess vitamin C is excreted, though very high doses can cause gastrointestinal upset and may increase oxalate kidney-stone risk. Vitamin C is also widely used as a food additive (antioxidant E300) and in cosmetics.

Chemistry and production

Ascorbic acid is a weak sugar acid related to glucose. Industrial production historically used the Reichstein process and now largely two-step fermentation methods. It is sensitive to heat, light, and oxygen in cooking and storage.
