Equation of time

The equation of time describes the discrepancy between two kinds of solar time: apparent solar time, shown by a sundial, and mean solar time, shown by a clock. The difference varies through the year, reaching about +16 minutes in early November and about −14 minutes in mid-February.

Equation of time
Maximum+16m 33s (early Nov)
Minimum−14m 6s (mid Feb)
Zero crossings~15 Apr, 13 Jun, 1 Sep, 25 Dec

Causes

Two effects combine: the eccentricity of Earth's orbit, which makes apparent solar days vary as Earth speeds up near perihelion, and the obliquity of the ecliptic — the tilt of Earth's axis — which projects the Sun's motion unevenly onto the celestial equator. Their sum produces the characteristic double-humped curve.

History and use

The phenomenon was known to Ptolemy. It mattered practically when accurate clocks and sundials coexisted: tables and later "equation clocks" converted between them, and mechanical watches sometimes displayed it. The figure-eight analemma photographed by imaging the Sun at the same clock time through the year is the equation of time combined with declination. Navigators and astronomers used it to reduce observations; today it is applied in solar-energy calculations and sundial design.
