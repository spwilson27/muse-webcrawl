Hip-hop culture

Hip-hop (also spelled hip hop) is a cultural movement and art movement that was created by African-American, Latin American, and Caribbean-American youth in the Bronx borough of New York City during the early 1970s. It is generally considered to have been born at a back-to-school party hosted by DJ Kool Herc on 11 August 1973 at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue. Since then it has become a worldwide phenomenon influencing music, dance, fashion, language, and visual art.

Elements

The culture is conventionally described as comprising several elements. Afrika Bambaataa and the Universal Zulu Nation codified four: MCing (rapping), DJing (turntablism), b-boying/b-girling (breakdance), and graffiti art; a fifth element, "knowledge", is often added, and beatboxing is sometimes counted as well. DJ Kool Herc pioneered the use of two turntables to extend the percussive "breaks" of funk records, while Grandmaster Flash, Grand Wizard Theodore, and others developed techniques such as scratching, cutting, and punch phrasing.

History

From block parties in the Bronx, hip-hop spread through New York in the late 1970s and entered the mainstream with records such as the Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight" (1979) and Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five's "The Message" (1982). A "golden age" in the late 1980s and early 1990s saw rapid innovation — sampling, politically conscious lyrics, and regional styles — followed by the commercial dominance of gangsta rap and the East Coast–West Coast rivalry of the 1990s. In the twenty-first century hip-hop became the most-consumed music genre in the United States, absorbing and generating subcultures in virtually every country.

Influence

Hip-hop has shaped global youth culture far beyond music: its slang has entered mainstream English, its fashion — from sneakers and sportswear to luxury-brand collaborations — drives the clothing industry, graffiti developed into the international street-art movement, and breakdance was contested as an Olympic sport at the Paris 2024 Games. Critics debate the culture's commercialisation and some of its lyrical themes, but its role as a voice for marginalised communities and as the defining popular culture of the past half-century is widely acknowledged.
