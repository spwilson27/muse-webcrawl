August Weismann

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Friedrich Leopold August Weismann (17 January 1834 – 5 November 1914) was a German evolutionary biologist. One of the most important thinkers in the history of evolutionary biology after Charles Darwin, he is best known for the germ plasm theory of heredity, which denied the inheritance of acquired characteristics and established a fundamental distinction between germline and somatic cells.

Career

Weismann studied medicine and natural science at Göttingen, practised briefly as a physician, and became professor of zoology at the University of Freiburg, where he remained for the rest of his career. Failing eyesight turned him from microscopy to theory, and he produced a series of influential essays on heredity and development, collected in works such as Studies in the Theory of Descent (1882), The Germ-Plasm (1892) and his Vorträge über Deszendenztheorie (1902).

Germ plasm theory

Weismann proposed that hereditary information is carried only by the germ cells (eggs and sperm), whose "germ plasm" is passed unchanged between generations, while the body (somatoplasm) is a disposable offshoot that cannot transmit acquired modifications to offspring. This "Weismann barrier" directly opposed Lamarckian inheritance; his famous experiment — cutting off the tails of mice for many generations and observing that offspring were always born with full tails — is a classic (if logically limited) demonstration. His insistence on the all-sufficiency of natural selection earned his school the label neo-Darwinism.

Legacy

Weismann's concepts anticipated the central role of the germline, meiosis (whose significance for halving chromosome number he predicted) and, ultimately, the modern separation of genotype from phenotype.
