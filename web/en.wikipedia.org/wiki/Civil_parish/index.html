Civil parish

In England, a civil parish is a type of administrative parish used for local government. It is a territorial designation which is the lowest tier of local government. Civil parishes can range in size from a single farm to a large town, and their names are generally those of the historic ecclesiastical parishes from which they originated, although their boundaries may differ. There are more than 10,000 civil parishes in England, covering roughly 40 per cent of the population; much of urban England, including most of London until 2007, historically had no civil parishes.

History

Civil parishes originated in the ancient parishes of the Church of England, which took on secular functions under the Poor Law system. From the sixteenth century, parishes were responsible for administering poor relief, maintaining highways, and appointing constables and overseers. The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 transferred poor relief to unions of parishes, and nineteenth-century legislation progressively separated civil and ecclesiastical functions.

The Local Government Act 1894 formally created civil parishes as secular bodies with elected parish councils, taking over the non-ecclesiastical functions of parish vestries. In urban areas, many parishes were absorbed into boroughs, while rural parishes retained councils. Successive reorganizations — notably the Local Government Act 1972 — preserved civil parishes in England as the tier of government closest to local communities.

Governance

A civil parish may have a parish council, or, if very small, a parish meeting of all electors. Parish councils are funded principally by a precept levied on council tax and may provide or maintain facilities such as village halls, allotments, public clocks, bus shelters, litter bins, and playing fields. They are statutory consultees on planning applications within the parish and may prepare neighbourhood plans under the Localism Act 2011. A parish council for a town may resolve to style itself a town council, and a small number of parishes have been granted city status for their councils (for example, Salisbury).

Creation and abolition

New civil parishes can be created, and existing ones altered or abolished, by district or unitary councils following a community governance review under the Local Government and Public Involvement in Health Act 2007. The Act also permitted parishes to be created in London for the first time since 1965; the first London parish council, Queen's Park in the City of Westminster, was established in 2014.

Elsewhere

Scotland and Wales have their own, differently organized lowest tiers (community councils in both countries), while civil parishes survive as a formal unit in parts of Ireland and in some other countries influenced by English law, including several Caribbean states and Canadian provinces' historical subdivisions.
