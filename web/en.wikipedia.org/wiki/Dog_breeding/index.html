Dog breeding is the practice of mating selected dogs with the intention of maintaining or producing specific qualities and characteristics. When dogs reproduce without human intervention, their offspring's characteristics are determined by natural selection; in contrast, "dog breeding" refers specifically to the artificial selection of dogs, in which dogs are intentionally bred by their owners. Breeding relies on the science of genetics, so the breeder with a knowledge of canine genetics, health, and the intended purpose for the dogs attempts to breed suitable dogs.

History

Humans have maintained populations of useful animals around their places of habitat since pre-historic times. Over the millennia, domesticated dogs were bred as distinct types or breeds for specific tasks, such as hunting, herding, guarding, and companionship. The formalisation of breeds and breed standards accelerated in the 19th century with the founding of kennel clubs, including The Kennel Club in the United Kingdom (1873) and the American Kennel Club (1884).

Breeding practices

Breeding begins with selection of a sire and dam, often guided by pedigree, conformation to a breed standard, temperament, and health screening for heritable conditions such as hip dysplasia. The female dog (bitch) is typically receptive during estrus, and gestation lasts about 63 days. Responsible breeders screen prospective owners, limit the number of litters, and remain accountable for puppies throughout their lives.

Health concerns

Selective breeding for extreme traits has contributed to health problems in some breeds, including breathing difficulties in brachycephalic breeds and skeletal disorders in very large or long-backed dogs. Inbreeding to maintain closed stud books can reduce genetic diversity; crossbreeding and outcrossing are sometimes used to address this. Animal welfare organisations campaign against commercial "puppy farms" (puppy mills), where dogs are bred intensively in poor conditions.

Regulation

Many jurisdictions require commercial breeders to be licensed and inspected, and some have introduced restrictions on breeding animals with extreme conformations or known heritable defects.
