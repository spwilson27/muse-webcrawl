Slate industry in Wales

The slate industry in Wales was the world's largest producer of slate from the mid-19th century until its decline in the later 20th century. Slate quarrying in Wales is attested from Roman times, when slate was used to roof the fort at Segontium (modern Caernarfon), but the industry grew on a large scale during the Industrial Revolution, reaching its peak production in the 1890s.

Major quarries

The largest quarries were concentrated in north-west Wales: the Penrhyn Quarry at Bethesda and the Dinorwic Quarry near Llanberis, together with the workings around Blaenau Ffestiniog (many of them underground mines) and the Nantlle Valley. Penrhyn and Dinorwic were among the largest slate quarries in the world, each employing around 3,000 men at their peak. The slate was exported through purpose-built ports such as Port Penrhyn and Y Felinheli, carried there by narrow-gauge railways including the Ffestiniog Railway and the Padarn Railway.

Working conditions and disputes

Quarrymen worked the slate in small partnerships under the "bargain" system, paid by results. The industry was marked by bitter industrial disputes, most famously the Penrhyn Lockout of 1900–1903, one of the longest disputes in British industrial history, arising from Lord Penrhyn's refusal to recognise the North Wales Quarrymen's Union.

Decline and legacy

Production fell sharply after the First World War as roofing tiles and other materials displaced slate, and as overseas competition grew. Many quarries closed in the 1960s, including Dinorwic in 1969. In 2021 the slate landscape of northwest Wales was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The National Slate Museum at Llanberis preserves the workshops of the Dinorwic quarry.
