Ergonomics

Ergonomics, also known as human factors or human factors engineering (HFE), is the application of psychological and physiological principles to the engineering and design of products, processes, and systems. Primary goals of human-centred design are to reduce human error, enhance productivity and system performance, and enhance safety, health and comfort with a specific focus on the interaction between the human and equipment.

History

The term was coined in 1857 by the Polish scholar Wojciech Jastrzębowski, and revived in 1949 by the Welsh psychologist Hywel Murrell when founding the Ergonomics Research Society. The discipline grew out of wartime research: during the Second World War, studies of pilot error and cockpit design (such as Paul Fitts's work on aircraft controls), and Alphonse Chapanis's shape-coding of controls, showed that designing for human capabilities reduced accidents. After the war the field expanded from military systems to industry, offices, computing, and consumer products.

Domains

The International Ergonomics Association distinguishes three domains:

  • Physical ergonomics — anatomy, anthropometry, biomechanics and physiology applied to posture, materials handling, repetitive movements, and workplace layout, aimed at preventing musculoskeletal disorders such as back pain and carpal tunnel syndrome.
  • Cognitive ergonomics — mental processes such as perception, memory, reasoning and decision-making, applied to displays, controls, workload and human–computer interaction.
  • Organizational ergonomics — the design of sociotechnical systems, work schedules, communication, and teamwork.

Methods and standards

Ergonomists use task analysis, anthropometric data, motion studies, usability testing, and risk assessments such as the NIOSH lifting equation and RULA/REBA posture scores. Standards include the ISO 9241 series on ergonomics of human–system interaction and regulations on display screen equipment and manual handling in many countries.
