Condenser (laboratory)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A Liebig condenser used in distillation

A condenser is laboratory apparatus used to condense vapors — that is, turn them into liquids — by cooling them down. Condensers are routinely used in laboratory operations such as distillation, reflux, and extraction, and typically consist of a large glass tube containing a smaller glass tube running its entire length, with coolant (usually water) circulating through the outer jacket.

Types

The simplest and oldest design is the Liebig condenser, a straight inner tube within a water jacket, efficient for distillation. The Allihn condenser (bulb condenser) has a series of bulges that increase surface area and is commonly used for reflux. The Graham and Dimroth condensers use coiled inner tubes for greater cooling efficiency, while the Vigreux column and fractionating columns are used for fractional distillation.

Air condensers — simple tubes without a water jacket — suffice for high-boiling liquids. In a reflux setup, the condenser is mounted vertically above a boiling flask so condensed solvent drips back; in distillation it is angled downward to deliver distillate to a receiving flask, often via a vacuum adapter.

Operation

Coolant enters at the lowest point of the jacket and exits at the highest, ensuring the jacket stays full. Condensers are made of borosilicate glass with ground-glass joints for easy assembly of apparatus. Efficient condensation depends on coolant temperature, flow rate, and the surface area of the cold surface.
