Stigma management

Stigma management is the process by which individuals and groups respond to, control, or resist the negative social judgements attached to a stigmatised attribute, condition, or identity. The concept derives principally from Erving Goffman's Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity (1963), which analysed how people bearing an "undesired differentness" handle social interactions. Depending on whether a stigma is visible or concealable, those affected employ strategies ranging from concealment and selective disclosure to open disclosure, education of others, and collective political action aimed at changing the stigma itself.

Goffman's framework

Goffman distinguished between the discredited, whose stigma is already known or visible (such as a physical disability), and the discreditable, whose stigma is concealable (such as a history of mental illness or a criminal record). For the discredited, the central problem is managing social tension in interaction; for the discreditable, it is managing information about the failing. He described passing—being accepted as normal by concealing the stigma—and covering—downplaying a known stigma's obtrusiveness so that interaction proceeds smoothly. Goffman also noted "courtesy stigma", the discredit borne by associates of the stigmatised, and the role of "the wise", non-stigmatised people who are privy to and sympathetic with the secret.

Individual strategies

Later research systematised the coping repertoire. Strategies include secrecy and withdrawal (avoiding situations in which discovery might occur), selective or controlled disclosure to trusted others, preventive telling—disclosing in a managed way before discovery—and therapeutic disclosure. Studies of concealable stigmas such as epilepsy, HIV status, sexual orientation, and mental illness show that concealment protects against discrimination but often carries psychological costs, including vigilance, anxiety, and isolation, whereas disclosure can secure social support at the risk of rejection. Jones and colleagues (1984) organised stigma along dimensions—concealability, course, disruptiveness, aesthetic qualities, origin, and peril—that predict which strategies are available and effective.

Collective strategies and resistance

Beyond individual coping, groups may challenge stigma publicly: reclaiming slurs as badges of pride, as in gay pride and disability pride movements; reframing the condition (for example, neurodiversity advocacy); and pursuing legal and policy change. Such activism converts stigma management from information control into social change, though it requires the visibility that individual strategies may seek to avoid.

Health-related stigma

Health psychologists apply stigma management to illnesses including HIV/AIDS, leprosy, tuberculosis, obesity, and mental disorders. Interventions range from disclosure-decision counselling and peer-support programmes to mass-media campaigns. Anticipated stigma—the expectation of prejudice—can deter testing, treatment adherence, and help-seeking even in the absence of enacted discrimination, making management strategies a target of public-health research in their own right.
