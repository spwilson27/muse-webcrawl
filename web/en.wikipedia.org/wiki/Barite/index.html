Barite (spelled baryte in British usage and in the mineral's IMA-approved name) is a mineral consisting of barium sulfate (BaSO₄). It is the principal ore of barium and is notable for its high specific gravity for a non-metallic mineral — the name derives from the Greek barys, "heavy". Barite commonly occurs as tabular or bladed crystals and in rosette aggregates known as "desert roses"; it is usually white or colourless but may be tinted blue, yellow or brown.

Occurrence

Barite forms in a wide range of environments: hydrothermal veins with lead and zinc ores, sedimentary deposits in limestones and sandstones, hot-spring deposits, and as nodules on the deep ocean floor. Major producers include China, India, Morocco, the United States and Iran. Historically, fine crystals came from localities such as Cumberland and Durham in England.

Uses

The largest use of barite, accounting for the great majority of world production, is as a weighting agent in drilling muds for oil and gas wells, where its density controls formation pressures. Barium sulfate's opacity to X-rays also makes it useful as a filler in paints, plastics and rubber, and purified barium sulfate is swallowed as a radiocontrast agent ("barium meal") in medical imaging of the digestive tract. Barite is the feedstock for barium chemicals such as barium carbonate and the pigment lithopone.
