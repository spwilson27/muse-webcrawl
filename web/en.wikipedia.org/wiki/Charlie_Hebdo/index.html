Charlie Hebdo (French pronunciation: [ʃaʁli ɛbdo]; "Charlie Weekly") is a French satirical weekly magazine featuring cartoons, reports, polemics and jokes. Irreverent and stridently non-conformist in tone, the publication is strongly secular and mocks organised religion, the far right, and political authority of all kinds. It became known worldwide after the January 2015 terrorist attack on its Paris offices.

History

The magazine traces its origins to Hara-Kiri, founded in 1960 by Georges Bernier ("Professeur Choron") and François Cavanna. When its weekly edition was banned in 1970 for mocking the official reaction to the death of Charles de Gaulle, the team relaunched it as Charlie Hebdo — the name also nodding to Charlie Brown of Peanuts. The first incarnation folded in 1981 amid falling sales. The magazine was relaunched in 1992 under editor Philippe Val, with cartoonists such as Cabu, Wolinski, Gébé and Charb, combining the old team with a new generation. Contributors including the cartoonist Riss (Laurent Sourisseau) and the writer Caroline Fourest kept its tradition of provocative satire. Persistent financial difficulties repeatedly brought it near closure.

Controversies

Charlie Hebdo has been repeatedly sued and threatened for its cartoons, including its republication of the Danish Muhammad cartoons in 2006 — an issue that ended in a 2007 court acquittal — and a 2011 firebombing of its offices after an issue "guest-edited" by Muhammad. Its defenders present it as an equal-opportunity offender in the French tradition of anticlerical satire; its critics have accused some cartoons of racism or Islamophobia.

2015 attack

On 7 January 2015, two gunmen, the brothers Saïd and Chérif Kouachi, forced their way into the magazine's Paris offices and murdered twelve people, including editor Stéphane "Charb" Charbonnier and the cartoonists Cabu, Wolinski, Tignous and Honoré, before being killed by police two days later. The attack, claimed by Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula, provoked worldwide solidarity under the slogan "Je suis Charlie" and huge marches in Paris. A "survivors' issue" published the following week sold millions of copies. The magazine continues to publish from secure premises, and a trial of accomplices was held in 2020.
