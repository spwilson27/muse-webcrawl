The Bowl Championship Series (BCS) was a selection system that created five bowl game match-ups involving ten of the top-ranked teams in NCAA Division I Football Bowl Subdivision (FBS) college football, including an opportunity for the top two teams to compete in the BCS National Championship Game. The system was in place for the 1998 through 2013 seasons and was replaced in 2014 by the College Football Playoff.

The BCS relied on a combination of human polls (the Associated Press poll — until it withdrew in 2005 — the Coaches Poll, and later the Harris Interactive poll) and computer rankings to produce BCS standings, from which bowl participants were selected. Its stated purposes were to match the No. 1 and No. 2 teams in a title game while preserving the traditional bowl system and conference tie-ins, especially the Rose Bowl's pairing of the Big Ten and Pac-10/Pac-12 champions.

History

The BCS grew out of earlier attempts to align the major bowls: the Bowl Coalition (1992–94) and the Bowl Alliance (1995–97), which failed to include the Big Ten, Pac-10, and the Rose Bowl. The BCS began in 1998, when those parties joined, allowing a unified No. 1 vs. No. 2 game that rotated among the Fiesta, Sugar, Orange, and Rose bowls. Beginning with the 2006 season, the championship became a separate fifth game, played a week after the traditional bowls at one of the four host sites.

Selections and formula

The final BCS standings combined three components, each weighted one-third: the Harris Interactive College Football Poll (later years), the USA Today Coaches Poll, and an average of six computer rankings (with each team's highest and lowest computer rank discarded). Champions of the six "automatic qualifying" (AQ) conferences — the ACC, Big East, Big Ten, Big 12, Pac-10/12, and SEC — received automatic berths in BCS bowls, while Notre Dame enjoyed a special arrangement and champions of non-AQ conferences could qualify under certain ranking conditions. The top two teams in the final standings, regardless of conference, met in the championship game.

Controversies

The BCS was persistently controversial. Only two teams could play for the championship, and several seasons produced disputed outcomes: in 2003, USC finished No. 1 in both human polls but was left out of the championship game (Oklahoma vs. LSU), producing a split national title when USC won the AP championship; in 2004, undefeated Auburn was excluded in favour of USC and Oklahoma; in 2008 and 2009, undefeated Utah and Boise State/TCU from non-AQ conferences were shut out. Critics also attacked the opacity of the computer rankings, conflicts of interest in the Coaches Poll, and the exclusion of most of the 120 FBS teams from the richest payouts. The system faced antitrust scrutiny, congressional hearings, and a long campaign by playoff advocates, including the book Death to the BCS (2010).

End of the BCS

In June 2012, university presidents approved a four-team playoff beginning with the 2014 season, administered by the College Football Playoff organisation. The last BCS championship game was played on January 6, 2014, at the Rose Bowl in Pasadena, where Florida State defeated Auburn 34–31. The four major BCS bowls survived as part of the "New Year's Six" rotation in the playoff era.
