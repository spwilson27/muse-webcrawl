Civil and political rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Civil and political rights are a class of rights that protect individuals' freedom from infringement by governments, social organisations and private individuals, and that ensure one's entitlement to participate, without discrimination or repression, in the civil and political life of society and the state. They are sometimes characterised as "first-generation" or "negative" rights (requiring forbearance), contrasted with economic, social and cultural rights ("second-generation", requiring provision) — a taxonomy whose hierarchy both Cold War camps exploited and the 1993 Vienna Declaration formally rejected in favour of indivisibility.

Content and instruments

The canonical list — codified in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966) — includes rights to life; freedom from torture and slavery; liberty and security of person; fair trial and due process; privacy; freedom of thought, conscience and religion; freedom of opinion, expression, assembly and association; the right to vote and take part in public affairs; equality before the law and minority rights. Enforcement machinery includes the UN Human Rights Committee (state reports, individual communications), regional courts (European, Inter-American, African) and domestic constitutional courts. Derogations are permitted in genuine emergencies for most rights (ICCPR Article 4), with a non-derogable core (life, torture, slavery, retroactive punishment among them) that defines the category's hard floor.
