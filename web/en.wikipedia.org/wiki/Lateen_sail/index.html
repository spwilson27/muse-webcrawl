Lateen sail

A lateen (from French latine, meaning "Latin") or latin-rig is a triangular sail set on a long yard mounted at an angle on the mast, running fore and aft. The lateen was one of the most important sail types of the ancient and medieval Mediterranean, and its ability to sail closer to the wind (to tack against the wind) than the square sails of earlier rigs made it a key development in the history of sailing. It is closely related to the settee rig and survives today in traditional craft such as the Arab dhow and the Maltese dgħajsa, as well as in the modern Sunfish recreational sailboat.

A dhow under lateen sail in the Indian Ocean.

History

Triangular fore-and-aft rigs developed in the eastern Mediterranean during late antiquity; the earliest depictions of lateen-type sails date from around the 2nd century BC to the Roman imperial period, and the rig became dominant in the Mediterranean by the early Middle Ages. It was adopted widely by Arab mariners, who spread it through the Indian Ocean, and it became characteristic of the dhow. In the Mediterranean it powered the Byzantine dromon, the Venetian galley, and later the caravel: Portuguese explorers of the 15th century used lateen-rigged caravels (the caravela latina) for voyages of discovery along the African coast, since the rig's windward performance suited exploration in unfamiliar waters. Columbus's ships Niña and Pinta were re-rigged from lateen to square sails for the Atlantic crossing of 1492.

Description

The lateen consists of a triangular sail laced to a long spar, the yard, which is slung from the mast at a low angle (typically around 30 degrees) so that the short forward end of the yard points down toward the bow and the long after end rises high above the stern. The forward corner of the sail (the tack) is secured near the bow. Because part of the sail lies forward of the mast, the lateen behaves in part like a foresail. Handling the rig can be laborious: on some traditional craft, tacking requires the yard to be dipped and swung around the mast to the new leeward side.

The settee rig is a variant in which the sail is quadrilateral with a short luff, combining features of the lateen and lug sails. The related crab-claw sail of the Pacific is also triangular but with a curved yard and boom.

Modern use

Lateen rigs remain in daily use on traditional fishing and cargo vessels around the Indian Ocean and East African coast. In recreational sailing, the simple lateen of the Sunfish and similar board boats — held in an A-frame of spars — has introduced millions of sailors to the rig. It is also seen on Nile feluccas and on the classic Maltese boats of the Grand Harbour.
