Chilean nationalization of copper

The Chilean nationalization of copper (Spanish: Nacionalización del cobre) was the progressive taking of foreign-owned copper mining operations into state control in Chile between 1966 and 1971. It culminated on 11 July 1971, when a constitutional amendment approved unanimously by Congress expropriated the large copper mines owned principally by the United States companies Anaconda and Kennecott. The anniversary is commemorated in Chile as the Day of National Dignity (Día de la Dignidad Nacional).

Background

Copper was Chile's dominant export, and its largest mines — Chuquicamata, El Teniente, El Salvador and others — belonged to subsidiaries of Anaconda Copper and Kennecott. From the 1950s, Chilean politicians across the spectrum demanded a greater share of the profits. Under President Eduardo Frei Montalva (1964–1970), the policy of "Chileanization" acquired majority stakes for the state in the big mines.

Nationalization under Allende

President Salvador Allende proposed full nationalization in 1970, and Congress approved the constitutional reform unanimously in July 1971. Compensation was calculated after deducting "excess profits" the companies were judged to have earned since 1955, which in the cases of Anaconda and Kennecott exceeded the book value of the assets, leaving little or no compensation. The decision deepened the conflict between the Allende government and the United States, contributing to the international pressure that preceded the 1973 coup d'état.

Legacy

The state copper corporation Codelco was formalised in 1976 under the military government, which nevertheless kept the great mines in state hands; a law under Pinochet also reserved 10 per cent of Codelco's export sales for the armed forces. Codelco remains the world's largest copper producer, and copper continues to be a pillar of the Chilean economy.
