The Jadids were Muslim modernist reformers within the Russian Empire in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Taking their name from usul-i jadid, the "new method" of teaching the alphabet phonetically in primary schools, they promoted educational reform, vernacular literature, and the modernization of Muslim society.

Origins

The movement is most closely associated with the Crimean Tatar educator Ismail Gasprinsky (1851–1914), who opened the first new-method school in Bakhchysarai in 1884 and founded the newspaper Tercüman ("The Translator"), which circulated among Turkic Muslims across the empire. Jadid schools spread through the Volga-Ural region, the Caucasus, and Russian Turkestan, offering secular subjects alongside religion.

Program and influence

Jadids founded publishing houses, libraries, and theatre troupes; debated women's education; and worked to standardize literary languages. After the 1905 Revolution they entered politics, and after 1917 many participated in national movements in Central Asia and Tatarstan. In the Soviet era the movement was suppressed and most of its leaders perished in the purges of the 1930s; its legacy was later reclaimed in the post-Soviet republics, especially Uzbekistan and Tatarstan.
