A priori and a posteriori

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

A priori ("from the earlier") and a posteriori ("from the later") are Latin phrases used in philosophy to distinguish two types of knowledge, justification, or argument. A priori knowledge is independent of experience — it can be known without investigating the world, as with truths of logic and mathematics. A posteriori knowledge depends on empirical evidence, as with most knowledge of the natural and social world. The terms originate in medieval scholastic logic, where they corresponded to reasoning from cause to effect (propter quid) and from effect to cause (quia), respectively.

Examples

The proposition "all bachelors are unmarried" is known a priori: once the meanings of the words are understood, no experience is needed to verify it, and no experience could refute it. By contrast, "it is raining outside" can be known only a posteriori, by observation. Pure mathematics is the standard example of an a priori discipline — George Gamow joked that even if counting stones were one's source for "7 + 5 = 12", the truth does not depend on the stones — while the sciences proceed largely a posteriori.

Kant

Immanuel Kant developed the distinction systematically in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781). For Kant, a priori judgments are marked by necessity and strict universality, which experience can never supply. He crossed this distinction with the analytic/synthetic distinction to yield his famous category of the synthetic a priori — judgments that extend knowledge yet are known independently of experience — which he claimed included arithmetic, geometry, and the fundamental principles of natural science, such as causality. Kant explained such knowledge by arguing that space, time, and the categories of the understanding are forms the mind imposes on experience.

Later debates

Rationalist philosophers held that substantial a priori knowledge of the world is possible; empiricists such as John Locke and David Hume restricted the a priori to relations of ideas. In twentieth-century analytic philosophy, W. V. O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) attacked the analytic/synthetic boundary underlying standard accounts. Saul Kripke's Naming and Necessity (1972) argued that the necessary/contingent, analytic/synthetic, and a priori/a posteriori distinctions come apart, giving examples of the necessary a posteriori (e.g., "water is H2O") and the contingent a priori (e.g., the standard metre). New theories of a priori justification — including appeals to intuition, understanding, and rational insight — remain an active area of epistemology.
