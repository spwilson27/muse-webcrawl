Magnet

A magnet is a material or object that produces a magnetic field, which exerts a force on other ferromagnetic materials, such as iron, nickel and cobalt, and attracts or repels other magnets. A permanent magnet is magnetised and retains its field, while an electromagnet produces a field only while electric current flows through it. Every magnet has at least one north and one south pole; like poles repel and unlike poles attract.

History

The first magnets were naturally occurring lodestones — pieces of the iron oxide mineral magnetite — known to ancient Greek, Chinese and Indian writers. Chinese compasses using lodestone were described by the Han dynasty period, and by the 12th century the magnetic compass was guiding navigation in China, Europe and the Islamic world. William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600) established that the Earth itself behaves as a great magnet; in the 19th century Oersted, Ampère and Faraday linked magnetism to electric current, and Maxwell unified the phenomena in electromagnetic theory.

How magnets work

Magnetism in materials arises from the magnetic moments of electrons, principally their spin. In ferromagnetic materials these moments align within microscopic regions called domains; magnetising the material aligns the domains so their fields add. Heating a magnet above its Curie temperature destroys the alignment and its magnetism. The strongest permanent magnets are rare-earth alloys such as neodymium–iron–boron (NdFeB, developed in the 1980s) and samarium–cobalt.

Uses

Magnets are fundamental to electric motors, generators, transformers, loudspeakers, magnetic storage and magnetic resonance imaging. Electromagnets lift scrap metal, confine plasma in fusion experiments, and propel magnetic-levitation trains. Small permanent magnets serve in everything from refrigerator doors to the compasses in smartphones.
