Socorro dove

The Socorro dove (Zenaida graysoni) is a dove that is extinct in the wild. It was endemic to Socorro Island in the Revillagigedo Islands off the west coast of Mexico. Last sighted in the wild in 1972, the species survives only in captivity, with a few hundred individuals held in zoos and private aviaries in Europe and North America, and a reintroduction programme aims to return it to its island home. It was formerly considered a subspecies of the mourning dove (Zenaida macroura) but is now recognised as a distinct species, named in honour of the American ornithologist Andrew Jackson Grayson.

Description

The Socorro dove is a medium-sized, largely terrestrial dove, 26.5–34 cm long, with warm cinnamon-buff plumage, a darker head with an iridescent sheen, and long legs suited to its ground-foraging island life. Having evolved without mammalian predators, it is notably tame — a trait that contributed to its downfall once predators were introduced.

Decline and extinction in the wild

The species declined through habitat degradation by introduced sheep, which stripped the island's understorey, and predation by feral cats introduced in the 1950s. The last confirmed wild sighting was in 1972, and expeditions in the 1980s and 1990s found no survivors. Fortunately, Grayson and others had taken birds into captivity in the 1920s, and all living Socorro doves descend from that stock; genetic studies have had to guard against hybridisation with the closely related mourning dove in the captive population.

Reintroduction

Sheep were eradicated from Socorro Island, and a breeding and reintroduction project — involving Mexican authorities and international zoo partners — has built breeding aviaries on the island, with the long-term goal of releasing genetically verified birds once cat control and habitat recovery make survival feasible.
