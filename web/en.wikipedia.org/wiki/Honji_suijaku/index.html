Honji suijaku

Honji suijaku (本地垂迹), literally "original ground and manifest traces", is a theory in Japanese Buddhism according to which Shinto kami are manifestations (suijaku) of universal buddhas and bodhisattvas (honji), who take local form in Japan to save sentient beings. The theory underpinned the fusion of Shinto and Buddhism (shinbutsu-shūgō) for over a millennium.

Development

The idea developed from the Nara period onward and became systematised in the Heian and Kamakura periods. Shrine-temple complexes (jingū-ji) were built in which kami received Buddhist services, and kami were given Buddhist titles such as gongen ("provisional manifestation"). Major pairings included Hachiman with Amida and the Sannō deity with Shaka.

Inversion

In the medieval period some schools, notably in Ise and Yoshida Shinto, inverted the hierarchy (han-honji suijaku), claiming the kami were the original ground and the buddhas their traces — an assertion of Shinto priority within the same framework.

End and legacy

The Meiji government's policy of separating Shinto and Buddhism (shinbutsu bunri, 1868) officially dismantled the system, though its influence persists in Japanese religious practice and culture.
