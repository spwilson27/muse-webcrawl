School corporal punishment

Implements such as the cane were historically used in schools

School corporal punishment is the deliberate infliction of physical pain on a student by a teacher or school administrator as a penalty for misbehaviour. Historically common throughout the world, it has included caning, paddling, strapping, and slapping, and was justified by the doctrine that teachers acted in loco parentis.

History

Physical discipline was a routine feature of schooling from antiquity through the 19th century. Instruments varied by country: the cane in Britain and its former colonies, the paddle in the United States, the tawse in Scotland, and the ruler or ferule in many classrooms.

Legal status

Sweden became the first country to ban all corporal punishment of children, including in schools, in 1979 (school bans had existed there since 1958). Most European countries prohibited school corporal punishment during the 20th century; England and Wales banned it in state schools in 1987 and in all schools by 2003 (Scotland and Northern Ireland followed similar paths). As of the early 21st century, school corporal punishment remains lawful in a number of U.S. states — the Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality in Ingraham v. Wright (1977) — though its use has declined sharply and many districts prohibit it locally. It remains practised in parts of Africa and Asia, despite widespread legal reforms and opposition from children's-rights bodies under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child.

Debate

Opponents cite research associating corporal punishment with harm to mental health and learning, and argue it is ineffective as discipline. Defenders argue it is a quick, deterrent correction and a matter of local or parental rights.
