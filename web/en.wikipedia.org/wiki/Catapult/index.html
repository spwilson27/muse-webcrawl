Catapult

A catapult is a ballistic device used to launch a projectile a great distance without the aid of gunpowder or other propellants — particularly various types of ancient and medieval siege engines. The word comes from the Greek καταπέλτης (katapeltēs), from κατά ("downward") and πάλλω ("to hurl"). Catapults stored mechanical energy in tension, torsion, or gravity and released it suddenly to hurl stones, bolts, and other missiles at fortifications or enemy troops.

Types

The earliest catapults, developed in the Greek world in the 4th century BC, included the gastraphetes and the torsion-powered ballista and onager, whose arms were driven by the spring of twisted skeins of sinew or hair rope. Diadochi and Roman armies used them extensively, and engineers such as Archimedes were famed for defensive engines at Syracuse. In medieval warfare the dominant design became the counterweight trebuchet, in which a falling weight swings a long arm tipped with a sling — a machine developed independently in China and the Mediterranean world. Other types include the mangonel and the traction trebuchet, powered by crews pulling on ropes.

Decline and later uses

Gunpowder artillery made catapults obsolete by the late Middle Ages, though the principle survives. In the modern era, aircraft carriers use steam and electromagnetic catapults to launch aircraft from short decks, and the word is also applied to devices such as ejection-seat mechanisms and the launching systems of gliders. Catapults remain popular in engineering education and in competitions such as pumpkin chucking.
