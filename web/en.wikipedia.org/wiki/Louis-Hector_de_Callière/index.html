Louis-Hector de Callière (12 November 1648 – 26 May 1703) was a French soldier and administrator who served as governor of Montreal and then as the 13th Governor General of New France, succeeding the Comte de Frontenac. His crowning achievement was the Great Peace of Montreal of 1701, which ended decades of war between the French and the Iroquois Confederacy.

Early career

Born into a Norman military family, Callière served in the French army and navy before arriving in Canada in 1684 as governor of Montreal. He championed a more aggressive military posture against the Iroquois and the English, proposed the (disastrous) 1689 expedition against New York that was abandoned in favour of Frontenac's raids, and acted as Frontenac's trusted lieutenant during King William's War.

Governor General

On Frontenac's death in 1698, Callière — who had been acting governor — was confirmed as Governor General. He consolidated French alliances with the Indigenous nations of the Great Lakes, managed the fragile neutrality of the Iroquois, and prepared the colony for the War of the Spanish Succession.

Great Peace of Montreal

In the summer of 1701 Callière assembled some 1,300 Indigenous delegates from roughly forty nations at Montreal. After weeks of negotiation and ceremony, on 4 August 1701 the delegates signed the Great Peace, ending the Beaver Wars that had raged since the 1640s: the Iroquois agreed to neutrality in conflicts between France and England, and disputes were to be referred to French arbitration. The peace secured New France's western alliance system for a generation.

Death

Callière died suddenly of a haemorrhage on 26 May 1703, aged 54, and was buried in Quebec City. Point Callières in Old Montreal — the site of the city's founding — and the Pointe-à-Callière museum of archaeology and history commemorate his family name and governorship.
