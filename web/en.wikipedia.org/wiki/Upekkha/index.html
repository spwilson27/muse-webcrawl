Upekkha

Upekkha: even-mindedness toward all beings and experiences

Upekkhā (Pali: উপেক্খা; Sanskrit: upekṣā) is the Buddhist concept of equanimity. It is a state of even-mindedness and impartiality — a balanced, clear response to experience that is neither pulled by attraction nor pushed by aversion. Upekkha is one of the four brahmavihāras ("divine abidings") alongside loving-kindness (mettā), compassion (karuṇā), and sympathetic joy (muditā), and it is also the last of the seven factors of enlightenment (bojjhaṅga) and one of the ten perfections (pāramī).

Meaning

The word derives from roots meaning "to look over" or "to observe from a distance" — seeing things as they are without being swept away by them. Buddhist commentators stress that true equanimity is not indifference or cold detachment. In the Visuddhimagga, Buddhaghosa distinguishes genuine upekkha from its "near enemy", the equanimity of ignorance based on disregard. Authentic equanimity is grounded in wisdom: it understands that beings are owners of their actions (kamma) and that all conditioned things are impermanent.

In meditation

Upekkha is both cultivated directly — as in the brahmavihāra meditations, where equanimity toward a neutral person, a benefactor, a hostile person, and oneself is developed — and arises naturally as a fruit of insight practice. It predominates in the fourth jhāna, which is characterized by "purity of mindfulness due to equanimity". In insight (vipassanā) meditation, "equanimity about formations" (saṅkhārupekkhā) is a mature stage of insight immediately preceding enlightenment.

Reflection

The traditional reflection for cultivating equanimity is: "All beings are the owners of their actions; whatever they do, for good or for evil, to that will they fall heir." This contemplation balances the other divine abidings so that loving-kindness, compassion, and joy do not degenerate into attachment, grief, or elation.
