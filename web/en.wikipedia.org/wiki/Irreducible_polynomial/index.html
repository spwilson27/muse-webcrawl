Irreducible polynomial

Irreducible polynomial

Field: Algebra

Example: x² + 1 over ℝ

Tests: Eisenstein's criterion, rational root theorem

In mathematics, an irreducible polynomial is a non-constant polynomial that cannot be factored into the product of two non-constant polynomials over a given field (or integral domain). Irreducibility depends on the coefficient ring: x² − 2 is irreducible over the rationals but factors over the reals, while x² + 1 is irreducible over the reals but factors over the complex numbers.

Properties

Over a field F, the polynomial ring F[x] is a unique factorization domain, and its irreducible elements play the role that prime numbers play in the integers. Every non-constant polynomial over a field factors uniquely into irreducibles. A polynomial of degree two or three over a field is irreducible if and only if it has no root in that field; this fails for degree four and above.

Criteria and examples

  • Rational root theorem: limits possible rational roots of integer polynomials.
  • Eisenstein's criterion: if a prime p divides all coefficients except the leading one, and p² does not divide the constant term, the polynomial is irreducible over ℚ.
  • Modular reduction: if a polynomial remains irreducible modulo a prime, it is irreducible over ℚ.

Finite fields rely on counting irreducible polynomials of each degree, which underpins constructions of field extensions and error-correcting codes.
