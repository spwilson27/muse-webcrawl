Seismic refraction

Seismic refraction is a geophysical method that uses the refraction of seismic waves by rock or soil layers to characterise subsurface conditions and geological structures. When a seismic wave crosses a boundary between materials of different seismic velocity, it is refracted according to Snell's law; at the critical angle, the wave travels along the boundary and continuously re-radiates energy back to the surface, where it can be detected by geophones.

Method

A seismic source — a hammer blow, weight drop, or explosive charge — generates waves recorded by a line of geophones. Analysts plot the first-arrival times against distance (a travel-time curve). Direct arrivals dominate at short offsets, but beyond a crossover distance the critically refracted arrivals from a faster underlying layer arrive first. From the slopes and intercepts of the travel-time segments, the velocities and depths of successive layers can be computed. Techniques such as the plus-minus method and generalised reciprocal method handle dipping and irregular interfaces.

Applications

Refraction surveys are used in engineering and environmental geophysics to determine depth to bedrock, the thickness of weathered layers, rippability of rock for excavation, groundwater depth, and the location of faults and buried channels. Large-scale refraction experiments, using distant explosions or earthquakes, revealed the major discontinuities of the Earth's interior: Andrija Mohorovičić discovered the crust–mantle boundary (the Moho) in 1909 from refracted earthquake waves.

Limitations

The method requires velocities to increase with depth; a low-velocity layer beneath a faster one is "hidden" from first-arrival analysis, and thin layers may not produce identifiable arrivals. Seismic refraction tomography mitigates some limitations by inverting dense arrival-time data for a two-dimensional velocity model.
