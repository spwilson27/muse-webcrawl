Ribozyme

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ribozyme is an RNA molecule capable of catalysing a chemical reaction, like a protein enzyme. Their discovery in the early 1980s by Sidney Altman and Thomas Cech (who shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry) overturned the dogma that only proteins catalyse biological reactions.

The first ribozymes found were self-splicing introns in the ciliate Tetrahymena (Cech) and RNase P, the enzyme that trims transfer RNA (Altman). Most consequentially, the ribosome itself is a ribozyme: its peptidyl-transferase centre, which builds proteins, is made of RNA — evidence central to the "RNA world" hypothesis, which proposes that early life used RNA both to store information and to catalyse reactions, before DNA and proteins took over those roles.

Natural ribozymes now known include the hammerhead, hairpin and hepatitis delta virus ribozymes; artificial ones are evolved in the laboratory for research and potential therapy, and they extend the reach of catalysis concepts deep into molecular evolution and the metabolism of the earliest cells.
