Meridian (astronomy)

In astronomy, the meridian is the great circle passing through the celestial poles, the zenith, and the nadir of an observer's location. Because it is fixed relative to the observer's horizon, a celestial object crosses the meridian at its highest point in the sky (upper culmination) and, for circumpolar objects, again at its lowest point (lower culmination).

Definitions

The meridian is divided by the zenith into the northern and southern halves. The plane of the meridian is perpendicular to the local horizontal plane and contains the north and south points of the horizon. The intersection of the meridian with the celestial sphere passing through the celestial poles makes it a reference circle for measuring hour angle.

Observational significance

An object crossing the meridian is at its maximum altitude, making meridian crossing the traditional moment for measuring a star's position. Historically, transit instruments and meridian circles — telescopes constrained to move only along the meridian — were the principal tools of astrometry, used to determine right ascensions and to keep time. Local sidereal time is defined by which hour circle is currently on the meridian.

The moment a celestial body crosses the meridian is called its transit or culmination. For the Sun, upper transit corresponds approximately to local apparent noon.
