Twelve spies

The twelve spies are figures mentioned in the Book of Numbers (chapters 13–14) of the Hebrew Bible. According to the narrative, after the Exodus from Egypt, Moses sent one leader from each of the twelve tribes of Israel to scout the land of Canaan, which God had promised to the Israelites, and to report on its inhabitants, cities, soil, and produce.

The narrative

The spies explored Canaan for forty days, travelling from the wilderness of Paran through the Negev to Hebron and as far as Rehob and Lebo-Hamath. They returned carrying a cluster of grapes so large it had to be borne on a pole between two men, along with pomegranates and figs, from the valley of Eshcol.

Ten of the twelve spies brought back a discouraging report: the land was fertile, "flowing with milk and honey," but its people were powerful, its cities heavily fortified, and among its inhabitants were the Anakim, giants before whom the spies felt "like grasshoppers." Only Caleb son of Jephunneh (of Judah) and Joshua son of Nun (of Ephraim) urged the people to trust God and proceed.

Consequences

The Israelites, terrified by the majority report, refused to enter the land and spoke of returning to Egypt. As punishment, God condemned that generation to wander in the wilderness for forty years — one year for each day of the spies' mission — until all the adults who had left Egypt had died, except Caleb and Joshua, who alone were promised entry into the Promised Land. The ten faithless spies died of a plague. The story is traditionally read on the fast of Tisha B'Av, and the spies' report (the lashon hara, or evil speech, against the land) is a major subject of rabbinic commentary.
