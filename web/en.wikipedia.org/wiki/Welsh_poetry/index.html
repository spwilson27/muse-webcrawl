Welsh poetry is poetry written in the Welsh language, one of the oldest continuous literary traditions in Europe. It is renowned for its strict metres and the intricate sound-patterning of cynghanedd, and it remains central to Welsh cultural life through the eisteddfodau.

Early poetry

The earliest surviving Welsh poems are attributed to the 6th-century bards Taliesin and Aneirin, preserved in later manuscripts such as the Book of Taliesin and the Book of Aneirin, which contains Y Gododdin, an elegy for warriors slain at the Battle of Catraeth around 600.

Poets of the Princes and the Nobles

Court poets (the Gogynfeirdd) served the Welsh princes until the Edwardian conquest of 1282–83. Thereafter, poets of the nobility (Beirdd yr Uchelwyr) were sustained by gentry patrons; the greatest was Dafydd ap Gwilym (c. 1315–1370), whose lively cywyddau on love and nature are considered the peak of medieval Welsh verse.

Strict metre and cynghanedd

Welsh strict-metre poetry uses combinations of alliteration and internal rhyme — cynghanedd — within metres such as the four-line englyn and the rhyming couplet cywydd. Mastery of the system historically required a long bardic apprenticeship.

Later centuries and revival

The tradition survived through hymn-writing and the free-metre lyric of figures like Huw Morys and, in the 20th century, Waldo Williams and R. S. Thomas (who wrote mainly in English). The National Eisteddfod continues to crown and chair winning bards each year.
