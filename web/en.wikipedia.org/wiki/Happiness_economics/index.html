Happiness economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The economics of happiness, or happiness economics, is the theoretical, qualitative and quantitative study of happiness and quality of life, including positive and negative affect, well-being and life satisfaction, and their relationship to economic variables. Rather than relying solely on conventional measures such as GDP, the field examines survey data on how people evaluate their own lives.

Key findings

The field was pioneered by Richard Easterlin, whose 1974 paper identified the Easterlin paradox: within countries, richer people are happier than poorer people, but average happiness in rich countries does not rise in proportion to income over time, a finding debated ever since. Later research by Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers argued that the income–happiness relationship is robust, while others emphasise diminishing returns and the importance of relative income—people compare themselves with others.

Happiness research consistently finds unemployment to be one of the largest sources of misery, with effects beyond the loss of income; inflation, inequality, health, social relationships and job security also matter. Air pollution and long commutes reduce reported well-being, while generous welfare states are associated with higher life satisfaction.

Policy

Governments and bodies such as the OECD have adopted well-being measures to supplement GDP: Bhutan's Gross National Happiness, the United Kingdom's Measuring National Well-being programme, and New Zealand's Wellbeing Budget are examples. Critics question whether governments should target happiness directly and whether survey measures are reliable enough to guide policy.
