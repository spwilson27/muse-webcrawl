Hybrid electric vehicle

A hybrid electric vehicle (HEV) is a type of hybrid vehicle that combines a conventional internal combustion engine (ICE) system with an electric propulsion system (hybrid vehicle drivetrain). The presence of the electric powertrain is intended to achieve either better fuel economy than a conventional vehicle or better performance.

How it works

HEVs recover energy normally lost during braking (regenerative braking) and store it in a battery. The electric motor assists the engine during acceleration and can power the vehicle alone at low speeds or when stopped; the engine can also shut off at idle (start-stop). Full hybrids such as the Toyota Prius use a power-split device to blend power sources continuously.

Types

  • Mild hybrids — the motor assists but cannot drive the car alone (48-volt systems).
  • Full hybrids — can drive on electric power alone for short distances.
  • Plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) — larger batteries chargeable from the grid, with substantial electric-only range.

History and market

Early hybrids date to the Lohner-Porsche of 1900, but the modern era began with the Toyota Prius (Japan 1997, global 2000) and Honda Insight (1999). Hybrids spread across vehicle segments, and hybrid technology is also applied to buses, trucks, trains and ships.

Benefits and drawbacks

HEVs offer improved fuel economy and lower CO₂ emissions than comparable petrol cars, especially in urban driving, but cost more to buy, carry the weight of two powertrains, and still burn fossil fuel.
