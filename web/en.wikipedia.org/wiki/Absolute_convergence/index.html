Absolute convergence

In mathematics, an infinite series of numbers is said to converge absolutely (or to be absolutely convergent) if the sum of the absolute values of its terms is finite. That is, the series Σan converges absolutely if Σ|an| converges. Absolute convergence is a stronger property than ordinary (conditional) convergence: every absolutely convergent series is convergent, but the converse is false.

Definition and consequences

Absolute convergence guarantees several convenient properties. The terms of an absolutely convergent series may be rearranged in any order without changing the sum, and the Cauchy product of two absolutely convergent series converges to the product of their sums. These properties make absolutely convergent series behave much like finite sums.

By contrast, a series that converges but not absolutely is called conditionally convergent. The alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯ is the standard example: it converges to ln 2, but the sum of its absolute values is the divergent harmonic series. By the Riemann series theorem, a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any desired value, or even to diverge.

Generalisation

The concept extends beyond series of real or complex numbers. In a normed vector space, a series Σxn is absolutely convergent if Σ‖xn‖ converges, and in complete spaces (Banach spaces) absolute convergence implies convergence. Similarly, an improper integral is said to converge absolutely if the integral of the absolute value of the integrand is finite; this is a key hypothesis in applying Fubini's theorem to interchange the order of integration.

Tests

Common methods for establishing absolute convergence include the comparison test, the ratio test, and the root test, each of which is applied to the series of absolute values.
