Viral haemorrhagic fevers (VHFs) are a diverse group of animal and human illnesses. A VHF is caused by any of five distinct families of RNA viruses: the family Arenaviridae, the family Filoviridae, the order Bunyavirales, the family Flaviviridae, and the family Rhabdoviridae. All types of VHF are characterized by fever and bleeding disorders and all can progress to high fever, shock and, in a lot of cases, death. Some of the VHF agents cause relatively mild illnesses, such as the Scandinavian nephropathia epidemica (a hantavirus), while others, such as Ebola virus, cause severe, life-threatening disease.

Signs and symptoms

Signs and symptoms of VHFs include (by definition of a hemorrhagic fever) a high fever. Other common signs include headache, fatigue, abdominal pain, and muscle pains. When the fever is worse, symptoms include bleeding under the skin, in internal organs, or from body orifices like the mouth, eyes, or ears; nervous system malfunction; coma; delirium; and, in severe cases, death. Patients with severe cases often show signs of kidney failure. Bleeding itself is rarely the direct cause of death; fluid loss and shock are more often responsible.

Causes

Five families of RNA viruses have been recognised as being able to cause hemorrhagic fevers. These include the arenaviruses (such as Lassa virus and Junín virus, the cause of Argentine hemorrhagic fever), the filoviruses (Ebola virus and Marburg virus), the bunyaviruses (such as Rift Valley fever virus, Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever virus, and the hantaviruses), and the flaviviruses (yellow fever virus and dengue virus). Most VHF viruses are zoonoses, maintained in animal hosts—rodents or bats, for example—and transmitted to people through contact with infected animals, their excreta, or arthropod vectors such as ticks and mosquitoes. Ebola and Marburg can spread from person to person through contact with blood and body fluids, which makes infection control critical during outbreaks.

Diagnosis

Definitive diagnosis is usually made in a reference laboratory using tests such as reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for antigens or IgM antibodies, virus isolation in cell culture, and, increasingly, genomic sequencing. Because several VHFs are highly infectious and require high containment, samples are handled under strict biosafety conditions.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment is generally supportive: maintenance of fluids and electrolytes, oxygen, blood pressure support, and treatment of complicating infections. The antiviral drug ribavirin is effective for some VHFs, notably Lassa fever, when given early. Specific monoclonal antibody treatments and a vaccine exist for Ebola virus disease (Zaire ebolavirus), and a vaccine protects against yellow fever. Prevention centers on avoiding contact with natural hosts and vectors, safe burial practices, isolation of patients, and personal protective equipment for health-care workers.
