Prosopagnosia

Prosopagnosia (from Greek prosopon, "face," and agnosia, "not knowing"), commonly called face blindness, is a cognitive disorder of face perception in which the ability to recognise familiar faces — including, in severe cases, one's own face in a mirror — is impaired, while other aspects of visual processing and intellectual functioning remain intact. Affected people typically recognise others by voice, hairstyle, gait, or clothing instead. The term was coined by the German neurologist Joachim Bodamer in 1947, who described soldiers with brain injuries sustained in the Second World War.

People with prosopagnosia see faces but cannot recognise them

Types and causes

Acquired prosopagnosia results from brain damage, usually to the inferior occipital and fusiform regions of the right hemisphere (or bilaterally), including the area known as the fusiform face area (FFA). Causes include stroke, head trauma, encephalitis, and neurodegenerative disease. Researchers distinguish apperceptive forms, in which the face percept itself is faulty, from associative forms, in which a normal percept cannot be linked to identity and memory.

Developmental (congenital) prosopagnosia occurs without any obvious brain lesion and has been present since childhood; it often runs in families, suggesting a hereditary component, and surveys suggest it may affect roughly 2–2.5% of the population to some degree. Many developmental prosopagnosics do not realise their deficit until adulthood, having assumed everyone recognises faces as unreliably as they do.

Diagnosis and impact

Diagnosis uses tests such as the Cambridge Face Memory Test and the Cambridge Face Perception Test, together with famous-faces recognition tasks. There is no cure, but people develop compensatory strategies, and training studies have shown modest improvements. The condition can cause substantial social anxiety and embarrassment, as failures to recognise colleagues, friends, or family members are easily misread as rudeness or indifference. Famous people reported to have prosopagnosia include the neurologist Oliver Sacks, who wrote about it in The Mind's Eye, the primatologist Jane Goodall, and the artist Chuck Close. Prosopagnosia has also been central to cognitive neuroscience as evidence that face recognition is handled by specialised neural machinery separable from general object recognition.
