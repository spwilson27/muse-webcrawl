Reclaimed lumber

Reclaimed lumber is wood that has been salvaged from its original application and reused. It most commonly comes from old barns, factories, warehouses, water towers, and other structures built in the 18th, 19th, and early 20th centuries, though it may also be recovered from retired ships, wine barrels, boxcars, and gym bleachers.

Characteristics

Reclaimed lumber is prized for qualities that newly milled wood often lacks. Much of it was cut from old-growth forests and is denser, harder, and more stable than modern plantation-grown timber. It also carries a distinctive appearance — weathering, nail holes, saw marks, and patina — that gives finished pieces a rustic character. Species commonly recovered include American chestnut, longleaf pine (heart pine), Douglas fir, and old oak.

Processing

Before reuse, reclaimed boards must be de-nailed, cleaned, and often re-milled and kiln-dried to remove insects and moisture. Metal detection is used to locate hidden fasteners that could damage planers and saws.

Environmental aspects

Reusing lumber reduces demand for new timber, diverts material from landfills, and preserves the carbon stored in the wood. It also keeps historically significant old-growth material in service. However, older wood may contain lead paint or chemical treatments that require care in handling.
