Trimethylaminuria

Trimethylaminuria (TMAU), also known as fish odour syndrome or fish malodour syndrome, is a rare metabolic disorder that causes a defect in the normal production of the enzyme flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3). As a result, trimethylamine — a compound with a strong smell of rotting fish produced by gut bacteria from dietary precursors such as choline and carnitine — is not properly oxidised to odourless trimethylamine N-oxide and is instead released in the person's sweat, urine and breath.

Causes

The primary form is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern and is caused by mutations in the FMO3 gene on chromosome 1. Secondary (acquired) forms can occur with liver or kidney disease, hormonal changes, or an unusually high dietary load of precursors overwhelming normal enzyme capacity. Symptoms often worsen around puberty and, in women, around menstruation.

Management

There is no cure, but symptoms can often be reduced by restricting dietary choline and carnitine (found in eggs, liver, fish and legumes), using low-pH soaps and body lotions to reduce the volatility of trimethylamine on the skin, and, in some cases, short courses of antibiotics or activated charcoal. The condition is physically harmless in itself but can cause severe psychological and social distress, including depression and social isolation.
