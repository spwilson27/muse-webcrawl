Cornelius Vermuyden

Born: 1595, Tholen, Netherlands

Died: 1677 (buried St Margaret's, Westminster)

Nationality: Dutch (naturalized English)

Occupation: Engineer

Known for: Draining the English Fens; Hatfield Chase; the Dutch River

Sir Cornelius Vermuyden (1595 – 1677) was a Dutch engineer who introduced Dutch land-drainage methods to England. He is best known for his work draining the Fens of East Anglia and the Hatfield Chase in South Yorkshire, projects that permanently transformed the English landscape and provoked lasting controversy among the commoners whose fenland livelihoods they destroyed.

Early life

Vermuyden was born on the island of Tholen in Zeeland into a family of engineers; his father, Bartholomeus Vermuyden, had worked on embankments and drainage. By 1621 Cornelius was in England, working on embanking work at Dagenham on the Thames, where a breach in the river wall threatened riverside marshland, and he was naturalised in 1630s England.

Hatfield Chase and the Isle of Axholme

In 1626 Charles I contracted Vermuyden to drain Hatfield Chase, a royal hunting ground of some 70,000 acres of marshy land in Yorkshire, the East Riding, and Lincolnshire, with Vermuyden and his Dutch partners (the "Participants") to receive a third of the reclaimed land. He diverted the rivers Don, Idle, and Torne: the Don was carried north in a new channel — the Dutch River — to join the Ouse at Goole. The works were technically successful but bitterly resented: fen commoners lost grazing, fishing, and fowling rights, riots broke out, sluices were attacked, and litigation followed for decades, culminating in the destruction of works and further riots during the Civil War, when the drainage was an easy target of local grievance against the Crown.

The Great Level of the Fens

In 1630 and again in the 1650s Vermuyden directed the drainage of the Great Level of the Fens for the Earl of Bedford and his company of Adventurers. His plan straightened and embanked the rivers of the Wash hinterland: the Old and New Bedford Rivers were cut across the Fens to the Denver Sluice, with the vast washes between them designed to store floodwater, and the Forty Foot and other drains re-organised the waters of the southern fens. Knighted in 1629, he continued under the Commonwealth, completing the second phase of the fen drainage in 1652. His Discourse Touching the Draining of the Great Fennes (1642) set out the principles — embanked rivers carried to outfalls, drainage districts, and washes — that still underlie fenland water management, though continuous pumping (first by windmills, then steam, diesel, and electric pumps) proved necessary as the drained peat shrank and land levels fell below river level.

Legacy

Vermuyden's name is commemorated in South Yorkshire, notably in schools in Armthorpe, and he is remembered both as the founder of modern English land drainage and as the agent of one of the largest enclosures of common wetland in English history.
