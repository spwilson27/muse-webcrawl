Acheson process

The Acheson process is an industrial process for synthesising silicon carbide (SiC) and, in modified form, graphite. It was invented by the American chemist Edward Goodrich Acheson, who patented it in 1893 and commercialised silicon carbide under the name "Carborundum".

Process

A mixture of silica (sand) and carbon (usually petroleum coke), with sawdust and salt as additives, is packed around a graphite core in a large electric resistance furnace. Passing a heavy current through the core heats the charge to roughly 1,600–2,500 °C. At these temperatures the reaction SiO2 + 3C → SiC + 2CO proceeds, forming a cylindrical ingot of silicon carbide crystals around the core, with purer green and black grades sorted after cooling.

Products and uses

Silicon carbide from the Acheson process is used as an abrasive, in refractories and heating elements, and as a metallurgical additive. Acheson also adapted the high-temperature furnace to graphitise carbon, producing synthetic graphite used for electrodes and lubricants. Modern wide-bandgap semiconductor wafers of SiC are grown by other methods, but bulk abrasive-grade material is still largely made in Acheson furnaces.
