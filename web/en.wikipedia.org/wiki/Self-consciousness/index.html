Self-consciousness

Self-consciousness is an acute sense of self-awareness: a preoccupation with oneself, as opposed to the philosophical concept of self-awareness, which is the awareness that one exists as an individual being. In psychology the term usually refers not to awareness of the self in general but to an intense, often uncomfortable, attention to oneself as a social object — how one appears, sounds, and is judged by others.

Psychology

Self-consciousness is associated with shyness and embarrassment, and in exaggerated form with social anxiety. Researchers distinguish private self-consciousness (attention to one's inner thoughts and feelings) from public self-consciousness (awareness of oneself as seen by others), measured by instruments such as the Self-Consciousness Scale of Fenigstein, Scheier, and Buss (1975). Developmentally, self-conscious emotions such as embarrassment emerge in toddlers together with mirror self-recognition, around 18 months of age. Objective self-awareness theory (Duval and Wicklund) holds that focusing attention on the self prompts comparison with standards, which can motivate behaviour change or escape from the self-aware state.

Philosophical usage

In philosophy, especially in the German tradition from Fichte and Hegel, self-consciousness (Selbstbewusstsein) denotes the mind's constitutive awareness of itself, central to accounts of personal identity, recognition, and intersubjectivity — Hegel's master–slave dialectic being the classic treatment of self-consciousness achieved through another's recognition. Everyday usage, however, usually carries the psychological sense of awkward self-attention, and the two meanings should not be confused.
