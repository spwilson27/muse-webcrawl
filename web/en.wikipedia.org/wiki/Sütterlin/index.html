Sütterlin

TypeHandwriting script (cursive)
CreatorLudwig Sütterlin (1865–1917)

Sütterlin (full name Sütterlinschrift, "Sütterlin script") is a form of German Kurrent handwriting designed by the graphic artist Ludwig Sütterlin and taught in German schools from about 1915 until it was banned in 1941. Together with the printed Fraktur typefaces, it is the script most commonly encountered in German documents of the early 20th century, and the one modern readers often loosely mean by "old German handwriting".

Design and adoption

Commissioned by the Prussian ministry of culture in 1911, Sütterlin's script simplified the older Kurrent ductus for steel-nib writing: letters are upright, evenly slanted strokes with distinctive forms — for example a lowercase e resembling two vertical strokes — intended to be easier for children to learn. Introduced in Prussia in 1915, it became the standard school script throughout Germany by the mid-1920s and was used for both Latin and German alphabets.

Abolition and legacy

In 1941 the Nazi regime, having already dropped Fraktur print in favour of Antiqua, prohibited Sütterlin and all "Schwabacher Jewish letters" by decree, replacing it with a Latin-based Normalschrift ("German normal handwriting"). After 1945 a modified Latin cursive (the Lateinische Ausgangsschrift, 1953) became standard. Because generations of letters, diaries and official files are written in Sütterlin, the ability to read it remains an essential skill for genealogists and historians of modern Germany.
