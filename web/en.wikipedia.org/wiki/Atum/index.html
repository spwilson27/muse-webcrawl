Atum

Atum (Egyptian: jtm(w)), sometimes rendered Atem or Tem, is the primordial god of creation in the mythology of ancient Egypt. He is the central deity of the creation theology of Heliopolis, where he was regarded as the self-created first being who emerged from the primordial waters of Nu and brought the other gods and the world into existence.

Name in hieroglyphs: Atum (jtm)

Cult centre: Heliopolis

Symbol: Double crown, scarab, primordial mound

Offspring: Shu and Tefnut

Mythology

In the Heliopolitan creation account preserved in the Pyramid Texts and later sources, Atum arose upon the primordial mound (benben) from the waters of chaos. Alone, he produced the first divine pair: by spitting, sneezing, or (in some versions) masturbating, he created Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture), who in turn produced Geb (earth) and Nut (sky), and then Osiris, Isis, Set and Nephthys—completing the Ennead of Heliopolis.

Role and iconography

Atum was closely associated with the sun, particularly the setting sun, complementing Ra as the midday sun and Khepri as the rising sun; the three were sometimes combined as aspects of a single solar deity. He was also a chthonic figure connected to the afterlife, protecting the dead king. In art, Atum is usually depicted as a man wearing the double crown (pschent) of Upper and Lower Egypt, sometimes holding the was-sceptre and ankh.

Worship

His main cult centre was Heliopolis (Iunu), one of the oldest and most important religious centres of Egypt. Temples and priesthoods dedicated to Atum existed throughout Egyptian history, and he remained prominent into the Late Period and the Greco-Roman era.
