Hidden curriculum

A hidden curriculum is a side effect of schooling: the lessons that are learned but not openly intended — such as norms, values and beliefs conveyed through the structure of the school, its routines, and the social relationships within it — distinct from the formal, explicit curriculum. The term was popularised by Philip W. Jackson's Life in Classrooms (1968).

Content of the hidden curriculum

Through practices such as queuing, waiting, obeying bells and schedules, seeking approval, and competing for grades, pupils learn punctuality, docility, conformity and respect for authority — what Jackson called the "crowds, praise and power" of classroom life. Sociologists such as Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis argued that this hidden curriculum of discipline and hierarchy reproduces the social relations of capitalist workplaces.

Debate

Critical theorists see the hidden curriculum as a mechanism of social reproduction that advantages dominant groups, while others note that implicit social learning — cooperation, perseverance, civility — can be beneficial, and that its effects vary with context and with the values a society wishes schools to transmit.
