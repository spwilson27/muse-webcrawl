Tarzan

Tarzan is a fictional character created by the American writer Edgar Rice Burroughs. He first appeared in the novel Tarzan of the Apes, serialised in The All-Story magazine in 1912 and published in book form in 1914. Tarzan — John Clayton, Viscount Greystoke, the son of English aristocrats shipwrecked on the coast of Africa — is orphaned as an infant and raised by a tribe of great apes, the Mangani. Growing to manhood in the jungle, he becomes a peerless hunter and fighter and eventually encounters other humans, including Jane Porter, whom he later marries.

The novels

Burroughs wrote 24 Tarzan books between 1912 and his death in 1950, beginning with Tarzan of the Apes, The Return of Tarzan and The Beasts of Tarzan. The series introduced lost cities and civilisations — Opar, Pal-ul-don, the subterranean world of Pellucidar — and a constructed ape language in which "Tarzan" means "white skin". In the novels Tarzan is articulate, intelligent and multilingual, quite unlike the monosyllabic film stereotype summed up by the (never actually spoken in a film) line "Me Tarzan, you Jane".

In other media

Tarzan became one of the most widely recognised characters in popular culture. The first film adaptation appeared in 1918, starring Elmo Lincoln; the most famous screen Tarzan was Olympic swimming champion Johnny Weissmuller, whose twelve films for MGM and RKO from 1932 (starting with Tarzan the Ape Man) fixed the character's image for generations and introduced his famous ululating yell. Later films include Disney's animated Tarzan (1999), with songs by Phil Collins. The character also appeared in authorised comic strips from 1929 (notably drawn by Hal Foster and Burne Hogarth), comic books, radio and television series, and countless licensed products — a franchise Burroughs managed through his own company, Edgar Rice Burroughs, Inc.

Reception

Tarzan has been called one of the best-known literary characters in the world. Modern criticism has also examined the novels' colonial and racial assumptions, products of their era, even as the figure of a child of nature civilised by no one continues to fascinate readers and to inspire new adaptations and studies.
