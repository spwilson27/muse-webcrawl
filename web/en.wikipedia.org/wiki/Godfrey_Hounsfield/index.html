Godfrey Hounsfield


Sir Godfrey Hounsfield (1919–2004), inventor of computed tomography.

Sir Godfrey Newbold Hounsfield CBE FRS (28 August 1919 – 12 August 2004) was an English electrical engineer who shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Allan MacLeod Cormack for his part in developing the diagnostic technique of X-ray computed tomography (CT).

Early life and career

Hounsfield was born in Sutton-on-Trent, Nottinghamshire, and grew up on a farm, developing an early fascination with electrical gadgets and self-built experiments. He served in the Royal Air Force during the Second World War as a radar instructor, then studied at the Faraday House Electrical Engineering College in London. In 1951 he joined EMI's research laboratories at Hayes, where he worked on radar, guided weapons and then computers; he led the team that built the EMIDEC 1100, among the first all-transistor commercial computers in Britain, before turning to pattern-recognition research.

Computed tomography

In 1967, reasoning that a computer could reconstruct the internal structure of an object from X-ray measurements taken through it from many angles, Hounsfield began work on what became the CT scanner. A laboratory prototype demonstrated the principle on preserved specimens, and with funding negotiated from the Department of Health and Social Security, EMI built the first clinical brain scanner, installed at Atkinson Morley Hospital in Wimbledon. The first patient was scanned on 1 October 1971, revealing a cerebral cyst with unprecedented clarity, and the results were announced in 1972. Whole-body scanners followed by 1975. The standard scale of CT numbers — calibrated so that water is 0 and air −1000 — is named the Hounsfield unit (HU) in his honour.

Later life and honours

Hounsfield was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1975, appointed CBE in 1976, and knighted in 1981. He never married and continued working at EMI's central research laboratories until his retirement in 1984, afterwards acting as a consultant. He died in 2004 in Kingston upon Thames. Despite the immense commercial value of CT — now used in well over a hundred million scans a year worldwide — he is remembered for modestly describing himself, in his Nobel lecture, as having been driven largely by curiosity.
