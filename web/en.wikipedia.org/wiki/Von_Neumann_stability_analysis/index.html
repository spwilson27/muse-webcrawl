Von Neumann stability analysis

In numerical analysis, von Neumann stability analysis (also known as Fourier stability analysis) is a procedure used to check the stability of finite difference schemes as applied to linear partial differential equations. Developed by John von Neumann during his work on hydrodynamics at Los Alamos in the 1940s, the method analyzes how numerical errors — decomposed into Fourier modes — grow or decay as a computation advances.

Method

The numerical solution is assumed to be the sum of the exact solution and an error. Because the scheme is linear, each Fourier component of the error evolves independently: a discrete mode exp(i k x) is multiplied at each time step by an amplification factor ξ(k). The scheme is stable if |ξ| ≤ 1 (more precisely |ξ| ≤ 1 + O(Δt)) for all wavenumbers k, so that no error mode grows without bound.

Example: the FTCS scheme

Applying the analysis to the forward-time centered-space (FTCS) discretization of the one-dimensional diffusion equation ut = α uxx gives ξ = 1 − 4r sin²(kΔx/2), where r = αΔt/(Δx)². Stability (|ξ| ≤ 1 for all k) requires r ≤ 1/2 — the familiar restriction that the time step must shrink with the square of the spatial step. The same scheme applied to the advection equation is unconditionally unstable. By contrast, the implicit Crank–Nicolson scheme is unconditionally stable for diffusion.

Scope and limitations

Von Neumann analysis is strictly valid for linear equations with constant coefficients and periodic or infinite domains, but it is routinely used as a local guide for nonlinear problems and schemes with boundary conditions, where it gives necessary (and often practically sufficient) stability conditions such as the CFL condition. It is a cornerstone of computational fluid dynamics and numerical PDE coursework.
