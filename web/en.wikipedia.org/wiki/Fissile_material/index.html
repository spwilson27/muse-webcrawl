Fissile material

Fissile materials

Principal fissile isotopes: uranium-233, uranium-235, plutonium-239, plutonium-241

Key property: can sustain a nuclear chain reaction with thermal (slow) neutrons

Fissile material is material that can undergo nuclear fission when struck by a low-energy (thermal) neutron and, crucially, can sustain a self-propagating chain reaction. The principal fissile isotopes are uranium-233, uranium-235, plutonium-239 and plutonium-241. Of these, uranium-235 is the only one that occurs in significant quantities in nature; the others are produced artificially in nuclear reactors or during the reprocessing of spent fuel.

Fissile versus fissionable

Not all materials that can fission are fissile. A broader class, called fissionable material, can undergo fission when struck by neutrons of sufficient energy, but cannot sustain a chain reaction with slow neutrons alone. Uranium-238, the dominant isotope in natural uranium, is fissionable by fast neutrons but not fissile. The distinction matters for both reactor design and weapons physics: a thermal-spectrum chain reaction requires fissile fuel, whereas fast fission of uranium-238 contributes additional energy in both reactors and thermonuclear weapons.

Criticality

A mass of fissile material becomes critical when the rate of neutron production by fission balances the rate of neutron loss through absorption and leakage. Below this critical mass, a chain reaction dies out; above it, the reaction grows exponentially. Critical mass depends on the isotopic purity of the material, its density, its geometry (a sphere minimises leakage), and the presence of neutron reflectors or moderators. Bare-sphere critical masses are roughly 52 kg for uranium-235 and about 10 kg for plutonium-239.

Production and uses

Uranium-235 is obtained by enrichment, which increases its concentration from the natural 0.72 per cent to the 3–5 per cent used in power reactors or the 90-plus per cent used in weapons. Plutonium-239 is bred when uranium-238 absorbs a neutron in a reactor and undergoes two successive beta decays. Uranium-233 is similarly bred from thorium-232, the basis of the proposed thorium fuel cycle.

Fissile materials are subject to stringent international controls under the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and the safeguards system of the International Atomic Energy Agency, because the same materials that fuel power stations can, in suitable form, be used in nuclear weapons.
