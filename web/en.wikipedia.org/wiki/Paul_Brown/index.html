Paul Brown

Paul Eugene Brown (September 7, 1908 – August 5, 1991) was an American football coach and executive, widely regarded as one of the most innovative and influential figures in the sport's history. He coached the Cleveland Browns — the team named after him — to championships in the All-America Football Conference and the NFL, and later founded and coached the Cincinnati Bengals. He was inducted into the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 1967.

Brown's systematic methods — playbooks, film study, and classroom instruction — professionalized coaching.

Career

Brown built a dynasty at Massillon Washington High School in Ohio, then led Ohio State to its first national championship in 1942 before coaching the naval team at Great Lakes during the Second World War. In 1946 he became coach and general manager of the new Cleveland franchise, which fans voted to name the Browns in his honour. With quarterback Otto Graham, the Browns won all four AAFC championships and, after joining the NFL in 1950, immediately won the NFL title, adding further championships in 1954 and 1955 — appearing in ten straight league title games. Brown was fired by owner Art Modell in 1963; in 1968 he returned as founder, owner, and coach of the AFL's Cincinnati Bengals, whom he led to the playoffs three times before retiring from coaching in 1975 and serving as team president until his death.

Innovations and legacy

Brown introduced or popularized the playbook and classroom study, game-film analysis, intelligence and psychological testing of players, the taxi squad (precursor of the practice squad), the draw play, the modern face mask, and the use of messenger guards to shuttle in play calls. He also helped reintegrate professional football, signing African-American players Marion Motley and Bill Willis in 1946, a year before Jackie Robinson broke baseball's colour line. His coaching tree — including Don Shula, Chuck Noll, and Bill Walsh — shaped generations of the sport, and the Bengals' home was long named Paul Brown Stadium.
