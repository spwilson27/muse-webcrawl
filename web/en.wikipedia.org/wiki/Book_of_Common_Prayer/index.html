The Book of Common Prayer (BCP) is the traditional service book of the Church of England, containing the liturgies, prayers and forms of service for daily and Sunday worship, the sacraments, and occasional offices such as baptisms, marriages and funerals. Compiled principally by Thomas Cranmer and first published in 1549, it was the first prayer book to contain the complete forms of service for daily and Sunday worship in English. The edition of 1662, authorised after the Restoration, remains the official doctrinal and liturgical standard of the Church of England and the ancestor of prayer books across the worldwide Anglican Communion.

History

Before the Reformation, English worship used a variety of Latin service books (the Sarum, York, Bangor and other "uses"). Cranmer's project — begun with the English Litany of 1544 — unified them into one vernacular rite. The 1549 book, imposed by the first Act of Uniformity, was relatively conservative, retaining much of the structure of the Mass while abolishing many ceremonies; its cautious tone failed to prevent the Western (Prayer Book) Rebellion of 1549, whose rebels demanded the old Latin services. The 1552 book was sharply more Protestant: altars became tables, vestments were simplified, prayers for the dead disappeared, and the communion service was recast as a commemoration. Mary I restored the Latin rite and burned Cranmer; Elizabeth I's 1559 book — a deliberate compromise combining phrases from both Edwardian books in the words of administration at communion — underpinned the Elizabethan religious settlement.

After the Civil War, during which the book was banned, the 1662 revision followed the Restoration and the Savoy Conference's failure to satisfy Puritan objections; some 600 mostly minor changes were made, and the book was annexed to the Act of Uniformity of 1662, prompting the Great Ejection of nonconforming clergy. Scottish and American Episcopalians produced their own adaptations — the American book of 1789 famously omitted the Athanasian Creed and created the template for autonomous provincial prayer books.

Contents and influence

The 1662 book contains Morning and Evening Prayer, the Litany, the Communion service, the Psalter (Coverdale's translation), baptism, confirmation, matrimony, visitation of the sick, burial of the dead, the Ordinal, and the Thirty-nine Articles. Its phrases have passed into the English language — "ashes to ashes, dust to dust", "speak now or forever hold your peace", "till death us do part", "read, mark, learn, and inwardly digest". Together with the King James Bible and Shakespeare, it is counted among the formative influences on English prose style. Twentieth-century liturgical revision produced alternatives such as the Church of England's Common Worship (2000), but the 1662 book remains in daily use in cathedrals and parish churches and is protected as a classic of English devotion.
