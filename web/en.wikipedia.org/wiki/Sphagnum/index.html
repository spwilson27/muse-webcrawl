Sphagnum

Sphagnum is a genus of approximately 380 accepted species of mosses, commonly known as sphagnum moss, peat moss or bog moss. Sphagnum species accumulate to form peat bogs, and through their decay produce peat, making them among the most important carbon-sequestering plants on Earth.

Ecology

Sphagnum mosses grow in dense clumps in wet, acidic habitats. Living plants acidify their surroundings by exchanging hydrogen ions for mineral cations, creating conditions that exclude most competitors and slow decomposition. Their large, empty hyaline cells hold many times the plant's weight in water. Partially decayed sphagnum accumulates as peat, and peatlands store an estimated third of the world's soil carbon.

Uses

Dried sphagnum has been used historically as a wound dressing (notably in the First World War) because of its absorbency and mild antiseptic properties. Peat moss is widely used in horticulture as a soil conditioner, and living sphagnum is used in terraria and for mounting orchids. Harvesting and peat extraction are controversial because of the carbon released and the destruction of bog habitats.

Structure

The plant consists of a main stem with clustered branches; distinctive features include the capitulum (a dense head of young branches) and leaves made of a network of small photosynthetic cells surrounding large, porous, dead hyaline cells that store water.
