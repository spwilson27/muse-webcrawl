Visual perception

Visual perception is the ability to detect light patterns from the visible spectrum in order to interpret surroundings. It is composed of many sub-processes, including colour vision, motion perception, depth perception and form perception. The resulting perception is also known as eyesight, sight or vision.

Process

Light entering the eye is focused by the cornea and lens onto the retina, where photoreceptors (rods and cones) transduce it into neural signals. These pass via the optic nerve through the lateral geniculate nucleus to the visual cortex, where features such as edges, colour and motion are extracted and integrated into perception of objects and scenes. Perception is not a passive recording: the visual system infers a stable world from ambiguous input, producing phenomena such as perceptual constancies and optical illusions.

Gestalt principles

The Gestalt psychologists (Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler) described principles by which elements are grouped into wholes: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure, and figure–ground segregation. The Rubin vase illusion demonstrates figure–ground reversal, while illusory contours show the system completing occluded shapes.

Approaches

Major theoretical approaches include Helmholtz's unconscious inference, Gibson's ecological theory of direct perception (emphasising optic flow and affordances), Gregory's constructivism, and Marr's computational theory, which modelled vision as stages from a primal sketch to a 3D model. Modern neuroscience describes parallel processing in the ventral ("what") and dorsal ("where/how") streams.
