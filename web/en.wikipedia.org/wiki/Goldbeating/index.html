Goldbeating

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Goldbeating is the process of hammering gold into an extremely thin, unbroken sheet known as gold leaf, used for gilding. Gold is the most malleable of all metals and can be beaten to a thickness of about 0.1 micrometres — a few hundred atoms thick — without cracking or tearing.

Process

The process begins with an ingot of gold, typically alloyed with small amounts of silver or copper for strength, which is rolled into a thin ribbon. The ribbon is cut into squares and placed between sheets of vellum or goldbeater's skin — a membrane made from the outer coat of an ox intestine — and beaten with heavy hammers. The partly thinned squares are cut into quarters and the process repeated in progressively finer packets, traditionally known as the cutch, shoder, and mold stages. A skilled goldbeater required a long apprenticeship; a single batch could take many hours of rhythmic hammering.

History

Goldbeating is an ancient craft: gold leaf was produced in Egypt some 5,000 years ago and used to adorn temples, statues, and manuscripts. The craft flourished in medieval Europe, where guilds of goldbeaters operated in cities such as Paris, London, and Nuremberg. In London the trade was concentrated around Clerkenwell. Today, much gold leaf is still produced by traditional methods, notably in Mandalay in Myanmar and in Italy, though mechanical hammers have replaced some hand work.

Uses

Gold leaf produced by goldbeating is used in architecture, religious art, illuminated manuscripts, bookbinding, picture framing, and food decoration, as edible gold leaf (food additive E175).
