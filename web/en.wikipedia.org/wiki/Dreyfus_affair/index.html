The Dreyfus affair (French: affaire Dreyfus) was a political scandal that divided the Third French Republic from 1894 until its resolution in 1906. Alfred Dreyfus, a 35-year-old Alsatian Jewish French artillery officer, was convicted of treason and sentenced to life imprisonment for allegedly communicating French military secrets to the German Embassy. He was imprisoned on Devil's Island in French Guiana.

The case

The conviction rested on a torn-up memorandum (the bordereau) recovered from the German embassy and on dubious handwriting analysis. In 1896, evidence came to light — primarily through an investigation by Lieutenant Colonel Georges Picquart, head of counter-espionage — identifying the real culprit as Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy. High-ranking military officials suppressed the new evidence, forged additional documents (the faux Henry), and acquitted Esterhazy at a brief court-martial while charging Picquart with his own offences.

J'Accuse...! and the political crisis

On 13 January 1898, novelist Émile Zola published the open letter J'Accuse...! on the front page of Georges Clemenceau's newspaper L'Aurore, accusing the army and government of obstruction of justice; Zola was convicted of libel and fled to England. France split into "Dreyfusards" and anti-Dreyfusards, with antisemitism, nationalism, and the role of the army and the Church at stake. A 1899 retrial at Rennes again convicted Dreyfus with "extenuating circumstances," after which he accepted a presidential pardon.

Exoneration and legacy

In 1906 the Cour de cassation annulled the Rennes verdict; Dreyfus was reinstated in the army as a major, served in the First World War, and was made an officer of the Legion of Honour. The affair reshaped French politics, hastened the separation of church and state (1905), and became a byword for miscarriage of justice, institutional cover-up, and antisemitic scapegoating; it also helped inspire Theodor Herzl's political Zionism.
