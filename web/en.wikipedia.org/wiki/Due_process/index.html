Due process

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Due process is the legal requirement that the state must respect all legal rights owed to a person. It balances the power of the state against the individual and holds that government may not act unfairly or arbitrarily. The concept descends from clause 39 of Magna Carta — judgement by peers or "the law of the land" — and is constitutionalised in many countries, most famously in the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution ("nor be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law").

Procedural and substantive

Procedural due process requires fair procedures before the state deprives a person of life, liberty or property: notice, an opportunity to be heard, an impartial decision-maker, and related safeguards. Substantive due process, more controversial, holds that certain fundamental rights are protected from government interference even by fair procedures — in US jurisprudence it has grounded decisions on privacy, family and bodily autonomy, as well as the discredited Lochner-era protection of freedom of contract. Related doctrines include natural justice in Commonwealth law and fair-trial guarantees in the European Convention on Human Rights (Article 6) and international human rights treaties.
