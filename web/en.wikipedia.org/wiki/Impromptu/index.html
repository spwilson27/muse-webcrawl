Impromptu

An impromptu (/ɪmˈprɒmptjuː/; French: [ɛ̃pʁɔ̃pty], loosely meaning "offhand") is a free-form musical composition with the character of an ex tempore improvisation, as if prompted by the spirit of the moment, usually for a solo instrument such as the piano.

History

The term came into use in the early nineteenth century. Jan Václav Voříšek published the first pieces titled Impromptus in 1822, and Franz Schubert composed celebrated sets of Impromptus (D. 899 and D. 935) in 1827, which established the genre as a lyrical piano piece, often in ternary (A–B–A) form.

Composers

Frédéric Chopin composed four Impromptus, including the famous Fantaisie-Impromptu in C-sharp minor, published posthumously. Other composers of impromptus include Robert Schumann, Gabriel Fauré (six impromptus for piano), Francis Poulenc, and Alexander Scriabin. Although the name suggests spontaneity, impromptus are fully composed and often highly structured works.
