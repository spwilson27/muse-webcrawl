Rail spike

A rail spike (also known as a cut spike or crampon) is a large nail with an offset head that is used to secure rails and base plates to railroad ties (sleepers) in the track structure. The spikes are driven into the wooden ties by hand with a spike maul or by machine. A typical cut spike is roughly chisel-shaped in cross-section, which helps it grip the wood fibres without splitting the tie.

Rail spikes were central to the rapid construction of North American railroads in the 19th century: the ceremonial "golden spike" completed the first transcontinental railroad at Promontory Summit, Utah, in 1869. Spikes were patented in Britain by Robert Livingston Stevens, who used them on the Camden and Amboy Railroad in the 1830s.

Limitations

Cut spikes hold mainly by friction and tend to work loose under repeated loading, requiring maintenance; they provide relatively little resistance to rail uplift and longitudinal creep. Modern track increasingly uses screw spikes or elastic fastening systems such as spring clips, which provide greater and more consistent clamping force, especially on concrete sleepers where spikes cannot be used.
