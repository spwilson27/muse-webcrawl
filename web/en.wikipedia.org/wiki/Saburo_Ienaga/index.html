Saburo Ienaga (家永 三郎, Ienaga Saburō, 3 September 1913 – 29 November 2002) was a Japanese historian and professor at Tokyo University of Education (now the University of Tsukuba). He is best known for his decades-long legal battle — the Ienaga textbook lawsuits (1965–1997) — against the Japanese Ministry of Education's system of screening school textbooks, which he argued unconstitutionally censored his accounts of Japanese wartime conduct.

Life and scholarship

Ienaga studied Japanese history at Tokyo Imperial University and became a prominent postwar historian. His works include a widely used high-school Japanese history textbook, Shin Nihonshi ("New Japanese History"), and in English translation, The Pacific War, 1931–1945 (1978), a critical account of Japan's wartime leadership, militarism, and atrocities such as the Nanjing Massacre and the activities of Unit 731, as well as the coercion of Okinawan civilians into group suicides in 1945.

The textbook lawsuits

When the Ministry of Education's screeners required him to delete or soften passages on wartime atrocities, Ienaga sued in 1965, arguing that the state textbook authorisation system violated constitutional guarantees of freedom of expression and academic freedom. Over three suits spanning 32 years, courts issued mixed rulings. The Tokyo District Court's 1970 Sugimoto judgment held the screening unconstitutional in important respects, though higher courts later reversed parts of it. In 1997 the Supreme Court of Japan delivered a final ruling: while it upheld the constitutionality of the screening system itself, it found that several of the ministry's demanded revisions in Ienaga's case had been unlawful abuses of discretion — a partial but landmark victory, notably recognising the legality of his descriptions of Unit 731 and of military coercion in Okinawa.

Legacy

Ienaga's cases became the most famous test of state control over historical memory in postwar Japan and influenced continuing controversies over Japanese history textbooks. He was nominated repeatedly for the Nobel Peace Prize and is remembered as a symbol of scholarly integrity against bureaucratic pressure.
