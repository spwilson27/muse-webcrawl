Dispersion relation

A dispersion relation relates the wavelength or wavenumber of a wave to its frequency. Given the dispersion relation, one can calculate the frequency-dependent phase velocity and group velocity of each sinusoidal component of a wave being propagated through a medium. These quantities may be used to characterize the medium and to predict the behaviour of waves in it.

Dispersion relations are central to wave physics, appearing in optics, acoustics, solid-state physics, and fluid dynamics. The name derives from the way a wave packet disperses (spreads out) when different frequency components travel at different speeds.

Plane waves

A plane wave propagating in a homogeneous medium can be written as a superposition of sinusoidal components of the form exp(i(kx − ωt)), where k is the wavenumber and ω the angular frequency. A medium imposes a functional relationship between these quantities:

ω = ω(k)

This function is the dispersion relation. From it, the phase velocity is vp = ω/k, and the group velocity — the speed at which a wave packet and its energy travel — is vg = dω/dk.

Examples

  • Vacuum: electromagnetic waves obey ω = ck, a linear relation; all frequencies travel at the speed of light c and there is no dispersion.
  • Deep water waves: ω2 = gk, where g is gravitational acceleration; long waves travel faster than short ones.
  • Electrons in a crystal: the energy–momentum relation E(k) plays the role of a dispersion relation and determines the effective mass of charge carriers.
  • Optical fibres and prisms: the refractive index depends on frequency, n = n(ω), which is why a prism splits white light into a spectrum.

History

Dispersion was investigated by Isaac Newton in his prism experiments of the 1660s and 1670s. The modern mathematical treatment developed alongside nineteenth-century work on elasticity, electromagnetism, and hydrodynamics.
