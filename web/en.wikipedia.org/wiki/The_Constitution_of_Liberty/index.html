The Constitution of Liberty

Author: Friedrich A. Hayek
Published: 1960 (University of Chicago Press)
Subject: Political philosophy, classical liberalism

The Constitution of Liberty is a 1960 book by the Austrian-British economist and philosopher Friedrich Hayek. Sixteen years in the making, it is Hayek's most comprehensive statement of his political philosophy — a defence of classical liberalism, the rule of law, and a limited state, and a critique of the expanding welfare and regulatory state of the post-war era. It is considered one of the foundational texts of modern libertarian and conservative thought.

Overview

The book is divided into three parts. "The Value of Freedom" defines liberty as the absence of coercion by others and grounds it in the limits of human knowledge. "Freedom and the Law" develops Hayek's account of the rule of law: general, abstract, known, and equally applied rules that enable individuals to plan their lives, contrasted with discretionary administrative command. "Freedom in the Welfare State" applies these principles to policy — taxation, social security, housing, trade unions, education, and monetary policy — accepting a limited safety net while opposing monopolistic state provision and coercive redistribution aimed at "social justice," which Hayek argues is meaningless in a spontaneous market order.

Reception and influence

The book famously influenced Margaret Thatcher, who is said to have produced a copy at a Conservative Party policy meeting in 1975, declaring "This is what we believe." Its postscript, "Why I Am Not a Conservative," remains a frequently cited statement of the distinction between liberalism and conservatism. In 2011 the University of Chicago Press issued a definitive edition as part of the Collected Works of F. A. Hayek.
