Behaviour therapy (or behavior therapy) is a broad term for types of psychotherapy that treat mental disorders by applying the principles of learning theory — classical and operant conditioning — to change maladaptive patterns of behavior. Unlike insight-oriented therapies, behaviour therapy is typically structured, time-limited, and focused on measurable present behavior rather than unconscious causes.

History

The approach grew out of behaviorism in the early twentieth century. Mary Cover Jones's 1924 work counterconditioning a child's fear of rabbits anticipated later exposure methods. In the 1950s, Joseph Wolpe developed systematic desensitization for phobias based on reciprocal inhibition, while Hans Eysenck championed behaviour therapy in Britain as a scientific alternative to psychoanalysis; in South Africa and the United States, researchers applied B. F. Skinner's operant methods, including token economies, in institutional settings. In the 1960s and 1970s, Albert Bandura's social learning theory broadened the field, and cognitive techniques were progressively integrated, giving rise to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

Techniques

Core techniques include exposure therapy, in which patients confront feared stimuli in a controlled way until anxiety declines; systematic desensitization, pairing relaxation with a graded fear hierarchy; flooding; contingency management and token economies; behavioral activation for depression; habit reversal training; and applied behavior analysis (ABA), widely used in the treatment of autism spectrum disorders. Treatment begins with a functional analysis of the problem behavior and its antecedents and consequences, and progress is monitored with measurable goals.

Evidence and applications

Behavioural methods have strong empirical support for anxiety disorders, phobias, obsessive–compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, insomnia, and childhood behavior problems. "Third wave" approaches — including dialectical behavior therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy — extend the tradition by incorporating mindfulness and acceptance strategies.
