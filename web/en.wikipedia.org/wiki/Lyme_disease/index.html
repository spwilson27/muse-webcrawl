Lyme disease

Lyme disease
Also called: Lyme borreliosis
Cause: Borrelia burgdorferi and related species
Vector: Ixodes ticks
Named after: Lyme, Connecticut

Lyme disease, also known as Lyme borreliosis, is a tick-borne disease caused by species of Borrelia bacteria, transmitted to humans through the bite of infected ticks of the genus Ixodes. It is the most common vector-borne illness in the Northern Hemisphere.

Signs and symptoms

The most characteristic early sign is erythema migrans, an expanding red rash — often described as a "bull's-eye" — that appears at the bite site in about 70–80% of infections, accompanied by fever, headache, and fatigue. Untreated, infection can spread to the joints, heart, and nervous system, causing arthritis, carditis, facial palsy, and meningitis.

Cause and transmission

In North America the main agent is Borrelia burgdorferi, carried by the black-legged tick Ixodes scapularis; in Europe, B. afzelii and B. garinii predominate, carried by Ixodes ricinus. Ticks generally must be attached for 36–48 hours before transmission occurs. The disease was recognized in 1975 after a cluster of cases in Lyme, Connecticut; the bacterium was identified by Willy Burgdorfer in 1982.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical in endemic areas when the rash is present; otherwise two-tiered serologic testing (ELISA followed by Western blot) is used. Early Lyme disease is treated with doxycycline; later manifestations may require amoxicillin, cefuroxime, or intravenous ceftriaxone.

Prevention

Preventive measures include protective clothing, insect repellents, tick checks after outdoor activity, and prompt tick removal. A vaccine (LYMErix) was marketed from 1998 to 2002 before being withdrawn.
