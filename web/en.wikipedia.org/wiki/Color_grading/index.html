Color grading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Color grading is the process of altering and enhancing the colour of a motion picture, video image or still photograph. It is the creative stage of colour work in film and video post-production, in which a colourist adjusts contrast, colour balance, saturation and related qualities to achieve a desired look, mood or visual consistency between shots.

Color correction vs. grading

Color correction generally refers to the technical process of fixing exposure and white balance and matching shots so that a scene looks natural and consistent; color grading refers to the subsequent creative manipulation — for example, giving a film a desaturated, teal-and-orange, or warm nostalgic appearance. In practice the two terms are often used interchangeably, and both are performed by a colourist.

History

In photochemical filmmaking, colour timing was performed by a film timer who adjusted red, green and blue printer lights when making prints, changing the overall balance of each scene. From the 1980s, telecine equipment allowed more flexible electronic colour adjustment for video. The digital intermediate (DI) process, widely adopted in the 2000s, made it possible to grade an entire feature film digitally with far greater control, using software with primary corrections (whole-image adjustments), secondary corrections (isolating specific colours or regions), masks, tracking and look-up tables (LUTs).

Tools and practice

Modern grading is performed on calibrated monitors in controlled environments using systems such as DaVinci Resolve, Baselight and Autodesk Lustre, guided by scopes (waveform monitors, vectorscopes and histograms) as well as by eye. Colourists work with directors and cinematographers to realise the visual intentions of a production, and grading is also standard in television, advertising, music videos and online video.
