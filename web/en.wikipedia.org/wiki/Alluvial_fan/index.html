Alluvial fan

An alluvial fan is an accumulation of sediment shaped like a section of a shallow cone, deposited where a fast-flowing stream flattens, slows and spreads — typically where a mountain stream emerges onto a flatter plain or into a wide valley. The flowing water, unable to carry its sediment load at the reduced gradient, drops alluvium that radiates outward from the fan's apex at the mountain front.

Formation

Fans grow through a combination of stream flow, debris flows and sheet floods over thousands of years. Channels on the fan surface continually shift and avulse, distributing sediment across the fan. Coalescing fans along a mountain front form a broad apron called a bajada. Fans are especially prominent in arid and semi-arid regions such as the Basin and Range of the western United States, the margins of Death Valley, and Central Asia, where sparse vegetation and episodic floods move large sediment volumes. They also occur on Mars and Titan, where they record ancient flowing liquids.

Size and morphology

Fans range from a few metres across to the enormous fluvial fans of the Himalayan foreland; the Kosi megafan in India and Nepal covers some 15,000 km². Slopes are steepest near the apex (proximal zone) and flatten toward the toe (distal zone), and grain size fines outward from coarse gravels to sands and silts.

Hazards and resources

Alluvial fans store groundwater and support agriculture and settlement in deserts, but they pose flood and debris-flow hazards because channels can shift unpredictably during rare large storms — a recognised risk for communities built on fans, such as those around Los Angeles and Las Vegas.
