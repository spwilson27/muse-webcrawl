Trapezoidal rule

In calculus, the trapezoidal rule (also known as the trapezoid rule or trapezium rule) is a technique for approximating the definite integral of a function. The rule works by approximating the region under the graph of the function as a trapezoid and calculating its area.

Formula

For an integral over [a, b], dividing the interval into n subintervals of equal width h = (b − a)/n, the composite trapezoidal rule is:

∫ab f(x) dx ≈ (h/2) [ f(x0) + 2f(x1) + 2f(x2) + … + 2f(xn−1) + f(xn) ].

That is, the endpoints receive weight 1 and interior points weight 2, all multiplied by h/2.

Error analysis

The trapezoidal rule is exact for linear functions. For a twice-differentiable function the error of the composite rule is of order h², and is proportional to the second derivative of the function: E = −(b − a)h²/12 · f″(ξ) for some ξ in (a, b). The rule overestimates the integral for functions that are concave up, and underestimates it for functions that are concave down.

Properties and applications

The trapezoidal rule converges particularly quickly for smooth periodic functions integrated over a full period, where it can be more accurate than higher-order rules. It is one of the Newton–Cotes formulas, the simplest (with the midpoint rule) used in practical numerical integration, and it also appears as the linear interpolation between the left and right Riemann sums. In applications such as naval architecture (Simpson's and trapezoidal rules for hull areas) and signal processing it remains in common use.
