Anna O.

Anna O. was the pseudonym given to a patient of Josef Breuer, whose case was described in the book Studies on Hysteria (1895) by Breuer and Sigmund Freud. The case is widely regarded as a founding case of psychoanalysis. In 1970s scholarship her identity was revealed to be Bertha Pappenheim (1859–1936), an Austrian-Jewish feminist and social worker who later founded the League of Jewish Women in Germany.

The case

Pappenheim was treated by Breuer from 1880 to 1882, beginning when she was 21. While nursing her father through a serious illness, she developed a range of symptoms including a severe cough, paralysis of her limbs, disturbances of vision and speech, hallucinations, and periods of altered consciousness. Breuer diagnosed hysteria. During treatment she developed the practice of narrating her experiences and fantasies, which she herself described as "chimney sweeping" and the "talking cure" — terms later central to psychoanalytic technique. Breuer found that symptoms appeared to remit when their originating circumstances were recalled with their associated emotion, an observation that fed into the theory of abreaction and catharsis.

Controversy

The published account presented the treatment as essentially successful. Later historical research, drawing on records from the Bellevue sanatorium at Kreuzlingen, where Pappenheim was admitted after Breuer ended the treatment, indicated that she was not cured at that time and continued to suffer symptoms, recovering only gradually over subsequent years. The accuracy and completeness of Breuer's case report remain subjects of scholarly debate.

Later life

Pappenheim recovered and became a prominent social reformer in Frankfurt am Main, campaigning against the trafficking of women, founding orphanages and educational institutions, and writing plays, stories, and translations. She opposed psychoanalysis as a treatment for the women in her care.
