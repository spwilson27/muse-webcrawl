Ampère's force law

In magnetostatics, the force of attraction or repulsion between two current-carrying wires is often called Ampère's force law. The law describes how two parallel conductors carrying electric currents exert forces on one another because each current produces a magnetic field that acts on the other current, an interaction first demonstrated experimentally by André-Marie Ampère in the 1820s.

Parallel wires

For two long, straight, parallel wires separated by a distance r and carrying currents I1 and I2, the magnetic force per unit length on each wire is:

F/L = μ0 I1 I2 / (2π r)

The force is attractive when the currents flow in the same direction and repulsive when they flow in opposite directions. The sign and magnitude follow from combining Ampère's circuital law, which gives the magnetic field of a wire, with the Lorentz force on a current in a magnetic field.

Definition of the ampere

Because the force depends only on the currents, the separation and the constant μ0, the phenomenon was historically used to define the SI unit of current: before the 2019 revision of the SI, the ampere was defined as the current that would produce a force of 2 × 10−7 newtons per metre between two infinitely long parallel conductors one metre apart in vacuum.

General form

Ampère's original formulation gave the force between two infinitesimal current elements as a function of their currents, separation and relative orientation. Integrated over complete circuits, it is equivalent to the Biot–Savart law combined with the Lorentz force, and it underlies the behaviour of electromagnets, electric motors and high-current busbars, where large parallel currents can exert considerable mechanical forces.
