The blue hour (from French l'heure bleue) is a period of twilight (in the morning or evening, around nautical dusk) when the Sun is at a significant depth below the horizon. During this time, the remaining sunlight takes on a mostly blue shade, because the red part of the light is scattered into space by the thickness of atmosphere it traverses, while blue light is scattered back — an effect related to the Chappuis absorption of ozone.

Description

The blue hour occurs when the Sun is roughly 4 to 8 degrees below the horizon; it is a phase of civil and nautical twilight. Despite the name, it rarely lasts a full hour — at mid-latitudes it typically spans 20 to 40 minutes, shorter near the equator and much longer at high latitudes in summer. The sky retains enough light for landscapes to be visible while artificial lights in buildings and streets stand out, giving the period a distinctive saturated appearance prized in photography and cinematography.

In photography and culture

Photographers value the blue hour for cityscapes and architectural work because the ambient light balances with artificial illumination, avoiding both the harsh contrast of midday and the black sky of full night. The phrase has a long literary life in French culture: l'heure bleue connotes a liminal, melancholic time of day, and it named a famous Guerlain perfume launched in 1912. In Scandinavian and Arctic regions, extended blue twilight in winter shapes seasonal experience and folklore.

Related periods

The blue hour contrasts with the golden hour shortly after sunrise and before sunset, when the low Sun casts warm, diffuse light, and with the stages of twilight defined astronomically: civil, nautical, and astronomical.
