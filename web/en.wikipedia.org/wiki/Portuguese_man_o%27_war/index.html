Portuguese man o' war

Scientific classification
Domain:Eukaryota
Kingdom:Animalia
Phylum:Cnidaria
Class:Hydrozoa
Order:Siphonophorae
Family:Physaliidae
Genus:Physalia
Species:P. physalis

The Portuguese man o' war (Physalia physalis) is a marine hydrozoan found in the Atlantic Ocean and the Indian Ocean. It is considered to be the same species as the Pacific man o' war. It is one of two species in the genus Physalia, the other being the Portuguese man o' war's smaller relative, the bluebottle (Physalia utriculus), found in the Pacific.

Despite its outward appearance, the man o' war is not a jellyfish but a siphonophore, a colonial organism made up of specialized individual animals called zooids. These zooids are so highly specialized that they cannot survive independently and must rely on each other, functioning together like the organs of a single animal.

Structure and life cycle

The man o' war has a gas-filled bladder, the pneumatophore, which floats on the surface of the water and acts as a sail, propelling the colony by wind and currents. Beneath the float hang long tentacles, commonly averaging about 10 metres (33 ft) in length but sometimes extending much further, which are armed with venom-filled nematocysts used to sting and kill prey such as small fish and crustaceans. Shorter digestive polyps (gastrozooids) digest the prey, while reproductive zooids produce gametes for sexual reproduction.

Venom

The sting of the Portuguese man o' war is painful to humans and can, in rare cases, cause systemic effects including fever, shock, and interference with heart and lung function. Detached tentacles can retain their stinging ability for hours or days after the animal has died or washed ashore. Vinegar is commonly recommended to deactivate remaining nematocysts after tentacles are removed, though treatment recommendations vary by species and region.

Distribution

The species is found mainly in warmer seas, drifting in groups of hundreds to thousands of individuals. It cannot swim and is moved entirely by wind, currents, and tides, so large numbers are frequently washed up on beaches, especially after storms.
