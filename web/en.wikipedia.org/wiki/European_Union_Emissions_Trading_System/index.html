The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is a carbon emission trading scheme ("cap and trade") that began in 2005 and is the world's first major carbon market and one of its largest. It is a cornerstone of the European Union's policy to combat climate change, covering emissions from electricity and heat generation, energy-intensive industry, commercial aviation within the European Economic Area, and, from 2024, maritime transport.

How it works

A cap is set on the total amount of greenhouse gases that can be emitted by installations in the system; the cap is reduced annually in line with the EU's climate targets. Companies receive or buy emission allowances (one allowance equals one tonne of CO₂-equivalent), which they can trade with one another. Each year a company must surrender enough allowances to cover its emissions or face heavy fines. The declining cap and the resulting carbon price are intended to drive investment in low-carbon technology.

Phases

  • Phase 1 (2005–2007) — pilot phase; over-allocation caused the allowance price to collapse.
  • Phase 2 (2008–2012) — aligned with the Kyoto Protocol commitment period.
  • Phase 3 (2013–2020) — a single EU-wide cap, auctioning as the default allocation method, and the Market Stability Reserve (from 2019) to absorb surplus allowances.
  • Phase 4 (2021–2030) — faster annual cap reduction (4.3%, rising to 4.4% after the 2023 revision); a separate ETS2 for buildings, road transport, and fuels is scheduled to start in 2027.

Impact

Emissions from installations covered by the system fell by roughly 47% between 2005 and 2023. The allowance price, long below €10 in the 2010s, rose sharply after reforms and exceeded €100 per tonne for the first time in February 2023. Economists generally consider the ETS among the most cost-effective parts of EU climate policy, though debate continues over free allocation to industry and competitiveness ("carbon leakage").
