G. E. Moore

George Edward Moore (4 November 1873 – 24 October 1958), usually known as G. E. Moore, was an English philosopher and one of the founders of analytic philosophy. With Bertrand Russell and Ludwig Wittgenstein, he reshaped British philosophy at Cambridge, defending common sense and clarifying philosophical problems through careful analysis of language.

Work

Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) argued that the concept of "good" is simple and indefinable, and that attempts to define it in terms of natural properties (such as pleasure) commit what he called the "naturalistic fallacy". He tested proposed definitions with the "open question argument": for any natural property offered, it remains an open question whether things having it are good.

In epistemology, Moore famously defended common sense against scepticism: in "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) and "Proof of an External World" (1939), he held up his hands — "Here is one hand, and here is another" — as a proof that external objects exist, insisting that we know such propositions with more certainty than the premises of sceptical arguments. The puzzle of how his proof works, and of sentences like "It is raining but I don't believe it" (Moore's paradox), remains standard material in philosophy.

Life and influence

Moore was a fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, professor of philosophy there from 1925 to 1939, and editor of the journal Mind. He was a member of the Cambridge Apostles and influenced the Bloomsbury Group, for whom his ethics — prizing personal relationships and aesthetic experience — was a touchstone. His lectures deeply influenced Wittgenstein.
