Petrine privilege

Field: Catholic canon law

Also called: Privilege of the faith

Contrast with: Pauline privilege

The Petrine privilege, or privilege of the faith (privilegium fidei), is a provision of Catholic canon law by which the Pope may dissolve a sacramental but non-consummated marriage, or a marriage in which one party was not baptised at the time of the wedding and certain other conditions apply, in favour of the faith of a party to the marriage.

Basis and scope

The privilege derives from the Church's understanding of the authority given to Saint Peter (Matthew 16:19, the "power of the keys") and is exercised by the Roman Pontiff personally. It differs from the older Pauline privilege, in which a marriage between two unbaptised persons is dissolved automatically when one converts to the faith and the other departs.

Typical Petrine-privilege cases involve a marriage between a baptised Catholic and an unbaptised person (or a person baptised in another Christian community) that has broken down irreparably, where dissolution would allow one party to marry a Catholic and practise the faith freely. Because the dissolution is an exercise of papal authority rather than a declaration of nullity, the first marriage is treated as having been valid but dissoluble. Petitions are investigated by the local diocese and decided in Rome, historically by the Congregation (now Dicastery) for the Doctrine of the Faith or the Dicastery for Divine Worship and the Discipline of the Sacraments, depending on the type of case.
