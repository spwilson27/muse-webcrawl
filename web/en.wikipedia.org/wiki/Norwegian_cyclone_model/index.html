Norwegian cyclone model

The Norwegian cyclone model is a classic conceptual model describing the formation, development and occlusion of extratropical cyclones. It was developed around 1919–1922 by the Bergen School of meteorology in Norway, principally Vilhelm Bjerknes, Jacob Bjerknes, Halvor Solberg and Tor Bergeron, and it introduced the fundamental concepts of weather fronts.

The model

According to the model, a mid-latitude cyclone develops along the polar front, the boundary between cold polar air and warm subtropical air. A small disturbance creates a wave on the front, with warm air bulging poleward. As the wave amplifies, a low-pressure centre forms with a warm front ahead of it and a cold front trailing behind, enclosing a wedge-shaped warm sector.

The faster-moving cold front eventually catches up with the warm front, lifting the warm sector off the ground and forming an occluded front. This occlusion process marks the cyclone's maturity and the beginning of its decay, as the storm is cut off from its supply of warm, moist air.

Significance and limitations

The Bergen School's frontal analysis revolutionised weather forecasting and remains the standard introductory framework for mid-latitude weather systems. Later models — such as the Shapiro–Keyser model, which emphasises frontal fracture and bent-back warm fronts, and the conveyor-belt model — refine the Norwegian model, particularly for marine cyclones undergoing rapid deepening, but do not replace its basic life-cycle description.
