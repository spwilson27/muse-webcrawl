Karyotype

A karyotype is the general appearance of the complete set of chromosomes in the cells of a species or an individual organism, mainly including their sizes, numbers, and shapes. Karyotyping is the process by which a karyotype is discerned by determining the chromosome complement of an individual, including the number of chromosomes and any abnormalities.

A karyotype is most commonly produced as a karyogram (or idiogram), a photograph or diagram in which the chromosomes of a cell are arranged in homologous pairs in order of decreasing size. Human karyotypes are described with a standard notation: for example, 46,XX denotes a normal female complement and 46,XY a normal male complement.

Preparation

Karyotyping is usually performed on cells arrested in metaphase of mitosis, when chromosomes are most condensed. Dividing cells—often white blood cells stimulated with a mitogen—are treated with colchicine to halt spindle formation, swollen in a hypotonic solution, fixed, and spread on a slide. The chromosomes are then stained. G-banding, in which Giemsa stain is applied after trypsin treatment, produces a reproducible pattern of light and dark bands unique to each chromosome, allowing all chromosome pairs to be distinguished.

The human karyotype

Normal human somatic cells are diploid, containing 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes. The correct human chromosome number was established in 1956 by Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan. Deviations from the normal complement cause chromosomal disorders: trisomy 21 produces Down syndrome, a missing or extra sex chromosome produces Turner syndrome (45,X) or Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), and structural rearrangements such as the Philadelphia chromosome translocation are associated with cancers.

Applications

Karyotyping remains a standard tool of clinical cytogenetics, used in prenatal diagnosis from amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling, in the investigation of infertility and miscarriage, and in characterising tumours. In evolutionary biology, comparisons of karyotypes reveal chromosomal rearrangements between species. Newer techniques such as FISH and chromosomal microarray complement, but have not wholly replaced, the classical karyotype.
