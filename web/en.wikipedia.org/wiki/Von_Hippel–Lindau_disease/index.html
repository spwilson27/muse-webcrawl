Von Hippel–Lindau disease

SpecialtyMedical genetics, oncology
GeneVHL, chromosome 3p25.3
InheritanceAutosomal dominant
Frequencyc. 1 in 36,000 births

Von Hippel–Lindau disease (VHL), also called familial cerebello-retinal angiomatosis, is a rare inherited disorder characterised by the growth of tumours and cysts in many parts of the body. It is named after the German ophthalmologist Eugen von Hippel, who described angiomas of the eye in 1904, and the Swedish pathologist Arvid Lindau, who described angiomas of the cerebellum and spinal cord in 1926.

Cause

VHL is caused by mutations in the VHL tumour suppressor gene on the short arm of chromosome 3 (3p25.3) and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. The VHL protein normally targets hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) for degradation when oxygen is plentiful; when VHL function is lost, HIF accumulates and switches on genes such as VEGF that drive blood-vessel growth, explaining the highly vascular nature of the tumours. Tumours typically follow Knudson's two-hit model: a person inherits one defective copy, and tumours arise where the second copy is lost.

Features

Manifestations may include:

  • Haemangioblastomas of the retina, cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord
  • Clear-cell renal cell carcinoma and renal cysts
  • Phaeochromocytomas of the adrenal glands
  • Pancreatic cysts and neuroendocrine tumours
  • Endolymphatic sac tumours of the inner ear, which can cause hearing loss
  • Cystadenomas of the epididymis and broad ligament

Management

There is no cure; management rests on lifelong surveillance — regular imaging of the brain, spine, and abdomen, annual eye examinations, and blood-pressure and catecholamine monitoring — with surgery or other intervention when lesions grow or threaten function. The HIF-2α inhibitor belzutifan was approved in 2021 for certain VHL-associated tumours, the first systemic therapy for the disease.
