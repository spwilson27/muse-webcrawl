Euclid's Elements

The Elements (c. 300 BC) is the most influential textbook in the history of mathematics.

The Elements (Ancient Greek: Στοιχεῖα, Stoikheîa) is a mathematical treatise in thirteen books attributed to the Greek mathematician Euclid, written in Alexandria around 300 BC. It systematically develops geometry, proportion theory and elementary number theory from a small set of definitions, postulates and common notions, and it remained the centrepiece of mathematical education for more than two thousand years.

Contents

Books I–IV treat plane geometry: congruence of triangles, the Pythagorean theorem (Book I, Proposition 47), areas, and circles. Book V expounds the theory of proportion due to Eudoxus, and Book VI applies it to similar figures. Books VII–IX are devoted to arithmetic, including the Euclidean algorithm and the proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers (Book IX, Proposition 20). Book X, the longest, classifies incommensurable magnitudes. Books XI–XIII develop solid geometry, culminating in the construction and classification of the five regular (Platonic) solids.

The axiomatic method

Euclid begins with 23 definitions, five postulates and five common notions. The fifth postulate — the parallel postulate — proved far less self-evident than the others, and centuries of attempts to derive it eventually led, in the 19th century, to the discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries by Bolyai, Lobachevsky and Riemann. The Elements became the paradigm of rigorous deductive exposition, influencing thinkers from Archimedes and Apollonius to Newton and Spinoza.

Transmission and influence

The text was preserved through Greek manuscripts, commented on by Theon of Alexandria and Proclus, translated into Arabic in the 9th century, and into Latin in the Middle Ages. The first printed edition appeared in Venice in 1482, and more editions have been published than of almost any other book except the Bible. Abraham Lincoln famously studied the Elements to learn what it means to "demonstrate". Modern editions descend largely from the critical edition of J. L. Heiberg (1883–1888) and the English translation of T. L. Heath (1908).
