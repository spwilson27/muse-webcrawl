Natural fiber

A selection of plant and animal natural fibres.

Natural fibers or natural fibres are fibers produced by geological processes or from the bodies of plants or animals. They can be used as a component of composite materials, or spun into yarn and woven or knitted into textiles. Natural fibers are contrasted with synthetic fibers, which are manufactured from petrochemicals or regenerated cellulose. Before the development of synthetics in the twentieth century, natural fibers provided virtually all of humanity's clothing, cordage, and furnishing materials.

Plant fibers

Plant fibers are generally composed of cellulose, often with lignin, and are classified by the part of the plant from which they come:

  • Seed fibers — cotton, the most widely used natural fiber, and kapok.
  • Bast (stem) fibers — flax (linen), jute, hemp, ramie, and kenaf.
  • Leaf fibers — sisal, abacá (Manila hemp), and pineapple fiber.
  • Fruit fibers — coir, from the husk of the coconut.

Animal fibers

Animal fibers are composed of proteins. The most important are wool from sheep, silk from the larvae of the silk moth Bombyx mori, and hair fibers such as cashmere and mohair from goats, alpaca, angora rabbit hair, and camel hair. Animal fibers are valued for warmth, elasticity, and moisture absorption.

Mineral fibers

Asbestos is the principal naturally occurring mineral fiber; its use has been largely banned because of its severe health hazards. Basalt fiber, produced from melted volcanic rock, is sometimes grouped with mineral fibers.

Properties and uses

Natural fibers are breathable, biodegradable, and renewable, but tend to be less uniform and less durable than synthetics and can be vulnerable to moisture, insects, and microbial attack. Beyond textiles, they are used in paper, rope and twine, insulation, geotextiles, and as reinforcement in biocomposites for automotive and construction applications. Growing interest in sustainability has revived demand for fibers such as hemp and flax, since many natural fibers require less energy and fewer chemicals to produce than synthetic equivalents, though cotton's heavy water and pesticide footprint is a notable exception.
