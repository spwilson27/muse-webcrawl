Wire-guided missile

A wire-guided missile is a missile that is guided by signals sent to it via thin wires connected between the missile and its guidance mechanism, which is located somewhere near the launch site. As the missile flies, the wires are reeled out behind it from spools in the missile or at the launcher. This guidance system is most commonly used in anti-tank missiles, where its resistance to electronic countermeasures and its ability to be operated in a "fire-and-update" manner make it effective.

Guidance methods

Early wire-guided systems used manual command to line of sight (MCLOS), in which the operator flew the missile with a joystick. Later systems use semi-automatic command to line of sight (SACLOS), in which the operator simply keeps a sight on the target while the guidance computer generates steering commands transmitted through the wire.

Examples

Well-known wire-guided weapons include the American BGM-71 TOW and M47 Dragon, the European MILAN and HOT missiles, and the Soviet 9M14 Malyutka (AT-3 Sagger). Wire guidance is also used in some torpedoes and in tethered unmanned systems.

Advantages and limitations

Wire guidance is essentially immune to radio-frequency jamming, but range is limited by wire length and the wire can snag or break; the launch position must also remain stationary during flight for many systems.
