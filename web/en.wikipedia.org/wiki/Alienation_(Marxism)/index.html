In Marxist theory, alienation (German: Entfremdung) describes the estrangement of people from aspects of their human nature under capitalism, as the worker is turned into a commodity and separated from the products of their labour. Karl Marx developed the theory in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, written in his youth and published only in 1932; it became a central theme of Marxist humanism and Western Marxism.

The four aspects

Marx distinguishes four interrelated forms of alienated labour: (1) alienation from the product, which belongs to the capitalist and confronts the worker as an alien power — the more the worker produces, the poorer they become relative to it; (2) alienation from the act of production, since work is coerced, external and not self-directed, experienced as torment rather than fulfilment; (3) alienation from species-being (Gattungswesen) — humanity's distinctively free, conscious, creative life-activity, which is reduced to a mere means of survival; and (4) alienation of worker from worker, as social relations are mediated by competition and the market.

Intellectual roots and legacy

Marx adapted the concept from Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, where spirit externalises and estranges itself, and from Feuerbach's account of religious alienation, in which humans project their own essence onto God. Under communism, Marx argued, alienation would be overcome as the means of production came under collective control and labour became free creative activity. The theory influenced existentialism, the Frankfurt School, Erich Fromm, and sociological studies of work and anomie.
