Polyuria

Polyuria
DefinitionUrine output > 3 litres/day in adults
SpecialtyNephrology, endocrinology
SymptomsExcessive urination, nocturia, thirst (polydipsia)
Major causesDiabetes mellitus, diabetes insipidus, primary polydipsia, diuretics
Diagnostic test24-hour urine volume; water deprivation test

Polyuria is excessive or abnormally large production or passage of urine, usually defined as more than 3 litres per day in adults (normal output is roughly 0.8–2 litres depending on fluid intake). Polyuria should be distinguished from urinary frequency and nocturia, in which the patient voids often but the total daily volume is normal. It is often accompanied by polydipsia (excessive thirst), and the combination is a classic presentation of both diabetes mellitus and diabetes insipidus.

Causes

Osmotic (solute) diuresis

When the urine contains large amounts of an osmotically active solute, water is obliged to follow it out of the body. The classic case is uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, where blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold and glucose spills into the urine, dragging water with it — historically giving diabetes mellitus its name, from the sweetness of the urine. Other solute diureses follow mannitol infusion, high-protein tube feeding (urea), or excretion of retained urea during recovery from obstructive uropathy or acute tubular necrosis.

Water diuresis (diabetes insipidus)

In diabetes insipidus the kidney cannot concentrate urine because antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) is absent or ineffective:

  • Central diabetes insipidus — inadequate ADH secretion from the hypothalamus/posterior pituitary, caused by head injury, tumours, surgery, or genetic defects.
  • Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus — renal resistance to ADH, caused by lithium, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, or mutations in the vasopressin V2 receptor or aquaporin-2 genes.
  • Gestational diabetes insipidus — placental vasopressinase destroys ADH during pregnancy.

Other causes

  • Primary polydipsia — compulsive water drinking, sometimes psychiatric, suppresses ADH physiologically.
  • Medications — diuretics (furosemide, thiazides), caffeine, alcohol (which transiently suppresses ADH).
  • Post-obstructive diuresis after relief of urinary tract obstruction.
  • Recovery phase of acute kidney injury.

Diagnosis

Evaluation begins by confirming the 24-hour urine volume and measuring urine osmolality: dilute urine (below ~300 mOsm/kg) indicates a water diuresis, while concentrated solute-rich urine indicates osmotic diuresis, where urine glucose or urea is measured instead. For water diuresis, the water deprivation test compares urine concentration before and after deprivation and after desmopressin (DDAVP) administration: central DI responds to DDAVP with concentrated urine, nephrogenic DI does not, and primary polydipsia concentrates normally during deprivation.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause: glycaemic control in diabetes mellitus, desmopressin for central diabetes insipidus, and withdrawal of offending drugs (especially lithium where possible) plus thiazide diuretics and a low-salt diet in nephrogenic disease. Access to water must be maintained, since patients who cannot drink freely can become dangerously hypernatremic, particularly infants and unconscious patients.
