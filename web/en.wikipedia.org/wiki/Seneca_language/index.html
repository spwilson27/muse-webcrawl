Seneca language

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Seneca — Onödowá'ga:'; an Iroquoian language of western New York and Oklahoma, critically endangered.

The Seneca language (in Seneca: Onödowá'ga:') is the language of the Seneca people, the westernmost nation of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy). It belongs to the northern branch of the Iroquoian language family and is the closest relative of Cayuga and Onondaga. Seneca was traditionally spoken in what is now western New York State, from the Genesee River westward to Lake Erie.

Status and revitalization

Seneca is critically endangered. By the early twenty-first century the number of fully fluent first-language speakers, mostly elders living in the Seneca Nation's Allegany and Cattaraugus territories and at Tonawanda, was estimated at around one hundred. The language suffered especially from the flooding of Allegany lands by the Kinzua Dam in the 1960s, which displaced hundreds of speakers. The Seneca Nation has responded with language nests, school programmes, the Faithkeepers' School, dictionaries, and online learning materials aimed at producing a new generation of speakers.

Grammar

Like other Iroquoian languages, Seneca is polysynthetic: verbs are complex words incorporating pronominal prefixes that encode agent and patient, noun incorporation, and markers of aspect and mood, often packaging a whole sentence's worth of information into one word. The language has a small consonant inventory with contrastive glottalization, nasalized vowels, and distinctive vowel length and accent.
