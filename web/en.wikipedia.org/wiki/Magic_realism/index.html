Magic realism

Magic realism, also spelled magical realism, is a style of literary fiction and art in which magical or fantastical elements are woven into an otherwise realistic, mundane setting and presented matter-of-factly, without astonishment from the characters. The term originated in art criticism — Franz Roh coined Magischer Realismus in 1925 for post-expressionist painting — and was later applied to Latin American literature, where it became the defining mode of the "Latin American Boom".

Characteristics

In magic realist fiction the supernatural is not a separate realm but part of ordinary reality: ghosts converse with the living, ascensions to heaven occur while hanging out laundry, and prophecies come true in banal ways. The narrative voice typically accepts these events with the same flat tone used for daily life — often called the author's "irreducible element" treated without explanation. Time is frequently cyclical or distorted, and myth, folklore and history are layered into the plot.

Latin American literature

The Cuban writer Alejo Carpentier proposed the related concept of lo real maravilloso ("the marvellous real") in 1949, arguing that Latin America's history and landscape were inherently marvellous. The style reached global prominence with Gabriel García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967), whose fictional town of Macondo became the emblematic magic realist setting. Other major practitioners include Juan Rulfo, Isabel Allende, Laura Esquivel and, in a broader sense, Jorge Luis Borges.

Beyond Latin America

Magic realism has been taken up worldwide, in Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children, Toni Morrison's Beloved, Haruki Murakami's novels, and the films of directors such as Guillermo del Toro. Critics debate whether the label is a liberating aesthetic or a marketing category imposed on postcolonial writing, but its influence on contemporary fiction is unmistakable.
