Mica

The micas (from Latin mica, "a crumb") are a group of phyllosilicate minerals characterised by a sheet-like structure and perfect basal cleavage, allowing them to be split into very thin, flexible, transparent flakes. The most important mica minerals are muscovite (light mica), biotite (dark mica), phlogopite and lepidolite.

Properties

Micas are monoclinic, with a layered crystal structure of tetrahedral and octahedral sheets that bond weakly between layers, giving the perfect cleavage. They have a Mohs hardness of about 2–4, a pearly lustre and are excellent electrical and thermal insulators, resistant to high temperatures.

Occurrence and mining

Mica occurs in igneous rocks such as granite and pegmatite, and in metamorphic schists. Major producers include India — the mica belt of Jharkhand and Bihar was historically the world's largest source — as well as China, Madagascar, Brazil and the United States. Mining in India and Madagascar has been criticised for the widespread use of child labour in informal mines, prompting industry traceability initiatives.

Uses

Ground mica is used as a filler and pearlescent pigment in cosmetics, paints and plastics, in joint compound for gypsum board, and in drilling fluids. Sheet mica is used in electrical insulation, furnace and stove windows, and capacitors. Historically it was used in lanterns, in the windows of Russian carriages (hence "muscovy glass") and in prehistoric ornament; large decorated mica cut-outs are known from the Hopewell culture of North America.
