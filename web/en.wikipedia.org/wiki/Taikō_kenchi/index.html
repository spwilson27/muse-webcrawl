Taikō kenchi

Date1582–1598
LocationJapan
Organised byToyotomi Hideyoshi
PurposeLand survey for taxation; standardisation of kokudaka

The Taikō kenchi (太閤検地) was a nationwide land survey conducted in Japan by Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who had succeeded Oda Nobunaga as the country's ruler. The surveys were conducted in stages beginning in 1582 and were effectively completed with Hideyoshi's death in 1598. The name derives from Hideyoshi's title of taikō (retired Imperial Regent), which he adopted in 1591. The survey is regarded as one of the key administrative measures in the unification of Japan, since it gave the central government, for the first time, a uniform cadastral record of the whole country's agricultural land.

Background

Land surveys (kenchi) had been carried out by daimyō within their own domains during the Sengoku period, but no survey had ever been extended over the entire country. After defeating the Hōjō at Odawara in 1590 and completing the military unification of Japan, Hideyoshi was in a position to impose a single standard of measurement and assessment on all domains.

Method

The survey standardised the units of land measurement. One tan was defined as 300 tsubo, and one chō as ten tan. The measuring rod (ken) was fixed at 6 shaku 3 sun. Each field was measured, its grade of soil (superior, medium, inferior, lowest) recorded, and its yield expressed in koku of rice — the kokudaka system. The name of the cultivator responsible for the tax was entered in the survey registers (kenchichō), which also recorded village totals. Survey teams were dispatched by the central government, and daimyō were required to cooperate; later surveys were often conducted by the daimyō themselves under central directives, such as the Keichō surveys of the early Tokugawa period.

Effects

The survey served several purposes at once. It established taxation on a uniform basis of assessed yield rather than actual harvest, giving Hideyoshi's government a predictable revenue and a measure of each daimyō's wealth and military obligation. It clarified the status of cultivators, who were registered as tax-paying peasants tied to their fields, and it separated the warrior class from the land — complementing the sword hunt of 1588 and the separation edicts of 1591 that fixed the hereditary status system of samurai, peasants, artisans and merchants. The total assessed yield of Japan under the survey was roughly 18.5 million koku. The kokudaka framework created by the Taikō kenchi remained the basis of land taxation throughout the Tokugawa period.
