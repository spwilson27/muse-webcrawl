American West

The American West, also called the Far West or simply the West, is the region comprising the westernmost states of the United States. The United States Census Bureau defines the West as the Mountain states (Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, Nevada, Arizona and New Mexico) and the Pacific states (Washington, Oregon, California, Alaska and Hawaii). The region is characterised by arid and semi-arid plateaus, deserts, mountain ranges and extensive public lands.

History

Before European colonisation, the West was home to numerous Native American peoples, including the Navajo, Apache, Sioux, Shoshone and Pueblo peoples. Spanish exploration and settlement began in the 16th century in the Southwest. The United States acquired most of the region during the 19th century through the Louisiana Purchase (1803), the annexation of Texas (1845), the Oregon Treaty (1846), the Mexican Cession following the Mexican–American War (1848) and the Gadsden Purchase (1853).

The era of the "Wild West" or "Old West" — roughly 1865 to 1895 — followed the Civil War and saw cattle drives, mining booms, the transcontinental railroad (completed 1869), homesteading under the Homestead Act of 1862, and violent conflict between settlers, the US Army and Native nations, ending symbolically with the Wounded Knee massacre of 1890. The Census Bureau's announcement that the frontier line had disappeared in 1890 prompted Frederick Jackson Turner's influential "frontier thesis".

Geography and economy

The West contains the Rocky Mountains, the Great Basin, the Mojave and Sonoran deserts, the Colorado Plateau and the Sierra Nevada. Water scarcity has shaped its development: federal reclamation projects and dams such as Hoover Dam underpin irrigation and cities including Phoenix, Las Vegas, Denver, Salt Lake City, Seattle, Portland and the Californian metropolises. Roughly half the land in the western states is owned by the federal government. Modern western economies are dominated by technology, aerospace, tourism, energy, agriculture and entertainment.

Culture

The West occupies a central place in American mythology through the Western genre in film, literature and television, cowboy imagery, and national parks such as Yellowstone (established 1872, the world's first) and the Grand Canyon. Rapid population growth since the Second World War has made the West the fastest-growing region of the country, a trend that continues in the 21st century.
