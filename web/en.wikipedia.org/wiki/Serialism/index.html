Serialism

Serialism
OriginVienna, early 1920s
FoundersArnold Schoenberg; Josef Matthias Hauer (independently)
Key figuresAnton Webern, Alban Berg, Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Milton Babbitt
Core techniqueOrdered series (rows) of pitches and, later, other parameters

Serialism is a method of musical composition that uses a series of values to manipulate different musical elements. It began most notably with Arnold Schoenberg's twelve-tone technique, in which the twelve notes of the chromatic scale are arranged in an ordered row that serves as the thematic basis of a composition, avoiding emphasis on any single pitch and thus dispensing with tonal hierarchy.

Twelve-tone technique

Schoenberg developed the method around 1921–23, describing it as a "method of composing with twelve tones which are related only with one another." The basic row (prime form) may be transformed by inversion, retrograde, and retrograde inversion, each at twelve transposition levels, yielding up to 48 row forms. Schoenberg's pupils Anton Webern and Alban Berg adopted the method with distinct personal styles — Webern's terse, pointillistic textures and Berg's more lyrical, tonally allusive rows.

Total serialism

After World War II, a younger generation — Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Luigi Nono, and in the United States Milton Babbitt — extended serial ordering beyond pitch to rhythm, dynamics, articulation, and timbre, a practice called total or integral serialism. Olivier Messiaen's Mode de valeurs et d'intensités (1949) was an influential precursor. Serialism dominated much post-war avant-garde concert music and academic composition, though it also attracted criticism for perceived intellectualism and difficulty, and from the 1970s many composers moved toward pluralist and minimalist approaches.

Terminology

"Serial music" is a broader term than "twelve-tone music": serial procedures can order sets of fewer or more than twelve elements and can be applied to parameters other than pitch.
