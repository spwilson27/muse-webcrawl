The Piazza Fontana bombing (Italian: Strage di Piazza Fontana) was a terrorist attack that occurred on 12 December 1969 at 16:37, when a bomb exploded at the headquarters of the Banca Nazionale dell'Agricoltura (National Agricultural Bank) in Piazza Fontana, Milan, Italy. The explosion killed 17 people and injured 88. It is widely regarded as the opening act of Italy's "Years of Lead" (Anni di piombo) and part of a "strategy of tension" intended to create fear and justify an authoritarian turn.

The attack

That afternoon, four other bombs were placed in Rome and Milan: three exploded in Rome (at the Banca Nazionale del Lavoro, the Altare della Patria and a museum entrance), injuring 16 people, while a fifth bomb in Milan, at the Banca Commerciale Italiana, was found unexploded and was destroyed by investigators in a controlled detonation that eliminated crucial evidence.

Investigations and trials

Police initially blamed anarchist circles. The anarchist railway worker Giuseppe Pinelli died after falling from a fourth-floor window of the Milan police headquarters during interrogation on the night of 15 December 1969, in circumstances that remain disputed; another anarchist, Pietro Valpreda, was arrested and held for years before being acquitted. Pinelli's death inspired Dario Fo's play Accidental Death of an Anarchist.

Subsequent investigations attributed the bombing to the far-right group Ordine Nuovo and its Venetian cell led by Franco Freda and Giovanni Ventura. After a labyrinthine series of trials lasting more than three decades, Freda and Ventura were definitively acquitted of the Piazza Fontana massacre in 2005 for insufficiency of evidence — although a 2001 Court of Assizes ruling and the final Cassation judgment stated that the massacre was carried out by the Freda–Ventura group, by then no longer punishable. No one has been definitively convicted for the bombing. Investigations also revealed obstruction by elements of the Italian secret services.

Legacy

The massacre is commemorated every year in Milan, and a memorial plaque in Piazza Fontana honours the victims. It remains one of the most controversial and symbolically charged episodes of post-war Italian history.
