Chamberland filter

A Chamberland filter (also known as a Pasteur–Chamberland filter) is a porcelain water filter invented by Charles Édouard Chamberland in 1884, while he was working with Louis Pasteur in Paris. It consists of an unglazed porcelain "candle" with pores small enough to retain bacteria, through which water or culture media can be passed to sterilise them. The Chamberland filter was a foundational tool of early microbiology and virology: because certain infectious agents passed through it, researchers were led to postulate the existence of viruses, entities smaller than bacteria.

Design

The filter is made as a hollow cylinder of unglazed porcelain, closed at one end, with an enamelled outlet neck at the other. Liquid is drawn or pressed through the porcelain wall; particles and microorganisms larger than the pore size remain on the surface. Chamberland produced candles in a graded series of porosities, designated by "L" numbers — the finer grades could retain even the smallest known bacteria of the period. The candles could be scrubbed, boiled or heated in an autoclave and reused.

Scientific impact

Pasteur's laboratory used the filters to prepare sterile media and to study infectious diseases. In 1892 Dmitri Ivanovsky showed that the agent of tobacco mosaic disease passed through a Chamberland filter that held back bacteria, and Martinus Beijerinck in 1898 characterised the agent as a "contagium vivum fluidum" — a virus. Chamberland filters were similarly used in the discovery of other viruses and of bacterial toxins, since sterile "filtrates" remained biologically active. The filters also found practical use in purifying drinking water, including for households during epidemics, and porcelain-candle water filters remain in use in some regions today.
