Camp follower

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Camp followers are civilians — historically often the spouses, children, servants, and traders attached to an army — who follow a military force on campaign and provide goods and services that the army's own logistics do not supply. Before modern state logistics systems, armies depended on camp followers for cooking, laundry, nursing, repairs, and the sale of food, drink, and other goods.

History

The baggage trains of early modern European armies could include almost as many followers as soldiers. Sutlers and vivandières were licensed traders who sold provisions; in French service the cantinière became a semi-official figure who served drinks to troops, sometimes under fire. During the American Revolutionary and Civil Wars, officers' families, laundresses (the U.S. Army officially authorised a set number of laundresses per company until 1878), and enslaved servants accompanied regiments. Commanders periodically tried to reduce the numbers of followers because they slowed marches and consumed supplies, yet the services they provided were indispensable.

Modern usage

With professional logistical corps, the traditional camp follower largely disappeared, though the term survives for civilians accompanying units informally and, figuratively, for hangers-on of any organisation or movement.
