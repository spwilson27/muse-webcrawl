Washi

Washi (和紙) is traditional Japanese paper produced by hand from the fibres of native plants. The word combines wa (Japanese) and shi (paper), distinguishing it from Western-style machine-made paper. Papermaking reached Japan from China, via Korea, around the early 7th century, and developed into a distinct craft with hundreds of regional varieties.

Materials and manufacture

The three classic fibres are kōzo (paper mulberry), which gives strong, versatile paper; mitsumata, which produces smooth, fine sheets; and gampi, prized for its lustrous, translucent quality. In the traditional nagashizuki method, the inner bark is steamed, stripped, cleaned and beaten, then dispersed in water with a viscous formation aid (neri) and scooped onto a bamboo screen, building the sheet layer by layer. Finished sheets are pressed and dried on boards.

Uses and recognition

Washi is used for calligraphy and woodblock printing (ukiyo-e), shoji screens, lanterns, fans, banknotes, bookbinding and conservation work, where its strength and longevity are valued. In 2014, UNESCO inscribed the craftsmanship of traditional Japanese washi papermaking — specifically the Hosokawa-shi, Hon-minoshi and Sekishu-banshi traditions — on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
