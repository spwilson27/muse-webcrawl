Woyzeck (German pronunciation: [ˈvɔʏt͡sɛk]) is a play written by Georg Büchner. Büchner began writing it between June and October 1836, but left it incomplete at his death in February 1837. The play was first published in an edited version in 1879 and first performed on 8 November 1913 at the Residenztheater in Munich. It is widely regarded as a foundational work of modern drama and of the German literary tradition.

Plot and themes

The fragment, whose scenes survive in no definitive authorial order, dramatises the descent of the poor soldier Franz Woyzeck, who supports his lover Marie and their child by doing menial jobs, including submitting to a doctor's experiments on a diet of peas. Taunted by his Captain, degraded by the Doctor and betrayed by Marie with a Drum Major, Woyzeck hears voices, stabs Marie by a pond, and — in most reconstructions — drowns trying to wash away the evidence. Büchner drew on the real case of Johann Christian Woyzeck, a Leipzig wigmaker executed in 1824 for murdering his mistress, and on debates about criminal responsibility and the social determination of the poor.

Legacy

The play's episodic structure, mixture of coarse colloquial speech and poetic imagery, and focus on a working-class protagonist anticipated naturalism and Brechtian theatre. Alban Berg adapted it as the opera Wozzeck (premiered 1925), one of the landmarks of twentieth-century music. Werner Herzog filmed it in 1979 with Klaus Kinski in the title role.
