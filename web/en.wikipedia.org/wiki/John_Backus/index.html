John Warner Backus (December 3, 1924 – March 17, 2007) was an American computer scientist. He led the team at IBM that developed Fortran, the first widely used high-level programming language, and was a prominent designer of ALGOL. He also invented the metalanguage notation later called Backus–Naur form for describing programming language syntax, and championed functional programming in his influential 1977 Turing Award lecture, "Can Programming Be Liberated from the von Neumann Style?"

Fortran

Backus joined IBM in 1950 and in 1954 proposed an algebraic "formula translation" system for the IBM 704 that would let scientists write programs in mathematical notation instead of assembly language — while generating code as efficient as hand-written assembly, a requirement for acceptance. The Fortran system, released in 1957, transformed programming and remained dominant in science and engineering for decades.

Later work

Backus served on the international committees that designed ALGOL 58 and ALGOL 60, contributing (with Peter Naur's refinements) the notation used in the ALGOL 60 report. In his later career at IBM's Almaden Research Center he developed FP and FL, function-level programming languages intended to free programmers from assignment-statement ("von Neumann") thinking.

Recognition

Backus received the ACM Turing Award in 1977, was named an IBM Fellow, and shared the 1993 Charles Stark Draper Prize for the development of Fortran.
