Abbotsford is a historic country house in the Scottish Borders, on the south bank of the River Tweed near Melrose. It was the home of the novelist and poet Sir Walter Scott, who bought the modest farm of Cartleyhole in 1811, renamed it after the ford used by the abbots of Melrose Abbey, and transformed it into one of the most celebrated literary houses in Britain. Abbotsford is widely regarded as the prototype of the Scots Baronial style.

History

Scott expanded the original farmhouse in stages; the main rebuilding of 1817–1825, designed by William Atkinson, produced the romantic, turreted mansion visitors see today. The interiors were filled with Scott's vast collection of arms, armour, antiquities and curiosities, and his library of some 9,000 volumes. The expense of building, together with the crash of his publishers in 1826, left Scott heavily in debt, which he spent his remaining years writing to repay.

Scott died at Abbotsford on 21 September 1832. The house remained with his descendants, opened to the public within months of his death — among the earliest literary "shrines" — and passed to the Abbotsford Trust in 2004. A major restoration was completed in 2013, and a new visitor centre opened in 2012.

Architecture and collections

The house is a picturesque jumble of turrets, gables and crow-steps incorporating fragments salvaged from older buildings, including the doorway of the old Tolbooth of Edinburgh. Notable rooms are the entrance hall, panelled with oak from Dunfermline and hung with armour; the library; the drawing room; and Scott's study, with his desk and chair. The grounds, planted under Scott's supervision, run down to the Tweed.
