Physical restraint

Physical restraint refers to the practice of holding or using devices to restrict a person's movement. It is used in various contexts, including medicine, mental health care, law enforcement, education and aged care, generally with the stated aim of preventing injury to the person or to others when less restrictive interventions have failed.

Restraint should be a last resort, used for the shortest possible time.

Types

  • Manual restraint: staff physically holding a person, using trained techniques.
  • Mechanical restraint: devices such as belts, cuffs, mitts, bed rails, or four-point restraints in psychiatric and medical settings.
  • Seclusion is often distinguished from restraint: confining a person alone in a room they cannot leave.
  • Chemical restraint — sedating medication — is usually categorised separately.

Risks

Restraint carries risks of physical injury and death, including positional asphyxia when a person is held face-down (prone restraint), as well as psychological trauma for both the person restrained and staff. Deaths in custody and in care settings have led to inquiries, regulation and campaigns to reduce or eliminate restraint, especially for children, people with dementia and people with learning disabilities.

Regulation and reduction

Most jurisdictions regulate restraint in health and social care, requiring that it be proportionate, a last resort, applied for the shortest time possible, monitored, and documented. Reduction programmes emphasise de-escalation, trauma-informed care, advance planning and post-incident review, and report substantial falls in restraint use when implemented.
