Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a condition in which a blood clot (thrombus) forms in a vein and may break off and travel through the circulation. It encompasses two related presentations: deep vein thrombosis (DVT), usually in the legs or pelvis, and pulmonary embolism (PE), in which a clot lodges in the arteries of the lungs. VTE affects roughly 1–2 people per 1,000 each year in developed countries and is a leading cause of preventable hospital death.

Mechanism and risk factors

Clot formation is explained by Virchow's triad: venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Provoking factors include major surgery (especially orthopaedic), trauma, hospitalization and immobility, active cancer, pregnancy and the postpartum period, oestrogen-containing contraceptives or hormone therapy, long-haul travel, obesity, and inherited thrombophilias such as factor V Leiden. About half of events are provoked; the rest are unprovoked (idiopathic), and recurrence risk differs accordingly.

Signs and symptoms

DVT typically causes unilateral leg pain, swelling, warmth and redness, though many clots are silent. PE presents with breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, cough (sometimes with blood), rapid pulse, and in massive cases collapse and shock. Clinical prediction rules such as the Wells score, combined with D-dimer testing and imaging (compression ultrasound for DVT, CT pulmonary angiography for PE), guide diagnosis.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment is anticoagulation — historically heparin and warfarin, now most often direct oral anticoagulants such as apixaban or rivaroxaban — typically for at least three months, and longer or indefinitely for unprovoked or recurrent events. Thrombolysis or thrombectomy is reserved for limb-threatening DVT or high-risk PE. Prevention in hospital patients uses early mobilization, compression stockings and prophylactic anticoagulants. Long-term complications include post-thrombotic syndrome and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension.
