Dissociative fugue

Dissociative fugue, formerly called a fugue state or psychogenic fugue, is a rare psychiatric condition characterised by reversible amnesia for one's identity, often accompanied by unexpected travel away from home and, in some cases, the assumption of a new identity. The word fugue derives from the Latin fuga, meaning flight.

Features

During a fugue, a person may travel far from home, take up new work, and behave normally to observers, while being unable to recall their past. Fugues typically last hours to days, occasionally longer, and usually end abruptly, with the person regaining awareness of their former identity but having little memory of the fugue period. The condition is generally attributed to severe psychological stress or trauma.

Classification

In the DSM-IV, dissociative fugue was a separate diagnosis; in the DSM-5 it is classified as a specifier of dissociative amnesia. One of the earliest described cases was that of Ansel Bourne, a 19th-century American preacher studied by William James. Famous fictionalised and alleged cases, such as the disappearance of Agatha Christie in 1926, are often speculatively linked to fugue states.
