Tempered glass

Tempered or toughened glass is a type of safety glass processed by controlled thermal or chemical treatments to increase its strength compared with normal glass. Tempering puts the outer surfaces into compression and the interior into tension. When broken, tempered glass shatters into small, relatively blunt granular chunks instead of splintering into jagged shards, reducing the risk of serious injury.

Manufacture

Thermal tempering involves heating annealed glass to above its transition temperature (over 620 °C / 1,150 °F) and then rapidly cooling the surfaces with forced air. The surfaces solidify and contract first; as the interior cools later, it pulls the surfaces into a state of compression. Fully tempered glass is typically four to five times stronger than untreated glass of the same thickness. Chemical strengthening, by contrast, uses an ion-exchange process (typically involving potassium nitrate) and is used for thin glass, such as smartphone screens.

Tempered glass must be cut to size and finished before tempering, because any later cutting or drilling would release the internal stresses and shatter it.

Uses

Tempered glass is used wherever strength, thermal resistance, and safety matter: vehicle side and rear windows, shower doors, glass doors and curtain walls in buildings, refrigerator trays, oven doors, diving masks, smartphone screen protectors, and table tennis and squash court enclosures. In many jurisdictions, building codes require tempered (or laminated) safety glass in hazardous locations such as doors and low windows.

Disadvantages

Because of its balanced internal stresses, tempered glass can occasionally shatter spontaneously, most famously due to nickel sulfide inclusions that expand over time. Heat-soak testing is used to reduce this risk in architectural glass. Tempered glass also cannot be reworked after production, and its edges are vulnerable to chipping, which can trigger complete breakage.
