Saccade

A saccade (from French saccade, a jerk or twitch) is a quick, simultaneous movement of both eyes between two phases of fixation in the same direction. Saccades are the fastest movements produced by the human body, reaching peak angular speeds of up to about 900°/s, and serve to bring a region of interest onto the fovea, the high-resolution centre of the retina.

Function

Humans and many animals do not scan a visual scene smoothly. Instead, the eyes make rapid jumps — typically three to four per second — pausing in fixations of roughly 200–300 ms during which most visual information is acquired. During the saccade itself, visual sensitivity is actively reduced (saccadic suppression), so the smear of the moving image is not perceived and the world appears stable.

Types

  • Reflexive saccades — directed toward a sudden peripheral stimulus.
  • Voluntary (scanning) saccades — deliberate shifts of gaze, e.g. in reading or visual search.
  • Memory-guided saccades — made toward a remembered location.
  • Antisaccades — movements deliberately made away from a stimulus, used experimentally to test inhibitory control.
  • Microsaccades — tiny saccades occurring during fixation that prevent perceptual fading of the retinal image.

Neural control

Saccades are generated by burst neurons in the brainstem (the paramedian pontine reticular formation for horizontal movements and the rostral interstitial nucleus of the medial longitudinal fasciculus for vertical ones), under control of the superior colliculus and cortical eye fields, including the frontal eye fields. The latency from stimulus to saccade is typically around 200 ms; unusually fast "express saccades" can occur in about 80–100 ms.

Reading and research

In reading, saccades move the eyes forward by roughly 7–9 character spaces at a time in left-to-right scripts, interspersed with regressions to earlier text. Eye-tracking studies of saccade patterns are used in psychology, marketing, human–computer interaction, and clinical neurology; abnormal saccades are a sign in conditions such as Parkinson's disease, progressive supranuclear palsy, and schizophrenia.
