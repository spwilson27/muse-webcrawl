Dissident

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dissident is a person who actively challenges an established political or religious system, doctrine or institution — publicly dissenting where the system demands conformity. The term gained its modern force in the Soviet bloc, where "dissidents" (dissidenty) — Sakharov, Solzhenitsyn, the Moscow Helsinki Group's monitors, Charter 77's signatories in Czechoslovakia (Havel's "The Power of the Powerless", 1978, is the movement's philosophy) — chose legalistic witness (demanding regimes honour their own constitutions and the Helsinki accords) over underground conspiracy, communicating through samizdat and Western media.

Significance

Dissidence's power is disproportionate to its numbers: it demonstrates that obedience is a choice, creates focal points for wider discontent, and supplies the personnel and moral charters of transitions (Havel's presidency being the purest case). The type is not Eastern-only — the word is applied to dissidents in China (the Charter 08 drafters; Liu Xiaobo, Nobel laureate in prison), Iran, Saudi Arabia, Cuba and elsewhere, and by analogy within democracies and organisations where "dissident" names principled internal opposition. Regimes' counter-measures evolve (forced exile, psychiatric commitment historically, foreign-agent branding, transnational repression of exiled dissidents), and digital surveillance has altered the samizdat equation — yet the dissident wager, that truth-telling is itself political action, keeps being placed.
