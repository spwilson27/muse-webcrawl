Pastoral epistles

The pastoral epistles are a group of three books of the canonical New Testament: the First Epistle to Timothy (1 Timothy), the Second Epistle to Timothy (2 Timothy), and the Epistle to Titus. They are presented as letters from Paul the Apostle to Timothy and to Titus, and are grouped together because they are addressed to individuals charged with the oversight of church communities — hence "pastoral", relating to the care of a congregation.

Contents

The letters give instructions on church order and organisation, including qualifications for overseers (bishops) and deacons, guidance on widows and elders, warnings against false teaching, and exhortations to personal conduct. 1 Timothy contains the well-known statement that "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil," and instructions that have figured prominently in debates over the role of women in ministry.

Authorship

The Pauline authorship of the pastorals has been questioned since the early 19th century. Many critical scholars argue, on the basis of vocabulary and style (including words not found in Paul's other letters), church structure, and theology, that the letters were written pseudonymously by a follower of Paul after his death, commonly dated to the late 1st or early 2nd century. Other scholars, including many in evangelical and traditional Catholic scholarship, defend Pauline authorship, sometimes proposing that Paul used a secretary (amanuensis) whose style differs from that of the undisputed letters.

Place in the canon

The three letters have been part of the New Testament canon since antiquity, appearing in early canon lists and in the Muratorian fragment tradition, and are read in most Christian traditions as Scripture.
