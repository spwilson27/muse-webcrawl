Uncanny valley

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The uncanny valley is a hypothesised relationship between an object's degree of resemblance to a human being and the emotional response it evokes. Proposed by Japanese roboticist Masahiro Mori in 1970, the concept holds that as a robot, avatar, or animated character becomes more humanlike, observers' affinity for it rises — until a point just short of perfect realism, where the response plunges into eeriness and revulsion, the "valley" in the curve.

Hypothesis

Mori's original graph plotted familiarity against human likeness: industrial robots (low likeness, neutral response), toy robots and stuffed animals (moderately likeable), then a sharp dip for prosthetic hands, corpses, and bunraku puppets viewed closely, before rising again for a healthy human being. Movement steepens both the peaks and the valley: a moving prosthetic hand is more disturbing than a still one.

Explanations and evidence

Proposed explanations include evolved pathogen-avoidance (near-human figures with subtle defects resemble the sick or dead), expectations violated by mismatched features (realistic skin but mechanical movement), and the salience of mortality. Experimental studies have found support in some settings — viewers rate morphed or subtly abnormal faces as eerie — though the shape and even existence of the valley are debated, and sensitivity varies with age, culture, and exposure. Computer-animated films such as The Polar Express (2004) were widely cited as examples, while game and film studios routinely design characters to stay stylised and avoid the valley, or push through it with performance capture.
