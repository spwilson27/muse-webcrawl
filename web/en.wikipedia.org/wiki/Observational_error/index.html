Observational error

Observational error (or measurement error) is the difference between a measured value of a quantity and its true value. No observation is exact, and quantifying how large the error may be is a fundamental part of experimental science, metrology, and statistics. Observational error must be distinguished from mistakes (blunders, such as misreading an instrument), which can in principle be caught and discarded.

Types of error

Random errors vary unpredictably from one measurement to the next and arise from small fluctuations in the instrument, the environment, and the observer. They can be reduced by repeating measurements and averaging, and they are described statistically by precision and standard deviation. Systematic errors, by contrast, bias measurements consistently in one direction — for example, from a miscalibrated balance, a clock running slow, or an instrument used outside its specified conditions. Averaging does not remove systematic error; it must be found and corrected by calibration or by redesigning the measurement. The closeness of a measurement to the true value (accuracy) thus depends on both components.

Sources and treatment

Sources of observational error include instrument resolution and drift, environmental effects such as temperature and vibration, observer effects, and in survey research the wording of questions and nonresponse bias. Scientists report results with an associated measurement uncertainty, propagate errors through calculations, and in statistics model measurement error explicitly — because error in an explanatory variable can bias regression estimates toward zero (attenuation), a problem addressed by errors-in-variables models.
