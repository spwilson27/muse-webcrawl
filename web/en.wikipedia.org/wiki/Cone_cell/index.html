Cone cell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A microscopic rendering of cone and rod cells in the human retina]
A microscopic rendering of cone and rod cells in the human retina

Cone cells are photoreceptor cells in the Retina of the eye that function best in bright light and enable colour vision and fine spatial detail. Humans have about six million cones of three types, sensitive to short (blue), medium (green) and long (red) wavelengths — the trichromatic basis of colour perception — concentrated in the fovea at the retina's centre. The other photoreceptors, rods, serve dim-light vision.

Function

Each cone type contains a different opsin pigment; the brain compares their signals to construct colour, as formalised by the Young–Helmholtz trichromatic theory and refined by opponent-process theory. Cones are less sensitive than rods (a single photon can trigger a rod; cones need hundreds) but respond faster and in dense foveal packing deliver acuity around 20/20 and better.

Colour vision deficiencies

Inherited defects of the red and green opsin genes on the X chromosome cause red–green colour blindness in about 8% of men and 0.5% of women of European ancestry; rarer defects cause blue–yellow confusion or total colour blindness (achromatopsia). Some women, carrying four opsin variants, may be tetrachromats with a fourth colour channel — a debated enhancement of colour discrimination.

In other animals

Many birds, reptiles and fish are tetrachromatic as standard, extending into ultraviolet; the mantis shrimp's twelve-plus channel system, by contrast, discriminates colours surprisingly poorly — a reminder that channel count alone does not make colour vision.
