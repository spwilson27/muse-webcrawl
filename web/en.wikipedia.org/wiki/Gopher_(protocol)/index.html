Gopher (protocol)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Screenshot-style image of a text-based Gopher menu of numbered items]

The Gopher protocol is a communication protocol designed for distributing, searching and retrieving documents in Internet Protocol networks. Released in 1991 by a team at the University of Minnesota — home of the Golden Gophers, hence the name, with its pun on 'go for' — Gopher presented the Internet as a hierarchy of menus from which documents, and other menus, could be fetched.

For a brief period Gopher was the Internet's most promising information system: thousands of 'gopherspace' servers, searchable with tools such as Veronica, which indexed menu titles across the world. In 1993, however, the University of Minnesota announced it would charge licensing fees for its server implementation, while at almost the same moment CERN placed the World Wide Web in the public domain for free. The web also offered pictures, formatting and free-form links instead of rigid menus. Gopher use collapsed within a few years.

Gopher survives as a curiosity maintained by hobbyists, and its design — simple, fast, text-first — periodically inspires minimalist revivals such as the Gemini protocol. Its rise and fall is a standard case study in how licensing and timing, not technical merit alone, decide standards contests.
