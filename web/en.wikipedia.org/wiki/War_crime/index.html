War crime

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A war crime is a violation of the laws of war (international humanitarian law) that gives rise to individual criminal responsibility for actions by combatants in armed conflict — including murder, ill-treatment or deportation of civilians and prisoners of war, hostage-taking, torture, attacks directed against civilians or protected objects, perfidy, pillage and the use of prohibited weapons. The modern law is codified principally in the Hague Conventions (1899, 1907) and the four Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols (1977), and criminalised comprehensively in the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court.

Enforcement

War crimes were prosecuted at Nuremberg and Tokyo after the Second World War, by the ad-hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia (ICTY) and Rwanda (ICTR) in the 1990s, and by the ICC and national courts exercising universal jurisdiction. Doctrines include command responsibility (superiors liable for subordinates' crimes they knew or should have prevented), the rejection of superior orders as a complete defence, and grave-breaches regimes obliging states to prosecute or extradite. Contemporary documentation efforts in Syria, Ukraine and elsewhere — including open-source evidence — aim to preserve accountability options amid ongoing conflict.
