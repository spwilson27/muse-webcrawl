A digital elevation model (DEM) is a digital representation of the elevation of the terrain at regularly spaced points, typically stored as a raster grid in which each cell holds a height value. The term is used loosely for several related products: a digital surface model (DSM) records the top of features including buildings and vegetation; a digital terrain model (DTM) represents the bare earth with those features removed; and DEM is often used generically for either.

Production

DEMs are produced from remote sensing and surveying data: airborne lidar (laser scanning), which can penetrate vegetation to yield bare-earth models; satellite radar interferometry, notably the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM, 2000), which produced near-global coverage at 90 m and later 30 m resolution, and the ASTER Global DEM; photogrammetry from aerial or satellite imagery; and digitised contours from topographic maps. Standard formats include GeoTIFF grids.

Applications

DEMs underpin geographic information systems (GIS) analyses such as slope, aspect and hillshade maps; hydrological modelling of watersheds, flow accumulation and flood risk; viewshed and line-of-sight calculations for telecommunications and defence; orthorectification of imagery; terrain rendering in games, simulators and virtual globes; and geomorphological and geological research.
