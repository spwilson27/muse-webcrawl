Street furniture

Assorted street furniture in a public square

Street furniture is a collective term for objects and pieces of equipment installed outdoors on streets, roads and public spaces for various purposes. Examples include benches, bollards, litter bins, post boxes, phone boxes, street lamps, traffic lights, traffic signs, bus stops, tram stops, taxi stands, public lavatories, drinking fountains, public bookcases, bicycle racks, and memorials.

Design and management

Street furniture shapes the appearance and usability of the public realm, and many cities coordinate its design—London's red telephone boxes and pillar boxes, Paris's Wallace fountains and Morris columns, and Barcelona's distinctive paving and lamps are closely tied to city identity. Poorly coordinated furniture can clutter footways and obstruct pedestrians, cyclists and people with disabilities, so highway authorities often run decluttering programmes. In some cities, advertising-funded furniture (such as advertising panels combined with bus shelters, pioneered commercially by JCDecaux in the 1960s) supplies equipment at no direct cost to the municipality in exchange for advertising rights.

Hostile design

Some street furniture is deliberately shaped to discourage particular uses—benches with dividers or sloped surfaces that prevent sleeping, spikes in doorways, and skateboard deterrents. Such "hostile architecture" is controversial: critics argue it targets homeless people and degrades public space, while defenders cite anti-social behaviour concerns.
