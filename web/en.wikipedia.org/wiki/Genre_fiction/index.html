Genre fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genre fiction, also known as popular fiction, is a term used in the book-trade for fictional works written with the intent of fitting into a specific literary genre, in order to appeal to readers and fans already familiar with that genre. It is often contrasted with literary fiction, which is treated as having greater artistic merit, though the boundary between the two is contested and many works straddle it.

Genres

The major commercial genres include science fiction, fantasy, mystery and crime, romance, horror, the Western, and the thriller. Each genre carries its own set of conventions — characteristic settings, plot structures, character types and reader expectations — which publishers use for marketing and booksellers for shelving.

History

Genre categories emerged with the mass-market publishing of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries: dime novels, pulp magazines and paperback originals organised fiction into recognisable product lines. The development of dedicated awards (the Hugo and Nebula for science fiction, the Edgar for mystery, the RITA for romance) and of fan communities consolidated genre identities.

Criticism and status

Genre fiction has historically been looked down upon by the literary establishment, yet works first marketed as genre — by authors such as Raymond Chandler, Ursula K. Le Guin and Philip K. Dick — are now widely taught and canonised. Contemporary debates focus on whether "genre" and "literary" describe kinds of writing at all, or merely marketing categories and reading protocols.
