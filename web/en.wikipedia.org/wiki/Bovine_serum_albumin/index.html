Bovine serum albumin

BSA
Molecular mass: ~66.5 kDa
Length: 583 amino acids
Source: bovine blood plasma

Bovine serum albumin (BSA or "Fraction V") is a serum albumin protein derived from the blood of cattle. It is a single polypeptide chain of 583 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of approximately 66.5 kDa, homologous to human serum albumin, with which it shares about 76 percent sequence identity. Because it is abundant, inexpensive and stable, BSA is one of the most widely used reagents in biochemistry and molecular biology.

Applications

BSA is used as a protein concentration standard in quantitative assays such as the Bradford and Lowry assays; as a blocking agent in immunoassays including ELISA and Western blotting, where it coats unoccupied surfaces and prevents non-specific binding; as a stabiliser for enzymes, notably restriction endonucleases, in molecular biology buffers; and as a nutrient supplement in cell culture media. Fraction V, the classical cold-ethanol fractionation product of Edwin Cohn's plasma fractionation process, is the most common commercial grade.

Properties

Albumin functions in vivo as the principal carrier protein of blood plasma, binding fatty acids, hormones, bilirubin and many drugs, and maintaining colloid osmotic pressure. Its heart-shaped, predominantly alpha-helical structure contains 17 disulfide bridges and one free cysteine (Cys34). Because BSA can elicit immune responses and, rarely, is implicated in beef allergy, human or recombinant albumin is preferred for clinical and some diagnostic uses.
