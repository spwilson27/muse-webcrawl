Jewish views on marriage

In Judaism, marriage is regarded both as a sacred covenant and as the normal, desirable state of adult life. The Hebrew term for the marriage rite, kiddushin, means "sanctification" or "setting apart": husband and wife are consecrated to one another. Marriage is traditionally understood as the framework for companionship, mutual support, sexual relations and the commandment to "be fruitful and multiply".

The marriage contract and ceremony

A traditional Jewish marriage is effected through the signing of a ketubah, a contract recording the husband's obligations to his wife, including the financial settlement due to her if the marriage ends. The ceremony takes place under a canopy (chuppah), includes the exchange of a ring, the recitation of blessings over wine, and the breaking of a glass. Halakhically (according to Jewish law), marriage has two stages: erusin (betrothal) and nissuin (the marriage proper), nowadays performed together.

Divorce and its difficulties

Judaism permits divorce, effected by the husband delivering a bill of divorce (get) to his wife before a rabbinical court. Because a religious divorce traditionally requires the husband's voluntary act, a woman whose husband refuses can become an agunah — "chained" to the marriage and unable to remarry within Orthodox law. This problem has prompted proposed solutions ranging from prenuptial agreements to, in Conservative Judaism, additional clauses in the ketubah.

Variations among movements

The movements of modern Judaism differ over questions such as intermarriage with non-Jews (prohibited by traditional law and long discouraged, though Reform Judaism has moved toward greater inclusion), same-sex marriage (endorsed by Reform, Reconstructionist and, since 2006, Conservative authorities, and rejected by Orthodoxy), and the role of the ketubah, which egalitarian movements have rewritten in gender-neutral form. All movements, however, continue to treat marriage as a central religious good and a matter of communal celebration.
