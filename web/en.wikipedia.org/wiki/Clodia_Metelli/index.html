Clodia (born Claudia, c. 95 – after 45 BC), often called Clodia Metelli ("Clodia, wife of Metellus"), was one of the most notorious women of the late Roman Republic. Sister of the demagogue Publius Clodius Pulcher and wife of the consul Metellus Celer, she was savaged by Cicero in his speech Pro Caelio and is traditionally — though uncertainly — identified as the "Lesbia" of Catullus's love poems.

Clodia's Palatine house was a centre of late-Republican society.

Life

Clodia was one of three daughters of Appius Claudius Pulcher, consul in 79 BC; like her sisters she changed the patrician spelling Claudia to the plebeian-sounding Clodia, matching her brother's populist styling. Educated and wealthy, she married her cousin Quintus Caecilius Metellus Celer, consul in 60 BC, whose sudden death in 59 BC gave rise to rumours — aired by Cicero — that she had poisoned him. Her Palatine mansion and her gardens on the Tiber were fashionable meeting places, and her independence, wit and rumoured lovers scandalised conservative opinion.

The trial of Caelius

In 56 BC Clodia stood behind the prosecution of her young former lover Marcus Caelius Rufus on charges of violence and attempted poisoning. Cicero, defending Caelius, turned the trial into an attack on Clodia herself: he portrayed her as a disgrace to her ancestors — a "Palatine Medea" — alleging incest with her brother, promiscuity and the murder of her husband. Caelius was acquitted, and Clodia's public reputation never recovered. The speech remains a masterpiece of character assassination — and a notorious example of the misogyny available to Roman orators.

Identification with Lesbia

Since an ancient note by Apuleius records that Catullus's "Lesbia" was really named Clodia, scholars have traditionally identified her with Clodia Metelli: the passionate, faithless mistress of the most famous love poems in Latin. The identification fits her dates and milieu but cannot be proven, and some historians prefer one of her sisters. Cicero's letters mention Clodia still active in property disputes in the 40s BC; her later life is unknown.
