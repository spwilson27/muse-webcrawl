Theatre of ancient Greece

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Mask replicas of tragedy and comedy flanking the stage of an ancient Greek theatre]
Mask replicas of tragedy and comedy flanking the stage of an ancient Greek theatre

The theatre of ancient Greece, originating in Athens around the sixth century BC, is the foundation of Western drama. Performances of tragedies and comedies were religious and civic festivals — above all the City Dionysia — staged in open-air theatres such as the Theatre of Dionysus and the surviving theatre at Epidaurus, built into hillsides with seating for thousands.

Origins and festivals

Drama grew from the choral hymns (Dithyramb) to Dionysus; tradition credits Thespis (c. 534 BC) with the first actor stepping from the chorus. At the City Dionysia, three tragedians each presented a tetralogy (three tragedies and a Satyr play) competing for prizes funded by wealthy citizens (choregoi); comedies were added in 486 BC. The Lenaia festival favoured comedy. Attendance was a civic duty, subsidised (the theorikon) so the poor could go.

Theatre and performance

Theatre architecture comprised a circular orchestra for the Chorus, a raised stage, and the skene building behind it, with devices like the mechane crane for flying gods and the ekkyklema for displaying tableaux. All performers were male, masked, and numbered at most three speaking actors plus chorus; costume and mask projected character to the back rows of theatres holding 14,000–17,000 spectators, whose acoustics — Epidaurus famously — carried whispers.

Playwrights and legacy

The great tragedians were Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides; comedy's masters were Aristophanes (Old Comedy's political satire) and Menander (New Comedy's domestic plots, ancestor of Roman and modern sitcom). Of more than a thousand plays, some forty survive complete. Aristotle's Poetics theorised the form, and Greek theatre's structures — acts, chorus, prologue, catharsis — underlie drama to this day.
