European integration

European integration is the process of industrial, economic, political, legal, social and cultural integration of states wholly or partly in Europe, pursued since the aftermath of the Second World War above all through the European Union and the Council of Europe. Its guiding idea, expressed in the Schuman Declaration of 1950, was to make war between Europe's historic rivals "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible" by pooling the industries of war.

Early steps

The European Coal and Steel Community, founded by the Treaty of Paris in 1951 by France, West Germany, Italy and the Benelux countries, placed coal and steel under a common High Authority. In 1957 the same six states signed the Treaties of Rome, creating the European Economic Community and the European Atomic Energy Community. The communities merged their institutions in 1967 and progressively built a customs union and a common agricultural policy.

Deepening and widening

The Single European Act (1986) set the goal of completing a single market by 1992. The Maastricht Treaty (1992) formally created the European Union, introduced EU citizenship and launched economic and monetary union, which produced the euro in 1999. The Treaties of Amsterdam, Nice and Lisbon reformed the institutions for a growing membership. Enlargement waves brought in the United Kingdom, Ireland and Denmark (1973), Mediterranean states in the 1980s, Austria, Finland and Sweden (1995), ten mostly Central and Eastern European states in 2004, Romania and Bulgaria (2007) and Croatia (2013).

Challenges

Integration has repeatedly met resistance: the eurozone sovereign debt crisis after 2009, disputes over migration and the rule of law, and the withdrawal of the United Kingdom following its 2016 referendum — the first departure of a member state. Scholars analyse the process through theories such as neofunctionalism, which emphasised spillover between sectors, and intergovernmentalism, which stressed bargaining between states.
