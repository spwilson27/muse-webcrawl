Zapotec civilization

The Zapotec civilization was a pre-Columbian civilisation that flourished in the Valley of Oaxaca in Mesoamerica, in present-day southern Mexico. One of the earliest complex societies in the region, it is noted for the hilltop capital of Monte Albán, for early writing and calendrical systems, and for monumental architecture, lasting in various forms from about 500 BCE until the Spanish conquest.

Origins

The Zapotec developed from earlier village cultures of the Oaxaca valley. The early centre of San José Mogote was superseded around 500 BCE by Monte Albán, founded on a flattened mountaintop at the valley's centre. Its carved stone slabs known as danzantes and later conquest slabs record figures and place names, making the Zapotec among the earliest peoples of Mesoamerica to use writing.

Monte Albán and expansion

At its height in the Classic period, Monte Albán housed tens of thousands of inhabitants, with a great plaza, ballcourt, observatory-like structures and elite tombs rich in offerings. Zapotec influence and colonies extended beyond the valley, and the Zapotec maintained long-standing relations — at times close, at times hostile — with the great central Mexican city of Teotihuacan, where a Zapotec barrio has been identified.

Decline and later centres

Monte Albán was largely abandoned by around 800-900 CE, and political power in the valley fragmented among competing city-states such as Zaachila and Mitla, the latter famed for its intricate stone mosaics. The Zapotec later contended with Mixtec expansion and, in the fifteenth century, with the Aztec Empire, before the Spanish conquest of the 1520s.
