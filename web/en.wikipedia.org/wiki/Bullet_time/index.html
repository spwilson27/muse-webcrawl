Bullet time

Bullet time (also known as frozen moment, dead time, flow motion or time slice) is a visual effect or visual impression of detaching the time and space of a camera (or viewer) from that of its visible subject. It is characterised by an extreme transformation of time (slow motion) combined with a camera that appears to move around the scene at normal speed. The effect is impossible in real life and is achieved with arrays of still cameras, computer interpolation, or fully computer-generated imagery.

History

The underlying technique derives from chronophotography: Eadweard Muybridge's multi-camera studies of motion in the 19th century are an early precedent. Arrays of still cameras were used commercially from the 1980s, and the effect appeared in films and advertisements in the 1990s, including Blade (1998). The term "bullet time" became famous from the 1999 film The Matrix, in which a character dodges bullets while the virtual camera orbits him in extreme slow motion; the effect was developed by visual effects supervisor John Gaeta and Manex Visual Effects using an array of over 100 still cameras fired in sequence, with interpolation to smooth the movement. "Bullet time" was later registered as a trademark by Warner Bros.

Use in video games

The concept migrated to video games as a gameplay mechanic in which time slows from the player's point of view while their own aiming remains fast — popularised by Max Payne (2001) and later used in series such as F.E.A.R., Red Dead Redemption ("Dead Eye") and many others.

Technique

Photographic bullet time uses a rig of cameras arranged along the desired camera path; the cameras fire simultaneously or in rapid sequence and the frames are assembled into a moving shot, often with morphing interpolation between frames. Modern productions increasingly use high-speed digital cameras or virtual cinematography in game engines to achieve the same result with greater flexibility.
