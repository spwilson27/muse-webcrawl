Arthur H. Robinson

The Robinson projection of the world

Arthur Howard Robinson (January 5, 1915 – October 10, 2004) was an American geographer and cartographer, a professor at the University of Wisconsin–Madison from 1947 until his retirement in 1980. He is best known as the creator of the Robinson projection, a compromise world map projection adopted by the National Geographic Society in 1988 as its standard for world maps.

Career

Robinson was born in Montreal to American parents and educated at Miami University (Ohio) and the University of Wisconsin–Madison, where he earned his Ph.D. in 1947. During the Second World War he served as chief of the map division of the Office of Strategic Services (OSS) in Washington, supervising the production of thousands of maps for the war effort — an experience that shaped his interest in cartographic design and communication.

The Robinson projection

In 1963 the Rand McNally company asked Robinson to design a new world projection that looked "right" rather than privileging any single measurable property. Robinson built the projection by numerical experimentation, shaping the meridians and parallels by eye and tabulating coordinates at five-degree intervals, with interpolation for other values. It presents the world with moderate distortion everywhere, a good overall visual balance, and poles shown as lines rather than points. The National Geographic Society adopted it in 1988, replacing the Van der Grinten projection, and kept it until 1998, when it switched to the Winkel Tripel projection.

Scholarship

Robinson was a central figure in establishing cartography as an academic discipline in the United States. His book The Look of Maps (1952) argued that maps should be designed around their users' perceptual needs, and his textbook Elements of Cartography (1953, and later editions with co-authors) trained generations of mapmakers. He also wrote on the history of thematic mapping in Early Thematic Mapping in the History of Cartography (1982).
