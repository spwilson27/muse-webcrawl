Eurozone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The eurozone (officially the euro area) is the group of European Union member states that have adopted the euro (€) as their currency and are subject to the single monetary policy of the European Central Bank. Launched on 1 January 1999 with eleven members (notes and coins followed in 2002), it has expanded to twenty members, Croatia being the most recent entrant (1 January 2023). EU members are in principle obliged to join once they meet the Maastricht convergence criteria (price stability, sound public finances, exchange-rate stability, long-term interest rates), with Denmark holding a formal opt-out and Sweden staying out in practice.

The eurozone is an incomplete monetary union: money is common but budgets, banks (partly remedied by the post-2012 banking union) and debt remain national — the design flaw exposed by the sovereign-debt crisis of 2010–12, answered by bailout funds (the permanent European Stability Mechanism, 2012), the ECB's “whatever it takes” backstop (Draghi, July 2012, and the OMT programme), and partial fiscal innovations (the 2020 NextGenerationEU recovery fund, financed by common borrowing). Membership is legally irrevocable (no exit clause), which both stabilises the currency and raises the stakes of its internal imbalances — the standing debate of European integration.
