Linguistic prescription

Linguistic prescription (or prescriptivism) is the establishment of rules defining preferred or correct use of a language. These rules may address such linguistic aspects as spelling, pronunciation, vocabulary, syntax and semantics. Sometimes informed by linguistic purism, prescriptivism may declare some usages incorrect, improper, illogical, lacking in communicative effect, or of low aesthetic value, even when such usages are common among native speakers.

Prescription and description

Prescription contrasts with the descriptive approach of modern academic linguistics, which observes and analyses how language is actually used without judging usages as correct or incorrect. The contrast is often summarised as "prescriptivism says how language should be; descriptivism describes how language is." In practice the two interact: dictionaries and style guides record usage while also recommending preferred forms.

Sources of authority

Prescriptive norms may be promulgated by official bodies such as the Académie Française or the Real Academia Española, by school curricula, or by influential usage writers and editors. Common targets of English prescriptivism have included the split infinitive, sentence-final prepositions, the use of "less" with count nouns, and the singular "they".

Criticism

Critics argue that many traditional rules rest on mistaken analogies with Latin, on the personal taste of 18th-century grammarians, or on social prejudice against regional and working-class varieties. Defenders reply that shared norms aid clear communication and that all speakers make stylistic judgements of appropriateness.
