Chinese city wall

The city wall of Xi'an, one of the best preserved in China

The Chinese city wall (城墙, chéngqiáng) refers to the defensive walls built around Chinese cities from antiquity through the imperial era. For over two thousand years, virtually every significant Chinese city was enclosed by walls, traditionally rectangular in plan and aligned to the cardinal directions, reflecting cosmological ideas about the ordered city. City walls served for defence, flood control, and as symbols of authority and urban status.

History

The earliest Chinese city walls were built of rammed earth (hangtu) as far back as the Neolithic and early Bronze Age. During the Ming dynasty (1368–1644), many walls were rebuilt or faced in brick and stone, making them far more durable. The Ming walls of Beijing enclosed both the Outer and Inner cities, with monumental gate towers and corner towers.

Surviving examples

Most city walls were demolished during the 20th century to make way for roads and modernization; Beijing's walls were largely removed in the 1950s–60s. Notable surviving examples include the nearly complete walls of Xi'an, among the largest and best preserved in the world, and the walls of Nanjing, the longest surviving city wall, as well as those of Pingyao, Jingzhou, and Datong (the latter substantially reconstructed).

Structure

A typical wall consisted of a rammed-earth core faced with brick, topped by a parapet with crenellations, and pierced by gates protected by barbicans and gate towers. Moats usually surrounded the walls. Watchtowers and corner towers projected at intervals for observation and defence.
