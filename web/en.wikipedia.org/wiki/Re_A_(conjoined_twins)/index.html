Re A (conjoined twins)

Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins: Surgical Separation)
CourtCourt of Appeal of England and Wales
Decided22 September 2000
Citation[2001] Fam 147; [2000] EWCA Civ 254
KeywordsMedical law, necessity, best interests, murder

Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins: Surgical Separation) [2001] Fam 147 is a landmark decision of the Court of Appeal of England and Wales concerning the surgical separation of conjoined twins, known in the proceedings as "Jodie" and "Mary". The court authorised an operation to separate the twins even though it was known that the weaker twin, Mary, would die as a result, while without the operation both twins would die within months. The case is a leading authority on the defence of necessity and on the best-interests analysis in medical law.

The twins in Re A were joined at the pelvis, with Mary dependent on Jodie's heart and lungs.

Facts

The twins were born in Manchester in August 2000 to parents from Gozo, Malta, who had come to England for treatment. The girls were joined at the pelvis with fused spines. Mary had severely abnormal heart and lung function and survived only because Jodie's heart pumped blood for both of them. Doctors at St Mary's Hospital wished to separate them; without separation, the strain on Jodie's heart meant both would die within three to six months. With separation, Jodie had a good prospect of a relatively normal life, but Mary would certainly die. The parents, devout Roman Catholics, refused consent, believing the outcome should be left in God's hands. The hospital sought a declaration that the operation would be lawful.

Judgment

The Court of Appeal (Ward, Brooke and Robert Walker LJJ) unanimously authorised the operation, though by different reasoning. Each judge concluded that separation was in Jodie's best interests, and each grappled with Mary's interests and the law of murder: the operation would foreseeably kill Mary. Lord Justice Brooke reasoned by analogy with the defence of necessity, applying the criteria proposed in Re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation) — the operation was necessary to save Jodie's life, the act was reasonable, and the harm inflicted was not disproportionate to the harm avoided. Ward LJ described Mary as, however innocently, "killing" Jodie by draining her life-blood, making the case analogous to self-defence.

Aftermath

The operation was performed in November 2000. As expected, Mary died; Jodie survived and returned to Malta with her parents. The twins' real names were later reported as Gracie and Rosie Attard. The case remains a staple of medical law and ethics teaching in the United Kingdom.
