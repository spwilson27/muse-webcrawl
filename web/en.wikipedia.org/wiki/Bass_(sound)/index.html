Bass (sound)

Bass (pronounced /beɪs/, like "base") describes tones of low (also called "deep") pitch, frequency and range — generally the portion of the audible spectrum from about 16 to 256 hertz, though definitions vary. In music, the bass is the lowest part of the harmony and underpins the rhythmic and harmonic foundation of a piece. The word derives from the Latin bassus, meaning low or short.

A subwoofer, used to reproduce low bass frequencies.

Role in music

In ensembles, the bass line is typically played by instruments such as the double bass, bass guitar, tuba, bassoon, or the left hand of a keyboard instrument. In the Baroque era, the basso continuo provided the harmonic foundation of nearly all music. In popular music and jazz, the bass — usually electric bass guitar or double bass — works together with the drums to form the rhythm section.

Vocal music classifies the lowest male singing voice as bass, with a typical range from around E2 to E4. In choral writing, the bass part is the lowest of the four standard parts (soprano, alto, tenor, bass).

Reproduction and perception

Reproducing bass requires moving large volumes of air, so bass loudspeakers (woofers and subwoofers) are large, and bass sound reinforcement is a major part of concert and cinema sound systems. Very low frequencies below about 20 Hz are termed infrasound and are felt as much as heard. Because bass wavelengths are long (about 17 metres at 20 Hz), bass is difficult to localise and tends to pass through or around obstacles, which is why a neighbour's music is often heard mainly as bass.

In audio engineering

Equalisation controls labelled "bass" adjust the level of low frequencies. The "bass boost" feature on consumer equipment accentuates this range. In electronic dance music, genres such as drum and bass, dubstep and house place particular emphasis on powerful, synthesised bass lines.
