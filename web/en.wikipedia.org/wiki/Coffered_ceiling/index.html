Coffer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A coffer (or coffering) in architecture is a series of sunken panels in the shape of a square, rectangle, or octagon in a ceiling, soffit or vault. A coffered ceiling is therefore a ceiling decorated with such recessed panels; the sunken panels are sometimes called lacunaria (singular: lacunar) in Latin.

Purpose and effect

Coffers lighten the structure of a ceiling or dome by removing material where it is not structurally needed, while creating a strong, ordered visual pattern that emphasises the geometry of the space. The depth of the panels and the play of light and shade across them give the ceiling an appearance of richness and monumentality. In barrel vaults and domes, coffers may decrease in size toward the crown, enhancing the sense of height.

History

Coffering was used in ancient Greek and Roman architecture. The most famous ancient example is the dome of the Pantheon in Rome, whose five rows of twenty-eight coffers reduce the weight of the concrete dome. Roman builders also coffered barrel vaults, as in the Basilica of Maxentius. The technique was revived in Renaissance architecture — notably in the dome of Florence Cathedral and St Peter's Basilica — and remained popular in Baroque, Neoclassical and Beaux-Arts buildings, as well as in grand twentieth-century civic interiors such as railway stations, banks and courthouses.

Construction

Traditional coffers were formed in stone, concrete, wood or plaster. Modern coffered ceilings may be built from timber beams applied to a flat ceiling, or from prefabricated lightweight panels of plaster, metal or synthetic material, giving the appearance of deep structural coffers at much lower weight and cost.
