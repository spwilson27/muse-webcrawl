Refractory

In materials science, a refractory (or refractory material) is a material that is resistant to decomposition by heat or chemical attack and that retains strength and form at high temperatures. Refractories are inorganic, nonmetallic, porous and heterogeneous, typically composed of oxides (or carbides, nitrides, etc.) of silicon, aluminium, magnesium, calcium and zirconium. By ASTM convention, refractories are materials with a pyrometric cone equivalent of at least 1,520 °C (2,770 °F).

Uses

Refractories line furnaces, kilns, incinerators and reactors. The iron and steel industry consumes roughly 70% of refractories produced — in blast furnaces, converters, ladles and casting systems. Other major uses include cement kilns, glass furnaces, aluminium smelters, petrochemical reactors and domestic fireplace linings (firebrick).

Classification

  • By chemistry: acidic (silica, alumina-silicate/fireclay), basic (magnesia, dolomite), and neutral (alumina, chromite, carbon).
  • By form: shaped products (bricks and tiles) and unshaped/monolithic products (castables, mortars, ramming mixes, gunning mixes).
  • By porosity: dense refractories for slag contact and porous insulating refractories for heat conservation.

Properties

Key selection criteria include refractoriness under load, thermal shock resistance, resistance to slag and gas corrosion, creep at temperature, and thermal conductivity. Failure is usually by chemical attack, spalling from thermal cycling, or erosion.
