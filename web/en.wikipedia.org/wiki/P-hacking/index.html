P-hacking

P-hacking, also called data dredging, data snooping or significance chasing, is the misuse of data analysis to find patterns that can be presented as statistically significant, without an underlying hypothesis. By trying many analyses — selecting outcomes, subgroups, covariates, exclusion rules or stopping points — and reporting only those that yield a low p-value, a researcher can manufacture "significant" findings from noise. Together with publication bias, p-hacking is considered a major driver of the replication crisis in psychology, medicine and other fields.

Mechanisms

Common researcher degrees of freedom include optional stopping (collecting data until p < .05), choosing among dependent variables, dropping observations, splitting samples by covariates, and HARKing (hypothesizing after the results are known). Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) demonstrated the danger vividly, using flexible analysis to "show" that listening to the Beatles' song "When I'm Sixty-Four" made listeners younger. With a nominal 5% false-positive rate, enough flexibility can push the effective false-positive rate above 60%.

Remedies

Proposed and adopted remedies include pre-registration of hypotheses and analysis plans, registered reports accepted before results are known, correction for multiple comparisons (e.g. Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg), reporting all conditions and measures, open data and code, and preferring effect sizes and confidence intervals over bare p-values. The satirical xkcd comic "Significant" — in which green jelly beans alone are linked to acne among twenty colours tested — is a widely cited illustration of the multiple comparisons problem.
