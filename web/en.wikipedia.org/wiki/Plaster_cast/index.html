Plaster cast

A plaster cast is a rigid dressing made of plaster of Paris bandages, moulded around a limb (or, historically, the torso) to immobilise and protect an injured part — most commonly while a fractured bone heals. The term also refers to copies of sculptures and other objects made in plaster, discussed below.

Medical casts

Plaster of Paris is calcium sulfate hemihydrate; when the dry bandage is wetted it recrystallises as gypsum in an exothermic reaction, hardening in minutes. The bandage is wound over padding and stockinette, then moulded to the limb's contours. Casts are typically worn for several weeks and removed with an oscillating cast saw that cuts the rigid shell without injuring skin. Complications include pressure sores, stiffness, muscle wasting and, rarely, compartment syndrome signalled by severe pain.

Plaster has been used for fractures since the 19th century (popularised by Antonius Mathijsen in 1852). Modern alternatives — fibreglass (orthopaedic) casting tape and thermoplastic splints — are lighter, stronger and water-resistant, but plaster remains valued for its superior mouldability and low cost, especially in initial ("backslab") treatment and in children.

Art and archaeology

Plaster casts reproduce sculptures and artefacts by taking a mould from the original and casting plaster in it. Victorian museums built great "cast courts" — the Victoria and Albert Museum's opened in 1873 — displaying copies of works such as Trajan's Column and Michelangelo's David for study. At Pompeii, from 1863 Giuseppe Fiorelli poured plaster into voids left in the ash by decomposed bodies, producing the famous casts of the eruption's victims; resin and other materials are used in modern repetitions of the technique. "Death masks" and life casts are related forms.
