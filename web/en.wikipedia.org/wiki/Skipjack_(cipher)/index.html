Skipjack (cipher)

Skipjack
Designers: U.S. National Security Agency
Published: 1998 (declassified)
Key size: 80 bits
Block size: 64 bits
Rounds: 32
Structure: Unbalanced Feistel network

Skipjack is a symmetric-key block cipher designed by the United States National Security Agency (NSA) in the 1980s. It was the encryption algorithm used in the controversial Clipper chip programme of the 1990s, which proposed key escrow for encrypted communications. The algorithm was classified until it was declassified and published by the NSA in 1998.

Design

Skipjack operates on 64-bit blocks with an 80-bit key over 32 rounds of an unbalanced Feistel structure. The 64-bit block is divided into four 16-bit words, which are updated in turn using a keyed permutation (the G function, based on an F-table derived from the key schedule) according to two alternating rules, A and B, for sixteen steps each. The relatively short 80-bit key and the secrecy of its original design attracted criticism from the cryptographic community.

History

Civilian experts, including a panel convened to evaluate the algorithm in 1993, were granted limited access and concluded that they found no weaknesses, though they noted the inability to fully assess a classified design. After declassification in 1998, Skipjack was standardised by NIST as part of FIPS 185 (the Escrowed Encryption Standard). Academic cryptanalysis found attacks on reduced-round variants — notably impossible differential cryptanalysis covering 31 of the 32 rounds — but no practical break of the full cipher; nevertheless, its 80-bit key is now considered too short for modern use, and NIST withdrew the standard in 2015.
