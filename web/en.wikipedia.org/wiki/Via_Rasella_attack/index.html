The Via Rasella attack was a partisan bombing carried out on 23 March 1944 in German-occupied Rome by the Gruppi di Azione Patriottica (GAP), a formation of the Italian Resistance linked to the Italian Communist Party. A bomb concealed in a rubbish cart was detonated as a column of the SS Police Regiment "Bozen" marched along the narrow Via Rasella, killing 33 German military policemen. The following day, in direct reprisal, German forces murdered 335 Italian prisoners and civilians in the Ardeatine massacre, one of the worst atrocities of the occupation of Italy.

The attack

The date chosen, 23 March, was the 25th anniversary of the founding of the Fasci di Combattimento. For weeks the Bozen regiment's 11th company had marched the same route through central Rome each afternoon. GAP partisans — among them Rosario Bentivegna, who pushed the cart and lit the fuse, and Carla Capponi — planted an improvised device containing roughly 18 kg of TNT in a street-cleaner's cart. The explosion killed 28 policemen immediately (others died later, for a total of 33) and two Italian bystanders, including a boy. The partisans escaped. The company, composed largely of German-speaking recruits from South Tyrol, was a police formation on occupation duty.

Reprisal: the Ardeatine massacre

Adolf Hitler reportedly demanded a massive reprisal. Herbert Kappler, the SS security chief in Rome, organized the killing of ten Italians for every German killed. On 24 March 1944, 335 victims — political prisoners, Jews, resistance members, and men seized in roundups — were shot in the Ardeatine Caves on the outskirts of Rome, five more than the quota because of a counting error. The caves were then dynamited shut. Kappler and officer Erich Priebke were tried after the war; Priebke was extradited from Argentina in 1995 and convicted in Italy.

Legacy and controversy

The attack and reprisal have been debated for decades in Italy: whether the partisans should have foreseen the reprisal, and whether they were obliged to surrender themselves (no such demand was ever communicated before the massacre). Italian courts have consistently held the attack to be a legitimate act of war and placed responsibility for the massacre solely on the German occupiers. The Fosse Ardeatine are now a national memorial.
