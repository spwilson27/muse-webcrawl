TypeSoil-improving practice / cover crop

In agriculture, green manures are crops grown specifically for incorporation into the soil while still green, in order to build fertility and improve soil structure. The practice is closely related to the use of cover crops, and is a cornerstone of organic farming and other low-input systems, where it substitutes for synthetic fertilisers.

Functions

Leguminous green manures — clovers, vetches, alfalfa (lucerne), field beans and lupins — host nitrogen-fixing rhizobium bacteria in their root nodules, adding plant-available nitrogen to the soil. Fast-growing species such as rye, mustard and buckwheat protect bare ground from erosion, suppress weeds, scavenge leftover nutrients that would otherwise leach away, and, when dug in or terminated, contribute large quantities of organic matter that feed soil biology and improve water retention. Deep-rooted species can break up compacted subsoil and draw up minerals from below the plough layer.

Practice

Green manures are sown after a cash crop is harvested, in the understorey of an orchard, or as a full-season "ley" in a rotation, and are cut, rolled, grazed or ploughed in before the following crop is planted — typically just before flowering, when nitrogen content peaks. The technique is ancient: Chinese, Greek and Roman agricultural writers all describe growing and burying legumes to enrich fields.
