The World Drug Report is launched each June at the UN in Vienna.

The World Drug Report is an annual publication of the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) that provides a comprehensive overview of the illicit drug situation worldwide, including estimates of drug production, trafficking routes, seizures, prices, consumption and drug-related health consequences. First published in 1997, it is the UN's flagship reference on global drug trends and is widely cited by governments, researchers and the media.

Release and contents

The report is conventionally launched in late June, around 26 June, the International Day Against Drug Abuse and Illicit Trafficking. Recent editions combine a summary volume and thematic booklets with an online data segment. Recurring content covers opium and heroin production (historically centred on Afghanistan), cocaine manufacture in the Andean region, cannabis — consistently the most widely used drug — amphetamine-type stimulants, and the rapid growth of synthetic drugs such as fentanyl and other synthetic opioids, methamphetamine and captagon.

Key findings of recent editions

The report has documented a long-term rise in drug use: the 2024 edition estimated that about 292 million people — 5.6 per cent of the global population aged 15–64 — had used a drug in 2022, with cannabis users numbering roughly 228 million, followed by opioids (around 60 million), amphetamines, cocaine and ecstasy. It has repeatedly highlighted record cocaine production in Colombia, the displacement of heroin markets by potent synthetic opioids in North America, the growth of online drug markets on the dark web and social media, and the fact that only a minority of people with drug use disorders — fewer than 20 per cent — are in treatment.

Sources and criticism

The report draws on annual questionnaires returned by member states, UNODC crop-monitoring surveys using satellite imagery (notably of coca and opium poppy cultivation), seizure statistics and scientific literature. Scholars and policy organisations have sometimes criticised its estimates for relying on incomplete government data and for reflecting an enforcement-centred framing of drug policy, while acknowledging it remains the most comprehensive single source of global drug statistics.
