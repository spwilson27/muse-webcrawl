Intensive farming


Combine harvesters on an intensive arable farm

Intensive farming, also known as intensive agriculture or industrial agriculture, is a type of agriculture, both of crop plants and of animals, with higher levels of input and output per unit of agricultural land area. It is characterised by a low fallow ratio, high use of inputs such as capital, fertiliser and pesticides, and high crop yields per unit land area. It contrasts with extensive farming, which uses large areas of land with relatively low inputs per hectare.

Techniques

Intensive crop farming relies on mechanisation, synthetic fertilisers, irrigation, plant breeding (including high-yielding varieties developed during the Green Revolution), and chemical pest and weed control. Crops are frequently grown in monocultures, and methods such as multiple cropping and dense planting raise output per unit of land.

Intensive animal farming — often called factory farming — keeps large numbers of livestock such as cattle, pigs and poultry indoors or in feedlots at high stocking densities, with feed brought to the animals rather than the animals grazing on pasture.

Advantages

Supporters argue that intensive methods produce more food per hectare, lowering food prices and reducing the amount of land needed for agriculture, which can spare natural habitats from conversion to farmland. Yields of staple cereals in intensive systems can be several times those of low-input farming.

Criticism

Intensive farming is criticised for its environmental and ethical effects: nutrient runoff and eutrophication from fertilisers, pesticide impacts on biodiversity and pollinators, high water use, greenhouse gas emissions, antibiotic use in livestock and associated antimicrobial resistance, soil degradation, and concerns about animal welfare in high-density housing. These concerns have encouraged alternative approaches such as integrated pest management, organic farming and precision agriculture.

History

Agricultural intensification developed over centuries through irrigation, manuring and crop rotation, but accelerated dramatically in the 20th century with the Haber–Bosch process for synthetic nitrogen fertiliser, mechanisation, and the Green Revolution's high-yielding crop varieties of the 1960s and 1970s.
