Byzantine Rite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Used by: Eastern Orthodox Church, Eastern Catholic Churches
Principal liturgy: Divine Liturgy of St John Chrysostom

The Byzantine Rite, also known as the Greek Rite, is a liturgical rite that was developed in the Byzantine Empire and is now used by the Eastern Orthodox Church and by many of the Eastern Catholic Churches. It comprises the cultural, liturgical and theological practices of the Greek-speaking church of Constantinople, as they evolved from late antiquity and were transmitted throughout Eastern Europe and the Near East.

Liturgy

The central act of worship is the Divine Liturgy, most commonly the Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom, with the Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great celebrated on certain days and the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts used on weekdays of Great Lent. Services are characterized by extensive chanting—traditionally without instruments—the use of icons and an iconostasis separating the sanctuary, incense, and frequent invocations of the Theotokos and the saints.

Daily cycle and calendar

The daily cycle of services includes Vespers, Compline, the Midnight Office, Matins and the Hours, aggregated in monasteries into long vigils. The liturgical year centres on Pascha (Easter), preceded by Great Lent, and includes twelve Great Feasts.

Spread

Through mission and imperial influence the rite spread to the Slavs—translated into Church Slavonic by the mission of Saints Cyril and Methodius—and to Romania, Georgia and the Middle East. It is today celebrated in many languages worldwide.
