The Carlisle Indian Industrial School, founded in 1879 in Carlisle, Pennsylvania, was the first federally funded off-reservation American Indian boarding school in the United States. Established by Captain (later Brigadier General) Richard Henry Pratt, the school became the model for a nationwide system of Indian boarding schools intended to forcibly assimilate Native American children into Euro-American culture.

History

Pratt, who had previously supervised Native American prisoners at Fort Marion in Florida, persuaded the federal government that education was the key to what he called "solving the Indian problem." He is associated with the phrase "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man," which summarized his assimilationist philosophy. The school opened on November 1, 1879, in the Carlisle Barracks, a former cavalry post, with an initial group of students drawn largely from the Lakota, Cheyenne, Kiowa, Comanche, and other Plains nations.

Students were required to cut their hair, wear military-style uniforms, adopt English names, and speak only English. The curriculum combined basic academic instruction with vocational training: boys learned trades such as carpentry, blacksmithing, printing, and farming, while girls were trained in sewing, cooking, and domestic service. Under Pratt's "outing system," students were placed with local families and businesses to work during the summer.

Athletics

Carlisle became nationally famous for its football program. Under coach Glenn "Pop" Warner, the Carlisle Indians fielded one of the strongest teams in the country, led by the legendary athlete Jim Thorpe, a Sac and Fox student who won gold medals in the pentathlon and decathlon at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics. The team defeated leading college programs including Harvard, Army, and the University of Pennsylvania.

Closure and legacy

The school closed in 1918, when the Army reclaimed the barracks for use as a hospital for soldiers returning from the First World War. Over its 39 years, more than 10,000 Native American children from nearly 140 tribes attended Carlisle. At least 186 students died at the school, many from disease, and were buried in its cemetery.

Carlisle's legacy is deeply contested. In the 21st century, tribal nations have worked to repatriate the remains of children buried at the school, and the site—now part of the U.S. Army War College—has become a focal point in national reckoning with the boarding school era. In December 2024, President Joe Biden designated the site a national monument.
