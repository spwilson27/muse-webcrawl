Symmetry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A snowflake with sixfold symmetry photographed against a dark background]

Symmetry is the property of an object that remains unchanged under some transformation — reflection, rotation, translation or scaling. A circle is symmetric under any rotation about its centre; a snowflake under turns of 60 degrees; the human face, approximately, under mirror reflection.

In mathematics, the symmetries of an object form a group, and group theory — born from the symmetries of equations in Galois theory — is now the universal language for symmetry. Emmy Noether proved in 1918 that every continuous symmetry of a physical system implies a conservation law: time symmetry gives conservation of energy, spatial symmetry conservation of momentum.

In nature and art

Bilateral symmetry dominates animal body plans; radial symmetry suits stationary organisms; spiral symmetries appear in shells and galaxies. Symmetry breaking — a symmetric system settling into an asymmetric state — is how physics explains everything from ice crystals to the masses of elementary particles.

Artists and architects from the builders of the Alhambra onward have systematised symmetry; the 17 wallpaper groups classify all repeating plane patterns, proven complete by crystallographers in the 1890s.
