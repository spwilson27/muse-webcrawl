Vocal cords

The vocal cords, also known as vocal folds, are folds of throat tissues in the larynx that vibrate and modulate the flow of air expelled from the lungs, producing phonation — the sound used in speech and singing. They are composed of twin infoldings of mucous membrane stretched horizontally across the larynx, controlled by the vagus nerve via its recurrent laryngeal branch.

Structure

The true vocal folds consist of layered tissue: an outer epithelium, the lamina propria (whose superficial layer, Reinke's space, is loose and gelatinous), the vocal ligament, and the vocalis muscle. Above them sit the vestibular folds ("false vocal cords"), which play little role in normal phonation but protect the airway. In adults, the membranous fold is roughly 12.5–17.5 mm long in males and 8.5–12.5 mm in females, contributing to the typical difference in voice pitch.

Function

During phonation, air pressure from the lungs builds beneath the closed folds until they are blown apart; elastic recoil and the Bernoulli effect then snap them shut, creating a pulsating airflow — the myoelastic-aerodynamic theory of phonation. The frequency of vibration determines pitch (about 125 Hz for an adult male speaking voice, 210 Hz for an adult female), and tension, controlled by the cricothyroid and other intrinsic laryngeal muscles, raises or lowers it.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the vocal folds include laryngitis, vocal nodules ("singer's nodes"), polyps, cysts, Reinke's edema, vocal fold paralysis (often from damage to the recurrent laryngeal nerve), and laryngeal cancer. Hoarseness (dysphonia) is the cardinal symptom; diagnosis is by laryngoscopy and stroboscopy, and treatment ranges from voice therapy to microsurgery (phonosurgery).
