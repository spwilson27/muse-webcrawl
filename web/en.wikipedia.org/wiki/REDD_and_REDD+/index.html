REDD and REDD+

REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation, plus conservation and related activities) is a framework developed by the parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) to guide activities in the forest sector that reduce emissions from deforestation and forest degradation, as well as the sustainable management of forests and the conservation and enhancement of forest carbon stocks in developing countries. It aims to create financial value for the carbon stored in forests and to offer incentives for developing countries to reduce emissions from forested lands and invest in low-carbon paths to sustainable development.

History

The original idea, "reducing emissions from deforestation" (RED), was tabled at the 2005 UN climate conference in Montreal by a coalition of rainforest nations. Degradation was added (REDD), and at Copenhagen in 2009 the scope was widened to REDD+, adding conservation, sustainable forest management and enhancement of carbon stocks. The Warsaw Framework for REDD+ was adopted in 2013, and the Paris Agreement of 2015 explicitly encouraged parties to implement and support REDD+.

How it works

REDD+ is typically implemented in phases: readiness (developing national strategies and monitoring systems), implementation of policies and measures, and results-based payments for verified emission reductions. Countries must establish a forest reference emission level, a national forest monitoring system, and a system for reporting on social and environmental safeguards—the Cancún safeguards—covering governance, respect for Indigenous peoples' rights, and biodiversity protection. Major funders include Norway's climate and forest initiative and the Green Climate Fund.

Criticism and debate

Debates around REDD+ concern the integrity of carbon credits (permanence, leakage, and additionality), land tenure and the rights of forest-dependent communities, the risk of payment systems favouring states over local custodians, and the performance of associated voluntary carbon markets, some of whose credits have been challenged by investigative analyses. Supporters respond that reformed standards and jurisdictional (whole-region) accounting can address these problems, and that finance for standing forests remains essential because deforestation accounts for roughly a tenth of global greenhouse gas emissions.
