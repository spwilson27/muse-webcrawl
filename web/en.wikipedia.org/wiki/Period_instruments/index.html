Period instruments

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Period instruments are musical instruments that were made to the designs of the era in which the music performed on them was composed, either original instruments preserved from that era or faithful modern reconstructions. Their use is central to the historically informed performance (HIP) movement in Western classical music, which seeks to approach the sound world a composer would have known.

Characteristics

Baroque and Classical instruments differ from their modern counterparts in materials and construction: violins used gut strings, lower neck angles and shorter fingerboards; flutes and oboes were wooden and keyless or simply keyed; trumpets and horns were valveless "natural" instruments whose players selected harmonics with the lips; and keyboard music was played on harpsichords, clavichords and early fortepianos rather than the modern concert grand. Pitch standards were lower (often A = 415 Hz for Baroque music) and temperaments unequal.

The movement

Pioneers such as Arnold Dolmetsch, Wanda Landowska and August Wenzinger revived early instruments in the first half of the 20th century. From the 1960s Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt, Ton Koopman, John Eliot Gardiner, Trevor Pinnock and Christopher Hogwood founded period-instrument ensembles — Concentus Musicus Wien, the English Concert, the Academy of Ancient Music, the Orchestre Révolutionnaire et Romantique — whose recordings of Bach, Handel, Mozart and eventually Beethoven, Brahms and beyond changed mainstream expectations of tempo, articulation and balance.

Criticism

Critics, notably Richard Taruskin, have argued that "authenticity" is itself a modern taste, and that survivals of instruments and treatises cannot fully recover past performance. The movement nonetheless permanently widened the palette of sounds available to performers of early music.
