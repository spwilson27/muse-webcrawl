White adipose tissue

White adipose tissue (WAT), or white fat, is one of two types of adipose tissue found in mammals, the other being brown adipose tissue. In humans, white adipose tissue normally composes as much as 20% of body weight in men and 25% in women.

Structure

White fat is composed of white adipocytes, cells whose central feature is a single large lipid droplet (unilocular) that pushes the nucleus and cytoplasm to the cell periphery. The tissue is held together by a connective tissue matrix containing fibroblasts, immune cells, nerves, and blood vessels — collectively the stromal vascular fraction. White adipocytes typically measure 25–200 micrometres in diameter.

Function

White adipose tissue serves as the body's main long-term energy store, accumulating triglycerides during periods of energy surplus and releasing fatty acids during fasting. It also provides thermal insulation and mechanical cushioning around organs. Beyond storage, WAT is an active endocrine organ, secreting hormones and signalling molecules (adipokines) such as leptin, adiponectin, and resistin, as well as inflammatory cytokines that influence appetite, insulin sensitivity, and immunity.

Distribution and health

White fat is distributed in subcutaneous depots and in visceral depots around the abdomen and organs. Excess visceral white adipose tissue is associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Under certain conditions (cold exposure, exercise), white adipocytes can acquire brown-fat-like characteristics, producing "beige" or "brite" adipocytes that express UCP1 and burn energy as heat.
