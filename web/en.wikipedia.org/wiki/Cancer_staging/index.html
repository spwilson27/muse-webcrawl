Cancer staging

Cancer staging is the process of determining the extent to which a cancer has developed and spread in the body. Assigning a stage is important for prognosis, for choosing treatment, and for comparing outcomes between patients and clinical trials.

TNM system

The most widely used system is the TNM system, maintained by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). It describes:

  • T – size and extent of the primary tumor;
  • N – spread to nearby (regional) lymph nodes;
  • M – distant metastasis.

Numbers or letters after each letter give more detail, e.g. T1, N0, M0. TNM combinations are grouped into an overall stage, usually written with Roman numerals from Stage I (small, localized) to Stage IV (distant spread).

Other systems

Some cancers use specialized systems: the FIGO system for gynaecological cancers, the Ann Arbor staging for lymphomas, and Dukes' staging historically for colorectal cancer. Blood cancers such as leukaemia are not staged by TNM because they are systemic from the outset.

Methods

Staging may be clinical, based on physical examination, imaging such as CT, MRI and PET, laboratory tests and biopsy, or pathological, based on examination of tissue removed at surgery. Restaging may be performed after treatment or if the cancer progresses.

Stage grouping and prognosis

In general, earlier-stage cancers confined to their site of origin have a better prognosis and may be cured by surgery or radiotherapy alone, while later stages often require systemic therapy such as chemotherapy, targeted therapy or immunotherapy.
