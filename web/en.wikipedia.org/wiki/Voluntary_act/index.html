Voluntary act

A voluntary act is a willed bodily movement, and in criminal law it is the core of the actus reus: a person cannot ordinarily be guilty of an offence for conduct that was not voluntary. Movements that are reflexive, convulsive, or performed while unconscious — during sleepwalking, an epileptic seizure, or under hypnosis in some accounts — lack the element of volition and so cannot constitute the prohibited act.

Automatism

The defence of automatism arises where the defendant's muscles acted without control by the mind. English law distinguishes insane automatism (caused by an internal "disease of the mind", leading to the special verdict under the insanity rules) from non-insane automatism (caused by an external factor, such as a blow to the head or a swarm of bees causing a driver to lose control, as in Hill v Baxter), which leads to outright acquittal. Diabetes cases illustrate the line: hyperglycaemia has been treated as internal and hypoglycaemia caused by insulin as external.

Possession and status offences

Voluntariness also matters for possession offences, which require knowing control of the item, and courts have resisted punishing mere status — in the United States, Robinson v. California (1962) held that punishing a person for the status of drug addiction was cruel and unusual punishment.

Model Penal Code

The Model Penal Code §2.01 states the requirement expressly: liability must be based on conduct that includes a voluntary act or a culpable omission to perform an act of which the person is physically capable.
