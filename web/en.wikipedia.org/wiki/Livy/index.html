Livy

Titus Livius (59 BC – AD 17), known in English as Livy, was a Roman historian from Patavium (modern Padua). His monumental history of Rome, Ab Urbe Condita ("From the Founding of the City"), covered Roman history from the legendary arrival of Aeneas to the reign of Augustus in 142 books, of which 35 survive in full. Livy wrote to celebrate and to lament Rome's past, offering moral exemplars drawn from its rise to world power.

Life

Little is recorded of Livy's life. He spent much of his career in Rome, where Augustus — despite teasing him as a "Pompeian" for his republican sympathies — treated him with respect; he also encouraged the future emperor Claudius in historical writing. He returned to Padua and died there in AD 17.

Ab Urbe Condita

Livy composed his history in instalments over some forty years, drawing on earlier annalists such as Fabius Pictor, Polybius (for eastern affairs) and Valerius Antias. The surviving books 1–10 cover Rome to 293 BC, and books 21–45 the Second Punic War and the wars in the Greek east down to 167 BC; the rest are known from summaries (the Periochae) and fragments. His dramatic, rhetorical narrative — full of set speeches and vivid battle pieces such as Hannibal's crossing of the Alps and the disaster at Cannae — prized moral instruction over critical source-work: history, he wrote, shows examples to imitate and warnings to avoid.

Legacy

Livy was the standard Roman historian of the Renaissance, admired by Dante (who places him among the great pagans), Petrarch and Machiavelli, whose Discourses are a commentary on Livy's first decade. The survival of only a quarter of the work is counted among the great losses of classical literature.
