Froth flotation

Froth flotation is a process for selectively separating hydrophobic materials from hydrophilic ones. It is used in mineral processing, paper recycling and waste-water treatment. Historically this was first used in the mining industry, where it was one of the great enabling technologies of the 20th century. It has been described as "the most important operation used for the recovery and upgrading of sulfide ores."

Principle

Finely ground ore is mixed with water to form a slurry, or pulp, in a flotation cell. Reagents called collectors render the target mineral particles water-repellent (hydrophobic), while depressants keep unwanted gangue minerals wetted. Air is bubbled through the pulp; the hydrophobic particles attach to the bubbles, rise to the surface and form a mineral-laden froth, stabilised by frothing agents, which is skimmed off as the concentrate. The remaining gangue sinks and is discharged as tailings.

Applications

Flotation is used to concentrate copper, lead, zinc, nickel, molybdenum, gold-bearing and other sulfide ores, as well as non-sulfide minerals such as fluorite, phosphate rock, potash and coal fines. Outside mining, dissolved air flotation removes oils and suspended solids from industrial wastewater, and froth flotation in the form of de-inking flotation recycles paper by separating ink particles from fibre.

History

The process developed in the first decade of the 1900s, notably at Broken Hill, Australia, where it allowed the treatment of enormous stockpiles of zinc-rich tailings that earlier gravity methods could not process. Its adoption transformed the economics of low-grade porphyry copper deposits worldwide.
