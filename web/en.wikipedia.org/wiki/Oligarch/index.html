Oligarch

An oligarch is a member of an oligarchy — a form of power structure in which authority rests with a small number of people. The term derives from the Greek oligarkhia (ὀλιγαρχία), from oligos (ὀλίγος, "few") and arkho (ἄρχω, "to rule or command"). In modern usage, and in the press, "oligarch" most often refers to extremely wealthy business magnates — particularly the business oligarchs who emerged in Russia and other post-Soviet states in the 1990s — who wield significant political influence through their fortunes.

Classical usage

Aristotle distinguished oligarchy, the rule of the few in their own interest, from aristocracy, which he regarded as rule by the best. In ancient Greek city-states such as Sparta and Corinth, oligarchic governments restricted political rights to a property-owning class. The Thirty Tyrants who ruled Athens briefly in 404–403 BC are a famous example of an oligarchic regime. Oligarchies contrast with democracies (rule by the many) and monarchies (rule by one).

Russian and post-Soviet oligarchs

During the privatization of state assets under President Boris Yeltsin in the mid-1990s, a small group of businessmen acquired control of major Russian industries — oil, metals, media and banking — often through the controversial loans-for-shares scheme. Figures such as Boris Berezovsky, Mikhail Khodorkovsky, Vladimir Gusinsky and Roman Abramovich amassed vast fortunes and, for a time, significant political power. After Vladimir Putin came to power in 2000, the influence of the Yeltsin-era oligarchs was sharply curtailed: Khodorkovsky was imprisoned and his Yukos oil company dismantled, while others went into exile or accommodated themselves to the Kremlin.

Ukrainian oligarchs

In Ukraine, oligarchs such as Rinat Akhmetov, Ihor Kolomoyskyi, Viktor Pinchuk and Dmytro Firtash controlled major industrial groups, television channels and political factions. Reducing oligarchic influence — "de-oligarchization" — became a stated goal of Ukrainian governments, and in 2021 Ukraine adopted a law defining oligarchs and restricting their political activities.

Wider usage

The word is also applied to wealthy elites in other countries, including China, India and the United States, where critics use it to describe billionaires perceived to exercise disproportionate political influence through campaign finance, lobbying and media ownership. Political scientists debate whether such usage is analytically precise or primarily rhetorical.
