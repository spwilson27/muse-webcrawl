The Great Plague of London, lasting from 1665 to 1666, was the last major epidemic of bubonic plague in England. It killed an estimated 100,000 people — roughly a quarter of London's population — in eighteen months, as recorded week by week in the city's Bills of Mortality.

Course of the epidemic

Cases appeared in the parish of St Giles-in-the-Fields over the winter of 1664–65 and exploded in the hot summer of 1665. King Charles II and the court fled to Oxford; Parliament was prorogued; trade halted. The Lord Mayor ordered draconian measures: infected households were shut up under guard with a red cross and the words "Lord have mercy upon us" painted on the door, watchmen supplied food, and dead-carts collected corpses at night for plague pits, crying "Bring out your dead". Deaths peaked in September 1665 at over 7,000 a week by the official bills, themselves undercounts. The epidemic waned through 1666, the last recorded cases fading as the Great Fire destroyed the rat-infested medieval city center in September of that year.

Records and legacy

Samuel Pepys's diary is the classic eyewitness account; Daniel Defoe's A Journal of the Plague Year (1722), though written two generations later, shaped the popular memory of the event. Modern aDNA studies of plague-pit burials (e.g. at Liverpool Street) have confirmed Yersinia pestis as the cause. The Great Plague ended the era of plague in England, though the disease continued on the continent into the 18th century.
