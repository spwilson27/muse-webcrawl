West African Craton

The West African Craton (WAC) is one of the five large cratons of the Precambrian basement rock of Africa that make up the African Plate, the others being the East Saharan, Congo, Tanzania, and Zimbabwe cratons. These terranes came together during the late Precambrian and early Palaeozoic to form the African continent. The West African Craton underlies much of West Africa, including parts of Algeria, Mali, Niger, Burkina Faso, Ghana, Ivory Coast, Guinea, Senegal, Mauritania, and Morocco's Anti-Atlas region.

Geology

The craton consists of two Archaean shields—the Reguibat shield in the north and the Man (or Leo) shield in the south—separated by the Palaeoproterozoic Taoudeni Basin, a vast accumulation of largely undeformed sedimentary rocks. The shields were assembled and stabilized during the Eburnean orogeny around 2 billion years ago. The craton has been a comparatively stable block since then, surrounded by younger mobile belts formed during the Pan-African orogeny about 600 million years ago.

Mineral resources

The Birimian greenstone belts of the southern shield are rich in gold and other minerals, and the craton hosts major deposits of iron ore, bauxite, diamonds, and phosphates. Artisanal and industrial gold mining in Ghana, Mali, and Burkina Faso largely exploits these ancient formations.
