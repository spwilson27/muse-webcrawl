Khalsa

The Khalsa (Punjabi: ਖ਼ਾਲਸਾ, "the pure ones") is the collective body of all initiated Sikhs, represented by the five beloved ones known as the Panj Pyare. The Khalsa was inaugurated by Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru, on Vaisakhi day — 30 March 1699 — at Anandpur Sahib in Punjab. Members of the Khalsa, known as Khalsa Sikhs or Amritdharis, are distinguished by the Five Ks and by the names Singh ("lion") for men and Kaur ("princess") for women.

History

On Vaisakhi 1699, Guru Gobind Singh emerged from a tent before a large gathering at Anandpur Sahib holding a sword and asked for a volunteer willing to give his head. Five men came forward one after another; the Guru baptised them with amrit (sweetened water stirred with a double-edged sword, the khanda) and they became the Panj Pyare, the first members of the Khalsa. The Guru then knelt and received initiation from them in turn, declaring that the Khalsa was the Guru and the Guru was the Khalsa.

The founding of the Khalsa transformed the Sikh community into a disciplined, visible body bound by a code of conduct (the Rehat Maryada). Khalsa Sikhs played a central military and political role in the following centuries, from the guerrilla bands (misls) of the eighteenth century to the Sikh Empire of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and in the Indian Army's Sikh regiments.

Initiation

Initiation into the Khalsa, called Khande di Pahul or Amrit Sanchar, is open to Sikhs of any caste, gender or background who feel ready to accept its discipline. Five initiated Sikhs conduct the ceremony, preparing amrit in an iron bowl while reciting the five banis (hymns). The initiate drinks the amrit, has it sprinkled on the eyes and hair, and accepts the obligations of the Khalsa.

The Five Ks

  • Kesh — uncut hair, a symbol of devotion and holiness.
  • Kangha — a wooden comb, symbolising cleanliness.
  • Karra — an iron or steel bracelet, symbolising restraint and the eternity of God.
  • Kachera — cotton undergarments, symbolising modesty and self-control.
  • Kirpan — a curved sword or dagger, symbolising the duty to protect the weak.

Code of conduct

Khalsa Sikhs undertake to abstain from cutting the hair, adultery, intoxicants including tobacco, and kutha (ritually slaughtered) meat, and to rise early, meditate on God's name (Naam Japna), earn an honest living (Kirat Karni) and share with others (Vand Chakna).
