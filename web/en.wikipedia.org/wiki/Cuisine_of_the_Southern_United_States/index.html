Cuisine of the Southern United States

The cuisine of the Southern United States is the historical regional culinary form of the states generally south of the Mason–Dixon line and west to Texas. It developed from a blending of Native American staples (corn, beans, squash), English and Scots-Irish cooking, and — most profoundly — the foodways of enslaved West Africans, who brought okra, black-eyed peas, sesame and rice-growing expertise, and shaped dishes central to the tradition. "Soul food" is the cuisine developed by African Americans in the South from these roots.

Signature foods

Hallmark dishes include Southern fried chicken, barbecue (with fierce regional disputes over pork vs. beef and the style of sauce), cornbread, biscuits with gravy, collard greens cooked with ham hock, macaroni and cheese, grits, hushpuppies, fried catfish, pecan pie and sweet iced tea — sometimes called "the house wine of the South". Pork in many forms (ham, bacon, chitterlings, fatback) has historically been the region's principal meat.

Regional varieties

  • Lowcountry (coastal South Carolina and Georgia): rice-based dishes such as shrimp and grits, Hoppin' John and she-crab soup.
  • Louisiana: Cajun and Creole cuisines — gumbo, jambalaya, étouffée, po' boy sandwiches, beignets.
  • Appalachia: soup beans, stack cakes, country ham, foraged greens and berries.
  • Texas: beef barbecue, chicken-fried steak and Tex-Mex blends at its borders.
  • Chesapeake: blue crab, crab cakes and Smith Island cake.

Drinks

Sweet tea, bourbon whiskey, mint juleps and lemonade are emblematic; Coca-Cola, Dr Pepper and other soft drinks originated in Southern drugstores and remain strongly associated with the region.
