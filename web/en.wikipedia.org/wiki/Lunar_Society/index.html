Lunar Society of Birmingham
Foundedc. 1765
Dissolvedc. 1813

The Lunar Society of Birmingham was a British dinner club and informal learned society of prominent figures in the Midlands Enlightenment, including industrialists, natural philosophers and intellectuals, who met regularly between about 1765 and 1813 in Birmingham, England. Members called themselves "Lunaticks," a pun on their practice of meeting on the Monday nearest the full moon so that the moonlight would light their journey home.

Members and meetings

The core of the society included manufacturer Matthew Boulton, engineer James Watt, physician and poet Erasmus Darwin, chemist Joseph Priestley, potter Josiah Wedgwood, geologist and physician William Withering, printer John Baskerville, and later the engineer William Murdoch. Meetings were held at members' homes, notably Boulton's Soho House, and combined dining with experiments, demonstrations and discussion of science and its industrial applications.

Influence

The Lunar Society is credited with fostering key developments of the Industrial Revolution: improvements to the steam engine, advances in chemistry (including Priestley's work on gases), geology, medicine and manufacturing methods. Its members' informal exchange of practical and theoretical knowledge is often cited as a model of the interaction between science and industry in the Enlightenment. The society declined after about 1800 as founding members aged and died; Priestley's home and laboratory had already been destroyed in the Birmingham riots of 1791. Its name is commemorated by the modern Lunar Society, founded in Birmingham in 1990.
