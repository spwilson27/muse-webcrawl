Brunch is a meal that combines breakfast and lunch, typically eaten in the late morning to early afternoon, most often on weekends and holidays. The word is a portmanteau of breakfast and lunch. Brunch menus blend breakfast staples — eggs, pancakes, French toast, bacon, pastries — with heartier lunch items and, in restaurants, celebratory drinks such as mimosas and Bloody Marys. Since the late 20th century brunch has become a major social institution and an economic mainstay of restaurants in North America and beyond.

History

The term "brunch" was coined in England in 1895 by the writer Guy Beringer, whose article "Brunch: A Plea" in Hunter's Weekly proposed a late Sunday meal for "Saturday-night carousers" — a lighter, later, more sociable alternative to the heavy post-church breakfast, beginning with tea and toast and working up to breakfast meats, so that "brunch is cheerful, sociable and inciting… it puts you in a good temper." The custom drew on older traditions of late-morning hunt breakfasts. Brunch spread to the United States in the 1930s, aided by Hollywood stars stopping in Chicago on transcontinental train journeys who ate a late-morning meal, and by post–Second World War changes: later Sunday rising as church attendance patterns changed, and more women in the workforce eager for a weekend respite from cooking. Hotels and restaurants promoted expansive Sunday brunches — buffets of eggs, roasts, seafood, and desserts — and by the 1980s and 1990s brunch was a fixture of urban weekend life in New York, Los Angeles, and other cities.

Food and drink

Classic brunch dishes include Eggs Benedict and its variants (Florentine, with spinach; royale, with smoked salmon), omelettes, quiches, pancakes, waffles, French toast, hash browns, shakshuka, breakfast sandwiches on English muffins or biscuits, and — since the 2010s — avocado toast, a dish whose restaurant popularity became shorthand for millennial food culture. Sweet offerings include scones, muffins, croissants, and cinnamon rolls. Coffee and tea anchor the meal; alcoholic staples are the mimosa (champagne and orange juice, associated with the Ritz in Paris and 1930s America), the Bloody Mary, and the Bellini. "Bottomless brunch," offering unlimited drinks for a fixed price, became a widely marketed — and sometimes regulated — format in the 2010s.

Cultural significance

Brunch is as much a social ritual as a meal: unhurried, communal, and often the centrepiece of a weekend day. It has been embraced by diverse communities — from the African American tradition of post-church Sunday dinners to "drag brunch," in which drag performers entertain diners, a format that spread from gay bars to mainstream venues. Critics have at times treated brunch as a symbol of frivolity or gentrification, while sociologists note its role as "third place" socialising for young urban adults. Equivalent late-morning meals exist worldwide, from dim sum in Cantonese culture (yum cha) to the German Frühschoppen and the elaborate hotel brunches of the Middle East.
