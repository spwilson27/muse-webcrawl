Atrial septal defect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An atrial septal defect (ASD) is a congenital heart defect in which blood flows between the atria of the heart through an opening in the interatrial septum. It is among the most common congenital cardiac anomalies, accounting for roughly 10% of congenital heart disease, and is more frequent in females.

Types

The main types are classified by location: ostium secundum (the most common, in the region of the fossa ovalis), ostium primum (part of the atrioventricular septal defect spectrum), sinus venosus (near the entry of the superior or inferior vena cava), and coronary sinus defects. A patent foramen ovale is a related but distinct finding.

Pathophysiology and symptoms

Because left atrial pressure exceeds right atrial pressure, blood typically shunts left-to-right, causing right atrial and right ventricular volume overload and increased pulmonary blood flow. Small defects may close spontaneously and cause no symptoms; larger defects may produce exercise intolerance, breathlessness, palpitations and, over decades, pulmonary hypertension. Rarely, shunt reversal (Eisenmenger syndrome) or paradoxical embolism with stroke can occur.

Diagnosis

Auscultation may reveal a systolic ejection murmur and fixed splitting of the second heart sound. Diagnosis is confirmed by echocardiography, with transoesophageal echo, CT or cardiac MRI used to define anatomy before intervention.

Treatment

Significant defects with right heart enlargement are usually closed, either surgically or — for suitable secundum defects — by a catheter-delivered occluder device. Small, asymptomatic defects may simply be monitored.
