Vegetative state

A vegetative state (VS), also called unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, is a disorder of consciousness in which a patient has recovered a sleep–wake cycle and basic autonomic functions following severe brain damage but shows no signs of awareness of themselves or their environment. Unlike a coma, a vegetative patient may open their eyes and exhibit reflex movements, but responses are reflexive rather than purposeful.

Causes and description

The condition results from damage to the cerebral hemispheres or their connections while the brainstem remains relatively intact. Causes include traumatic brain injury and non-traumatic insults such as stroke, anoxia after cardiac arrest, and degenerative disease. The term was introduced by Bryan Jennett and Fred Plum in 1972. Patients in a vegetative state may breathe unaided, have regular sleep–wake cycles, and display reflexes such as startle responses, grinding of teeth, and roving eye movements, without reproducible voluntary behaviour.

Diagnosis and related states

Diagnosis is clinical, supplemented by neuroimaging and electrophysiology, and must be distinguished from the minimally conscious state, in which patients show limited but definite signs of awareness, and from locked-in syndrome, in which awareness is preserved but movement is almost entirely lost. Rates of misdiagnosis are substantial when assessment relies on unstructured examination; standardized scales such as the Coma Recovery Scale–Revised improve accuracy. Functional MRI studies have shown that a minority of behaviourally unresponsive patients can follow commands covertly.

Prognosis and ethics

Recovery of consciousness becomes increasingly unlikely the longer the state persists; conventionally, a vegetative state following traumatic injury is termed "permanent" after 12 months, and after 3–6 months for non-traumatic causes, though late recoveries are documented. The condition has been at the centre of prominent legal and ethical debates about life-sustaining treatment and artificial nutrition and hydration, including the cases of Karen Ann Quinlan, Terri Schiavo, and Tony Bland.
