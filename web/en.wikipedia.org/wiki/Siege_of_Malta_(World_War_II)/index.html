Siege of Malta (World War II)

The siege of Malta in the Second World War was a military campaign in the Mediterranean theatre. From June 1940 to November 1942, the fight for the control of the strategically important island of the British Crown Colony of Malta pitted the air and naval forces of Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany against the Royal Air Force (RAF) and the Royal Navy. Malta's position astride the sea routes between Italy and North Africa made it a vital base from which British forces attacked Axis supply convoys to Libya, and a target the Axis sought to bomb or starve into submission.

Background

Malta lay some 90 km south of Sicily and commanded the narrow passage between the eastern and western Mediterranean. When Italy declared war on 10 June 1940, the island was defended by a handful of aircraft, including the famous Gloster Gladiator biplanes later christened Faith, Hope and Charity.

The siege

The Italian Regia Aeronautica, and from early 1941 and again in 1942 the German Luftwaffe under Field Marshal Albert Kesselring, subjected Malta to one of the heaviest bombing campaigns of the war — over 3,300 alerts and some 15,000 tonnes of bombs on the Grand Harbour area alone. Supplies arrived only by hard-fought convoys, most famously Operation Pedestal in August 1942, when the tanker Ohio was nursed into harbour with aviation fuel that kept the island fighting. Malta-based submarines, surface ships and aircraft meanwhile sank a large share of the shipping supplying Axis forces in North Africa. On 15 April 1942 King George VI awarded the George Cross to the island collectively "to bear witness to a heroism and devotion that will long be famous in history".

Aftermath

The Axis abandonment of the invasion plan (Operation Herkules) and the Allied victory at El Alamein ended the siege in November 1942. Malta then served as headquarters for the Allied invasion of Sicily in 1943.
