Charles Stewart Parnell (27 June 1846 – 6 October 1891) was an Irish nationalist politician who served as a Member of Parliament from 1875 and led the Irish Parliamentary Party from 1882 until his fall in 1890. A Protestant landowner from County Wicklow, he became the dominant figure of Irish politics — the "Uncrowned King of Ireland" — by fusing parliamentary obstruction, agrarian agitation, and disciplined party organization into a movement that came close to winning Home Rule for Ireland.

Rise and the Land War

Parnell entered Parliament in 1875 and won prominence through filibustering tactics that forced Westminster to attend to Irish grievances. In 1879 he became president of Michael Davitt's Irish National Land League, which fought for the "three Fs" (fair rent, fixity of tenure, free sale) during the Land War, using boycotts — a tactic named for its first target, land agent Captain Charles Boycott (1880). Gladstone's Land Act of 1881 conceded much of the program; Parnell, imprisoned in Kilmainham Gaol in 1881–82 for denouncing it as insufficient, negotiated the "Kilmainham Treaty" securing his release and arrears relief in exchange for calming agitation.

Home Rule and the fall

Parnell rebuilt the movement as the Irish Parliamentary Party, a tightly disciplined national machine. Holding the balance of power after the 1885 election, he converted Gladstone to Home Rule; the first Home Rule Bill failed in the Commons in 1886, splitting the Liberal Party. In 1887 The Times published letters — the "Pigott forgeries" — purporting to link Parnell to the Phoenix Park murders; a special commission exposed them as fakes, and Parnell was vindicated in 1890 to wild acclaim. Months later, Captain William O'Shea's divorce suit named Parnell as co-respondent with O'Shea's wife Katharine, whom Parnell had lived with for a decade. Nonconformist Liberal opinion and the Irish Catholic hierarchy turned against him; Gladstone made his removal a condition of alliance. Parnell refused to step down, splitting his party in a bitter committee-room confrontation (December 1890). He married Katharine in June 1891, fought three losing by-elections, and died that October, aged 45.

Legacy

Parnell's fall delayed Home Rule for a generation and left a permanent scar on Irish politics; the split of 1890 haunted nationalist memory and literature — James Joyce's family were Parnellites, and A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man and the story "Ivy Day in the Committee Room" return obsessively to his betrayal and death. Modern historians rank him among the most effective parliamentary leaders of the 19th century.
