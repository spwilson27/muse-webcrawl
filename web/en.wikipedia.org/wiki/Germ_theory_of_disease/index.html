Germ theory of disease

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The germ theory of disease is the scientific theory that many diseases are caused by microorganisms — bacteria, viruses, fungi and other pathogens — invading a host. It displaced older explanations such as miasma ("bad air") and humoral imbalance, and it is the foundation of modern medicine, hygiene and infection control.

Development

Precursors include Girolamo Fracastoro's sixteenth-century idea of contagion by "seeds", Ignaz Semmelweis's 1847 demonstration that hand disinfection slashed childbed fever deaths in Vienna, and John Snow's mapping of the 1854 London cholera outbreak to a contaminated water pump. The theory was established experimentally by Louis Pasteur's work on fermentation and spontaneous generation in the 1860s, Joseph Lister's antiseptic surgery (1867), and Robert Koch's proofs for anthrax (1876), tuberculosis (1882) and cholera (1883).

Consequences

Germ theory led directly to sterilisation, aseptic technique, water treatment and sanitation, vaccination programmes (see vaccine), antibiotics, and the modern understanding of epidemic spread applied during the COVID-19 pandemic. Some diseases once blamed on germs alone are now understood to involve genetics and environment as well, and a few conditions (such as peptic ulcers, long attributed to stress) were conversely shown to be infectious — refinements of, not exceptions to, the theory.
