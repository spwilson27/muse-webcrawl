Employer of last resort

The employer of last resort (ELR), also known as a job guarantee program, is an economic policy proposal in which the government offers a job to anyone willing and able to work who cannot find employment in the private sector, typically at a fixed, socially acceptable wage. The policy is a cornerstone of Modern Monetary Theory (MMT), developed by economists including Hyman Minsky, Warren Mosler, Bill Mitchell, and L. Randall Wray, who also call it a "buffer stock" employment scheme.

Public employment programs such as the WPA are historical analogues of ELR proposals.

Concept

Under ELR, the state maintains a standing offer of employment, acting as an employer of last resort just as a central bank acts as a lender of last resort. The program expands automatically in recessions (employing more people) and shrinks in booms as private employers hire workers out of the pool — an automatic stabilizer replacing a "buffer stock" of unemployed people with a buffer stock of employed people. Proponents argue the fixed program wage acts as a price anchor that stabilizes wages and inflation, analogous to a commodity buffer stock, and that ELR achieves true full employment with less inflationary pressure than conventional aggregate-demand stimulus (contrasting with the NAIRU framework, which maintains unemployment to discipline prices).

Proposed work and administration

Proposed jobs focus on public and community services: environmental restoration, infrastructure maintenance, eldercare and childcare assistance, public health outreach, and the arts. Modern proposals (e.g., a federal job guarantee in the US, advocated estimates by the Levy Economics Institute) typically set the wage at a living wage (often $15/hour in US proposals) with benefits, administered locally but funded federally to avoid local budget constraints.

Precedents and experiments

Historical analogues include the New Deal's Works Progress Administration and Civilian Conservation Corps, Sweden's postwar public employment, Argentina's Jefes de Hogar program (created after the 2001 crisis, employing ~2 million at its peak, mostly women, in community services), and India's Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (2005), which legally guarantees 100 days of wage employment per rural household per year — the closest large-scale legal implementation of a job guarantee.

Criticism

Critics raise concerns about cost and fiscal risk; displacement of existing public-sector and private jobs; administrative complexity; whether make-work undermines skills or stigmatizes participants; inflationary pressure from wage competition; and political feasibility of terminating projects. Supporters respond with cost-offset analyses (reduced welfare, health, and crime costs) and point to Jefes and MGNREGA to argue feasibility.
