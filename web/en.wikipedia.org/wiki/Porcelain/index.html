Porcelain

Porcelain
TypeCeramic material
Main ingredientsKaolin, feldspar, quartz

OriginChina, by the Tang dynasty (618–907)

Porcelain is a ceramic material made by heating materials, generally including a material like kaolin, in a kiln to temperatures between 1,200 and 1,400 °C (2,200 and 2,600 °F). The strength, toughness, whiteness and translucence of porcelain, relative to other types of pottery, arise mainly from vitrification and the formation of the mineral mullite within the body at these high temperatures. End applications include tableware, decorative ware such as figurines, and products in technology and industry such as electrical insulators and laboratory ware.

A blue-and-white porcelain vase of the Ming dynasty style

History

Porcelain was developed in China over a long period: proto-porcelain wares appear by the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE), and mature porcelain was being produced by the Tang dynasty (618–907 CE). The kilns of Jingdezhen in Jiangxi province became the world's porcelain capital, mass-producing fine wares for the imperial court and for export. Under the Yuan and Ming dynasties, blue-and-white porcelain — white ware painted under the glaze with cobalt blue — became China's most famous ceramic export, reaching markets from Southeast Asia to Europe and profoundly influencing pottery traditions in Persia, Turkey and the Netherlands (Delftware).

Europeans imported Chinese porcelain for centuries without learning how to make it: the secrets of hard-paste porcelain were closely guarded, and European imitations used soft-paste bodies. In 1708–1709, at Meissen in Saxony, Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus and Johann Friedrich Böttger succeeded in producing true hard-paste porcelain, leading to the founding of the Meissen factory in 1710 — the first European porcelain manufactory. Factories followed across Europe, including Sèvres in France and, in England, producers of bone china, a body strengthened with bone ash that became the English standard.

Materials and manufacture

The classic porcelain body combines kaolin (a white clay essential for plasticity and whiteness), feldspar (a flux that lowers the melting point and promotes vitrification) and quartz or flint. The formed ware is typically fired twice: a biscuit firing, after which a glaze is applied, and a higher-temperature glost firing in which body and glaze mature together. At peak temperature the body vitrifies into a glassy, non-porous mass, which is why porcelain is impermeable without relying on its glaze and rings when struck.

Types

  • Hard-paste porcelain — the original Chinese type, made from kaolin and a feldspathic rock, fired at about 1,400 °C.
  • Soft-paste porcelain — European early attempts using clay mixed with a glassy frit, fired at lower temperatures; softer and more prone to chipping.
  • Bone china — contains at least 30% bone ash; highly white, strong and translucent, developed in England in the late eighteenth century.

Uses

Porcelain is used for tableware and decorative objects, sanitary ware, dental crowns and bridges, electrical insulators (where its dielectric strength and durability are valued), laboratory crucibles, and tiles. In Chinese, the same word, cí (瓷), covers porcelain generally, and in English the material gave its name metonymically to the country of its origin: fine porcelain tableware is still commonly called simply "china".
