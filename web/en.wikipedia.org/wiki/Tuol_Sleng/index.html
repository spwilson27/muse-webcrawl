Tuol Sleng (S-21)
LocationPhnom Penh, Cambodia
FunctionKhmer Rouge security prison and interrogation centre, 1975/76–1979
CommandantKang Kek Iew ("Duch")
VictimsAn estimated 14,000–18,000+ prisoners; only a handful known to have survived
NowTuol Sleng Genocide Museum (opened 1980); archives inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register

Tuol Sleng (Khmer for "hill of the poisonous trees") was the Khmer Rouge regime's most notorious prison, codenamed S-21, in Phnom Penh, Cambodia. A former high school (Tuol Svay Prey) converted in 1975–76, it became the central node of Democratic Kampuchea's internal terror: party cadres, officials, soldiers, intellectuals, foreigners and finally the regime's own purged ranks were brought here, photographed on arrival, tortured into confessing fictitious espionage, and trucked to the Choeung Ek killing field outside the city.

The prison and its aftermath

Under its commandant Duch, S-21 was run with bureaucratic meticulousness: mugshots, biographies, forced confessions filed in triplicate — documentation that now condemns it. Of the at least 14,000 people processed, only a tiny number are known to have survived — famously including the painter Vann Nath and the mechanic Chum Mey, spared because their skills were useful. When Vietnamese forces liberated Phnom Penh in January 1979, they found the last victims still chained to iron beds; the site was preserved and opened in 1980 as the Tuol Sleng Genocide Museum, its classrooms still divided into brick and wooden cells, its walls lined with the arrival photographs of the dead. Duch, found living under an alias in 1999, became the first Khmer Rouge leader convicted by the UN-backed tribunal (ECCC) — sentenced in 2010, increased on appeal to life imprisonment — and died in 2020. Tuol Sleng, paired with Choeung Ek's stupa of skulls, is Cambodia's central memorial to the years that killed roughly a quarter of its people (see Phnom Penh and the Khmer Rouge era).
