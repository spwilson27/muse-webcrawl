The Fokker D.VII was a German World War I fighter aircraft designed by Reinhold Platz of the Fokker-Flugzeugwerke. It entered service in the spring of 1918 and is widely regarded as one of the finest fighters of the war, notable for its thick cantilever wings, welded steel-tube fuselage and superb handling, including an ability to remain controllable at very high angles of attack.

Design and development

Platz's design, the V.11 prototype, won the German fighter competition held at Adlershof in January–February 1918, impressing front-line pilots including Manfred von Richthofen. Its cantilever wings required no external rigging wires, simplifying production and reducing drag. Aircraft were produced by Fokker at Schwerin and under licence by Albatros at Johannisthal and the Ostdeutsche Albatros Werke.

Operational history

The D.VII reached front-line Jastas in April–May 1918 and quickly proved superior to most Allied types. Powered initially by the 160 hp Mercedes D.III engine, later aircraft received the 185 hp BMW IIIa, which gave excellent performance at altitude. The type was so respected that it was the only aircraft specifically named in the Armistice terms: Germany was required to surrender all Fokker D.VII aircraft to the Allies.

After the war, many D.VIIs served with foreign air forces, including those of the Netherlands, Switzerland, Belgium and the Soviet Union. Anthony Fokker smuggled several hundred engines and airframes from Germany to the Netherlands, where production continued. Numerous originals and replicas survive in museums today.
