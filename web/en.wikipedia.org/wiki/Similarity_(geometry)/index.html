Similarity (geometry)

In Euclidean geometry, two objects are similar if they have exactly the same shape, though not necessarily the same size — that is, if one can be obtained from the other by a uniform scaling (enlargement or shrinking), combined with translation, rotation and reflection. Similar figures are a generalisation of congruent figures, which must also be the same size.

Similar triangles

Two triangles are similar if their corresponding angles are equal (the AA criterion), or equivalently if their corresponding sides are in the same proportion. Similarity of triangles is used to measure inaccessible distances, in trigonometry and in proofs throughout geometry. The common ratio of corresponding lengths is called the scale factor or ratio of similitude; areas of similar figures are in the squared ratio and volumes in the cubed ratio of their lengths.

General properties

All circles are similar to one another, as are all squares and all equilateral triangles. Similarity transformations preserve angles and ratios of distances, and the concept extends to similarity of curves such as parabolas and spirals.
