Chain letter

A typical chain letter

A chain letter is a message that attempts to convince the recipient to make a number of copies of the letter and then pass them on to a certain number of recipients. The "chain" is a form of social proof and exponentiating publicity. Common methods used in chain letters include emotionally manipulative stories, get-rich-quick pyramid schemes, religious messages, and threats of bad luck or supernatural consequences for breaking the chain.

Chain letters began as handwritten or printed letters sent by post; with the advent of electronic communication they spread through email, fax, and social media, where they are a form of spam and chain messaging.

History

One of the earliest known chain letters is the "Letter from Heaven" (Himmelsbrief) tradition, in which a letter supposedly written by God or an angel promised protection to its bearer, provided it was copied and passed on. Such letters circulated in Europe for centuries. Prayer chain letters, in which a prayer was to be copied and sent onward, were common by the late 19th century.

In 1935, during the Great Depression, a money-based chain letter scheme known as the "Send-a-Dime" craze swept the United States, starting in Denver, Colorado. Participants sent a dime to the name at the top of a list, removed that name, added their own at the bottom, and mailed copies to friends. The craze collapsed within weeks as the supply of new participants was exhausted, illustrating the mathematical unsustainability of such schemes.

Types

  • Good luck / bad luck letters: promise fortune to those who forward the letter and misfortune to those who break the chain.
  • Money chains and pyramid schemes: ask recipients to send money to names on a list; these are illegal in many jurisdictions.
  • Prayer chains: religious messages asking recipients to copy and distribute a prayer.
  • Petitions and hoaxes: false appeals claiming, for example, that a company will donate money for each forwarded message, or warning of nonexistent dangers.
  • Chain emails and messages: electronic equivalents spread by email and social networking services, often containing urban legends.

Legality

In the United States, chain letters that involve money or items of value and promise a return are considered a form of lottery or fraud and are illegal under federal postal law (18 U.S.C. § 1302). The United States Postal Service and postal authorities in other countries warn the public against participating. Chain letters that do not involve money are generally legal, though they may violate the terms of service of email and social media platforms.
