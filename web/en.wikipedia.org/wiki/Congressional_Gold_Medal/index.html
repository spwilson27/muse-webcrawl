Congressional Gold Medal

The Congressional Gold Medal is the highest civilian award bestowed by the United States Congress, together with the Presidential Medal of Freedom. It is awarded by an act of Congress to individuals or groups who have performed an achievement with an impact on American history and culture that is likely to be recognized long after the achievement. Each medal is individually authorized by statute, designed and struck by the United States Mint, and typically presented in a ceremony at the United States Capitol.

History

The practice began during the American Revolution, when Congress awarded gold medals to military heroes; the first went to George Washington in 1776 for the liberation of Boston, and early recipients were predominantly officers such as Horatio Gates and Anthony Wayne. Over the 19th and 20th centuries the award gradually extended to explorers, scientists, humanitarians, artists, lifesavers, and public servants. Notable recipients have included the Wright brothers, Thomas Edison, Jonas Salk, Mother Teresa, Nelson Mandela, the Tuskegee Airmen, the Navajo Code Talkers, and (collectively) the Apollo 11 crew along with John Glenn. Bronze duplicates of most medals are sold by the Mint to the public.

Process

Legislation authorizing a Congressional Gold Medal must be cosponsored by at least two-thirds of the House of Representatives (290 members) and 67 senators before it is considered in committee. By custom, the medals recognize distinguished achievement of national significance; Congress has in recent decades authorized several medals per session, prompting occasional debate over whether their frequency diminishes the honour.

Distinction from the Medal of Honor

The Congressional Gold Medal is frequently confused with the Medal of Honor, the highest U.S. military decoration, which is awarded by the President in the name of Congress for conspicuous gallantry in combat. The two awards are distinct in authority, purpose, and design.
