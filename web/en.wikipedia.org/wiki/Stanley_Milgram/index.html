Stanley Milgram

Stanley Milgram (August 15, 1933 – December 20, 1984) was an American social psychologist, best known for his controversial experiments on obedience conducted in the 1960s at Yale University. Influenced by the trial of Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann, Milgram sought to investigate how far ordinary people would go in obeying an authority figure who instructed them to harm another person. He also originated the concept of the "small world" phenomenon and the theory of the agentic state, and invented techniques — such as the lost-letter technique and the familiar stranger — that reshaped experimental social psychology.

Early life and education

Milgram was born in the Bronx, New York City, to Jewish immigrant parents. He studied political science at Queens College, then took up social psychology at Harvard, earning his doctorate in 1960 under Gordon Allport, and worked with Solomon Asch, whose conformity experiments he had tried to replicate cross-culturally in Norway and France. He joined the Yale faculty in 1960.

Obedience experiments

In the Milgram experiment (1961–62), volunteers ("teachers") were instructed by an experimenter to administer increasingly powerful electric shocks to a "learner" — in fact a confederate who received no shocks — whenever he erred in a word-pair task. To Milgram's shock, 65 percent of participants in the best-known version continued to the maximum 450 volts despite the learner's screams and eventual silence. The results, published in 1963 and in his 1974 book Obedience to Authority, suggested that ordinary people could be induced to commit harmful acts by a perceived legitimate authority, an idea widely invoked in discussions of the Holocaust and later atrocities. The experiments also drew lasting ethical criticism over deception and participant distress, and helped drive modern research-ethics reforms.

Other work

Milgram's 1967 small-world experiment, in which packets were forwarded between acquaintances toward a target person in Boston, gave rise to the popular notion of "six degrees of separation" and anticipated network science. His other studies included the lost-letter technique for measuring community attitudes, the "familiar stranger" phenomenon in urban life, city-living and mental overload, and the cyranoid — a person who speaks words relayed from another. He also made experimental films and wrote on obedience, urban psychology and the psychology of television.

Later life

Denied tenure at Harvard, Milgram moved in 1967 to the City University of New York Graduate Center, where he built a leading social psychology programme. He died in 1984 of a heart attack, aged 51. His work remains among the most famous — and debated — in psychology; partial replications in the 2000s found comparable levels of obedience.
