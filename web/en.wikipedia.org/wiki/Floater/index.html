Floater

Floaters or eye floaters are small deposits — tiny clumps of collagen, cells, or other material — suspended in the vitreous humour, the clear gel that fills the inside of the eye. Because of their location, they cast shadows on the retina and appear in the field of vision as spots, threads, rings, or cobweb-like shapes that drift as the eye moves and seem to dart away when one looks directly at them. The medical term is muscae volitantes ("flying flies"). In most cases they are a normal consequence of aging and are harmless, though a sudden shower of new floaters can signal a retinal tear or detachment, which requires urgent examination.

Causes

With age, the vitreous gradually liquefies and shrinks (syneresis), and its collagen fibers aggregate into clumps that become visible. Eventually the vitreous may pull away from the retina — a posterior vitreous detachment — often producing a characteristic ring-shaped floater and flashes of light. Floaters are more common and appear earlier in people with myopia, after cataract surgery, eye injury, or intraocular inflammation, and in diabetics. They are most noticeable against a plain bright background such as a blue sky or white screen.

Diagnosis

Examination uses a slit lamp and a dilated fundus examination to view the vitreous and check the retina for tears. Any sudden increase in floaters, flashes, or a curtain-like shadow in the vision warrants same-day assessment because of the risk of retinal detachment.

Treatment

Reassurance is usually all that is needed; the brain adapts and floaters often become less noticeable with time. For severe, persistent, vision-obscuring floaters, options include vitrectomy (surgical removal of the vitreous, effective but carrying risks of cataract and retinal detachment) and laser vitreolysis, in which a laser vaporizes the deposits; the evidence for the latter is limited. Guidelines generally advise against surgery for benign floaters except in carefully selected cases.
