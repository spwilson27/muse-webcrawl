The House of Bourbon (English: /ˈbʊərbən/, also /bɔːrˈbɒn/; French: Maison de Bourbon) is a European royal house of French origin, a branch of the Capetian dynasty, the royal House of France. Bourbon kings first ruled France and Navarre in the 16th century. A branch descended from the French Bourbons came to rule Spain in the 18th century and is the current Spanish royal house.

France

The Bourbons descended from Robert, Count of Clermont, sixth son of Louis IX of France. The senior line acceded to the French throne when Henry of Navarre became Henry IV in 1589, the first Bourbon king, ending the Valois line. His descendants — Louis XIII, Louis XIV (the "Sun King"), Louis XV, and Louis XVI — made France the dominant power of Europe. The dynasty was overthrown in the French Revolution, restored in 1814–15 under Louis XVIII and Charles X, and finally displaced in France by the July Revolution of 1830, which installed the Orléans cadet branch until 1848.

Spain and Italy

In 1700 Philip of Anjou, grandson of Louis XIV, became Philip V of Spain, founding the Spanish Bourbon line — after the War of the Spanish Succession and the Treaty of Utrecht, which barred union of the French and Spanish crowns. The Spanish Bourbons rule Spain today in the person of Felipe VI. Cadet lines ruled the Kingdoms of Naples and Sicily (later the Two Sicilies) and the Duchy of Parma until Italian unification in the 19th century; the Bourbon-Parma line now reigns in Luxembourg through the descendants of Grand Duchess Charlotte.

Pretenders and branches

Because of France's Salic law and competing legitimist and Orléanist claims, the headship of the house in France is disputed: Legitimists support the Spanish-born Louis Alphonse, Duke of Anjou, as "Louis XX", while Orléanists support Jean, Count of Paris, as "Jean IV". The family's name derives from the lordship of Bourbon-l'Archambault in central France.
