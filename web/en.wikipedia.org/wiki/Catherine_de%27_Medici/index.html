Catherine de' Medici (1519–1589) was an Italian noblewoman of the Medici family who became Queen of France as the wife of King Henry II and, after his death, the politically dominant mother of three successive kings — Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III — during the French Wars of Religion.

Early life and marriage

Born in Florence to Lorenzo de' Medici, Duke of Urbino, and Madeleine de La Tour d'Auvergne, Catherine was orphaned within weeks of her birth. In 1533, aged fourteen, she married the future Henry II. For a decade she was overshadowed at court by the king's mistress, Diane de Poitiers.

Power after 1559

Henry II's death in a jousting accident in 1559 brought Catherine to the centre of power. As regent for Charles IX she pursued a policy of compromise between Catholics and Huguenots (Protestants), issuing edicts of toleration, though civil war repeatedly broke out. She is controversially associated with the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, in which thousands of Huguenots were killed; historians debate her degree of responsibility.

Patronage and legacy

Catherine was a major patron of the arts, credited with introducing Italian influences to French cuisine, ballet (through the Ballet Comique de la Reine, 1581), architecture and court culture. Her reputation in later literature, as a sinister schemer, is now regarded by many historians as exaggerated "black legend", though she remains a symbol of Renaissance political intrigue.
