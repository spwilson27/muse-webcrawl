The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III is a black limestone Assyrian sculpture with many scenes in bas-relief and inscriptions. It comes from Nimrud (ancient Kalhu), in northern Iraq, and commemorates the deeds of King Shalmaneser III (reigned 858–824 BC). It is the most complete Assyrian obelisk yet discovered, and is notable for its early depiction of foreign tribute bearers, including the earliest known representation of an Israelite king.

Discovery

The obelisk was discovered by the British explorer and archaeologist Austen Henry Layard in 1846 during excavations at Nimrud. It was shipped to London and is now displayed in the British Museum.

Description

The obelisk stands nearly two metres high and is inscribed on all four sides with five registers of relief panels — twenty panels in total — above and below which are bands of cuneiform text. Each register depicts the king receiving tribute from a conquered region or ruler. The accompanying inscription records military campaigns of Shalmaneser's reign, listing tribute from lands including Tyre, Sidon, and the kingdom of Israel.

Jehu panel

The second register from the top is the most famous: it depicts the Israelite king Jehu — or possibly his ambassador — prostrating before Shalmaneser, accompanied by bearers carrying vessels of gold and silver. The cuneiform caption reads, in part, "The tribute of Jehu, son of Omri," and the scene, dated to about 841 BC, is the earliest known depiction of an Israelite or Judean figure and a rare instance of an Israelite king appearing in a non-biblical source. Jehu is styled "son of Omri" in reference to the dynasty that preceded him on Israel's throne.

Other registers

The remaining registers show tribute from Sua of Gilzanu (in the northwest of modern Iran), from Musri, from Marduk-apil-usur of Suhi, and from Qarparunda of Patin, including exotic animals such as camels, monkeys, an elephant, a rhinoceros, and antelopes — evidence of the reach of Assyrian diplomacy and trade.
