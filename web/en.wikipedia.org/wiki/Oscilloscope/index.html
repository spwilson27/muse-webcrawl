An oscilloscope (informally scope or O-scope) is a type of electronic test instrument that graphically displays varying voltages of one or more signals as a function of time. Their main purpose is capturing information on electrical signals for debugging, analysis, or characterization. The displayed waveform can be analyzed for properties such as amplitude, frequency, rise time, time interval, distortion, and others.

History

The oscilloscope descends from Ferdinand Braun's cathode-ray tube of 1897 and from mechanical oscillographs used to record waveforms. The modern triggered-sweep oscilloscope was commercialised by Tektronix (founded 1946), whose 511 model made stable display of repetitive waveforms routine. Analogue scopes dominated until digital storage oscilloscopes (DSOs) spread in the 1980s–90s; these digitise the signal with an analogue-to-digital converter and store it, enabling single-shot capture, automatic measurements, and computer interfaces.

Operation

A probe connects the circuit under test to the input; a vertical amplifier scales the signal, a timebase sweeps it across the screen, and a trigger circuit synchronises the sweep to a chosen level and slope so the waveform appears stationary. Key specifications are bandwidth (modern scopes range from tens of megahertz to over 100 GHz), sample rate, record length, and the number of channels. Probes are usually compensated passive ×10 types to minimise loading of the circuit. Mixed-signal oscilloscopes add digital channels for logic signals, and many instruments include serial-bus decoding and FFT spectrum analysis.

Uses

Oscilloscopes are used in electronics design and repair, embedded-systems debugging, automotive diagnostics, power engineering, medical monitoring (in adapted form), and physics research — anywhere time-varying electrical signals must be observed rather than merely measured.
