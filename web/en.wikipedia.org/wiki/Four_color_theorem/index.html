Four color theorem

In mathematics, the four color theorem, or the four color map theorem, states that no more than four colors are required to color the regions of any map so that no two adjacent regions have the same color. Adjacent means that two regions share a segment of their border, not just a point. It was the first major theorem to be proved using a computer, in 1976, and its proof remains a landmark — and a controversy — in the philosophy of mathematical proof.

History

The conjecture was first proposed in 1852 by Francis Guthrie, then a student of Augustus De Morgan at University College London, who noticed that four colours seemed sufficient while colouring a map of English counties. The problem circulated through the mathematical community; Alfred Kempe published a purported proof in 1879 that stood for eleven years before Percy Heawood found a flaw in 1890, salvaging from it the weaker five color theorem.

The 1976 proof

Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken at the University of Illinois announced a proof in 1976. Their strategy, building on ideas of Heinrich Heesch, had two parts: show that any minimal counterexample must contain one of a set of 1,936 "reducible" configurations (checked by computer), and show that at least one such configuration must appear in any planar map (an "unavoidable set", via a discharging argument). The computer ran for over a thousand hours. Because no human can feasibly check the machine computation by hand, the proof provoked debate about whether it counted as a proof at all.

Later developments

In 1997 Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas published a simpler proof using only 633 configurations, still computer-assisted. In 2005, Georges Gonthier produced a fully formalised proof checked by the Coq proof assistant, removing doubt about the correctness of the case-checking software (though not about the software's own correctness). Equivalent formulations connect the theorem to graph theory: it states that the vertices of every planar graph can be coloured with four colours so that adjacent vertices differ — the regions of a map correspond to vertices of its dual graph.
