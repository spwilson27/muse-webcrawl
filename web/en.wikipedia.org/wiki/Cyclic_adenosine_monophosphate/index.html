Cyclic adenosine monophosphate

Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP, cyclic AMP, or 3',5'-cyclic adenosine monophosphate) is a second messenger important in many biological processes. It is a derivative of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and is used for intracellular signal transduction in many organisms, conveying the effects of hormones such as glucagon and adrenaline that cannot pass through the cell membrane.

History

Earl Sutherland and Theodore Rall discovered cAMP in 1957–58 while studying how adrenaline and glucagon promote glycogen breakdown in the liver. Sutherland received the 1971 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work, which established the concept of second messengers.

Synthesis and breakdown

cAMP is synthesised from ATP by adenylyl cyclase, an enzyme at the cell membrane that is regulated by G proteins coupled to receptors for hormones and neurotransmitters. Phosphodiesterase enzymes degrade cAMP to ordinary AMP; caffeine and several drugs act by inhibiting these phosphodiesterases or stimulating adenylyl cyclase.

Functions

In eukaryotes, cAMP mainly works by activating protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates target proteins, and by activating the transcription factor CREB, altering gene expression. It also acts on exchange proteins (Epac) and cyclic-nucleotide-gated ion channels. Through these effectors cAMP regulates glycogen, sugar, and lipid metabolism, heart rate, water reabsorption in the kidney, and memory formation in the brain. In bacteria, cAMP binds the catabolite activator protein to control the use of alternative sugars such as lactose. In slime molds, secreted cAMP acts as a chemoattractant coordinating aggregation.
