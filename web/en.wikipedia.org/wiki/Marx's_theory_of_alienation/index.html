Marx's theory of alienation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Marx's theory of alienation (German: Entfremdung) describes the estrangement of people from aspects of their human nature as a consequence of living in a society divided into social classes, in particular the separation of the worker from the products of their labour under capitalism. Karl Marx developed the theory most fully in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 (first published in 1932) and returned to related themes throughout his later work, including Capital.

The four forms of alienation

In the 1844 Manuscripts Marx distinguishes four connected ways in which the worker is alienated in capitalist production:

  • Alienation from the product of labour: the object the worker produces confronts the worker as something alien and hostile; the more the worker produces, the more powerful the world of commodities owned by others becomes, and the poorer the worker's own inner world.
  • Alienation from the activity of production: work is experienced not as self-expression or satisfaction but as forced labour — external, coerced, and performed only as a means to survive; the worker feels at home only outside work.
  • Alienation from "species-being" (Gattungswesen): humans' distinctively free, conscious and creative life-activity is degraded into a mere means of individual existence.
  • Alienation from other people: the worker's relations to others are mediated by competition and by the capitalist who appropriates the product, so that human beings confront each other as buyers, sellers and rivals rather than as collaborators.

Intellectual background

Marx adapted the concept of alienation from German idealism: Hegel had treated alienation (and its overcoming) as a stage in the self-development of Spirit, while Feuerbach argued that religion alienates human qualities by projecting them onto God. Marx turned the idea materialist, grounding it in labour and private property. He held that private property is as much the consequence as the cause of alienated labour, and that communism — the positive abolition of private property — would overcome alienation and restore unalienated, communal human activity.

Influence and debate

Because the Manuscripts appeared only in 1932, the alienation theory reshaped 20th-century readings of Marx, fuelling "Marxist humanism" and debates — notably Louis Althusser's claim of an "epistemological break" — over whether the humanist concerns of the young Marx persist in the mature critique of political economy. Concepts descended from the theory include commodity fetishism in Capital and the Frankfurt School's theories of reification (following Georg Lukács). Alienation remains a central topic in sociology, critical theory and the philosophy of work.
