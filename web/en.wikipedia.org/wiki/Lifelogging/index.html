Lifelogging

Lifelogging is the process of tracking personal data generated by our own behavioural activities through digital technologies in order to gain knowledge from personal data in various areas such as health care services, studying patterns and habits of human behaviour, and learning new things from data generated habitually. In computer science, lifelogging is the process of recording and archiving all of the information of one's life, including emails, documents, web pages visited, photographs, and physiological data, often captured passively by wearable devices.

History

The idea of a wearable memory aid goes back to Vannevar Bush's hypothetical Memex (1945). In the 1980s and 1990s, Steve Mann pioneered wearable computing and continuous first-person recording, coining related concepts such as sousveillance. From 2002, Gordon Bell's MyLifeBits project at Microsoft Research attempted to record a lifetime of documents, photos, audio and video, inspired by Bush. The SenseCam, a wearable camera developed by Microsoft, was used in memory research, including studies of patients with amnesia and Alzheimer's disease.

Applications

Lifelogs have been used as a memory aid for people with memory impairment, for diet and health tracking, for the "quantified self" movement's self-knowledge through numbers, and for productivity and time-management analysis. Modern smartphones and fitness trackers passively log location, activity, sleep and heart rate, making a form of lifelogging mainstream.

Privacy concerns

Continuous recording raises concerns about the privacy of bystanders who are recorded without consent, the security of intimate personal archives, and legal questions about whether lifelogs can be subpoenaed. Devices such as Google Glass drew public controversy, and some establishments banned wearable cameras.
