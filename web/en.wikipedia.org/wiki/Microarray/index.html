Microarray

A microarray is a multiplex laboratory tool used to detect the expression of thousands of genes at the same time, or to genotype multiple regions of a genome. It consists of a solid surface — usually a glass slide or silicon chip — bearing an ordered array of microscopic spots, each containing a known DNA sequence (a probe). When a labelled sample is applied, molecules in the sample bind (hybridize) to complementary probes, and the resulting signal at each spot is measured by a scanner.

Principle

The core principle is hybridization between two complementary strands of nucleic acid. In a typical two-colour expression experiment, mRNA from two samples is converted to cDNA and labelled with different fluorescent dyes (commonly Cy3 and Cy5), mixed, and hybridized to the array. The relative fluorescence at each spot indicates the relative abundance of each transcript. Single-colour platforms, such as those of Affymetrix, label one sample per array with a single dye.

Types

  • DNA microarrays — for gene expression profiling and genotyping (SNP arrays).
  • Protein microarrays — capture molecules such as antibodies are printed to measure proteins or detect antibodies in serum.
  • Tissue microarrays — many small tissue cores assembled on one slide for histological analysis.
  • Cellular and peptide microarrays — for drug screening and epitope mapping.

Applications

Microarrays transformed functional genomics in the late 1990s and 2000s, enabling genome-wide studies of gene expression in development, disease, and drug response. They are used in cancer classification, pharmacogenomics, detection of chromosomal abnormalities (array comparative genomic hybridization), and SNP genotyping for genome-wide association studies. Since the 2010s, RNA sequencing has largely superseded expression microarrays in research, but arrays remain common in clinical and genotyping applications because of their low cost and standardized analysis.
