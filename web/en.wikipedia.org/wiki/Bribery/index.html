Bribery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bribery is the offering, giving, receiving or soliciting of any item of value to influence the actions of an official or other person in charge of a public or legal duty. It is among the oldest and most universally criminalised forms of corruption, and the centre of modern anti-corruption law domestically and across borders.

Law and practice

Modern regimes reach further than the cash-filled envelope: the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1977) criminalises US-linked bribery of foreign officials and imposed accounting duties on issuers; the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention (1997) multilateralised the supply side; the UK Bribery Act 2010 goes further still — strict corporate liability for failure to prevent bribery, no facilitation-payments exception. Enforcement asymmetries (a few countries prosecuting most cases), the grease-versus-sand debate in development economics (petty "speed money" where bureaucracies fail versus the systemic distortions grand bribery buys), facilitation payments' grey zone, and the migration of bribery into legal-adjacent forms (lavish hospitality, post-office employment, campaign support) define the field's live questions. Measurement relies on perception indices (Transparency International's CPI) and experience surveys, which tell different stories about different strata of the problem.
