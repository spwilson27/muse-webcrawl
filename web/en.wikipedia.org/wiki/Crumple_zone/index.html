Crumple zone

Crumple zones, crush zones, or crash zones are specially designed areas of a vehicle that deform and crumple in a collision. This absorbs some of the kinetic energy of the impact, preventing it from being transmitted directly to the occupants, and lengthens the time over which the vehicle's speed changes — reducing the peak deceleration (and thus the forces) the occupants experience. Crumple zones are most often located at the front and rear of a car, surrounding a rigid passenger safety cell designed to resist intrusion.

In a frontal crash test the front structure crumples to absorb energy while the passenger cell remains largely intact.

History

The concept was developed by the Austro-Hungarian engineer Béla Barényi, who took out patents in the 1930s–1952 period while working for Daimler-Benz, and was first put into series production by Mercedes-Benz in the 1959 "Fintail" (W111) models, which combined a rigid passenger cell with front and rear crumple zones. Barényi's insight overturned the then-common belief that a safe car should be as rigid as possible. Crash testing and safety programmes such as Euro NCAP later made effective energy management a central criterion of vehicle safety ratings.

Function

In a collision, the kinetic energy of the vehicle must be dissipated. If the structure is completely rigid, the occupants are stopped in a very short distance and time, producing injurious decelerations. By engineering the front and rear structures to crush progressively at a controlled force, the vehicle decelerates over a distance of perhaps half a metre or more, spreading the energy absorption over time, reducing peak forces on the occupants, and helping keep the survival space around them intact. Engine components, rails and subframes are arranged to guide the deformation and to avoid penetrating the cabin.

Design

Modern body structures use tailored materials — high-strength and ultra-high-strength steels, aluminium, and tailored blanks with varying thickness — and features such as concertina-like folds, notches and collapsible steering columns to control the crush pattern. Compatibility between vehicles of different sizes and ride heights, protection of pedestrians, and performance in offset and small-overlap impacts (in which only part of the front structure is engaged) are active design concerns. Electric vehicles, with no large engine block, gain extra design freedom for the front crumple zone but must additionally protect the battery pack.
