Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are a drug class that provide analgesic (pain-relieving), antipyretic (fever-reducing), and, at higher doses, anti-inflammatory effects. The term nonsteroidal distinguishes them from corticosteroids, which also suppress inflammation but have a broader range of effects and side effects. The most prominent NSAIDs are aspirin, ibuprofen, diclofenac, and naproxen, available worldwide in pharmacies and supermarkets.

Mechanism of action

NSAIDs work by inhibiting the cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes COX-1 and COX-2, which catalyse the formation of prostaglandins and thromboxanes from arachidonic acid. Prostaglandins mediate pain, fever, and inflammation, but also protect the stomach lining and support kidney function and platelet aggregation; blocking their production therefore explains both the therapeutic effects and the characteristic side effects of NSAIDs. Selective COX-2 inhibitors (coxibs, such as celecoxib) were developed to reduce gastrointestinal harm. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is often grouped with NSAIDs in practice but has only weak anti-inflammatory action and an incompletely understood, largely central mechanism.

Uses

NSAIDs are used for musculoskeletal pain, arthritis, dysmenorrhoea, headaches, dental pain, post-operative pain, and fever. Low-dose aspirin is also used long-term for its anti-platelet effect in preventing heart attacks and strokes.

Adverse effects

Well-known risks include gastric irritation and peptic ulcers with bleeding, kidney impairment, fluid retention and raised blood pressure, and increased cardiovascular risk with some agents at high doses. The COX-2 inhibitor rofecoxib (Vioxx) was withdrawn in 2004 after being linked to heart attacks. NSAIDs are among the most widely used medicines in the world, and their safety profile — especially in older patients and with long-term use — is a major topic in pharmacology.
