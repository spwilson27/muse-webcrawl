The Hart–Devlin debate is a famous exchange in legal philosophy between the judge Patrick Devlin (later Lord Devlin) and the philosopher H. L. A. Hart concerning the legal enforcement of morality, particularly whether the criminal law may be used to punish private consensual conduct regarded as immoral.

Background

The debate was sparked by the Wolfenden Report of 1957, which recommended decriminalising private homosexual acts between consenting adults in Britain. In his 1959 Maccabaean Lecture, "The Enforcement of Morals", Devlin argued that society may use the criminal law to protect its shared moral fabric, just as it protects its government, and that the suppression of vice is as much the law's business as the suppression of subversive activity.

Hart's response

Hart answered in his 1963 book Law, Liberty, and Morality, developing John Stuart Mill's harm principle. He distinguished between the fact that a society shares a morality and the claim that it may enforce that morality by law, and argued that using law to punish harmless private immorality is itself a form of harm and an unjustifiable restriction of liberty. Hart's position broadly prevailed in later jurisprudence and influenced reforms decriminalising homosexuality in England and Wales in 1967.
