Lib–Lab pact

The Lib–Lab pact was a parliamentary agreement in the United Kingdom between the Labour Party and the Liberal Party that sustained James Callaghan's minority Labour government from March 1977 to September 1978. In return for Liberal support on confidence and supply votes, Labour accepted a consultative mechanism giving Liberal leader David Steel's party a voice on policy, and moved (unsuccessfully) toward proportional representation for elections to the European Parliament.

Background

Labour had won a majority of just three seats in October 1974, and by-election defeats and defections erased it entirely by early 1977. In March 1977 the Conservatives under Margaret Thatcher tabled a motion of no confidence. Rather than face almost certain defeat, Callaghan struck a deal with Steel: the Liberals would vote with the government, and in exchange a joint consultative committee would review legislation and economic policy before presentation to Parliament.

Operation and achievements

The pact stabilized British politics during a fragile economic recovery following the 1976 IMF loan. It delivered devolution bills for Scotland and Wales (which proceeded to referendums), direct elections to the European Parliament — though under first-past-the-post after MPs rejected the regional list system the Liberals wanted — and a measure of industrial calm. Steel argued the arrangement showed minority government could work; critics on both sides called it influence without responsibility.

End and legacy

Steel ended the pact in the summer of 1978, judging that its benefits were exhausted. Callaghan's decision not to call an autumn election, followed by the Winter of Discontent, led to Labour's defeat on a confidence vote in March 1979 and the Conservatives' victory that May. The phrase "Lib–Lab pact" has since been applied to earlier informal cooperation between the parties (notably 1903's Gladstone–MacDonald pact) and is regularly revived whenever hung parliaments are discussed.
