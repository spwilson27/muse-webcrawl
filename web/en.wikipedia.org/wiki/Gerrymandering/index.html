Gerrymandering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gerrymandering is the practice of manipulating the boundaries of electoral districts to favour one party or class. The term was coined in 1812 in Massachusetts, combining Governor Elbridge Gerry's name with "salamander", the shape of a district drawn under his governorship. Although his name is attached to it, Gerry himself reportedly disliked the map.

Techniques

The two classic techniques are cracking — dispersing an opposing group's voters across many districts so they form a minority everywhere — and packing — concentrating them into a few districts they win by wasteful margins. Computerised redistricting after each census (in the US, conducted mostly by state legislatures) has made modern gerrymanders precise, entrenching incumbents and reducing competitive seats; measures such as the "efficiency gap" attempt to quantify partisan bias.

Responses

Most democracies assign boundary-drawing to independent commissions (as in the UK, Canada and Australia), making partisan gerrymandering rare; in the United States it remains litigated under the Voting Rights Act (racial gerrymandering) while the Supreme Court held in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) that partisan gerrymandering claims are non-justiciable political questions in federal court, leaving reform to Congress, state courts and ballot initiatives establishing independent commissions.
