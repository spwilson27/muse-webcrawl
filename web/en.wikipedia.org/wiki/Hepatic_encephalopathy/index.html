Hepatic encephalopathy

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a spectrum of neuropsychiatric abnormalities that occur in people with liver dysfunction, after exclusion of other known brain diseases. It is characterised by personality changes, intellectual impairment, and a depressed level of consciousness. The condition may develop as a complication of acute liver failure or, more commonly, of chronic liver disease such as cirrhosis, in which blood from the gut bypasses the liver through portosystemic shunts and nitrogenous toxins reach the brain unfiltered.

Ammonia produced in the gut is normally detoxified by the liver; in liver failure it reaches the brain.
SpecialtyGastroenterology, hepatology, neurology
SymptomsConfusion, forgetfulness, altered sleep, asterixis, slurred speech, coma in severe cases
CausesLiver failure, portosystemic shunting; precipitated by infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, electrolyte disturbance, constipation, sedatives
TreatmentLactulose, rifaximin, treating precipitants; liver transplantation in suitable cases

Classification and signs

HE is commonly graded with the West Haven criteria, from grade 0 (no abnormality detected) through minimal/covert changes in psychometric testing, to grade 1 (trivial lack of awareness, shortened attention), grade 2 (lethargy, disorientation for time, asterixis — a flapping tremor of the outstretched hands), grade 3 (somnolence, gross disorientation) and grade 4 (coma). Fetor hepaticus, a sweet musty breath odour, may be present.

Pathophysiology

The central toxin implicated is ammonia, generated by gut bacteria and the metabolism of dietary protein. In liver failure, ammonia is not converted to urea and enters the systemic circulation; in the brain it is combined with glutamate in astrocytes to form glutamine, whose osmotic effect causes astrocyte swelling and altered neurotransmission. Inflammation, manganese deposition in the basal ganglia, false neurotransmitters and short-chain fatty acids also contribute.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by tests of attention and psychomotor speed and by exclusion of other causes of confusion such as hypoglycaemia, infection, alcohol withdrawal and intracranial bleeding; blood ammonia levels correlate poorly with severity. Management aims to identify and treat precipitating factors and to reduce ammonia production and absorption: lactulose, a non-absorbable disaccharide that acidifies the colon and acts as a laxative, is first-line therapy, with the non-absorbable antibiotic rifaximin added for recurrent episodes. Protein restriction is no longer routinely advised. Definitive treatment of the underlying liver disease, including transplantation, may reverse the condition.
