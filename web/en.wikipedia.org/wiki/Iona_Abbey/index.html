Iona Abbey is an abbey on the island of Iona in the Inner Hebrides, Scotland. The site began as a monastery founded in 563 by the Irish monk Columba (St Columba), who with twelve companions established a base from which to Christianise Pictish Scotland. From Iona, missions spread across Scotland and northern England, and the island became one of the most important centres of learning and art in early medieval Europe; the illuminated manuscript known as the Book of Kells is traditionally associated with Iona's scriptorium.

History

Viking raids beginning in 795 repeatedly plundered the monastery, and in 849 many of Columba's relics were removed for safety, some to Kells in Ireland. A Benedictine abbey was founded on the site around 1203 by Reginald, son of Somerled, with an Augustinian nunnery nearby. After the Scottish Reformation the buildings fell into ruin, though the adjacent Reilig Odhráin burial ground remained the traditional resting place of Scottish kings, including, by tradition, Macbeth and Duncan I. The ruins were restored in the 20th century, largely through the work of the Iona Community, founded by George MacLeod in 1938, which still uses the abbey church for worship.

The site today

The abbey church, substantially rebuilt between 1902 and 1965, stands beside carved Celtic high crosses including St Martin's Cross (9th century), which remains in its original position. The abbey is in the care of Historic Environment Scotland and is one of Scotland's most visited sacred sites, reached by ferry from Mull.
