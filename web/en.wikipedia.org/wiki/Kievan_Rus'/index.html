Kievan Rus'

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: St Sophia's Cathedral in Kiev, founded in the 11th century]

Kievan Rus' was a loose federation of East Slavic and Finno-Ugric peoples in northern and eastern Europe from the late 9th to the mid-13th century, ruled by the Rurik dynasty from Kiev. Born of Varangian (Viking) traders and warriors who organised the river routes 'from the Varangians to the Greeks', it became the largest state in medieval Europe, adopted Orthodox Christianity in 988 under Vladimir the Great, and produced the earliest East Slavic law, chronicles and literature. Its principalities — Kiev, Novgorod, Vladimir-Suzdal, Galicia-Volhynia and others — are the common ancestors claimed by Russia, Ukraine and Belarus.

Rise and Christianisation

Tradition (the Primary Chronicle) dates the invitation of the Varangian Rurik to 862; his kinsman Oleg seized Kiev in 882. Kievan princes traded furs, wax and slaves down the Dnieper to Constantinople, fighting and treating with Byzantium by turns. Vladimir's baptism in 988 and the marriage alliance with the Byzantine emperor brought Orthodox Christianity, Slavonic literacy and Byzantine art; under Yaroslav the Wise (r. 1019–1054) Kiev built St Sophia's Cathedral and issued the Russkaya Pravda law code.

Fragmentation and the Mongol invasion

Princely succession by lateral rotation bred civil wars; power shifted north-east to Vladimir-Suzdal (Andrei Bogolyubsky sacked Kiev itself in 1169) while Novgorod flourished as a merchant republic. In 1237–1240 the Mongol armies of Batu Khan destroyed Ryazan, Vladimir and finally Kiev, placing the Rus' lands under the 'Mongol yoke' of the Golden Horde for two centuries — a catastrophe whose demographic and political effects remain debated, and central to later Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian national histories.
