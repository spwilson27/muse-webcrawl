Knot theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A trefoil knot drawn as an interlaced blue cord]

Knot theory is the study of mathematical knots: closed loops embedded in three-dimensional space, classified up to continuous deformation (without cutting). Its simplest nontrivial example is the trefoil knot; a loop that can be untangled completely is the unknot.

The subject began in the 1860s with Lord Kelvin's speculation that atoms were knotted vortices in the ether — wrong physics, but it prompted Peter Guthrie Tait to catalogue knots, founding the field. Modern knot theory instead uses invariants: quantities such as the Alexander polynomial (1928) and the Jones polynomial (1984) computed from a knot diagram, equal for equivalent knots.

Connections

Knot theory now feeds back into physics after all (quantum field theory and statistical mechanics), into chemistry (knotted molecules), and into biology, where enzymes cut and retie knotted DNA.

Deciding whether a given knot is the unknot is algorithmically possible but was long of unknown complexity; it remains a benchmark problem in computational topology.
