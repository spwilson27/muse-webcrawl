Atta cephalotes

Atta cephalotes is a species of leafcutter ant in the tribe Attini, first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1758. It is one of the most widespread and intensively studied of the roughly 45 species of Atta, ranging through the tropical forests of Central and South America from Nicaragua to Paraguay. Its colonies, which may number several million individuals, are among the largest and most complex insect societies known.

Behaviour and ecology

Workers cut fragments of fresh leaves, flowers and grasses — not to eat directly, but to feed a symbiotic fungus, chiefly Leucoagaricus gongylophorus, which the ants cultivate in underground gardens and on which they feed. This fungus-growing mutualism, tens of millions of years old, makes A. cephalotes one of the dominant herbivores of Neotropical forests; colonies are estimated to harvest a significant fraction of local leaf production. A bacterial symbiont on the ants' cuticle produces antibiotics that suppress parasitic fungi of the gardens.

Castes

The species is famously polymorphic. Tiny minima workers tend the fungus gardens and ride on leaf fragments as guards; minors and mediae forage and cut vegetation; and huge majors (soldiers), with massive heads and mandibles able to cut through leather and slice flesh, defend the colony and clear foraging trails. A mature colony occupies a nest of hundreds of chambers extending several metres underground, with dedicated chambers for refuse, in which waste is processed by a specialised waste-management workforce.

Colony founding

After mating flights at the start of the rainy season, a fertilised queen lands, sheds her wings, and excavates a burrow, carrying in her mouth a pellet of fungus from her natal nest with which she seeds her first garden, initially fertilising it with her own faeces. Most founding colonies fail; survivors can grow for over a decade and live 15 years or more.

Interaction with humans

Because it defoliates crops, citrus groves, cacao and forestry plantations, A. cephalotes is a major agricultural pest in much of its range, and considerable research has gone into its control. Its conspicuous foraging columns and giant nests also make it a staple of natural-history documentaries on tropical forests.
