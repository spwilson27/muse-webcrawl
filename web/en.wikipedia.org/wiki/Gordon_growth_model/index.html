Gordon growth model

The Gordon growth model (also known as the dividend discount model, in its constant-growth form) is a method of valuing a company's stock that assumes the company will pay dividends that grow at a constant rate in perpetuity. It is named after Myron J. Gordon of the University of Toronto, who published it in 1959.

The model values a stock as the present value of all future dividends, assuming a constant growth rate g and a required rate of return k:

P = D0(1 + g) / (k − g)

where P is the current stock price, D0 is the most recent dividend paid, g is the expected constant dividend growth rate, and k is the required rate of return. The model only works when k is greater than g.

The model is widely used for valuing mature companies with stable, predictable dividend growth. Its main limitations are its sensitivity to the assumed growth rate and discount rate, and the assumption that dividends grow at a constant rate forever; it is therefore unreliable for high-growth firms or those that do not pay dividends. See also dividend discount model and capital asset pricing model.
