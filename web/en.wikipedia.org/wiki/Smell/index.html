Smell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Smell is the chemical sense — olfaction — by which organisms detect airborne molecules (odorants) with receptors in the nose. Together with taste it produces the perception of flavour, and it is closely tied to memory and emotion through direct connections to the limbic system.

Mechanism

Odorants dissolve in the mucus of the olfactory epithelium and bind to olfactory receptor neurons, each expressing one of roughly 400 functional receptor types in humans. Combinations of activated receptors encode thousands of distinguishable odours. Signals pass through the olfactory bulb to the olfactory cortex and limbic structures, notably without an obligatory relay through the thalamus — unusual among the senses. Dogs and many other mammals have vastly larger receptor repertoires and olfactory brain regions, giving them far keener smell.

Importance and disorders

Smell warns of smoke, gas and spoiled food, guides taste and appetite, and in animals mediates pheromone communication. Loss of smell (anosmia) accompanies ageing, viral infections — it became a widely recognised symptom of COVID-19 — and early Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases.
