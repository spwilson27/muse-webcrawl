Hedge maze

A hedge maze is a type of puzzle garden maze, a formal garden design in which the walls of the maze are formed by clipped hedges. Hedge mazes evolved from the knot gardens and labyrinths of Renaissance Europe and became a popular feature of the great formal gardens of the 17th and 18th centuries. Unlike a unicursal labyrinth, a hedge maze presents the visitor with choices of path and direction, with dead ends, loops and a goal—usually the centre—hidden from view by the height of the hedges.

History

The earliest hedge mazes date from the late 16th and early 17th centuries. One of the most famous, the maze at Hampton Court Palace in England, was planted around 1690 for William III using hornbeam, later replanted in yew. The maze at Versailles, the Labyrinthe de Versailles created by André Le Nôtre in 1665, contained 39 fountains illustrating fables of Aesop, though it was removed in 1778. Longleat House in Wiltshire has one of the largest modern hedge mazes, designed by Greg Bright and opened in 1978, with nearly 2.7 km (1.7 miles) of paths.

Design

Hedge mazes are traditionally made from yew, box, privet, holly or hornbeam—plants that tolerate clipping and form dense walls. The designer lays out a network of paths, often on a geometric or symmetric plan, and may include raised platforms, grottoes or towers at the centre from which the pattern can be appreciated. Because hedges take years to reach full height, new mazes are sometimes initially planted with quick-growing species or supplemented with fences.

Notable hedge mazes

  • Hampton Court maze, London, England (c. 1690)
  • Longleat maze, Wiltshire, England (1978)
  • Villa Pisani labyrinth, Stra, Italy
  • Schönbrunn Palace maze, Vienna, Austria
  • Leechi's hedge mazes and modern corn mazes are related seasonal attractions
