Phi value analysis
Phi values probe the folding transition state
FieldProtein folding, biophysics

MeasuresDegree of native-like structure at a residue in the transition state

Phi value analysis (Φ-value analysis) is an experimental technique in protein engineering used to map the structure of the transition state of protein folding. Developed by Alan Fersht and colleagues in the late 1980s and early 1990s, originally in studies of barnase and chymotrypsin inhibitor 2 (CI2), it remains the principal method for obtaining residue-level structural information about folding transition states, which are too short-lived to observe directly.

Principle

A single amino acid residue is mutated, usually to alanine or glycine, and the effect of the mutation on the folding rate and on the overall stability of the protein is measured. The phi value is defined as the ratio of the change in the activation free energy of folding to the change in the free energy of folding:

Φ = ΔΔG‡ / ΔΔG

A Φ value of 1 indicates that the mutated residue's interactions are fully formed in the transition state, as in the native state; a Φ value of 0 indicates they are as unformed as in the unfolded state. Fractional values suggest partial formation of structure, parallel folding pathways, or a diffuse transition state. Non-classical (negative or greater-than-one) values can reveal non-native interactions or movement of the transition state along the reaction coordinate.

Applications and limitations

Sets of Φ values measured across many sites in a protein are used to infer which parts of the structure fold early, to identify folding nuclei, and to restrain molecular dynamics simulations of folding pathways. The interpretation assumes that mutations only delete interactions without creating new ones or perturbing the unfolded state, and that the folding pathway is unchanged — assumptions that require careful controls, typically using conservative mutations with small energetic effects.
