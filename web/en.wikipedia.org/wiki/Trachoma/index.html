Trachoma

Trachoma is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis. The infection causes a roughening of the inner surface of the eyelids. This roughening can lead to pain in the eyes, breakdown of the outer surface or cornea of the eyes, and eventual blindness. Untreated, repeated trachoma infections over years build up scarring inside the eyelid, causing the eyelashes to turn inward (trichiasis) and scratch the cornea with every blink.

Signs and symptoms

Active trachoma, most common in young children, often causes mild itching and irritation of the eyes and eyelids and discharge; many cases are asymptomatic. After years of repeated infection, scarring of the conjunctiva (Arlt's line) develops, the eyelid margin turns inward (entropion), and the lashes abrade the cornea, producing pain, corneal opacity, and irreversible blindness if uncorrected.

Transmission

The bacterium spreads by direct contact with eye and nose discharge of infected people, especially young children, and indirectly via fomites and eye-seeking flies such as Musca sorbens. Trachoma thrives where there is poverty, crowding, and inadequate access to clean water and sanitation. It is endemic in parts of Africa, the Middle East, Asia, and Central and South America, and among some Indigenous communities in Australia.

Prevention and treatment

The World Health Organization endorses the SAFE strategy: Surgery for trichiasis, Antibiotics (mass distribution of azithromycin, donated by Pfizer through the International Trachoma Initiative), Facial cleanliness, and Environmental improvements such as water supply and latrines. A single oral dose of azithromycin is effective for active infection.

Epidemiology

Trachoma is the leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide. Mass treatment campaigns have greatly reduced its burden since the WHO Alliance for the Global Elimination of Trachoma was formed in 1996, and a growing number of countries have been validated as having eliminated trachoma as a public health problem, though tens of millions of people remain at risk in endemic districts.
