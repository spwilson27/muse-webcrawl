Microstate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A microstate is a sovereign state with a very small population, land area, or both. There is no single threshold: microstates range from the Vatican City — 0.49 km² and about 800 residents, the world's smallest state — to island nations like Barbados or the Maldives that are small but regionally weighty. What unites them is the strategic problem of smallness: how to be sovereign, secure and solvent with a tax base of a town, and their ingenious answers have made microstates a distinctive genre of statecraft.

The European and island microstates

Europe's microstates are medieval survivals preserved by geography and diplomacy: San Marino (301 AD, the world's oldest republic), Liechtenstein, Monaco (2 km² of Riviera wealth), Andorra (a Pyrenean co-principality) and the Vatican, plus island Malta. In the Pacific, Nauru (21 km², the smallest island nation), Tuvalu, Palau, the Marshall Islands and the Federated States of Micronesia are microstates whose vast ocean zones (EEZs) dwarf their land — Tuvalu's .tv domain and its "digital nation" response to sea-level rise show sovereignty being monetised and reimagined. Caribbean and Indian Ocean states (Seychelles, São Tomé) complete the map. Microstates survive by selling what sovereignty uniquely offers: flags of convenience and shipping registries, banking and tax regimes, citizenship-by-investment, stamps and domains, diplomatic votes (all UN members vote equally — a microstate's General Assembly vote counts the same as China's), and niche tourism. Their vulnerabilities — climate change for atoll nations, blacklists for financial centres, dependence on a single patron or industry — are the price of the model, and international bodies periodically debate whether the smallest should sit at all in clubs of states; the microstates reply that they are sovereignty in its purest form: chosen, contractual, and surprisingly durable.
