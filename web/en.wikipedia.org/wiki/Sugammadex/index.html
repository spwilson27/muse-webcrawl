Sugammadex is a modified γ-cyclodextrin

Sugammadex, sold under the brand name Bridion, is a medication used to reverse the effects of the neuromuscular blocking agents rocuronium and vecuronium during surgery. It was the first selective relaxant binding agent (SRBA) developed for clinical use, and works by a mechanism fundamentally different from older reversal drugs such as neostigmine: rather than inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, sugammadex directly encapsulates the blocking agent in the plasma, removing it from its site of action.

Mechanism of action

Sugammadex is a modified gamma-cyclodextrin, a ring-shaped molecule with a lipophilic core and a hydrophilic outer surface. Its cavity is sized and shaped to fit the aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers rocuronium and vecuronium almost exactly. When injected intravenously, sugammadex binds free rocuronium in the plasma in a tight 1:1 complex. This lowers the free plasma concentration of the blocker, creating a concentration gradient that draws rocuronium away from nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction, rapidly restoring muscle function. The sugammadex–rocuronium complex is then excreted in the urine. Its affinity for rocuronium is greater than for vecuronium, and it has essentially no useful effect against benzylisoquinolinium agents such as atracurium or against succinylcholine.

Clinical use

Sugammadex is given as a single intravenous bolus, with the dose depending on the depth of neuromuscular blockade: lower doses are used for routine reversal when some spontaneous recovery has occurred, and a high dose (16 mg/kg) can immediately reverse even profound blockade induced by a large intubating dose of rocuronium. Recovery is typically faster and more complete than with neostigmine, and sugammadex can reverse deep blockade that neostigmine cannot. Because it does not act through the cholinergic system, it avoids the muscarinic side effects of anticholinesterases (such as bradycardia and increased secretions) and does not need to be co-administered with an anticholinergic like glycopyrrolate.

Adverse effects and cautions

Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, pain at the injection site, and a transient metallic or altered taste. Hypersensitivity reactions, including rare anaphylaxis, have been reported, including in healthy volunteers exposed for the first time. Sugammadex can transiently interfere with some laboratory tests of coagulation and can bind progestogen, potentially reducing the effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives for several days after administration. Because the complex is cleared by the kidneys, use in severe renal impairment requires caution.

History

Sugammadex was developed by researchers at Organon in Scotland, building on cyclodextrin chemistry; its name derives from "sugar" and "gamma-dextrin". It was approved in the European Union in 2008 and, after an initial refusal over hypersensitivity concerns, by the US Food and Drug Administration in December 2015. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. Although considerably more expensive per dose than neostigmine, it has become standard in many operating theatres, particularly for situations where rapid or reliable reversal is critical.
