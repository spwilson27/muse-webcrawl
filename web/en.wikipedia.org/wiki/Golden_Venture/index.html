Golden Venture

The Golden Venture was a 147-foot cargo ship that ran aground off the coast of New York City on 6 June 1993, carrying 286 undocumented Chinese passengers, most of them from Fujian province, who had paid smugglers — known as "snakeheads", linked to the Fuk Ching gang — large sums (around US$30,000 each) for passage to the United States.

The voyage and grounding

The passengers endured a voyage of more than three months from Thailand (where they boarded after an aborted embarking in Kenya) across the Indian Ocean, around the Cape of Good Hope and across the Atlantic, confined in a single cargo hold with little food or water; two passengers died during the voyage. Intending to transfer the passengers to small boats near shore, the smugglers instead ran the ship aground off Fort Tilden in the Rockaways, Queens, in the early hours of 6 June 1993. Passengers jumped into the cold water to swim ashore; ten drowned or died of hypothermia. The survivors were rescued and detained by the Immigration and Naturalization Service.

Aftermath

The incident became a watershed in American immigration politics. The Clinton administration detained the passengers for years in county jails while their asylum claims were processed — most were ultimately deported, though some, including minors, were released; a final group was freed in 1997 after a campaign supported by members of Congress. The case influenced the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, including its expedited removal provisions, and was widely cited in debates over the detention of asylum seekers. Many of the detained passengers made intricate paper sculptures while in jail in York, Pennsylvania; these works were later exhibited as the "Golden Venture" paper sculptures.
