Linus Torvalds

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Linus Torvalds, portrait photograph at a Linux conference]

Linus Benedict Torvalds (born 1969) is a Finnish-American software engineer who created the Linux kernel (1991) and the Git version-control system (2005) — two of the most consequential open-source projects ever made.

Born in Helsinki into a family of journalists and academics, Torvalds encountered computing through his grandfather's Commodore VIC-20 and the University of Helsinki's systems. He began Linux as a student project to explore his new PC's Intel 386 processor.

Career

Torvalds emigrated to the United States in 1997, working at Transmeta in California, then at the Open Source Development Labs / Linux Foundation in Oregon, where he remains the kernel's chief maintainer and final decision-maker. His management style — famously blunt technical criticism on public mailing lists — drew both results and criticism; he apologised and took a break in 2018 to work on his behaviour, returning with a code of conduct adopted.

Git, written in a few weeks after the kernel community lost access to the proprietary BitKeeper tool, now underpins most of the world's software development. Torvalds's memoir Just for Fun (2001, with David Diamond) describes his philosophy of open development.

Recognition

Honours include the Millennium Technology Prize (2012), the Computer History Museum Fellow award (2008), and IEEE Computer Pioneer Award (2014). Astoundingly for a software creator, Linux runs most of the world's servers and — via Android — most of its phones.
