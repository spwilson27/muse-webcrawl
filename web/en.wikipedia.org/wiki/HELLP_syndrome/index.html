HELLP syndrome is a life-threatening complication of pregnancy, considered by most authorities to be a severe variant of pre-eclampsia. The name is an acronym for its three defining laboratory abnormalities: Hemolysis, ELevated Liver enzymes and Low Platelet count. It affects roughly 0.5% to 0.9% of all pregnancies and 10% to 20% of pregnancies complicated by severe pre-eclampsia. The only definitive treatment is delivery of the baby.

Signs and symptoms

HELLP most often arises in the third trimester, though it can occur earlier and in up to a third of cases develops after delivery. The most characteristic symptom is epigastric or right upper quadrant abdominal pain, caused by stretching of the liver capsule, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting, malaise, headache and visual disturbances. Swelling and rapid weight gain are common. Blood pressure is usually elevated, but importantly it may be normal or only mildly raised in a significant minority of patients, and proteinuria may be absent, so the syndrome can be missed if clinicians rely on hypertension alone.

Pathophysiology and diagnosis

The underlying process is widespread endothelial dysfunction and abnormal placentation, as in pre-eclampsia. Injured endothelium and fibrin deposition in small blood vessels shear passing red cells, producing a microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia with fragmented red cells (schistocytes) on the blood film, raised lactate dehydrogenase and low haptoglobin. Fibrin deposition and haemorrhage in the liver raise the transaminases (AST and ALT), and platelet activation and consumption in damaged vessels cause thrombocytopenia, typically a platelet count below 100 × 109/L.

Diagnosis is laboratory-based; commonly used criteria (the Tennessee and Mississippi classifications) set thresholds for haemolysis, transaminase elevation (often more than twice the upper limit of normal) and platelet count. Because the same triad can occur in thrombotic microangiopathies such as TTP and HUS and in acute fatty liver of pregnancy, the differential diagnosis is important.

Management

The mother is stabilised — controlling severe hypertension, giving magnesium sulfate to prevent seizures, correcting coagulopathy with blood products as needed — and the definitive treatment is delivery, usually promptly once the diagnosis is made at or after 34 weeks, or earlier if the condition of mother or fetus deteriorates. Corticosteroids may be given to accelerate fetal lung maturity and have been used in an attempt to improve maternal platelet counts. Mode of delivery depends on obstetric factors; caesarean section is frequent but not mandatory.

Complications and prognosis

Complications include disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), placental abruption, acute kidney injury, pulmonary oedema, subcapsular liver haematoma and its catastrophic rupture, and progression to eclampsia. Maternal mortality is reported around 1–3% in modern series, with higher rates where diagnosis is delayed, and perinatal mortality is substantially increased, mostly through prematurity and abruption. Symptoms and laboratory values typically worsen for 24–48 hours after delivery before recovering. Women who have had HELLP syndrome face an increased risk of recurrence and of pre-eclampsia in later pregnancies, and of cardiovascular disease in later life.

History

The syndrome was first clearly described and named by the American obstetrician Louis Weinstein in 1982, although cases recognisable as HELLP had been reported in the literature decades earlier.
