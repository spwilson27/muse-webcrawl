Narrative nonfiction

Narrative nonfiction, also called creative nonfiction or literary nonfiction, is a genre of writing that uses literary styles and techniques to create factually accurate narratives. A narrative nonfiction writer presents information in an engaging way, incorporating elements of storytelling — scene, character, dialogue, and structure — while remaining committed to the truth of events, in contrast to standard reportorial or journalistic writing.

Characteristics

Works of narrative nonfiction read like novels or short stories but are based on verifiable fact. The writer typically immerses in the subject, researches extensively, and shapes the material into a narrative arc. Because the genre blends factual reporting with literary technique, it raises ongoing ethical questions about reconstruction of dialogue, compression of events, and composite characters.

History

Although literary nonfiction has earlier antecedents, the genre gained prominence in the 1960s with the New Journalism of writers such as Tom Wolfe, Joan Didion, Hunter S. Thompson, and Gay Talese, and with Truman Capote's In Cold Blood (1966), which Capote called a "nonfiction novel". Later prominent practitioners include John McPhee, Tracy Kidder, Susan Orlean, and Erik Larson.

Notable examples

  • In Cold Blood (1966) by Truman Capote
  • The Right Stuff (1979) by Tom Wolfe
  • The Soul of a New Machine (1981) by Tracy Kidder
  • The Devil in the White City (2003) by Erik Larson
  • The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks (2010) by Rebecca Skloot
