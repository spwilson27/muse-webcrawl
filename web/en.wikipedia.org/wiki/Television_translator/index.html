Television translator

A television translator, also called a relay transmitter, rebroadcaster, or gap filler, is a low-power broadcast relay station that receives the signal of a television station and rebroadcasts it, usually on a different channel, to extend coverage into areas the parent station cannot reach because of terrain or distance. Translators are especially common in mountainous regions and large rural areas, and they played a major role in bringing television to remote communities in North America, Australia, and elsewhere.

Operation

Unlike an originating station, a translator produces no programming of its own: it simply receives the parent signal off-air, by satellite, or by microwave link, converts it to its assigned channel, amplifies it, and retransmits it. In the United States, translators are licensed by the Federal Communications Commission as a secondary service. They originated in the late 1940s and 1950s, when community groups and local governments in the mountainous West installed unlicensed "boosters" to bring in distant signals; the FCC legalised and regulated the service in the 1950s and 1960s. Thousands of translators were operated by counties, translator districts, and broadcasters themselves.

Digital transition

The transition to digital television complicated translator operation, since simple frequency conversion of digital signals still works but consumer receivers may be confused by identical channel branding. In the United States, translators were initially exempt from the 2009 full-power digital deadline but were required to convert by 2015, with many receiving federal funding assistance. Distributed transmission systems and single-frequency networks in DVB countries perform a similar gap-filling role using co-channel transmitters. The related American class of low-power television (LPTV) stations differs from translators in that LPTV stations may originate programming.
