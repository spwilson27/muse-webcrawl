Intangible cultural heritage

A traditional dance performance, an example of living heritage transmitted between generations.

Intangible cultural heritage (ICH) is a practice, representation, expression, knowledge, or skill considered by UNESCO to be part of a place's cultural heritage. It includes cultural products and processes such as oral traditions, performing arts, social practices, rituals, festive events, knowledge concerning nature and the universe, and traditional craftsmanship.

The concept was formalised by the UNESCO Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, adopted in 2003 and in force from 2006. Unlike tangible heritage such as monuments and sites protected under the 1972 World Heritage Convention, intangible heritage is living and continually recreated by communities.

Definition

The 2003 Convention defines intangible cultural heritage as the practices, representations, expressions, knowledge, and skills — as well as the instruments, objects, artefacts and cultural spaces associated therewith — that communities, groups and, in some cases, individuals recognize as part of their cultural heritage. It is transmitted from generation to generation and provides communities with a sense of identity and continuity.

Lists

UNESCO maintains several lists, including the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding, and a Register of Good Safeguarding Practices. Well-known inscribed elements include the Mediterranean diet, tango, Chinese calligraphy, the French gastronomic meal, flamenco, yoga, and reggae music.

Safeguarding

Safeguarding measures include documentation, research, preservation, promotion, education, and revitalisation. States parties to the Convention are required to draw up inventories of intangible heritage present in their territories, with the participation of the communities concerned.

Criticism

Scholars have debated whether listing can freeze living practices, encourage commercialisation, or privilege state-approved versions of traditions over local diversity. Defenders argue that inscription raises awareness and supports transmission to younger generations.
