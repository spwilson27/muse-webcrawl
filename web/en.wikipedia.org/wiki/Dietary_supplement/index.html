A dietary supplement is a manufactured product intended to supplement a person's diet by taking a pill, capsule, tablet, powder, or liquid. A supplement can provide nutrients either extracted from food sources or that are synthetic. Classes of nutrient compounds in supplements include vitamins, minerals, fiber, fatty acids, and amino acids. Dietary supplements can also contain substances that have not been confirmed as being essential to life, such as plant pigments or polyphenols, marketed as having a beneficial biological effect.

Definition

In the United States, the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA) defines a dietary supplement as a product taken by mouth that contains a "dietary ingredient" intended to supplement the diet, including vitamins, minerals, herbs or other botanicals, amino acids, and substances such as enzymes, organ tissues, and metabolites. Unlike drugs, supplements are not required to demonstrate efficacy or safety before marketing, though manufacturers must ensure their products are safe and claims are truthful and not misleading.

Uses and prevalence

Supplements are widely used: surveys in the United States have found that more than half of adults report using dietary supplements, with multivitamins, vitamin D, calcium, and fish oil among the most common. People take supplements to fill nutrient gaps, support specific health goals, or because of diagnosed deficiencies. Certain groups, such as pregnant women (folic acid), older adults (vitamin B12 and vitamin D), and people with restricted diets, are commonly advised to supplement specific nutrients.

Efficacy and safety

Evidence for the benefit of most supplements in healthy, well-nourished populations is limited. Large trials have generally found little benefit of multivitamins for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer, while some supplements clearly help defined deficiency states. Safety concerns include contamination, adulteration with undisclosed pharmaceutical ingredients, interactions with medications, and toxicity at high doses — for example, excess vitamin A, iron, or vitamin B6. Because premarket approval is not required in the US, quality varies widely between brands, and third-party testing programs such as USP Verified exist to certify purity and potency.

Regulation

Regulatory frameworks differ internationally. In the European Union, food supplements are regulated as foods under Directive 2002/46/EC, which sets permitted vitamins and minerals and labelling rules. Health claims require authorization under separate legislation. In the US, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates supplements under a different, less stringent set of rules than those covering conventional foods and drug products, though it can act against unsafe products after they reach the market.
