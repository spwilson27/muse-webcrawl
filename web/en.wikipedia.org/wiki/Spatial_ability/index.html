Spatial ability

Spatial ability or visuo-spatial ability is the capacity to generate, retain, retrieve and transform well-structured visual images. It is one of the primary mental abilities identified by psychometric research and is measured by tests involving the mental manipulation of two- and three-dimensional figures.

Components

Researchers commonly distinguish several spatial factors, including:

  • Spatial visualisation — manipulating complex figures through multiple steps, as in paper-folding and surface-development tests;
  • Mental rotation — deciding whether a rotated object is the same as a reference object (the classic Shepard–Metzler task);
  • Spatial perception — determining spatial orientations despite distracting information, e.g. judging horizontal in a tilted frame;
  • Spatial orientation — imagining how a scene appears from a different viewpoint;
  • Closure speed and flexibility of closure — finding simple figures embedded in complex patterns.

Significance

Spatial ability predicts achievement in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) fields, over and above verbal and mathematical ability: longitudinal studies such as Project Talent and the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth found that adolescents with high spatial scores were substantially more likely to earn STEM degrees and patents. It is also relevant to occupations from surgery and dentistry to architecture and piloting.

Differences and training

Average sex differences are found on some tests — most robustly mental rotation, where males on average score higher — with the size and interpretation of the difference debated and attributed variously to biology, experience and test conditions. Spatial skills are malleable: meta-analyses show that training, video-game play and spatial coursework improve performance, with gains transferring to untrained tasks and persisting over time.
