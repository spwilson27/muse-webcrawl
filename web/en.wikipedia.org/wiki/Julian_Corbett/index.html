Sir Julian Stafford Corbett (12 November 1854 – 21 September 1922) was a prominent British naval historian, geographer and strategist of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, whose works helped shape a standard for students of naval warfare and influenced Royal Navy thinking in the era of the First World War.

Life

Corbett was educated at Marlborough College and Trinity College, Cambridge, and was called to the bar, but abandoned law to travel and write. He became a lecturer in history at the Royal Naval College, Greenwich, and later at the Royal Naval War College, and a close associate of Admiral Sir John Fisher's circle of reformers. He was knighted in 1917.

Theory of maritime strategy

Corbett's most important work, Some Principles of Maritime Strategy (1911), adapted Clausewitzian ideas of limited war to the sea. He argued that the object of naval warfare is control of maritime communications—"command of the sea" being normally disputed and local rather than absolute—and that battle is a means, not an end. He emphasised joint operations, the defence, and limited war for limited aims, in contrast to the battle-fleet orthodoxy associated with Alfred Thayer Mahan, with whom he is often paired as the two classic theorists of sea power.

Historical works

Corbett wrote acclaimed histories including Drake and the Tudor Navy (1898), The Successors of Drake (1900), England in the Seven Years' War (1907) and The Campaign of Trafalgar (1910). He was chosen to write the official naval history of the First World War, Naval Operations, whose first volumes appeared before his death in 1922; its candid treatment caused friction with the Admiralty.
