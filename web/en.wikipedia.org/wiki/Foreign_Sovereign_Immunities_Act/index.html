Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976 (FSIA) is a United States federal law codified at 28 U.S.C. §§ 1330, 1391(f), 1441(d), and 1602–1611. It establishes the limitations as to whether a foreign sovereign nation (or its political subdivisions, agencies, or instrumentalities) may be sued in U.S. courts — federal or state.

Provisions

The FSIA adopted the "restrictive" theory of sovereign immunity, under which a foreign state is immune from suit for its public acts (jure imperii) but not for its commercial activities (jure gestionis). The statute creates a presumption of immunity and sets out specific exceptions, including waiver, commercial activity with a sufficient connection to the United States, certain expropriations, and property and tort claims. It also governs service of process, attachment of foreign-state property, and execution of judgments.

Amendments and notable cases

The Act has been amended several times, notably in 1996 to add a "state sponsor of terrorism" exception, expanded by the National Defense Authorization Act of 2008, allowing suits against designated states for acts such as torture, extrajudicial killing, and hostage taking. In 2016, Congress enacted the Justice Against Sponsors of Terrorism Act (JASTA), overriding other provisions to permit certain terrorism-related suits — most prominently litigation arising from the September 11 attacks. The Supreme Court has interpreted the FSIA in cases including Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria (1983), Republic of Argentina v. NML Capital (2014), and Bank Markazi v. Peterson (2016).
