Qvevri (Georgian: ქვევრი) are large earthenware vessels used for the fermentation, storage and ageing of traditional Georgian wine. Resembling huge, egg-shaped amphorae without handles, they are buried below ground up to their necks, which keeps the wine at a stable temperature, and sealed with a wooden or stone lid covered with clay. The qvevri winemaking method — crushing grapes and fermenting the juice together with skins, stalks and pips in the buried vessel — is among the oldest winemaking techniques in the world, with archaeological evidence of Georgian viticulture stretching back some 8,000 years.

Construction

Qvevri are hand-built by coiling from local clay and fired in kilns; capacities range from about 100 litres to several thousand. The interior is typically coated with a thin layer of beeswax (or, in some regions, limewash) to waterproof it and ease cleaning. Making large qvevri is a specialised, endangered craft practised by a small number of master potters, concentrated in villages such as Atsana, Makatubani and Shilda.

Winemaking method

In the traditional method, harvested grapes are lightly crushed and the entire must — juice, skins, stalks and pips (the chacha) — is poured into the qvevri, where wild yeasts ferment it. For white grapes this extended skin contact produces the deep amber "orange wines" now fashionable worldwide; reds ferment on their skins in the usual way. After fermentation the vessels are topped up, sealed, and the wine left to mature through the winter before racking in spring. In 2013 UNESCO added the "ancient Georgian traditional Qvevri wine-making method" to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Modern revival

Once nearly displaced by industrial Soviet-era production in stainless steel, qvevri winemaking has been revived since the 1990s and emulated internationally — in Italy (where Friuli producers pioneered skin-contact whites), Slovenia, Austria, Australia, the United States and elsewhere — as part of the natural wine movement, which values the method's minimal intervention, ambient yeasts and lack of additives.
