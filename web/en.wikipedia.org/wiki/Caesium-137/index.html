Caesium-137 (also spelled cesium-137) is a radioactive isotope of caesium formed as one of the more common fission products of uranium-235 and other fissile isotopes in nuclear reactors and nuclear weapons. It is among the most problematic of the short-to-medium-lived fission products, because it easily moves and spreads in nature, with its half-life of about 30 years, its strong gamma emission, and the chemical behaviour of caesium as a water-soluble alkali metal.

Decay and uses

Caesium-137 decays by beta emission to metastable barium-137m, which in turn decays with a half-life of about 2.6 minutes emitting a characteristic 662 keV gamma ray — the signature line used to identify the isotope in gamma spectroscopy. Sealed sources of 137Cs are used in industrial gauges, well logging, moisture-density meters, flow meters and medical radiation therapy, and in the calibration of radiation detectors.

Environmental contamination

Caesium-137 was spread worldwide by atmospheric nuclear weapons testing and was released in significant quantities by the Chernobyl disaster (1986) and the Fukushima Daiichi disaster (2011). Because caesium behaves chemically like potassium, it is taken up by plants, fungi and animals and accumulates in food chains; contaminated land may remain restricted for decades, decaying away over roughly ten half-lives (300 years). Accidental dispersal from abandoned medical sources caused the Goiânia accident in Brazil in 1987, one of the worst radiological accidents on record.
