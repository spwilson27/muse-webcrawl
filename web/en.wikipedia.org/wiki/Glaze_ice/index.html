Glaze ice, also called glaze or (in North America) an ice storm's coating, is a smooth, transparent and homogeneous layer of ice that forms when freezing rain or drizzle strikes a surface whose temperature is at or below freezing. Unlike rime ice, which is milky and rough, glaze is clear and dense, closely coating the shape of whatever it covers.

Formation

Glaze forms when supercooled raindrops fall through a shallow layer of sub-freezing air near the ground and freeze on contact with surfaces. Because the drops spread before freezing, they create a continuous sheet rather than granular pellets.

Effects

Glaze is among the most destructive forms of ice. A radial coating of only a few millimetres adds great weight to branches and power lines; severe storms, such as the North American ice storm of January 1998, have left millions without electricity for weeks. Glaze on roads — often nearly invisible as "black ice" — is a major winter driving hazard, and on aircraft wings it degrades lift, requiring de-icing before flight.
