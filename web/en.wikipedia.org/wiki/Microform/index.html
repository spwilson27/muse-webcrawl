Microform

Microfilm reels and microfiche are the most common microforms

Microforms are scaled-down reproductions of documents, typically either photographic film or paper, made for the purposes of transmission, storage, reading, and printing. The three most common formats are microfilm (reels of film), aperture cards, and microfiche (flat sheets of film). Microforms are widely used in libraries and archives to preserve newspapers, books, and records while saving storage space.

History

The earliest microphotographs were made in the 1830s and 1840s by John Benjamin Dancer. During the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, René Dagron used microfilm to carry messages by carrier pigeon out of besieged Paris. Commercial microfilming of library and banking materials began in the late 1920s and 1930s, and the format expanded greatly after the Second World War — including the V-mail correspondence of Allied servicemen. Libraries adopted microfilm extensively in the mid-20th century to save brittle newspapers from decay and to acquire large back-files cheaply.

Use and preservation

Reading microforms requires a dedicated reader, often combined with a printer or scanner. Properly processed silver-halide film stored in cool, dry conditions can last for centuries, which is why archives still regard it as a stable preservation medium even in the digital era. Conversely, acetate-based film suffers from "vinegar syndrome" degradation. Many institutions now digitise microfilm collections to provide online access while retaining the film as a preservation master.
