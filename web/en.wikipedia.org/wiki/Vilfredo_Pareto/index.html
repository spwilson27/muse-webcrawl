Vilfredo Pareto (1848–1923).

Vilfredo Federico Damaso Pareto (15 July 1848 – 19 August 1923) was an Italian engineer, economist, sociologist and philosopher, and a founder of modern microeconomics and welfare economics. He is known for the concepts of Pareto efficiency and the Pareto distribution of income — the empirical basis of the popular "80/20 rule" — and for his theory of the circulation of elites, which influenced pluralist and elite theorists alike.

Life and career

Born in Paris to an exiled Genoese family, Pareto studied engineering at the Polytechnic of Turin and worked as a civil engineer and manager for Italian railway and iron companies. Turning to economics, he succeeded Léon Walras in the chair of political economy at the University of Lausanne in 1893, becoming, with Walras, a leader of the "Lausanne School" of general equilibrium theory. His Cours d'économie politique (1896–97) presented his empirical law of income distribution; his Manuale di economia politica (1906) developed indifference-curve analysis and the criterion of optimality now called Pareto efficiency: a state in which no one can be made better off without making someone worse off.

Sociology

In his monumental Trattato di sociologia generale (1916; The Mind and Society), Pareto argued that most human action is "non-logical," driven by relatively constant sentiments he called residues, which people rationalise after the fact with variable justifications called derivations. He analysed elites as a recurring social stratum: history, he wrote, is "a graveyard of aristocracies," as governing elites decay and are displaced by new ones — the circulation of elites. His work influenced later sociologists including C. Wright Mills, who engaged critically with elite theory.

Legacy and controversy

Pareto's economics underpins modern welfare theory and cost–benefit practice, and the Pareto principle is applied across business and software. His later political writings, sceptical of democracy and humanitarian ideals, were admired in Mussolini's Italy — Pareto was made a senator shortly before his death — and the extent and significance of his sympathy for fascism remain debated among scholars.
