Dual citizenship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dual citizenship (or multiple citizenship) is the status of a person concurrently regarded as a citizen of more than one country under their laws. It arises from the interaction of Jus soli and Jus sanguinis at birth, from naturalisation without renunciation, from marriage, or from restoration of an ancestral citizenship.

States' attitudes have shifted from hostility (the 1930 Hague Convention aimed to reduce multiple nationality; many states once demanded exclusive allegiance because of military service) toward tolerance: a majority of states now permit dual citizenship in at least some circumstances, and several that restricted it (India substitutes the Overseas Citizen status rather than dual citizenship; China does not recognise it) maintain notable exceptions. Practical issues include conflicting military obligations, taxation (the United States taxes citizens worldwide), diplomatic protection (generally unavailable against another state of one's nationality), eligibility for sensitive offices, and exit rights. Public debate focuses on loyalty, fairness between citizens, and diaspora engagement.
