Valencian language

Valencian (endonym: valencià) is the name given in the Valencian Community of Spain to the variety of the Catalan language spoken there, where it is co-official with Spanish. It belongs to the Western Catalan dialect group, along with the varieties of northwestern Catalonia and the Aragonese strip. Most linguists regard Valencian and Catalan as the same language with regional varieties, and the Acadèmia Valenciana de la Llengua, its official regulator, acknowledges the shared linguistic system while upholding the traditional Valencian denomination.

History

The language arrived in the Kingdom of Valencia with the conquest of James I of Aragon in the 13th century. The 15th century is remembered as the Valencian Golden Age of literature, producing works such as Ausiàs March's poetry, Joanot Martorell's chivalric novel Tirant lo Blanc, and the first Bible printed in the Iberian Peninsula. Suppressed during the Franco era, Valencian regained official status with the 1982 Statute of Autonomy and the 1983 Law on the Use and Teaching of Valencian.

Features and the naming debate

Valencian varieties are characterised by features such as the preservation of certain verb forms, distinctive vocabulary, and in some areas the loss of final -r in infinitives. The question of whether Valencian is a separate language or part of Catalan — the so-called Valencian linguistic conflict, fiercely contested in the "Battle of Valencia" of the transition era — remains politically sensitive, though academic consensus treats it as one language.

Use today

Around half of the Valencian Community's population can speak Valencian, with higher proportions in smaller towns than in the cities of Valencia and Alicante. It is used in regional media, education and administration, though Spanish predominates in everyday urban life.
