Cox–Ingersoll–Ross model

In mathematical finance, the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) model describes the evolution of interest rates. Introduced in 1985 by John C. Cox, Jonathan E. Ingersoll and Stephen A. Ross as a general-equilibrium model of the term structure, it is a one-factor short-rate model.

Specification

The short rate follows the stochastic differential equation drt = a(b − rt)dt + σ√rt dWt, where a is the speed of mean reversion, b the long-run mean level, σ the volatility, and Wt a Wiener process. Because the diffusion term is proportional to the square root of the rate, the rate remains non-negative.

Properties

The stationary distribution of the rate is a gamma distribution. If 2ab ≥ σ² (the Feller condition), the origin is unattainable. Closed-form prices for zero-coupon bonds exist, making the model tractable for calibration. The CIR process is also widely used as a model of stochastic volatility in the Heston model.
