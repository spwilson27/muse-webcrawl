Olympic medal

Gold, silver, and bronze: the standard Olympic medal set.

An Olympic medal is awarded to the successful competitors at one of the Olympic Games. There are three classes of medal to be won: gold, silver, and bronze, awarded to first, second, and third place respectively. The granting of awards is laid out in detail in the Olympic protocols.

History

At the ancient Olympic Games only the winner was honoured, with an olive wreath (kotinos). At the first modern Games, Athens 1896, winners received a silver medal and an olive branch, and runners-up a copper medal; at Paris 1900 some events awarded prizes rather than medals. Gold, silver, and bronze for the top three became standard at St. Louis 1904. From 1928 to 1968 the medal design was standardized on Giuseppe Cassioli's "Trionfo" obverse, showing Nike; since 1972 the host city has designed the reverse, and since Athens 2004 a new obverse depicting Nike before the Panathenaic Stadium has been used.

Composition and production

Despite their names, modern "gold" medals are mostly silver: Olympic regulations require gold medals to be at least 92.5 percent silver, gilded with at least 6 grams of pure gold; silver medals are 92.5 percent silver, and bronze medals are typically copper alloy. The last solid gold medals were awarded at Stockholm 1912. Host organizing committees design and produce the medals, beginning with a national competition and approval by the IOC; in recent Games the number of medals produced runs to several thousand, including Paralympic medals (which are made to the same standard and, since 2018 designs, incorporate Braille-like features and tactile marks).

Notable facts

The athlete with the most Olympic medals is American swimmer Michael Phelps, with 28 (23 gold). Medals are routinely made to be bitten in photographs — a pose popularized by athletes imitating the old test for pure gold. Host designs have incorporated local materials: Beijing 2008 set jade in the reverse, and Tokyo 2020 medals were cast entirely from metals recovered from recycled consumer electronics.
