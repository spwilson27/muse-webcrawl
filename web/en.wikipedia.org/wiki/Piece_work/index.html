Piece work

Piece work has been common in garment manufacturing, where workers are paid per item produced.

Piece work (or piecework) is any type of employment in which a worker is paid a fixed piece rate for each unit produced or action performed, regardless of time. It contrasts with payment by the hour or by salary. Piece rates are common in manufacturing, agriculture (for example fruit picking), garment production, textiles, and in some service and gig-economy work.

Advantages and disadvantages

Employers favour piece work because it ties labour costs directly to output and can raise productivity, since workers earn more by producing more. For workers, the system can reward speed and skill, but it can also lead to variable and insecure earnings, pressure to work faster at the expense of quality and safety, and disputes over how pieces are counted and whether defective items are paid for.

History and regulation

Piece payment was a feature of the putting-out system and of early factories, and figured in many 19th- and 20th-century labour disputes; Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management built piece-rate incentives into its system of differential rates. Modern labour law generally requires that piece-rate earnings still average at least the minimum wage for hours worked, and some jurisdictions require paid rest periods to be compensated separately. In the gig economy, payment per delivery, ride or task is a contemporary form of piece work and the subject of ongoing debate about worker classification and protections.
