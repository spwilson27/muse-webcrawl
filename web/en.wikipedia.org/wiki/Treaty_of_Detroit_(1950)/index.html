Treaty of Detroit (1950)

The Treaty of Detroit is the name given to the landmark 1950 collective bargaining agreement between General Motors (GM) and the United Auto Workers (UAW), negotiated principally by GM president Charles Erwin Wilson and UAW president Walter Reuther. It is regarded as a defining settlement of postwar American industrial relations.

The agreement

The contract ran for an unusually long five years. In exchange for labor peace and a no-strike commitment, GM granted workers an annual cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) tied to consumer prices, plus an annual "improvement factor" wage increase of about 2 percent reflecting productivity growth. The agreement also expanded pensions and introduced company-supported health insurance benefits.

Significance

The Treaty of Detroit became a pattern for contracts across the automobile industry and other mass-production sectors. It linked blue-collar wages to productivity and prices, underpinning the growth of the postwar American middle class, and it marked a shift in union strategy away from demands for a voice in corporate management toward securing a rising share of economic growth through wages and benefits.
