Syngas
Full nameSynthesis gas
Main componentsHydrogen (H₂), carbon monoxide (CO); also CO₂, methane
Produced byGasification of coal/biomass; steam reforming of natural gas; partial oxidation
UsesAmmonia and methanol production, Fischer–Tropsch fuels, hydrogen, electricity (IGCC)

Syngas, or synthesis gas, is a mixture of hydrogen and carbon monoxide, in various ratios, often with carbon dioxide and other trace gases. The name comes from its use as an intermediate in creating synthetic natural gas and in producing ammonia and methanol. Syngas is combustible and historically was manufactured as "town gas" for lighting and heating before the advent of natural-gas distribution.

Production

The principal production routes are:

  • Gasification — reacting coal, biomass, or waste with a controlled amount of oxygen and/or steam at high temperature:

    C + H₂O → CO + H₂  (water-gas reaction)

  • Steam reforming of natural gas or naphtha:

    CH₄ + H₂O ⇌ CO + 3 H₂

  • Partial oxidation and autothermal reforming, which combine oxidation and reforming.

The hydrogen-to-CO ratio is adjusted for the intended use via the water–gas shift reaction (CO + H₂O ⇌ CO₂ + H₂): about 3:1 for methanation, 2:1 for Fischer–Tropsch and methanol synthesis, and higher for pure hydrogen production.

Applications

Syngas is the platform intermediate of much of the chemical industry. Its hydrogen feeds the Haber–Bosch process for ammonia fertiliser; its 2:1 mixture becomes methanol; Fischer–Tropsch synthesis converts it to liquid hydrocarbons (gas-to-liquids and coal-to-liquids fuels, as practised at scale by Sasol in South Africa). Integrated gasification combined-cycle (IGCC) plants burn syngas for electricity with the option of pre-combustion carbon capture. "Green" syngas routes — biomass gasification, biogas reforming, and co-electrolysis of water and CO₂ — are pursued for sustainable aviation fuel and chemicals, though economics remain challenging.
