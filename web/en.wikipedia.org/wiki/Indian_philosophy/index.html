Indian philosophy comprises the philosophical traditions of the Indian subcontinent, developing over more than two millennia in dialogue and debate among Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and materialist schools. Its classical systems share a soteriological orientation — philosophy as a path to liberation from rebirth — while disagreeing profoundly about the self, God and the world.

The orthodox schools

Six schools came to be classed as āstika (accepting the authority of the Veda): Nyāya (logic and epistemology), Vaiśeṣika (atomistic metaphysics), Samkhya (dualist enumeration), Yoga (meditative discipline, systematised by Patañjali), Mīmāṃsā (Vedic exegesis) and Vedanta (Upaniṣadic theology and metaphysics, in Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita forms).

The heterodox traditions

Buddhist philosophy — from early Abhidharma analysis through Nagarjuna Madhyamaka emptiness doctrine and Yogācāra "mind-only" — debated the Hindu schools for over a thousand years and was carried across Asia. Jain philosophy contributed the doctrines of anekāntavāda (many-sidedness of reality) and syādvāda (conditional predication). The Cārvāka or Lokāyata school defended materialism and perception-only epistemology, surviving mainly in opponents' refutations.

Themes and later history

Shared concerns include karma and rebirth (Reincarnation), liberation (Moksha, Nirvana), the means of knowledge (pramāṇa), and sophisticated traditions of debate and logic. Indian philosophy continued through medieval commentarial traditions, early modern Navya-Nyāya ("new logic"), and the modern period's encounters with Western thought, from Vivekananda and Aurobindo to the analytic engagement of contemporary scholars.
