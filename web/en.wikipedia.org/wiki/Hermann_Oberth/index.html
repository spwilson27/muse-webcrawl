Hermann Oberth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hermann Julius Oberth (1894–1989) was an Austro-Hungarian-born German physicist and engineer regarded, with Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and Robert H. Goddard, as one of the three founding fathers of rocketry and astronautics. His 1923 book The Rocket into Planetary Space worked out the mathematics of spaceflight — staging, orbital mechanics, human factors — with a rigour that influenced a generation of European rocketeers.

Oberth advised on the film Frau im Mond, helped inspire the German rocket societies whose members included the young Wernher von Braun, and briefly worked at Peenemünde during the war. After 1955 he joined von Braun in the United States, returning to Germany in retirement.

Ideas

Among Oberth's far-sighted proposals were space mirrors, electric propulsion, and detailed designs for space stations and lunar bases. The "Oberth effect" — the principle that a rocket burn is most effective at the fastest point of an orbit, foundational to Gravity assist manoeuvres — bears his name. He lived to see Apollo 11 land and died weeks after the fall of the Berlin Wall, aged 95.
