William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition

The William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition, often abbreviated to the Putnam Competition, is an annual mathematics competition for undergraduate college students in the United States and Canada. It is widely considered the most prestigious university-level mathematics competition in the world, and its difficulty is such that the median score is typically zero or one point out of a possible 120. The competition is administered by the Mathematical Association of America, and the top scorers are named Putnam Fellows.

History

The competition was founded in 1927 by Elizabeth Lowell Putnam in memory of her husband William Lowell Putnam, a Harvard alumnus and mathematics enthusiast who had advocated intercollegiate intellectual competitions. The first competition administered under the current arrangements was held in 1938, and the contest has been run by the Mathematical Association of America since 1956. It was suspended during parts of the Second World War and held virtually during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Format

The competition is held on the first Saturday in December. The examination consists of twelve problems, divided into two three-hour sessions (A1–A6 in the morning and B1–B6 in the afternoon), each problem worth ten points. Problems require ingenuity more than advanced coursework, although a background in real analysis, abstract algebra, combinatorics and number theory is helpful. Students compete both individually and as members of three-person institutional teams.

Prizes and Putnam Fellows

The five highest-scoring individuals are designated Putnam Fellows and receive scholarships; the top-scoring woman receives the Elizabeth Lowell Putnam Prize. Top teams, whose scores are the sum of their members' ranks in recent years or individual scores historically, receive cash awards for their institutions' mathematics departments. Winners of the competition have included many later-famous mathematicians and scientists, including several Nobel laureates and Fields medallists such as John Milnor and David Mumford.

Notable features

The problems are famous for being elementary to state but hard to solve; a popular example style asks for elegant closed forms or combinatorial arguments that can be found within the three-hour session only with considerable insight. Past papers and solutions are published annually and are widely used in problem-solving courses and olympiad training.
