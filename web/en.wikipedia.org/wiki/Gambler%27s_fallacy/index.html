Gambler's fallacy

The gambler's fallacy, also known as the Monte Carlo fallacy, is the mistaken belief that if a particular event occurs more frequently than normal during the past, it is less likely to happen in the future (or vice versa), when the events are in fact independent. It typically arises in gambling contexts, such as believing that a roulette wheel that has landed on black several times in a row is "due" to land on red.

The Monte Carlo incident

The fallacy takes its alternative name from a famous episode at the Monte Carlo Casino on August 18, 1913, when the ball fell on black 26 times in a row. Gamblers lost millions betting against black, reasoning that the streak had to end. In reality, each spin was independent, and the probability of red remained the same on every spin.

Explanation

The fallacy stems from a mistaken application of the law of large numbers: while long-run frequencies do converge to the expected probability, this happens because later outcomes swamp earlier ones, not because the random process "corrects" itself. The related hot-hand fallacy is the opposite belief — that a streak of successes makes further success more likely.

Research

Psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman attributed the fallacy to the representativeness heuristic: people expect even short sequences of random events to "look" random, and so judge streaks as unrepresentative.
