Environmental determinism

Environmental determinism, also known as climatic determinism or geographical determinism, is the belief that the physical environment, particularly the climate and terrain, determines the patterns of human culture and societal development. It was a dominant approach in geography and anthropology in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and is usually contrasted with possibilism, which holds that the environment sets constraints but culture is otherwise an independent variable.

History

Ideas linking climate to temperament go back to antiquity, in works such as Hippocrates' Airs, Waters, Places and later in Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748). In the modern discipline of geography, Friedrich Ratzel's anthropogeography laid foundations that were taken up by Ellen Churchill Semple in the United States and, most controversially, by Ellsworth Huntington, who argued that climate shaped the energy and progress of civilisations.

Criticism and decline

Environmental determinism fell into disrepute in the mid-20th century. Critics argued that it was simplistic, that it had been used to justify colonialism, imperialism and racism by ranking peoples according to climate, and that it could not explain cultural differences between societies sharing similar environments. French geographers under Paul Vidal de la Blache developed possibilism as an alternative, and later geographers recast the environment as one influence among many.

Modern echoes

Debate about climate's role in history persists in more qualified forms, for example in Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997), which emphasises geographic and environmental factors in the differing development of continents while rejecting racial explanations, and in contemporary research on climate and economic outcomes.
