Valence (chemistry)

Valence (or valency) is the combining power of an element: the number of hydrogen atoms (or equivalent) with which one atom of the element combines or which it displaces. Carbon is tetravalent (valence 4), oxygen divalent (2), sodium monovalent (1).

In modern terms, valence is governed by the atom's outer-shell (valence) electrons — those available for bonding — which explain group numbers in the periodic table and the formulas of compounds (why water is H₂O and methane CH₄). The concept, developed by Frankland, Kekulé and others in the 1850s, preceded electrons and led directly to structural chemistry.

Related ideas

Valence differs from oxidation state, which assigns charges by electronegativity rules, and from coordination number, which counts neighbours in a structure. Transition metals show variable valence (iron(II) and iron(III)), a hallmark of their chemistry.

Valence shell

The valence shell is the outermost electron shell; atoms bond to complete it (the octet rule), which is why elements in a group — same valence configuration — behave alike.
