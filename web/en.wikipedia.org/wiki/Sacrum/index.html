The sacrum (Latin: os sacrum, "sacred bone"; plural sacra) is a large, triangular bone at the base of the spine in humans and most other mammals, formed by the fusion of five sacral vertebrae (S1–S5). It sits between the two hip bones of the pelvis, with which it forms the sacroiliac joints, and transmits the weight of the upper body to the pelvic girdle and lower limbs.

Anatomy

The sacrum is curved, concave on its pelvic (anterior) surface and convex dorsally. Its broad base articulates above with the fifth lumbar vertebra at the lumbosacral joint; its apex points downward and articulates with the coccyx (tailbone). Four pairs of anterior and posterior sacral foramina transmit the sacral spinal nerves and vessels. The sacral canal, the continuation of the vertebral canal, runs through the bone and opens below at the sacral hiatus. The lateral auricular surfaces form the sacroiliac joints with the ilia. The bone is sexually dimorphic: the female sacrum is shorter, wider and less curved, accommodating childbirth.

Development

The sacral vertebrae are separate at birth and begin fusing during adolescence, the process usually completing by the mid-20s to mid-30s. Incomplete fusion of the first sacral vertebra ("lumbarisation") or incorporation of the fifth lumbar vertebra ("sacralisation") are common anatomical variants of the lumbosacral junction.

Clinical significance

The sacrum is involved in sacroiliac joint dysfunction and lower back pain; sacral fractures occur in high-energy trauma and in osteoporotic insufficiency. Tumours such as chordoma characteristically arise in the sacrum and clivus. The sacral foramina are landmarks for anaesthetic nerve blocks, and the sacral hiatus is used for caudal epidural injections.

Name

The Latin name os sacrum is a calque of the Greek hieron osteon ("sacred bone"). Explanations vary: the bone may have been offered in sacrifice, believed to be the seat of the soul, or thought to be the last bone to decay and thus the nucleus of bodily resurrection.
