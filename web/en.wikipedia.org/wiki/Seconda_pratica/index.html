Seconda pratica

Seconda pratica ("second practice"), sometimes called the stile moderno, is a term coined by Claudio Monteverdi to describe the new compositional style of the early Baroque era, in contrast to the prima pratica (the stile antico) of Renaissance polyphony. In the seconda pratica the words are the master of the harmony — l'oratione sia padrona dell'armonia — meaning that the rules of counterpoint, especially the preparation and resolution of dissonance, may be bent or broken when the expressive meaning of the text demands it. The new style is inseparable from the invention of monody, the basso continuo, and opera itself.

The Artusi–Monteverdi controversy

The terms arose from a famous polemic. In 1600 the theorist Giovanni Maria Artusi published a treatise attacking passages from Monteverdi's then-unpublished madrigals for their unprepared dissonances and irregular voice leading. Monteverdi answered in the preface to his Fifth Book of Madrigals (1605), announcing a forthcoming defence, the Seconda pratica, overo Perfettione della Moderna Musica, and distinguishing his "second practice" from the "first practice" of Zarlino's rules. The promised treatise never appeared; the fullest statement of the doctrine is the Dichiaratione written by Monteverdi's brother Giulio Cesare in the Scherzi musicali of 1607, which argued that Plato's dictum — that music consists of speech, harmony, and rhythm, with speech foremost — justified licensing dissonance by expressive intent.

Musical features

Practices characteristic of the seconda pratica include free treatment of dissonance for expressive effect, the dominance of a single melodic line over thoroughbass accompaniment, affective chromaticism (as in the madrigal Cruda Amarilli, the very piece Artusi attacked), and text-driven rhythmic declamation. The stile antico did not disappear: it persisted for centuries in church music, but composers increasingly understood it as one style among several rather than the universal rule of music.
