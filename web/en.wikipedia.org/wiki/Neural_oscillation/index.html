Neural oscillation

Neural oscillations, or brainwaves, are rhythmic or repetitive patterns of neural activity in the central nervous system. Neural tissue can generate oscillatory activity in many ways, driven either by mechanisms within individual neurons or by interactions between neurons. In individual neurons, oscillations may appear either as oscillations in membrane potential or as rhythmic patterns of action potentials, which then produce oscillatory activation of post-synaptic neurons. At the level of neural ensembles, synchronized activity of large numbers of neurons can give rise to macroscopic oscillations, which can be observed in an electroencephalogram (EEG).

Oscillatory activity in groups of neurons generally arises from feedback connections between the neurons that result in the synchronization of their firing patterns. The interaction between neurons can give rise to oscillations at a different frequency than the firing frequency of individual neurons. A well-known example is the alpha activity of the brain.

History

Oscillatory activity in the brain was observed as early as 1875 by Richard Caton, who reported fluctuating electrical potentials from the brains of rabbits and monkeys. Hans Berger's invention of the EEG in the 1920s allowed these rhythms to be recorded from the human scalp; Berger described the alpha rhythm (8–13 Hz) and noted that it was blocked by eye opening. Later research by Edgar Adrian, Grey Walter, and others catalogued the classical frequency bands and their associations with states such as sleep, attention, and epilepsy.

Mechanisms

Single-neuron properties

Some neurons have intrinsic pacemaker properties: voltage-gated ion channels can produce subthreshold oscillations of membrane potential, and cells can fire rhythmically even when isolated. Thalamic relay neurons and inferior olive neurons are classic examples.

Network interactions

Recurrent connections between excitatory pyramidal cells and inhibitory interneurons can generate synchronised rhythms. In the widely studied PING (pyramidal-interneuron gamma) model, a burst of excitatory activity recruits fast-spiking interneurons, whose inhibition silences the population until the inhibition decays, setting the gamma period.

Functions

Neural oscillations have been proposed to support communication between brain regions through coherence: distant populations can exchange information most effectively when their excitability windows align in phase. Oscillations also provide a temporal reference frame for processes such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity, phase coding in the hippocampus, and the grouping of features in perception. Disturbances of oscillatory activity are associated with disorders including schizophrenia, Parkinson's disease, and epilepsy.
