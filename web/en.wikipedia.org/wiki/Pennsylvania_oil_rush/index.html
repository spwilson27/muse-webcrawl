Pennsylvania oil rush
Date1859 – early 1870s
LocationOil Creek valley, northwestern Pennsylvania, United States
CauseEdwin Drake's successful oil well at Titusville (27 August 1859)
OutcomeBirth of the modern petroleum industry; boomtowns such as Pithole

The Pennsylvania oil rush was a boom in petroleum production that began in northwestern Pennsylvania in 1859, following the drilling of the first successful commercial oil well in the United States. It is generally regarded as the beginning of the modern oil industry, preceding the later booms in Texas, Oklahoma and California.

Background

Oil had long been known in the region, seeping from the ground along Oil Creek and collected by Native Americans and early settlers for medicinal use. In the 1850s the Pennsylvania Rock Oil Company hired Edwin Drake, a former railroad conductor, to drill for oil near Titusville. On 27 August 1859 Drake's driller, William A. "Uncle Billy" Smith, struck oil at a depth of about 69 feet (21 m), using a steam engine to drive the drill through a pipe that prevented the borehole from collapsing — a technique adapted from salt-well drilling.

The boom

News of the strike triggered a frantic rush. Land along Oil Creek was leased at soaring prices, derricks crowded the valley, and speculators drilled thousands of wells. Production rose from roughly 2,000 barrels in 1859 to several million barrels within a few years, and the price of crude collapsed from about $20 a barrel to as little as 10 cents as supply outran transport and refining capacity. Boomtowns such as Pithole sprang up almost overnight: founded in May 1865, Pithole had some 15,000 inhabitants within months, then was virtually abandoned within a few years as its wells ran dry.

Infrastructure and industry

The rush spurred rapid innovation in transport: oil was floated down Oil Creek in barges, then carried by railroad tank cars and, from 1865, by the first successful oil pipelines, which broke the power of teamster cartels. Refining centred first on Pittsburgh and Cleveland, where Samuel Andrews's refining methods and the organisational genius of John D. Rockefeller laid the foundations of Standard Oil. Titusville and Oil City became durable centres of the trade, and Pennsylvania remained the world's leading oil-producing region until the turn of the twentieth century.

Legacy

The Pennsylvania oil rush established the template for later petroleum booms, demonstrated the commercial viability of drilled oil, and created the technological and corporate framework of the modern oil industry. The Drake Well is preserved as a museum and National Historic Landmark site near Titusville.
