Basidiomycota


Mushrooms are basidiomycetes

The Basidiomycota are one of the two great phyla of higher fungi, together with the Ascomycota forming the subkingdom Dikarya. They are distinguished by specialised spore-producing cells called basidia, on which sexual spores (basidiospores) are produced externally, typically four at a time following meiosis. The phylum contains more than 30,000 described species — roughly a third of all described fungi.

Life cycle and forms

Most basidiomycetes are filamentous, with a long-lived dikaryotic secondary mycelium whose hyphae often bear clamp connections. Familiar members include the mushrooms, puffballs, bracket fungi, jelly fungi and boletes of the class Agaricomycetes, many of which form conspicuous fruiting bodies (basidiocarps) such as the gilled mushroom. Others are plant pathogens with no large fruiting bodies: the rusts (Pucciniomycotina) and smuts (Ustilaginomycotina), which cause major crop diseases such as wheat stem rust and corn smut. Some are single-celled yeasts, including the human pathogen Cryptococcus neoformans.

Ecology and importance

Basidiomycetes are the principal decomposers of wood, unique among organisms in efficiently breaking down lignin through white-rot and brown-rot processes, and many form ectomycorrhizal partnerships with trees. They include prized edibles (porcini, chanterelles), the cultivated button mushroom, deadly poisonous species such as the death cap's relatives in Amanita, and the model laboratory fungus Schizophyllum commune.
