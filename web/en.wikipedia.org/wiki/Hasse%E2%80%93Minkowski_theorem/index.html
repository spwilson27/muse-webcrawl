Hasse–Minkowski theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Image placeholder: Hasse–Minkowski theorem

The Hasse–Minkowski theorem is a fundamental result in number theory which states that two quadratic forms over a number field are equivalent (represent the same values and have the same structure) if and only if they are equivalent over every completion of that field — that is, over the real numbers and over the p-adic numbers for every prime p. In particular, a quadratic form over a number field represents zero nontrivially if and only if it does so over all completions. The theorem is the archetype of a local–global principle.

Statement

For quadratic forms over the rational numbers, equivalence can be decided by a finite set of invariants: dimension, discriminant, the signature over the reals, and the Hasse invariant over each p-adic field; only finitely many primes need checking, since for all but finitely many primes the invariant is automatic. The corresponding local–global statement fails for forms of higher degree — there exist cubic forms, for example, that have points everywhere locally but not globally — which motivates the theory of obstructions such as the Brauer–Manin obstruction.

History

The theorem grew out of Hermann Minkowski's work on quadratic forms and was given its definitive form by Helmut Hasse in the 1920s as part of his broader local–global programme in algebraic number theory, which also includes the Hasse norm theorem and the Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem on central simple algebras.
