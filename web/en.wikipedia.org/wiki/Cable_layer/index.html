Cable layer

A cable layer or cable ship is a deep-sea vessel designed and used to lay underwater cables for telecommunications, electric power transmission, military, and other purposes. Cable ships are distinguished by large cable sheaves over the bow or stern (or both), cable tanks amidships in which the cable is coiled for transport, and specialized machinery for laying, grappling, and repairing cable on the seabed.

History

The first submarine telegraph cables were laid in the 1850s by converted ships. The SS Great Eastern, the largest ship of her era, famously laid the first successful transatlantic telegraph cable in 1866 and laid several more. Purpose-built cable ships such as CS Hooper (1873), the first ship designed specifically as a cable layer, followed. Companies such as the British Cable & Wireless and the French and American fleets operated large cable-ship fleets through the telegraph and telephone eras.

Design and operation

Modern cable layers carry thousands of kilometres of cable in circular tanks, and use dynamometers and linear cable engines to control paying-out speed and tension. Many are equipped with dynamic positioning systems, remotely operated vehicles (ROVs), and sea ploughs that bury cable beneath the seabed in shallow water to protect it from anchors and trawling. Cable repair ships use grapnels to raise damaged cable to the surface, where new sections are spliced in.

Notable cable ships

Historically notable vessels include the Great Eastern, CS Monarch (several ships of the name served the British GPO), and CS Long Lines. Modern fleets are operated by companies such as SubCom, ASN Marine, Orange Marine, and Global Marine, and include vessels like the CS Dependable class and the large purpose-built layers used for transoceanic fibre-optic systems.
