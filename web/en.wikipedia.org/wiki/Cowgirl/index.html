Cowgirl

Image: A cowgirl on horseback at a rodeo, early 20th century (placeholder)

A cowgirl is the female equivalent of a cowboy: a woman who works on a ranch in the western United States or Canada, herding and managing cattle, typically on horseback. The term emerged in the late 19th century as women took on ranch work — often alongside fathers, husbands and brothers — breaking horses, roping cattle, riding fences and participating in cattle drives.

History

Women had worked cattle in the American West since the era of Spanish and Mexican ranching, but the cowgirl became a distinct public figure with the rise of Wild West shows and rodeo in the 1880s and 1890s. Performers such as Annie Oakley and Lucille Mulhall — billed as the first "cowgirl" — popularised the image of the skilled horsewoman. Mulhall was credited by Theodore Roosevelt with great skill as a roper, and the term is often said to have been applied to her as early as 1900.

In early rodeo, women competed in many of the same events as men, including bronc riding, steer roping and bull riding. Prairie Rose Henderson, Fox Hastings, Tad Lucas and Mabel Strickland were among the celebrated women bronc riders of the 1910s and 1920s. Following the death of Bonnie McCarroll in a 1929 bronc-riding accident at Pendleton, Oregon, many rodeos curtailed women's rough-stock events, and women's participation narrowed largely to barrel racing, trick riding and relay races.

Modern era

The Women's Professional Rodeo Association (founded in 1948 as the Girls Rodeo Association) sanctions barrel racing, now a standard event at professional rodeos, and breakaway roping has grown rapidly in popularity. Working cowgirls remain a part of ranch life throughout the West, and the cowgirl has become an enduring figure in American popular culture, celebrated in song, film and fashion. The National Cowgirl Museum and Hall of Fame in Fort Worth, Texas, honours women of the American West.
