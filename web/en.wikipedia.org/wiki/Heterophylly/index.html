Heterophylly

Heterophylly is the occurrence of more than one distinct leaf form on a single plant. The different leaf types may differ in shape, size, thickness and internal anatomy, and are often produced in response to the environment in which each leaf develops — most strikingly in aquatic plants, which bear finely divided submerged leaves and broad floating or aerial leaves.

Examples

Classic examples include water crowfoots (Ranunculus subgenus Batrachium), whose underwater leaves are filamentous while their surface leaves are rounded lobes; arrowhead (Sagittaria), with ribbon-like submerged leaves and arrow-shaped emergent ones; and many amphibious Callitriche species. Heterophylly also occurs on land: juvenile and adult foliage of Eucalyptus and ivy (Hedera helix) differ markedly.

Mechanisms

The switch between leaf forms is a form of phenotypic plasticity controlled by environmental signals — submergence, light quality, humidity and temperature — acting through plant hormones, especially abscisic acid and ethylene. In model systems such as Rorippa aquatica, the KNOX gene network and gibberellin signalling have been shown to regulate the degree of leaf dissection.

Adaptive significance

Divided underwater leaves reduce drag and increase surface area for gas exchange in a medium where carbon dioxide diffuses slowly, while broad aerial leaves maximise light capture in air. Heterophylly therefore lets a single genotype thrive across the fluctuating waterline of ponds and marshes.
