Marsilius of Padua (Italian: Marsilio da Padova; born Marsilio dei Mainardini, c. 1275 – c. 1342) was an Italian scholar and political philosopher of the late Middle Ages. His treatise Defensor pacis (The Defender of the Peace, 1324) mounted one of the most radical medieval attacks on papal claims to temporal power and advanced theories of popular sovereignty and the separation of church and state that anticipated later political thought.

Life

Marsilius studied medicine and natural philosophy at the University of Padua, then went to Paris, where he served briefly as rector of the University of Paris in 1313 and taught in the arts faculty. In Paris he associated with John of Jandun, with whom he was long (probably wrongly) said to have co-authored the Defensor pacis. When the work's doctrines were condemned by Pope John XXII, Marsilius fled in 1326 to the court of the Holy Roman Emperor Louis IV of Bavaria at Nuremberg and Munich. He accompanied Louis on his Italian expedition and coronation in Rome (1328), where an antipope was installed. Marsilius remained at the imperial court, writing shorter defences of imperial authority (including the Defensor minor), and died in Munich around 1342.

The Defensor pacis

The work argues that the proper end of the civil community is peace and sufficiency of life, and that the chief destroyer of peace in Italy was the papacy's claim to plenitudo potestatis — fullness of coercive power. Marsilius contended that:

  • The "legislator" — the source of law — is the people or the weightier part of the citizen body (universitas civium), which institutes and may correct or depose the ruler.
  • Coercive jurisdiction belongs only to the secular ruler; priests, including the pope, have a teaching and sacramental role but no coercive power, and Christ himself refused temporal kingship.
  • Scripture's interpretation and matters of faith should be settled by a general council representing the whole body of the faithful, not by the pope alone.
  • Church property and clerical taxation fall under civil authority.

Legacy

Condemned as heretical in 1327, the Defensor pacis nonetheless circulated widely, influenced conciliarist thought, and was translated into English in 1535 under Thomas Cromwell to support the Henrician Reformation. Historians regard Marsilius as a forerunner of theories of popular sovereignty, representative government and the secular state.
