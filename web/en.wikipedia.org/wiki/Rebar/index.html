Rebar

Rebar (short for reinforcing bar), known in Britain as reinforcement steel, is a steel bar or mesh of steel wires used as a tension device in reinforced concrete and reinforced masonry structures to strengthen and aid the concrete under tension. Concrete is strong under compression but weak under tension; embedding rebar — whose surface is usually patterned with ribs to bond better with the concrete — allows the composite material to carry tensile loads.

Properties and grades

Rebar is typically made from carbon steel hot-rolled with deformation patterns. Sizes are designated by bar numbers corresponding to diameters in eighths of an inch in the United States (e.g., #4 = ½ inch) and by diameter in millimetres elsewhere. Grades denote yield strength, such as Grade 60 (60,000 psi / 420 MPa). Because steel and concrete have similar coefficients of thermal expansion, the composite behaves well across temperature changes. For corrosive environments, epoxy-coated, galvanized, or stainless steel bars, or fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) bars, may be used.

Placement

Bars are cut, bent, and tied with wire into cages or mats on site or prefabricated, then held at the correct cover distance from formwork by chairs and spacers before concrete is poured. Welded wire mesh serves a similar purpose in slabs. Recycled steel is the predominant feedstock, and rebar itself is highly recyclable.

History

Reinforced concrete was pioneered in the mid-19th century by figures such as Joseph Monier and François Hennebique, whose systems of embedded iron and steel made modern concrete construction possible.
