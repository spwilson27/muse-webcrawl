British deer

Six species of deer are established in the wild in Britain. Two — the red deer and the roe deer — are truly native, having been present since the last Ice Age. The fallow deer was introduced by the Normans (with possible earlier Roman introductions) for hunting in deer parks. Three further species were introduced in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries from Asia: the sika deer, the Reeves's muntjac and the Chinese water deer, largely through escapes from collections such as Woburn Abbey.

Species

Red deer (Cervus elaphus) are Britain's largest land mammals, with major populations in the Scottish Highlands, Exmoor, the Quantocks, the Lake District and Thetford Forest. Roe deer (Capreolus capreolus) are small, solitary woodland deer found throughout much of Britain. Fallow deer (Dama dama) survive in park herds and feral populations across England and Wales. Sika deer (Cervus nippon) are established in areas including Dorset, the New Forest, Lancashire and Scotland, and hybridise with red deer. Reeves's muntjac (Muntiacus reevesi), a small barking deer, has spread widely across England since escapes in the twentieth century. Chinese water deer (Hydropotes inermis), distinguished by tusks and the absence of antlers, are centred on Bedfordshire, Buckinghamshire and the Norfolk Broads; Britain holds a significant share of the world population.

Management

With no natural predators, deer populations are managed by culling under close seasons set by the Deer Act 1991 and the Deer (Scotland) Act 1996. Expanding deer numbers cause impacts including damage to woodland regeneration, crops and vehicles in deer–vehicle collisions, and concerns about overgrazing and tick-borne disease. The British Deer Society, founded in 1963, promotes deer welfare and research.
