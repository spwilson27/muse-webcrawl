The Gulf rupee (Arabic: روبية خليجية) was a currency issued by the Government of India and the Reserve Bank of India for circulation exclusively in the Persian Gulf region, where the Indian rupee had long been the dominant currency. It was introduced in 1959 and remained in use until the various Gulf states introduced their own currencies during the 1960s.

A one-rupee Gulf banknote

History

For much of the 19th and 20th centuries, the Indian rupee circulated widely in the Gulf sheikhdoms and Oman because of extensive trade with India. After India introduced stricter foreign exchange controls in the 1950s—partly to curb gold smuggling—the Reserve Bank of India (Amendment) Act 1959 created a separate Gulf rupee, identical in value to the Indian rupee but printed in different colours and with serial numbers beginning with "Z", for use only outside India.

Replacement

Kuwait was the first to leave, introducing the Kuwaiti dinar in 1961. Bahrain followed with the Bahraini dinar in 1965. When India devalued the rupee by about 36.5 percent in June 1966, the remaining Gulf states moved quickly to protect their holdings: Qatar and Dubai introduced the Qatar and Dubai riyal, Abu Dhabi adopted the Bahraini dinar (later the UAE dirham), and Oman introduced the Omani rial in 1970. The Gulf rupee thus disappeared from circulation by the early 1970s.

Banknotes

Gulf rupee notes were issued in denominations of 1, 5, 10, and 100 rupees. They resembled the contemporary Indian issues but were printed in distinct colours to prevent their re-importation into India, and bore no promise to pay within India.
