David John Wheeler FRS (15 February 1927 – 13 December 2004) was a British computer scientist and professor of computer science at the University of Cambridge. He was a pioneering figure of the earliest days of stored-program computing.

Career

Wheeler studied mathematics at Trinity College, Cambridge, and joined Maurice Wilkes's team building the EDSAC, one of the first stored-program computers. For his PhD (awarded 1951 — among the first doctorates in computer science anywhere), he invented the subroutine: the "Wheeler jump" allowed programs to call reusable blocks of code, a foundational concept of programming, documented in the landmark 1951 book The Preparation of Programs for an Electronic Digital Computer, written with Wilkes and Stanley Gill.

His later work included the Cambridge CAP computer, the Cambridge Ring local-area network, and contributions to computer security. With Michael Burrows he invented the Burrows–Wheeler transform (1994), a reversible data transformation at the heart of the bzip2 compressor. He is also associated with the aphorism that "any problem in computer science can be solved by another level of indirection" — often called the fundamental theorem of software engineering — along with the rueful addition "except for the problem of too many layers of indirection".

Honours

Wheeler was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1981, received the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award in 2003, and in 2004 became the first recipient of the Computer History Museum's fellow award in part for his subroutine work. He was married to Joyce Wheeler, herself an early computer scientist who used EDSAC for astronomical calculations.
