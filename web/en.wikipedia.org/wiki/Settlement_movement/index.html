Settlement movement

The settlement movement was a reformist social movement that began in the 1880s and peaked around the 1920s in England and the United States. Its goal was to bridge the gap between rich and poor by establishing "settlement houses" in impoverished urban areas, where volunteer middle-class "settlement workers" would live alongside their working-class neighbours and provide services to improve their lives.

History

The movement began in London with the founding of Toynbee Hall in 1884, inspired by Samuel and Henrietta Barnett. Its most famous American institution was Hull House in Chicago, founded in 1889 by Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr. Settlement houses offered childcare, English lessons, health services, clubs and cultural programmes, and their residents produced influential research on urban poverty that fed into Progressive Era reforms such as tenement regulation, child labour laws and public health provision.

Philosophy

Unlike charity organisations that treated poverty as a failure of the poor, settlement workers emphasised reciprocal learning: residents and neighbours would learn from each other, and social problems were understood as products of environment rather than character. The movement contributed to the emergence of professional social work and influenced later community development practice.
