Solenoid

A solenoid (from the Greek solen, "channel, pipe") is a coil wound into a tightly packed helix. In physics, the term refers specifically to a coil used as an electromagnet, but it is also widely used for any coil of wire designed to produce a controlled magnetic field. In engineering, "solenoid" often refers to an electromechanical device in which a coil drives a movable ferromagnetic plunger to produce mechanical motion.

A solenoid: a helical coil of wire whose length is substantially greater than its diameter.

Magnetic field

When an electric current passes through the coil, a magnetic field is generated. For an ideal solenoid — an infinitely long coil with tightly wound turns — the field inside is uniform and parallel to the axis, while the field outside is negligible. The strength of the interior field is given by the formula B = μ0nI, where n is the number of turns per unit length and I is the current. The field at the ends of a finite solenoid is roughly half the interior value at the centre.

The concept of the solenoid was introduced by André-Marie Ampère in 1823, after Hans Christian Ørsted's discovery in 1820 that electric currents produce magnetic fields. Ampère proposed that the field produced by a cylindrical coil would resemble that of a bar magnet.

Analysis

Inside the solenoid

Using Ampère's circuital law, the magnetic field inside a long solenoid can be shown to be uniform and proportional to the number of turns and the current. The direction of the field follows the right-hand rule: curling the fingers of the right hand in the direction of the current, the thumb points in the direction of the field along the axis.

Inductance

The inductance of a solenoid is approximately L = μ0N²A/l, where N is the total number of turns, A the cross-sectional area, and l the length of the coil. Adding a ferromagnetic core, such as iron, increases the inductance greatly by raising the effective permeability.

Applications

  • Electromechanical actuators: A movable iron plunger is drawn into the coil when energised, operating valves (solenoid valves), door interlocks, pinball flippers, and electric bells.
  • Starter motors: A starter solenoid switches the high current to a vehicle's starter motor and engages the pinion gear.
  • Inductors: Solenoids serve as inductors in electronic circuits, chokes, and transformers.
  • Scientific instruments: Large superconducting solenoids provide the strong, uniform fields used in MRI scanners and particle detectors.
  • Relays and contactors: A small control current in the coil switches a separate circuit.
