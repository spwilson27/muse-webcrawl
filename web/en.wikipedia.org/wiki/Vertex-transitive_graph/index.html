Vertex-transitive graph

The Petersen graph is vertex-transitive: its automorphism group maps any vertex to any other.

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a vertex-transitive graph is a graph G such that the automorphism group of G acts transitively on its vertices: given any two vertices v1 and v2 of G, there exists an automorphism f of G with f(v1) = v2. In other words, a graph is vertex-transitive if its automorphism group acts transitively upon its vertices. A graph is vertex-transitive without isolated vertices if and only if it is also regular.

Finite examples

Finite vertex-transitive graphs include the symmetric graphs (such as the Petersen graph, the Heawood graph, and the vertices and edges of the Platonic solids). The finite Cayley graphs (such as cube-connected cycles) are also vertex-transitive, as are the vertices and edges of the Archimedean solids — though only two of the Archimedean solids' graphs are also edge-transitive. Every complete graph and every cycle graph is vertex-transitive.

Properties

Edge-transitivity implies vertex-transitivity only when the graph has no isolated vertices and is not a disjoint union of edges; conversely, vertex-transitivity does not imply edge-transitivity — the Petersen graph is vertex-transitive but not edge-transitive. Every vertex-transitive graph is regular, and every connected vertex-transitive graph has a Hamiltonian path; by a conjecture of Lovász, every finite connected vertex-transitive graph other than the Petersen graph and a few exceptional examples has a Hamiltonian cycle.

Infinite vertex-transitive graphs

Infinite vertex-transitive graphs include infinite Cayley graphs, the infinite trees (whose automorphism structure makes each regular infinite tree vertex-transitive), and the Rado graph. Two countable vertex-transitive graphs are called quasi-isometric if the ratio of their distance functions is bounded above and below by positive constants.
