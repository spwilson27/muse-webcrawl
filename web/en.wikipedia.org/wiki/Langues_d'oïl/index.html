Langues d'oïl

The langues d'oïl (French pronunciation: [lɑ̃ɡ dɔjl]) are a dialect continuum that includes standard French and its closest Gallo-Romance relatives, traditionally spoken in the northern half of France, southern Belgium and the Channel Islands. The name comes from the northern Old French word for "yes", oïl (modern oui), contrasting with the southern oc of Occitan.

Varieties

Traditional varieties include Norman, Picard, Walloon, Gallo, Poitevin–Saintongeais, Champenois, Lorrain, Bourguignon and Francien, the dialect of the Île-de-France on which standard French is principally based. Some, such as Norman, developed overseas forms including Anglo-Norman and the Norman languages of Jersey and Guernsey (Jèrriais and Guernésiais).

Status

Most regional oïl varieties have declined sharply since the compulsory spread of standard French through schooling, and several are classified as endangered. Walloon, Picard and Norman retain cultural associations and limited teaching and media, but intergenerational transmission is weak outside a few areas.
