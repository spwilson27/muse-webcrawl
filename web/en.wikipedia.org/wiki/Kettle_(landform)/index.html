Kettle (landform)

A kettle (also known as a kettle hole, kettle lake or pothole) is a depression or hole in an outwash plain formed by retreating glaciers or draining floodwaters. Kettles are formed when blocks of dead ice left behind by a retreating glacier become wholly or partly buried in glacial till or outwash sediment and then melt, leaving a steep-sided, bowl-shaped depression that often fills with water.

Formation

As a glacier retreats, chunks of ice may calve off and become stranded in the sediment deposited by meltwater streams. Sediment buries and insulates the ice; when it eventually melts — sometimes centuries later — the overlying sediment collapses into the void, creating the depression. Kettles range from a few metres to over ten kilometres in diameter and may be tens of metres deep.

Kettle lakes and bogs

If the depression extends below the water table or is fed by precipitation and runoff, a kettle lake forms. Kettle lakes are abundant in the formerly glaciated regions of North America and northern Europe — for example the "prairie potholes" of the northern Great Plains and the kettle ponds of Cape Cod, Massachusetts. Because many kettle lakes have no surface inlet or outlet, their water chemistry is dominated by precipitation and groundwater. Over time, some fill with peat and become kettle bogs, with acidic waters supporting distinctive plant communities.

Examples

Walden Pond in Massachusetts is a famous kettle lake. The Kettle Moraine region of Wisconsin is named for its dense concentration of kettles formed between the lobes of the Laurentide Ice Sheet. In Europe, kettle holes known as Sölle dot the young moraine landscapes of northern Germany and Poland.
