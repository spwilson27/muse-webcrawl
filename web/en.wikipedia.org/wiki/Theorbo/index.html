Theorbo

Theorbo
Italian: tiorba
Classification: string instrument, lute family (plucked)
Developed: late 16th century, Florence, Italy
Features: extended neck with a second pegbox; unstopped bass strings (diapasons)
Related instruments: lute, archlute, chitarrone, Baroque guitar

The theorbo is a large plucked string instrument of the lute family, distinguished by its very long neck and a second pegbox that carries long bass strings, called diapasons, which are played open and are not stopped by the fingers. Developed in Italy around the end of the sixteenth century, it became one of the principal continuo instruments of the Baroque era.

Origins

The theorbo emerged in Florence in the 1580s–1590s, in the milieu of the Florentine Camerata and the earliest experiments in opera and monody. Singers needed an accompaniment instrument with a stronger, richer bass than the ordinary lute could supply. Makers responded by extending the neck so that much longer bass strings could be fitted. The closely related chitarrone is essentially an early or large form of the same idea; the terms were not always sharply distinguished in period sources.

Construction and tuning

A typical theorbo has a large, vaulted body like a lute, a main set of courses on the fingerboard — often six or seven, singly or doubly strung — and a further six to eight long bass strings running to the second pegbox. Total string counts of fourteen or more are common, and the instrument can exceed two metres in overall string length. Because the longest fingerboard courses would sound too high if tuned straight, the top one or two courses are usually tuned an octave lower ("re-entrant" tuning), which shapes the instrument's characteristic arpeggiated style.

Repertory and use

The theorbo's principal role was in the basso continuo, realising the bass line and harmony in opera, oratorio, chamber cantata, and sacred music from Monteverdi through the age of Bach and Handel. It was especially prized for accompanying the voice because of its resonance and soft attack. Solo repertory also exists, notably pieces by Giovanni Girolamo Kapsperger and Alessandro Piccinini, full of idiomatic arpeggios and campanella effects.

The instrument fell out of general use in the later eighteenth century, surviving longest in Italy and in opera orchestras, but was revived in the twentieth century as part of the historically informed performance movement and is now a standard member of Baroque ensembles.
