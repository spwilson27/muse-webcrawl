Risk compensation is a theory which suggests that people typically adjust their behaviour in response to perceived levels of risk, becoming more careful when they sense greater risk and less careful when they feel more protected. As a result, measures designed to improve safety may bring smaller benefits than expected, or occasionally none, because people offset part of the benefit by taking more risk. The strong form of the theory, risk homeostasis (proposed by Gerald Wilde), holds that people maintain a constant target level of risk, so safety interventions are entirely cancelled out in the long run; this strong form is controversial and generally not supported.

Examples and evidence

The economist Sam Peltzman's 1975 study of U.S. automobile safety regulation argued that mandated safety devices (seat belts, padded dashboards) reduced deaths per accident but were partly offset by more aggressive driving, leaving total fatalities roughly unchanged — the so-called Peltzman effect. Later research generally finds partial compensation: seat belt laws produced large net reductions in deaths, though some studies detect behavioural offsets, including risks shifted to pedestrians and cyclists. Risk compensation has been debated in many domains: antilock brakes and bicycle or ski helmets (mixed evidence), HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis and condom use (studies mostly find little or no offsetting increase in risk behaviour), sunscreen use, sports protective equipment, and COVID-19 precautions. In each case the magnitude of compensation appears to depend on how visible and salient the safety improvement is to the user.

Mechanism and criticism

The hypothesised mechanism is psychological: people monitor outcomes against a preferred balance of risk and reward ("target risk"), and interventions that lower perceived risk permit them to reclaim some benefit as speed, convenience, or thrill. Critics note that risk homeostasis theory is hard to falsify, that aggregate accident statistics rarely show complete offsetting, and that some popular examples (such as spikes on steering wheels proposed as thought experiments by John Adams) are illustrative rather than empirical. The mainstream consensus in safety science is that compensation is real but partial and context-dependent, and should inform — not paralyse — safety policy.

Related ideas

Risk compensation resembles moral hazard in insurance economics (coverage changes incentives), the rebound effect in energy efficiency (Jevons paradox), and the "safety in numbers" debate in cycling. It is also invoked in discussions of automated vehicles, where drivers may over-trust driver-assistance systems ("automation complacency").
