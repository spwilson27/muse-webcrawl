Red brick university

Red brick university (or redbrick university) is a term used to describe a group of British universities founded in the major industrial cities of England during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, before the Second World War. The term comes from the red brick or terracotta used in their characteristic Victorian and Edwardian buildings, contrasting them with the stone of the ancient universities of Oxford and Cambridge.

The civic universities

The original red brick institutions grew out of civic colleges established to teach science, engineering and medicine to local industries and communities. They include the University of Birmingham, the University of Bristol, the University of Leeds, the University of Liverpool, the University of Manchester and the University of Sheffield, all of which received university charters between 1900 and 1909. Reading and others followed shortly after.

Characteristics

The red bricks were non-denominational, admitted students on merit without religious tests, and oriented their teaching toward practical and scientific subjects serving industrial Britain. The term is now often applied more loosely to any British civic university of the period, and the institutions are generally research-intensive and highly regarded.
