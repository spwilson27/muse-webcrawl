Maki-e

Maki-e (蒔絵, literally "sprinkled picture") is a Japanese lacquer decoration technique in which pictures, patterns, and letters are drawn with lacquer on the surface of lacquerware, and then metal powder — usually gold or silver — is sprinkled and fixed on the surface before the lacquer hardens. It is one of the most characteristic and refined techniques of Japanese lacquer art.

Techniques

The main varieties include hira maki-e (flat maki-e), in which the design lies level with the surface; togidashi maki-e, in which the design is covered with lacquer and polished until it reappears flush with the ground; and taka maki-e (raised maki-e), in which the design is built up in relief with lacquer mixed with charcoal or clay powder. Techniques are often combined with inlay of mother-of-pearl (raden) and other materials.

History

Maki-e developed during the Heian period (794–1185) and reached great refinement in the Kamakura and Edo periods, adorning boxes, writing implements, combs, and furnishings for the imperial court, samurai, and wealthy merchants. Finely decorated maki-e objects exported to Europe were prized luxury goods, and master workshops in Kyoto and later Wajima maintained the craft, which continues today.
