Sir George Cayley, 6th Baronet (27 December 1773 – 15 December 1857) was an English engineer, inventor and aviator, regarded as the father of aeronautical engineering. He was the first person to understand the underlying principles of heavier-than-air flight, identifying the four forces of flight — weight, lift, drag and thrust — and in 1799 set out the concept of the modern aeroplane: a fixed-wing flying machine with separate systems for lift, propulsion and control.

Scientific work

Cayley published his fundamental three-part paper "On Aerial Navigation" in 1809–10. Using a whirling-arm apparatus he measured how air pressure acts on an inclined plane, and he determined that a cambered (curved) wing surface produces more lift than a flat one. His silver disc of 1799, engraved with a design for a fixed-wing machine on one side and a diagram of the forces on a wing on the other, is preserved in the Science Museum, London. Cayley's work laid the basis for the science of aerodynamics.

Gliders

Cayley built and flew model gliders from 1804 and full-size machines later in life. In 1849 he tested a triplane glider that carried a ten-year-old boy on short tethered flights, and in 1853 his "new flyer" carried his coachman — who reportedly resigned on landing, protesting he had been hired to drive, not to fly — across a valley at Brompton Dale. This is generally considered the first recorded flight of an adult in a heavier-than-air machine.

Other work and legacy

Cayley also invented the tension-spoke wheel, an early caterpillar track ("universal railway"), a hot-air engine, and devices for land reclamation and railway safety. He served as a Whig Member of Parliament for Scarborough (1832–35). The German pioneer Otto Lilienthal and the Wright brothers built on the foundations Cayley established, and his contribution is commemorated by the aerospace profession worldwide, as well as by memorials at Brompton and Scarborough.
