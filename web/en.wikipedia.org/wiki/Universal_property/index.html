Universal property

In mathematics, more specifically in category theory, a universal property is a property that characterizes a mathematical object up to unique isomorphism by describing its relationships to other objects via morphisms, rather than by describing its internal construction. Universal properties occur throughout mathematics: examples include the tensor product of vector spaces, the free group on a set, the product and coproduct of objects in a category, quotients of groups and rings, and completions of metric spaces.

Definition

Roughly, an object X has a universal property if, given any other object Y with certain data relating to X, there exists a unique morphism between X and Y (in the appropriate direction) compatible with that data, so that every diagram of the relevant shape commutes. The uniqueness clause implies that any two objects satisfying the same universal property are isomorphic by a unique isomorphism.

Examples

  • Products. The Cartesian product of sets, the direct product of groups and the product topology are all categorical products: projections from A × B such that maps into A × B correspond uniquely to pairs of maps into A and B.
  • Free objects. The free group on a set S comes with an injection of S such that any function from S to a group extends uniquely to a homomorphism.
  • Tensor product. The tensor product V ⊗ W is universal for bilinear maps out of V × W.
  • Quotients. The quotient of a group by a normal subgroup is universal for homomorphisms that annihilate the subgroup.

Formal treatment

Formally, universal properties are expressed through initial and terminal objects in suitable comma categories, or equivalently through representable functors: a universal construction corresponds to a representation of a functor, and the Yoneda lemma then explains why objects defined by universal properties are unique up to unique isomorphism. In this language, adjunctions — such as the free-forgetful adjunction — package universal properties, and limits and colimits in a category are all instances of the same pattern.

History

Universal properties were isolated explicitly in the work of Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane that founded category theory in the 1940s, though particular instances — for instance in the work of Emmy Noether's school on quotient structures — are older.
