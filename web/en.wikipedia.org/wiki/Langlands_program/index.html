Langlands program
Proposed byRobert Langlands (1967)
FieldsNumber theory, representation theory, harmonic analysis
Core ideasFunctoriality, reciprocity, automorphic forms ↔ Galois representations

The Langlands program is a web of far-reaching conjectures in mathematics relating Galois groups in algebraic number theory to automorphic forms and representation theory of algebraic groups over local and global fields. Proposed by Robert Langlands in a 1967 letter to André Weil, it is often called a "grand unified theory of mathematics".

Overview

Classical reciprocity laws — quadratic reciprocity, and its generalisations in class field theory — connect solutions of polynomial equations to splitting behaviour of primes. Langlands conjectured a vast non-abelian extension: representations of the Galois group of a number field should correspond to automorphic representations, so that arithmetic L-functions equal automorphic L-functions. Key principles include functoriality (maps between groups induce transfers of automorphic representations) and the existence of the Langlands dual group.

Results and influence

Confirmed cases have driven modern number theory: the modularity theorem (used by Andrew Wiles in proving Fermat's Last Theorem) is a special case; Laurent Lafforgue and Ngô Bảo Châu won Fields Medals for work on the function-field case and the fundamental lemma. A parallel "geometric Langlands" program connects the subject to algebraic geometry and even mathematical physics, where Edward Witten and others related it to S-duality in quantum field theory. Langlands received the Abel Prize in 2018 for the program.
