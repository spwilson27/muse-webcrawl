Dirk

A dirk is a long-bladed thrusting dagger. Historically, it was a personal sidearm of Highland Scots and of naval officers and midshipmen during the Age of Sail, worn as part of ceremonial and everyday dress. The Scottish dirk developed in the early modern period from the medieval ballock dagger and became closely associated with Highland culture, often worn with a smaller knife and fork in the scabbard.

History

The term "dirk" is Scots, first attested in the early seventeenth century, and may be related to the Low German dulk or Danish dolk. Early dirks had single-edged blades — many recycled from broken broadsword blades — and hilts carved from hard woods such as bog oak, frequently decorated with Celtic knotwork and brass or silver studs. After the Jacobite defeat at Culloden in 1746 and the Disarming Acts, dirks became increasingly ornamental; in Highland regiments they survived as part of full dress uniform.

Naval dirk

From the late seventeenth century, naval officers wore short dirks as fighting weapons and status symbols, since a full sword was cumbersome aboard ship and during boarding actions. Naval dirks remained regulation dress items in many navies into the twentieth century, including the Royal Navy, the United States Navy, and the Imperial Japanese Navy, where the tantō-style dirk formed part of an officer's uniform.

Modern usage

Today dirks are chiefly ceremonial: pipers and drummers of Scottish regiments wear them with Highland dress, and naval academies present dirks to midshipmen. The word also survives as a surname and given name, and in heraldry as a charge.
