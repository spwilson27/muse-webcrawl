Fallopian tube

The fallopian tubes, also known as uterine tubes, oviducts or salpinges (singular: salpinx), are paired tubular structures in the human female reproductive system that stretch from the uterus to the ovaries. Each tube is about 10–14 cm long and transports the oocyte (egg) from an ovary to the uterus; fertilisation of the egg by sperm normally occurs in the tube. They are named after the Italian anatomist Gabriele Falloppio, who described them in the 16th century.

Structure

Each tube is divided into four parts, from ovary to uterus: the infundibulum, a funnel-shaped opening fringed with finger-like fimbriae that sweep the released oocyte into the tube; the ampulla, the widest and longest segment, where fertilisation usually occurs; the isthmus, a narrow muscular section; and the intramural (uterine) part traversing the uterine wall. The inner lining bears ciliated cells whose beating, with muscular peristalsis, moves the egg or early embryo toward the uterus over about three to four days.

Clinical significance

Blockage or damage to the tubes — commonly from pelvic inflammatory disease (salpingitis), often caused by chlamydia or gonorrhoea — is a major cause of female infertility ("tubal factor"). If a fertilised egg implants in the tube, the resulting ectopic (tubal) pregnancy is a medical emergency that can rupture the tube. Surgical sterilisation (tubal ligation) occludes the tubes, and salpingectomy (surgical removal) is performed for some ectopic pregnancies, infections, and as ovarian-cancer risk reduction, since many high-grade serous ovarian cancers are now thought to arise in the fimbrial end of the tube.
