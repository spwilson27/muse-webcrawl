Hunsrik (natively Hunsrik, Portuguese: hunsriqueano), also known as Riograndenser Hunsrückisch, is a Moselle Franconian language derived primarily from the Hunsrückisch dialect of West Central German. It is spoken in southern Brazil, in parts of Paraguay and in the Argentine province of Misiones, by descendants of German immigrants who arrived beginning in 1824. Estimates of the number of speakers range up to about three million, making it one of the most widely spoken Germanic minority languages in South America.

History

German immigration to Brazil began in 1824 with the founding of São Leopoldo in Rio Grande do Sul. Many settlers came from the Hunsrück region of the Rhineland, and their dialects levelled over generations into a Brazilian koine. For much of the 19th and early 20th centuries Hunsrik was the everyday language of rural German-Brazilian communities, used in homes, churches, and German-language schools.

The nationalization campaign of the Vargas era (notably from 1937–1945) prohibited German-language instruction and public use, driving the language into private spheres and stigmatizing its speakers. Transmission weakened sharply in urban areas, though the language remained vigorous in rural communities.

Status and recognition

Since the early 2000s, Hunsrik has gained official recognition in several municipalities, and in 2012 it was declared part of the historical and cultural heritage of Rio Grande do Sul (state law). It has co-official status in municipalities such as Santa Maria do Herval. Teaching materials, dictionaries, and literature have been developed, including by linguists at Brazilian universities, and a standardized orthography has been proposed to support literacy efforts.

Characteristics

Hunsrik retains characteristic features of Hunsrück Moselle Franconian — for example, the pronunciation of Standard German st and sp without the palatalization found in the standard language in some varieties, dat/ wat interrogatives and pronouns ("dat" for "das"), and substantial lexical borrowing from Brazilian Portuguese, including words for local flora, fauna, foods, and technology. It is also influenced by neighbouring languages including Talian (Venetian) and regional Portuguese dialects.
