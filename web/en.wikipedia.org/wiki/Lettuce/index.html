Lettuce

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Heads of butter lettuce and romaine standing in neat field rows]

Lettuce (Lactuca sativa) is a leafy annual of the daisy family, the world's default salad green, descended from the weedy Lactuca serriola and first cultivated in ancient Egypt — where it was sacred, milky-sapped and carved on tomb walls — before becoming a salad plant of Greece and Rome. Four great types structure the modern market: crisphead (iceberg), romaine (cos), butterhead, and loose-leaf. Lettuce is almost all water and almost no calories, grown overwhelmingly in China and the United States, whose Salinas Valley calls itself the Salad Bowl of the World.

From sacred plant to iceberg

Egyptians bred lettuce from a seed-oil and fodder plant into a leafy vegetable associated with the fertility god Min; Romans served it to begin or end a meal (authorities disagreed, as they still do). The decisive modern chapter was logistics: in the 1920s–40s California growers shipped crisp, slow-bolting heads across America packed in ice, and "iceberg" — the name is said to come from those ice-mounded rail cars — set the American idea of lettuce for half a century. Iceberg's crunch and keeping quality conquered fast food even as chefs rediscovered romaine (the backbone of Caesar salad), butterheads and mesclun mixes.

Lettuce lore

Wild lettuces exude a sedative latex — lactucarium, "lettuce opium" — once sold as a mild soporific, and garden lettuce keeps a trace of the family sleepiness in folklore (Beatrix Potter's soporific lettuces nearly ended the Flopsy Bunnies). Modern lettuce production is high-tech and high-anxiety: field-packed "salad bags" revolutionised convenience, while outbreaks of E. coli on romaine have repeatedly shown how fast a leafy, eaten-raw crop can carry contamination across a continent.
