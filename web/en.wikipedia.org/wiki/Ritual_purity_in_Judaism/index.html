Ritual purity in Judaism

Ritual purity (Hebrew: tumah and taharah) is a system of laws in Judaism concerning states of ritual impurity and purity. In the Hebrew Bible, a person or object is in a state of tumah (impurity) after contact with certain sources — notably corpses, certain bodily discharges, and dead animals — and is thereby unfit to enter the Temple in Jerusalem or to handle sacred objects and foods until purified, typically by washing, waiting until sunset, or in some cases by special rites such as the red heifer ceremony.

Sources of impurity

The Torah (especially Leviticus 11–15 and Numbers 19) describes several sources of impurity: contact with a human corpse (the most severe, tumat met), carrion, childbirth, menstruation (niddah), seminal and other abnormal discharges (zav), and tzaraath, a skin condition often translated as leprosy. Impurity can be transmitted by touch, carrying, or being under the same roof (in the case of corpse impurity), and can pass secondarily to persons, vessels, food and liquids in graded degrees.

Purification

Purification generally requires immersion in a mikveh (ritual bath) or other valid body of natural water, laundering of garments, and the passage of time until sunset. Corpse impurity lasts seven days and requires sprinkling with water mixed with the ashes of the red heifer on the third and seventh days. Some impurities also required a sin offering or guilt offering upon the person's return to the Temple.

After the Temple

After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, most purity laws became inapplicable in practice, since purification from corpse impurity is impossible without the red heifer ashes, and everyone is presumed corpse-impure. Nevertheless, several laws remain in force or in custom: the laws of niddah governing marital relations and immersion in the mikveh; the washing of hands before meals and upon waking (netilat yadayim); and the prohibition on kohanim (priests) entering cemeteries or coming into contact with the dead except for close relatives.

Interpretation

Scholars and theologians have long debated the rationale of the purity system. Explanations range from hygienic theories (advanced by Maimonides, among others) to symbolic readings in which impurity is associated with death and the forces opposed to life, holiness and the divine presence. Modern anthropologists, notably Mary Douglas in Purity and Danger, have interpreted biblical purity law as a system of classification and boundary-maintenance.
