Cytochrome

Cytochromes are redox-active proteins containing a heme (haem) group, whose central iron atom cycles between the Fe(II) and Fe(III) oxidation states. They function as electron carriers in cellular respiration, photosynthesis and other redox reactions. Cytochromes are classified by their heme type and absorption spectra into classes a, b, c and d.

Structure and classification

The heme group is an iron atom coordinated within a porphyrin ring. In cytochrome c, the heme is covalently attached to the protein by thioether bonds to cysteine residues; in a- and b-type cytochromes it is held non-covalently. Classes are named for the position of their longest-wavelength absorption band in the reduced state: b-type around 565 nm, c-type around 550 nm and a-type around 605 nm. David Keilin rediscovered the cytochromes in 1925 (they had first been described by MacMunn in the 1880s) and recognised their role in the respiratory chain.

Function

In the mitochondrial electron transport chain, cytochromes occur in complex III (cytochromes b and c1), as the mobile carrier cytochrome c, and in complex IV (cytochromes a and a3 of cytochrome c oxidase), which passes electrons to oxygen. In chloroplasts, the cytochrome b6f complex links the two photosystems. The large cytochrome P450 superfamily of enzymes catalyses oxidation reactions in metabolism and detoxification. Because electrons flow through cytochromes one at a time, they link two-electron donors (such as quinols) to one-electron acceptors. Inhibitors such as cyanide and carbon monoxide act by binding to cytochrome oxidase.
