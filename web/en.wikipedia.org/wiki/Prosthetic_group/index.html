Prosthetic group

A prosthetic group is a non-amino-acid component of a conjugated protein that is tightly or covalently bound and is required for the protein's biological activity. It can be organic (such as a vitamin derivative, sugar, or lipid) or inorganic (such as a metal ion). Prosthetic groups differ from cofactors and coenzymes in the tightness of their association: a coenzyme binds loosely and transiently, whereas a prosthetic group remains attached through the catalytic cycle.

Examples

  • Heme in hemoglobin and myoglobin: an iron-containing porphyrin that binds oxygen.
  • Flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) in succinate dehydrogenase and other flavoproteins.
  • Biotin in carboxylase enzymes, covalently attached to a lysine residue.
  • Retinal in rhodopsin, the light-sensitive pigment of the retina.
  • Thiamine pyrophosphate and lipoamide in the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex.
  • Metal ions such as zinc in carbonic anhydrase and molybdenum in nitrogenase and xanthine oxidase.

A protein without its prosthetic group is called an apoprotein; the complete, active combination is a holoprotein. Many prosthetic groups are derived from vitamins, which is one reason those vitamins are essential nutrients.
