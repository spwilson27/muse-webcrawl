Parikshit (Sanskrit: परीक्षित्) is a legendary king of the Kuru dynasty in Hindu tradition, celebrated in the Mahabharata and the Bhagavata Purana. The posthumous son of the warrior Abhimanyu and grandson of Arjuna, he succeeded Yudhishthira as ruler of Hastinapura after the Pandavas' departure, and is remembered as the listener of the Bhagavata Purana, recited to him by the sage Shuka during the last seven days of his life before his fated death by snakebite.

Birth

According to the Mahabharata, Uttara was pregnant when her husband Abhimanyu was killed in the Kurukshetra War. Ashvatthama directed the Brahmashira weapon at her womb to exterminate the Pandava line; Krishna intervened and saved the unborn child, who was nonetheless stillborn and then revived by the god — hence his name Parikshit, "the examined one" (or, in popular etymology, because he sought the figure of Vishnu who had examined/protected him in the womb). Parikshit was crowned at Hastinapura when the Pandavas retired to the Himalayas, and is said to have ruled justly for decades; tradition associates his reign with the onset of the Kali Yuga.

Curse and death

Hunting one day, Parikshit, thirsty and angered by silence, draped a dead snake over the shoulders of the meditating sage Shamika. The sage's son Shringi cursed the king to die by the bite of the serpent-king Takshaka within seven days. Parikshit accepted his fate, abdicated in favour of his son Janamejaya, and spent his last week on the banks of the Ganges hearing the Bhagavata from Shuka, attaining liberation (moksha) before Takshaka's bite ended his life. To avenge him, Janamejaya later performed the great snake sacrifice (sarpa satra), halted by the sage Astika.

Historical considerations

Some scholars have sought a historical kernel: Vedic texts mention a Kuru king Parikshit, father of Janamejaya, and archaeology associates the later Kuru realm with the Painted Grey Ware culture of the western Ganges plain (roughly early 1st millennium BCE). The Puranas assign Parikshit a reign of several decades beginning shortly before the traditional date of the Kali Yuga (3102 BCE), which is not treated as chronology by historians.
