Nuremberg trials

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Nuremberg trials were a series of military tribunals held after the Second World War to prosecute prominent leaders of Nazi Germany for war crimes, crimes against peace and crimes against humanity. The principal trial, before the International Military Tribunal (1945–46), tried 22 major defendants — including Hermann Göring, Joachim von Ribbentrop, Wilhelm Keitel and Alfred Jodl — under the London Charter; twelve were sentenced to death (Göring cheated the gallows by suicide), three acquitted, and the rest imprisoned, including Rudolf Hess for life. Twelve subsequent trials before American tribunals (1946–49) prosecuted doctors, judges, industrialists, SS officers and commanders.

Nuremberg's legal legacy is foundational to international criminal law: the Nuremberg principles reject “acting under orders” and official position as defences, define the core international crimes, and lead in a direct line to the ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda and to the International Criminal Court (2002). Contemporaries and later critics raised objections — victors' justice, retroactive law (nullum crimen sine lege), the absence of Allied conduct (such as area bombing or the Katyn massacre, falsely charged to Germany by Soviet prosecutors) from the dock — which historians weigh against the tribunal's restraint, its documentary record, and its alternative to summary execution, which some Allied leaders had preferred.
