Macuahuitl

A macuahuitl (Nahuatl: ma:kwawitl) is a weapon of Mesoamerica, a wooden club or sword-stick whose edges are set with prismatic blades of obsidian. Used by Aztec, Maya, Mixtec, and other warriors, it combined the swinging mass of a club with the cutting edge of volcanic glass, which can be sharper than surgical steel.

Description and use

Macuahuitl ranged from one-handed weapons about a metre long to large two-handed versions. Obsidian blades were glued into grooves along both edges of a flat wooden paddle with an adhesive such as copal or bat guano mixed with resin. Spanish chroniclers reported blows capable of decapitating a horse, though modern reconstructions suggest the weapon was chiefly effective at inflicting deep lacerations — useful in warfare oriented toward taking captives for sacrifice, since it could wound and disable without immediately killing.

Survival

No original macuahuitl survives; the last known example, held in Madrid's Royal Armoury, was destroyed by fire in 1884. Knowledge of the weapon comes from codices such as the Florentine Codex, images in manuscripts, and experimental reconstructions.
