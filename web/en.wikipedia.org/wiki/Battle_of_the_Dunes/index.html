The Battle of the Dunes was fought on 14 June 1658 near Dunkirk between a French army commanded by the Vicomte de Turenne, supported by English Commonwealth troops sent by Oliver Cromwell, and a Spanish relief army under Don John of Austria the Younger. It was the decisive battle of the closing phase of the Franco-Spanish War (1635–1659).

Background

France and England had allied by the Treaty of Paris (1657): England would supply troops and naval support for the capture of Dunkirk, Gravelines and Mardyck, with Dunkirk to be handed to England. Turenne besieged Dunkirk in May 1658. A Spanish army, including the exiled English Royalist regiments and French Frondeur rebels under the Prince of Condé, marched to relieve the town and took up position among the coastal dunes.

The battle

Turenne attacked on 14 June, timing his advance so that the falling tide exposed the flank along the shore, which English warships bombarded. The English "New Model" veterans stormed a strongly held dune in the centre despite Spanish tercios' resistance, while French cavalry turned the Spanish right. The Spanish army collapsed with heavy losses, though Condé's cavalry withdrew in good order.

Aftermath

Dunkirk surrendered on the day of the battle and was handed to the English, who held it until Charles II sold it back to France in 1662. The victory led directly to the Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659, ending the war in France's favour and marking the eclipse of Spanish military predominance in Europe.
