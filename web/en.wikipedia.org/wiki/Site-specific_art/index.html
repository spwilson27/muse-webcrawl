Site-specific art

Site-specific art

Site-specific art is artwork created to exist in a certain place, designed in direct response to the location's physical, historical or social character. Typically, the artist takes the location into account while planning and creating the work; if removed from its site, a site-specific work loses much or all of its meaning. The term was promoted by the California Arts Council in the mid-1970s and is closely associated with land art, environmental sculpture and large-scale public commissions.

Development

Although earlier art — from cave painting to Baroque ceiling fresco — was made for specific settings, site-specificity as a self-conscious strategy emerged in the 1960s–70s as a reaction against the modernist idea of the autonomous, portable artwork and against the commercial gallery system. Minimalist and post-minimalist artists such as Richard Serra argued that a work should engage the viewer's movement through a real space; land artists such as Robert Smithson and Michael Heizer built works in remote landscapes, like Smithson's Spiral Jetty (1970) in Utah's Great Salt Lake and Heizer's Double Negative in Nevada.

Public art and controversy

Public percent-for-art programmes generated many site-specific commissions — and the movement's most famous dispute. Serra's Tilted Arc (1981), a 120-foot steel wall bisecting Federal Plaza in New York, was removed in 1989 after public hearings; Serra maintained that to move the work was to destroy it. The case became a landmark in debates about artists' moral rights and public consent. Christo and Jeanne-Claude's temporary wrappings and installations, such as The Gates in Central Park (2005), are site-specific works designed to vanish.

Later developments

From the 1990s, theorists such as Miwon Kwon traced a shift from physical to "discursive" site-specificity, in which the "site" might be a community, an institution or an issue — practices sometimes grouped as "new genre public art". Site-specific performance, dance (for example Trisha Brown's roof and wall pieces) and sound art extended the idea beyond objects. Contemporary commissions often involve long-term community engagement, and digital and augmented-reality works have added virtual sites to the concept.
