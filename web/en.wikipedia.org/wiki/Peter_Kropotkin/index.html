Peter Kropotkin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Prince Pyotr Alexeyevich Kropotkin (9 December 1842 – 8 February 1921) was a Russian anarchist, geographer, zoologist and philosopher, the leading theorist of anarcho-communism. From an aristocratic family, he served as an officer and geographer in Siberia, where his expeditions and his observations of animal cooperation shaped his thought, before abandoning a promising scientific career for revolutionary politics.

Imprisoned in Russia and later in France, and exiled for decades in Switzerland and England, Kropotkin argued in Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902) that cooperation within and between species is as important to evolution as competition — and in human society the natural basis for a free, communist order. In The Conquest of Bread (1892) and Fields, Factories and Workshops (1899) he sketched a decentralised society of integrated labour and local self-sufficiency, abolishing wage labour and the state together.

Later life and influence

Kropotkin's support for the Allies in the First World War (the Manifesto of the Sixteen, 1916) split the anarchist movement, most of which was anti-militarist. He returned to Russia in 1917 after forty years of exile, criticised Bolshevik dictatorship in letters to Lenin, and died at Dmitrov in 1921; his funeral in Moscow was the last large anarchist demonstration permitted in Soviet Russia. His reputation rests equally on scientific work — on glaciation and the geography of Asia — and on his writings, which remain the classic statement of communist anarchism.
