Rider-Waite Tarot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Rider-Waite Tarot is a widely popular deck for tarot card reading, first published in December 1909 by the Rider Company in London. The cards were drawn by the artist Pamela Colman Smith under the direction of the academic and mystic Arthur Edward Waite, both members of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. The deck is also known as the Waite-Smith or Rider-Waite-Smith deck, names that restore credit to its illustrator.

Overview

Waite designed the deck to embody the esoteric symbolism of the Golden Dawn's system of correspondences, including astrological and Kabbalistic attributions. Its most influential innovation was that Smith illustrated all 78 cards: where earlier decks, such as the Tarot de Marseilles, showed only pip patterns on the numbered Minor Arcana cards, Smith created full narrative scenes for every card, making the deck far more accessible for intuitive reading and story-telling.

Waite also made two structural changes to the Major Arcana: he transposed the positions of Strength (VIII) and Justice (XI), following Golden Dawn usage, and renamed the traditional Pope and Popess cards as the Hierophant and the High Priestess. His accompanying book, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1910), gives his interpretation of every card.

Publication history and influence

The deck was printed by Rider & Co. and remained little known for decades; its modern popularity dates from the 1970s, when U.S. Games Systems acquired the rights and marketed it widely. It became the best-selling and most imitated tarot deck in the English-speaking world, and its imagery — the Fool stepping off a cliff, the ten swords of the Ten of Swords, the lovers and the tower — has become the default visual language of tarot. The original printing plates were destroyed in the London Blitz; surviving early decks are collectible. Smith's contribution was long minimised, and modern scholarship and reissues increasingly credit the deck to her artistry.
