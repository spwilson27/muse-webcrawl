Superstition in sport

Superstition in sport is widespread among athletes, coaches, and fans, encompassing lucky rituals, clothing, numbers, foods, and routines believed to influence performance or outcomes. Such beliefs persist despite the absence of causal evidence, and are studied by psychologists as examples of illusory correlation and operant conditioning.

Common forms

Athletes may wear the same unwashed garment during a winning streak, follow fixed pre-game routines, avoid stepping on lines, or refuse to shave during playoffs — the "playoff beard" of ice hockey being a celebrated example. Baseball is particularly associated with superstition, including rally caps and the convention of not mentioning a no-hitter in progress. Tennis player Rafael Nadal is known for elaborate on-court rituals involving his water bottles, while basketball players often repeat precise free-throw routines.

Psychological explanations

Researchers attribute sporting superstitions to the human tendency to detect patterns, the desire for control in uncertain high-stakes situations, and reinforcement: a ritual followed by success is retained. Rituals may also genuinely help by reducing anxiety and increasing perceived self-efficacy.
