The Orchidaceae are a diverse and widespread group of flowering plants, with blooms that are often colourful and fragrant. Orchids are cosmopolitan plants that are found in almost every habitat on Earth except glaciers. The world's richest diversity of orchid genera and species is in the tropics. Along with the Asteraceae, the Orchidaceae is one of the two largest families of flowering plants, with between 21,950 and 26,049 currently accepted species in about 880 genera (depending on the classification used); recent checklists recognise roughly 28,000 species.

Morphology

Orchids are perennial herbs lacking permanent woody structure. Their flowers are strongly bilaterally symmetric (zygomorphic): of the three petals, the middle one is modified into a labellum, often elaborate, which serves as a landing platform for pollinators. The male and female reproductive parts are fused into a column, and pollen is released in compact masses called pollinia. Seeds are minute — a single capsule may contain millions — and lack endosperm, so germination in nature requires infection by a mycorrhizal fungus that supplies nutrients.

Ecology and pollination

Many orchids are epiphytes growing on trees in tropical forests; others are terrestrial or lithophytic. Pollination strategies are extraordinarily varied: about a third of species offer no nectar reward and deceive pollinators, some mimic female insects to induce pseudocopulation (e.g. Ophrys), and Darwin's orchid (Angraecum sesquipedale) famously predicted the existence of a hawkmoth with a matching proboscis. Vanilla, the family's most important food product, comes from the pods of Vanilla planifolia.

Cultivation

Orchids have been cultivated in China for well over a thousand years and became a craze in Victorian England ("orchidelirium"), when collectors funded expeditions worldwide. Today, mass-produced Phalaenopsis hybrids are among the best-selling potted plants in the world, propagated by tissue culture. Trade in wild orchids is regulated under CITES.
