Delhi Sultanate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Qutb Minar in Delhi, the victory tower begun under the early sultanate]

The Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) was a series of five successive Muslim dynasties that ruled large parts of the Indian subcontinent from Delhi: the Mamluk (Slave) dynasty, the Khalji, the Tughlaq, the Sayyid and the Lodi. Founded after Muhammad of Ghor's victories in northern India, the sultanate at its height under Muhammad bin Tughlaq ruled almost the whole subcontinent, resisted repeated Mongol invasions, fused Persian and Indian cultures into Indo-Islamic civilisation, and collapsed after Timur's sack of Delhi (1398) into a regional power before Babur destroyed it at Panipat in 1526.

Dynasties and expansion

Qutb al-Din Aibak, a Turkic slave-general, founded the sultanate in 1206; Iltutmish consolidated it and Razia (r. 1236–1240) became its only female sultan. Ala al-Din Khalji (r. 1296–1316) repelled the Mongols, conquered Gujarat, Rajasthan and the Deccan kingdoms, and imposed sweeping market and revenue controls. Muhammad bin Tughlaq (r. 1325–1351) extended rule to the deep south but ruined his authority with failed experiments — a token currency and a disastrous capital shift to Daulatabad.

Decline and legacy

Under the later Tughlaqs the empire split: Bengal, the Deccan (Bahmani Sultanate) and Vijayanagara seceded. Timur's invasion in 1398 massacred Delhi's population and shattered what remained; the Sayyid and Lodi successors ruled a fraction of the old realm until Babur's guns and cavalry ended the sultanate at the First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526), founding the Mughal Empire. The sultanate left Delhi a great capital, monuments such as the Qutb Minar, and the beginnings of Urdu.
