The Texas Longhorn is an American breed of beef cattle, characterised by its long horns, which can span more than 8 feet (2.4 m) from tip to tip. It derives from cattle brought by Spanish colonists to the Americas in the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, which interbred and lived feral in Texas for centuries, developing great hardiness, drought tolerance and resistance to disease.

A Texas Longhorn steer

History

After the American Civil War, huge herds of feral Longhorns roamed Texas, and from 1866 cowboys drove millions of them north along trails such as the Chisholm Trail to railheads in Kansas. The cattle drives supplied beef to the growing eastern United States and became a defining episode of the American West. With the arrival of barbed wire, railroads in Texas itself, and fatter British breeds such as the Hereford and Angus, the Longhorn declined and by the 1920s was near extinction.

Conservation

In 1927 the United States Forest Service assembled a small breeding herd at the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge in Oklahoma, one of several efforts that saved the breed. Today Longhorns are raised for lean beef, as rodeo and riding stock, and as living symbols of Texas heritage; the breed is the namesake and mascot of the University of Texas at Austin, whose live mascot is a Longhorn steer named Bevo.

Characteristics

Longhorns are medium-framed, long-lived and fertile, with cows known for easy calving and strong maternal instincts. Coat colour is extraordinarily variable — red, black, brown, white, brindle and speckled — and no two animals are marked alike.
