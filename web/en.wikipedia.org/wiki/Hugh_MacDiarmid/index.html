Hugh MacDiarmid is the pen name of Christopher Murray Grieve (11 August 1892 – 9 September 1978), a Scottish poet, journalist, essayist and political figure. He is considered one of the principal forces behind the Scottish Renaissance, a literary and cultural movement of the interwar period, and a leading figure in 20th-century Scottish literature — indeed, in modernist poetry worldwide.

Early life

Grieve was born in Langholm, Dumfriesshire, the son of a postman, and educated at Langholm Academy and Broughton Higher Grade School in Edinburgh. During the First World War he served in the Royal Army Medical Corps in Salonika and France. After the war he worked as a journalist, notably in Montrose, where he edited literary magazines that gave early notice of his ambitions for Scottish letters.

Poetry in Scots and English

MacDiarmid's early collections Sangschaw (1925) and Penny Wheep (1926) were written in a rich, synthetic Scots — "Lallans" — assembled from dialects and from Jamieson's dictionary. His long masterpiece, A Drunk Man Looks at the Thistle (1926), is a sprawling modernist poem of national and cosmic self-examination. From the 1930s he turned increasingly to long philosophical poems in English, beginning with To Circumjack Cencrastus (1930) and the Lenin hymns, and culminating in the vast epistemological works In Memoriam James Joyce (1955) and The Kind of Poetry I Want.

Politics

MacDiarmid was a founder member of the National Party of Scotland (1928), a forerunner of the SNP, and was expelled from it for his communism; he was later expelled from the Communist Party of Great Britain for his Scottish nationalism — he remarked that the expulsions showed he was doing something right. He stood for Parliament several times and was briefly placed under surveillance as a supposed security risk during the Second World War. In later life he was reconciled with both movements in spirit, if not in membership.

Later life and legacy

From 1951 he lived at Brownsbank Cottage near Biggar, South Lanarkshire, later secured for him for life. He received a civil list pension in 1950. MacDiarmid's combative criticism — epitomized by the slogan "Not Burns — Dunbar!" — reoriented Scottish literature away from Kailyard sentimentality toward Europe and modernism, and his influence on later Scottish poets and on the modern Scottish nationalist movement remains substantial.
