The Anglo-Persian Oil Company (APOC) was a British company founded in 1909 following the discovery of a large oil field in Masjed Soleyman, Persia (now Iran). The British government purchased 51% of the company in 1914, gaining a controlling number of shares, effectively nationalizing the company and securing a supply of oil for the Royal Navy in the build-up to the First World War. It was the first company to extract petroleum from Iran.

History

In 1901, William Knox D'Arcy obtained a concession from Mozaffar ad-Din Shah Qajar to explore for oil in Persia. After years of unproductive exploration by geologist George Bernard Reynolds, oil was struck at Masjed Soleyman on 26 May 1908. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company was formed the following year to develop the concession, and a refinery was built at Abadan, which began operating in 1912 and grew into the largest refinery in the world.

In 1935, following Reza Shah's demand that foreign countries refer to Persia by its endonym Iran, the company was renamed the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (AIOC).

Nationalization and the Abadan Crisis

In 1951, the Iranian parliament, under Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh, voted to nationalize the oil industry, triggering the Abadan Crisis. Britain responded with an embargo and legal challenges, and the dispute contributed to the 1953 Iranian coup d'état that overthrew Mosaddegh. A consortium agreement in 1954 restored foreign participation in Iranian oil production under the National Iranian Oil Company.

Renaming to BP

In 1954 the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company adopted the name British Petroleum Company, the direct corporate ancestor of today's BP plc.
