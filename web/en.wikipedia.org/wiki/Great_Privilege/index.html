The Great Privilege (Dutch: Groot Privilege; French: Grand Privilège) was an instrument signed by Mary of Burgundy on 11 February 1477, granting a number of rights to the States General of the Netherlands, the assembly of the Burgundian provinces. It was extorted from the 19-year-old duchess in the crisis following the death of her father, Charles the Bold, at the Battle of Nancy (5 January 1477), when France invaded and the provinces demanded the reversal of Burgundian centralization as the price of their support.

Provisions

The charter annulled the centralizing ordinances of Charles the Bold, restored the privileges and customary rights of the individual provinces and towns, and provided that the States General could meet on their own initiative. Key clauses abolished the Parliament of Mechelen (replaced by the restored Great Council), required that offices be held by natives of the provinces, barred declarations of war and new taxes without the consent of the States, and specified that the sovereign would govern in the language of the country. Companion charters were issued for individual provinces, notably Flanders and Brabant (the latter building on the Joyous Entry tradition).

Fate and significance

After Mary's death in a riding accident in 1482, her husband Maximilian of Austria repudiated the Great Privilege in the ensuing wars with the Flemish cities, and it formally lapsed — yet it remains a foundational text of Netherlandish constitutionalism, frequently compared to Magna Carta and invoked in later Dutch political thought as a precedent for consent to taxation and limits on princely power.
