Nikon (Russian: Ни́кон; born Nikita Minin, 1605–1681), the sixth Patriarch of Moscow and all Rus', was the dominant figure in the 17th-century Russian Orthodox Church. His programme of liturgical and textual reform, intended to bring Russian practice into line with Greek usage, provoked the great schism known as the Raskol, which split off the Old Believers and permanently altered Russian religious life. His assertion that the priesthood stood above the monarchy led to a dramatic breach with Tsar Alexis and to his deposition.

Early life and rise

Born to a Mordvin peasant family, Nikita married and served as a village priest before the deaths of his children led him and his wife to enter monastic life. As a monk of the Solovetsky Monastery and then abbot of the Kozheozersk hermitage, he gained a reputation for austerity and force of character. Brought to Moscow as archimandrite of the Novospassky Monastery, he became a close adviser and friend of the young Tsar Alexis, who appointed him Metropolitan of Novgorod in 1649 and, on Patriarch Joseph's death in 1652, pressed him to accept the patriarchate. Nikon accepted only on condition that the Tsar swear obedience to him in matters of faith.

The reforms

Beginning in 1653, Nikon revised Russian service books and ritual to conform to Greek and Ukrainian models. Changes included spelling the name of Jesus Isus instead of Iisus in the Greek manner, making the sign of the cross with three fingers instead of two, singing a triple rather than double Alleluia, altering the direction of processions, and amending the Nicene Creed's wording. To supporters these were corrections of scribal errors; to many clergy and laity, led by Archpriest Avvakum, altering the ancestral rite was apostasy. The resulting schism saw the Old Believers anathematised in 1666–67; many fled to the frontiers or chose self-immolation rather than accept the "Nikonian" books.

Conflict with the Tsar and fall

Nikon's doctrine of the superiority of the spiritual over the temporal power, his title of "Great Sovereign" (shared with the Tsar), and his building projects — above all the New Jerusalem Monastery near Moscow, a scale replica of the Holy Sepulchre — alienated Alexis. In 1658, after a public slight, Nikon withdrew to New Jerusalem and ceased to exercise his office, though he refused to resign. A Great Moscow Synod in 1666–67, attended by the Patriarchs of Alexandria and Antioch, deposed him and reduced him to the rank of a simple monk. He was exiled to the Ferapontov and Kirillo-Belozersky monasteries.

Death and legacy

In 1681 the ailing Nikon was permitted to return to New Jerusalem but died on the journey, near Yaroslavl; he was buried at New Jerusalem with patriarchal honours by order of Tsar Feodor III. His reforms remained in force, as did the schism: the anathemas on the old rite were lifted only in 1971. Historians continue to debate whether Nikon was a visionary reformer, an authoritarian whose methods guaranteed disaster, or both.
