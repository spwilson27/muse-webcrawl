Big Oil is a name sometimes used to describe the world's six or seven largest publicly traded and investor-owned oil and gas companies, also known as supermajors. The term, particularly in the United States, often carries a connotation of excessive size, influence, or profit.

The supermajors

As of the 2020s, the supermajors are generally considered to be BP, Chevron, ExxonMobil, Shell, TotalEnergies, and ConocoPhillips. The group emerged from consolidation in the late 1990s and early 2000s: Exxon merged with Mobil (1999), Chevron with Texaco (2001), Conoco with Phillips (2002), and BP absorbed Amoco and ARCO. Total (now TotalEnergies) acquired Petrofina and Elf Aquitaine. Together the supermajors control a minority of world oil reserves — most reserves are held by national oil companies such as Saudi Aramco — but they dominate refining, distribution, and trading in many markets.

History of the term

The phrase echoes the era of the "Seven Sisters", the seven Anglo-American companies that dominated the mid-20th-century petroleum industry following the breakup of Standard Oil in 1911. "Big Oil" gained currency in American political discourse during the energy crises of the 1970s and resurfaces in debates over fuel prices, subsidies, windfall profits, and lobbying.

Criticism and climate politics

Big Oil companies are frequent targets of criticism over climate change: investigations and litigation have alleged that major producers understood the climatic effects of fossil fuels from internal research in the 1970s and 1980s while publicly emphasising uncertainty. The companies point to investments in lower-carbon energy, carbon capture, and efficiency, and argue that oil and gas remain essential during an energy transition. Several supermajors have set net-zero targets of varying scope, drawing both investor pressure and accusations of greenwashing.
