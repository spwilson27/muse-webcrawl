Henri Léon Lebesgue (28 June 1875 – 26 July 1941) was a French mathematician known for his theory of integration, which generalised the classical Riemann integral and transformed real analysis, measure theory, and probability.

Life

Lebesgue was born in Beauvais, the son of a typesetter who died of tuberculosis when Henri was young; his mother, a schoolteacher, supported his education. He studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris and defended his doctoral thesis, Intégrale, longueur, aire ("Integral, length, area"), in 1902 under Émile Borel. He taught at Rennes and Poitiers before moving to Paris, becoming a professor at the Sorbonne in 1919 and at the Collège de France in 1921, where he remained until his death.

Work

The Lebesgue integral partitions the range of a function rather than its domain, allowing integration of a far wider class of functions, including highly discontinuous ones such as the Dirichlet function. Underpinning it is Lebesgue measure, an extension of the notion of length and area to complicated sets. His associated limit theorems — the monotone convergence theorem and the dominated convergence theorem — made it routine to interchange limits and integrals, and the Lebesgue differentiation theorem and the class of absolutely continuous functions unified differentiation and integration. His methods became foundational for Fourier analysis, functional analysis, and the modern axiomatic theory of probability.
