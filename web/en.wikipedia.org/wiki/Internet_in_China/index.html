The Internet in China is one of the largest national internet populations in the world, with more than a billion users. China was first permanently connected to the global Internet in 1994, and its domestic online ecosystem has since developed a distinctive character shaped by state regulation, large domestic technology companies and widespread mobile access.

History and growth

China's first international email was sent in 1987, and a full TCP/IP connection followed in April 1994 through the China Science and Technology Network. Commercial services expanded rapidly in the late 1990s. Growth accelerated with broadband and then mobile internet; by the 2010s most users accessed the internet primarily by smartphone, and mobile payments, e-commerce and social media became deeply integrated into daily life through platforms such as WeChat, Alipay, Taobao, Baidu and Weibo.

Regulation and censorship

The Chinese internet operates under an extensive system of controls often called the Great Firewall, which blocks many foreign websites and services — including Google, Facebook, YouTube, Wikipedia (in all languages since 2019) and numerous news outlets — and filters content within domestic platforms. The Cyberspace Administration of China, founded in 2014, is the principal regulator. Laws require real-name registration, data localisation for certain operators and platform responsibility for user content; the Cybersecurity Law (2017), Data Security Law and Personal Information Protection Law (2021) form the core statutory framework.

Economy

China's internet sector includes some of the world's largest technology firms, among them Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, ByteDance and JD.com, spanning e-commerce, gaming, video, cloud computing and artificial intelligence. A regulatory crackdown beginning in 2020 targeted monopolistic practices, data handling and the private tutoring and gaming sectors.
