Reductio ad absurdum

Reductio ad absurdum (Latin for "reduction to absurdity"), also known as proof by contradiction (argumentum ad consequentiam in some usages), is a form of argument and an informal fallacy counterpart in which the arguer temporarily assumes a claim is true (or false) and then logically derives an absurdity or contradiction from it, thereby concluding that the original assumption must be false (or true).

In logic and mathematics

In classical logic, proof by contradiction establishes a proposition P by assuming ¬P and deriving a contradiction (Q ∧ ¬Q); by the law of excluded middle, P then follows. The method is ubiquitous in mathematics: Euclid's proof of the infinitude of the primes and the proof that √2 is irrational are classical examples. Intuitionistic logic does not accept the general inference from ¬¬P to P, so constructive mathematicians distinguish such proofs from direct constructions.

History

The technique was employed by Greek philosophers and mathematicians: Zeno of Elea's paradoxes are early examples, and Aristotle discussed the principle in the Prior Analytics. Socratic dialogues frequently reduce an interlocutor's thesis to absurdity (the elenchus).

As a rhetorical strategy

In debate, reductio ad absurdum extends an opponent's position to an extreme and implausible conclusion. When abused — by exaggerating the position rather than following it faithfully — it degenerates into the straw man fallacy or a slippery slope argument.
