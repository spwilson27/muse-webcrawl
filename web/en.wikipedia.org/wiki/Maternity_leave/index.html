Maternity leave

Maternity leave is a period of absence from work granted to a mother before and after the birth of her child. It forms part of parental leave policy, which may also include paternity leave for fathers and shared parental leave. The length of maternity leave and whether it is paid vary widely between countries and employers.

International standards

The International Labour Organization's Maternity Protection Convention (No. 183, 2000) recommends at least 14 weeks of maternity leave, including six weeks of compulsory postnatal leave, with cash benefits of at least two thirds of previous earnings. Many countries exceed this minimum: several European states provide six months or more, often shared between parents.

By country

In the United Kingdom, statutory maternity leave lasts up to 52 weeks, with statutory maternity pay for 39 weeks. In Canada, maternity benefits are combined with parental benefits under the employment insurance system. The United States is notable among developed countries for having no federally mandated paid maternity leave; the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 guarantees 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave to eligible employees.

Health and economic effects

Research associates adequate paid maternity leave with lower infant mortality, higher rates of breastfeeding, improved maternal mental health and greater female labour-force attachment. Critics of very long leave periods argue they can widen gender pay gaps if mothers take disproportionately more time away from work than fathers, which has led several countries to reserve non-transferable leave quotas for each parent.
