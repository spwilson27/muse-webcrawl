The Underwater Demolition Teams (UDT) were United States Navy units created during the Second World War for the reconnaissance and clearance of beach obstacles ahead of amphibious landings. Their members — known as "frogmen" — swam ashore in daylight, often under fire, wearing only swim trunks, fins, masks and knives, mapping beach gradients and demolishing mines, hedgehogs and other obstacles. The UDTs are the direct predecessors of the modern Navy SEALs.

History

Origins

The need for dedicated beach-clearance units became clear after the costly landings at Tarawa in November 1943, where landing craft grounded on reefs far from shore. Drawing on earlier Naval Combat Demolition Units and the Scouts and Raiders, the Navy organised the first UDTs in December 1943 at Waimanalo, Hawaii, under the influence of Commander Draper Kauffman, who had established the naval demolition school at Fort Pierce, Florida.

Second World War

UDTs operated throughout the Pacific at Saipan, Guam, Peleliu, Iwo Jima and Okinawa, and were preparing reconnaissance for the planned invasion of Japan when the war ended. In Europe, naval demolition units cleared obstacles on the Normandy beaches on D-Day, suffering very heavy casualties at Omaha Beach.

Korea and Vietnam

In the Korean War, UDTs expanded into inland demolition raids against bridges, tunnels and rail lines, and conducted mine-clearance operations. During the Vietnam War they operated extensively in the rivers of the Mekong Delta. In 1962 the first SEAL teams were commissioned, drawn largely from UDT personnel. The remaining UDTs were redesignated as SEAL teams or SEAL Delivery Vehicle Teams in 1983, ending the UDT designation.
