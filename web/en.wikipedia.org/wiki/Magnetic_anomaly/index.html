Magnetic anomaly

Measured withMagnetometer (ground, airborne, marine, satellite)

In geophysics, a magnetic anomaly is a local variation in the Earth's magnetic field relative to the value predicted by a reference model of the main (core-generated) field. The anomaly reflects the magnetisation of rocks in the crust and upper mantle: iron-rich minerals such as magnetite acquire both induced magnetisation aligned with the present field and remanent magnetisation frozen in when the rock formed. Anomalies are usually measured in nanotesla (nT) and mapped by magnetometers towed behind ships, flown in aircraft, or carried on satellites such as Swarm.

Oceanic striping and plate tectonics

The most consequential magnetic anomalies are the long, parallel stripes of alternating positive and negative polarity flanking mid-ocean ridges. As new oceanic crust solidifies at a ridge, its minerals record the direction of the geomagnetic field, which reverses irregularly over geological time; seafloor spreading then carries symmetrical bands of crust away from the ridge. Interpreted by Vine, Matthews and Morley in 1963 (the Vine–Matthews–Morley hypothesis), this "magnetic tape recording" provided decisive evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics, and because the reversal timescale is calibrated, anomaly patterns let geologists date oceanic crust worldwide.

Applications and notable anomalies

On land, magnetic surveys are a standard tool in mineral exploration (magnetite and other orebodies produce strong anomalies), archaeology, unexploded-ordnance detection, and mapping basement geology beneath sediments. The Kursk Magnetic Anomaly in Russia, the world's largest, arises from enormous iron-ore deposits and can deflect compasses locally. The South Atlantic Anomaly, by contrast, is a feature of the main field — a region of weakened intensity caused by core dynamics — and is important because its reduced shielding exposes satellites to elevated radiation. "Magnetic anomaly detectors" (MAD) are also flown on maritime patrol aircraft to localise the steel hulls of submerged submarines.
