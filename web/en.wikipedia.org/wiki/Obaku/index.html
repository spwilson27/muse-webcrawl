Ōbaku (Japanese: 黄檗宗, Ōbaku-shū) is the third and smallest of Japan's schools of Zen Buddhism, alongside Sōtō and Rinzai. It was introduced from China in the mid-17th century by the Linji Chan master Ingen Ryūki (Chinese: Yinyuan Longqi, 1592–1673) and retains a distinctly Chinese character in its rituals, chanting, architecture and arts.

History

Ingen arrived at Nagasaki in 1654, invited by Chinese merchant temples of the port; his learning and reputation drew Japanese monks and patrons, and in 1661 the Tokugawa shogunate granted him land at Uji, south of Kyoto, where he built Manpuku-ji in the Ming-dynasty Chinese style — the school's head temple to this day. The name Ōbaku ("Yellow Quay") comes from Mount Huangbo in Fujian, where Ingen had been abbot; for its first generations the school's abbots were Chinese émigré monks.

Practice and characteristics

Ōbaku practice combines zazen and kōan study in the Linji manner with nembutsu recitation and observance of Chinese monastic codes; sutras are chanted in a Japanese reading of Ming-era Chinese pronunciation (Tō-on). The school is also credited with cultural imports that spread well beyond it: the sencha (steeped green tea) tradition — Ingen is said to have brought the tea seeds and the name — Chinese-style vegetarian temple cooking (fucha ryōri), Ming calligraphy and seal carving, and even the "Ingen bean" (ingen-mame, the common kidney bean), named after him.

Today

Ōbaku remains centred on Manpuku-ji with several hundred affiliated temples across Japan. Although formally part of the Rinzai grouping for administrative purposes for much of the modern era, it maintains its own teaching lineage and Chinese-influenced forms, and Manpuku-ji's archives and Ming-style buildings are designated cultural treasures.
