Viceroy

A viceroy is an official who runs a polity in the name of and as the representative of the monarch of the territory. The term derives from the Latin prefix vice- ("in the place of") and the French word roi ("king"). A viceroy's territory may be a colony, province or other large possession too distant for the sovereign to govern directly; the corresponding office is sometimes called a viceroyalty.

Spanish Empire

The institution reached its greatest extent in the Spanish Empire. The Crown of Aragon had used viceroys in its Mediterranean possessions, and after the conquest of the Americas Spain created great viceroyalties: New Spain (1535, first viceroy Antonio de Mendoza), Peru (1542), and later New Granada and the Río de la Plata. Viceroys acted as the king's alter ego, presiding over the audiencia, commanding the military, supervising the treasury and patronising the Church.

British usage

In British usage, the governor-general of India was popularly styled the Viceroy of India from 1858, when rule passed from the East India Company to the Crown, until independence in 1947; Lord Canning was the first and Lord Mountbatten the last. The lord lieutenant of Ireland was also sometimes described as a viceroy. In the Commonwealth realms, governors-general perform an analogous representative role, though the title viceroy is not used.

Other empires

Portugal appointed viceroys in India and Brazil. France used the title for its representative in New France in the 17th century. The Ottoman Empire and Qing China had analogous offices, such as the Chinese zongdu, sometimes translated as viceroy. In the Russian Empire the namestnik of the Caucasus and of Poland served a similar function.

Modern use

With decolonisation the office largely disappeared. The term survives mainly historically, though it is occasionally applied informally to officials exercising delegated sovereign authority.
