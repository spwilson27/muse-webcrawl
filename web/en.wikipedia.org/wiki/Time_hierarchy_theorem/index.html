In computational complexity theory, the time hierarchy theorems are important statements about time-bounded computation on Turing machines. Informally, they say that given more time, a Turing machine can solve strictly more problems. They are among the first results of complexity theory and follow from a diagonalisation argument. The theorems were proved by Juris Hartmanis and Richard E. Stearns in 1965, work that helped found the field and earned them the Turing Award in 1993.

Statement

For deterministic Turing machines, if f and g are time-constructible functions with f(n) log f(n) = o(g(n)), then

DTIME(f(n)) ⊊ DTIME(g(n)).

A similar theorem holds for nondeterministic machines: if f(n + 1) = o(g(n)), then NTIME(f(n)) ⊊ NTIME(g(n)). In both cases the inclusion is strict: there are languages decidable within the larger time bound that cannot be decided within the smaller.

Proof idea

The proof constructs a machine that simulates other machines with a clock enforcing the smaller time bound and then does the opposite of what the simulated machine would do — a version of Cantor's diagonal argument. The simulation overhead (a logarithmic factor for deterministic machines) is why the hypothesis needs the little-o condition, ensuring that the diagonalising machine itself fits inside the larger time bound.

Consequences

The best-known consequence is that P ⊊ EXPTIME: there exist problems solvable in exponential time that are not solvable in polynomial time. Refined applications separate many concrete classes, for example showing that DTIME(n) is strictly contained in DTIME(n1.5), and together with the space hierarchy theorem the results imply that L ⊆ P ⊆ NP ⊆ PSPACE ⊆ EXPTIME cannot have all inclusions equalities. Unlike the P versus NP problem, hierarchy theorems give unconditional separations — though they say nothing directly about which side any particular natural problem falls on.
