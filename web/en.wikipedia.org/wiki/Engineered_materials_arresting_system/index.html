Engineered materials arresting system

An engineered materials arresting system (EMAS), or arrestor bed, is a bed of crushable material built at the end of a runway to stop an aircraft that overruns the runway, in a controlled and predictable way that minimises damage to the aircraft and risk to occupants. The material — typically lightweight, cellular cement or engineered foam glass — crushes under the wheels, absorbing kinetic energy much like a runaway truck ramp.

Development and standard

EMAS was developed in the 1990s by the Engineered Arresting Systems Corporation (ESCO, later part of Zodiac Aerospace, now Safran) working with the FAA, which published design standards in Advisory Circular 150/5220-22. A standard EMAS is designed to stop an aircraft leaving the runway at up to 70 knots (130 km/h). Where the standard 1,000-foot runway safety area cannot be provided — at airports hemmed in by water, highways, or terrain — the FAA accepts EMAS as an alternative means of compliance.

Notable uses

Installations exist at over a hundred runway ends, notably at New York's JFK and LaGuardia airports, Chicago Midway, and Reagan National. Credited saves include a Gemini Cargo freighter at JFK (1999), a Southwest Airlines jet at Burbank (2000, before full standardisation of the bed), an Air France-affiliated regional jet, and in October 2016 the Boeing 737 carrying US vice-presidential candidate Mike Pence, which skidded off a wet LaGuardia runway and was stopped by the EMAS bed with no injuries. After use, the crushed blocks must be replaced before the bed is restored to service.
