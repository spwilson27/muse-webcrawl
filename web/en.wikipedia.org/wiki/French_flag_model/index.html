French flag model

Positional information divides a field of cells into zones, like the stripes of the French tricolour.

The French flag model is a conceptual model in developmental biology, proposed by Lewis Wolpert in 1969, that explains how cells in an embryo acquire different fates according to their position. It uses the metaphor of the French tricolour flag: a gradient of a signalling molecule (a morphogen) spreads across a field of initially equivalent cells, and cells respond to different concentration thresholds by differentiating in different ways — high concentration producing one fate (blue), intermediate another (white), and low or none a third (red) — thereby generating a pattern of stripes in a fixed order regardless of the size of the field.

Positional information

The model rests on Wolpert's concept of positional information: cells first acquire a positional value according to their location along the gradient, and then interpret that value according to their genetic programme and developmental history. The model explained the phenomenon of pattern regulation, in which embryos can regenerate missing parts and produce normal proportions even when pieces are removed or duplicated.

Evidence and influence

The discovery of real morphogen gradients — notably Bicoid protein in the Drosophila embryo and retinoic acid and Sonic hedgehog signalling in vertebrate limb development — gave the model strong empirical support, though real systems also involve feedback, cross-repression and dynamic effects beyond the simple threshold picture. The French flag model remains one of the most influential frameworks in developmental biology and a standard teaching model for pattern formation.
