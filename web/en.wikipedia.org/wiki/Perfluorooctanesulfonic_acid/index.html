Perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS), also called perfluorooctane sulfonate, is a synthetic organofluorine compound belonging to the family of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). It is a fully fluorinated eight-carbon chain with a sulfonic acid head group, making it both hydrophobic and lipophobic and an exceptionally effective surfactant. For decades it was the key ingredient in 3M's Scotchgard fabric protector and was used in firefighting foams, hydraulic fluids, chrome plating and semiconductor manufacture.

Persistence and health concerns

PFOS is extremely resistant to environmental degradation and is classified as a persistent organic pollutant. It bioaccumulates in protein-rich tissues, with a half-life in human serum estimated at about 3–5 years. Epidemiological studies have associated PFOS exposure with immune system effects (including reduced vaccine response in children), thyroid disruption, reproductive and developmental effects, and increased cholesterol. PFOS was added to Annex B of the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants in 2009, restricting its production and use worldwide, with some specific exemptions.

Regulation and phase-out

3M, the principal manufacturer, voluntarily phased out PFOS production between 2000 and 2002 after pressure from the US Environmental Protection Agency. The European Union banned most uses in 2008. In 2024 the US EPA set a drinking-water maximum contaminant level of 4.0 parts per trillion for PFOS individually — among the strictest standards ever set for a drinking-water contaminant. Legacy contamination around military airfields, where aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) were used for training, remains a major remediation challenge.
