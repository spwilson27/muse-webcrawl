Giant planet

A giant planet is a diverse type of planet with much greater mass than Earth. Giant planets are usually primarily composed of low-boiling-point materials — gases or ices — rather than rock or other solid matter, although massive solid planets can also exist. There are four giant planets in the Solar System: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Many giant exoplanets have been identified orbiting other stars.

Terminology and types

TypeMembersMain composition
Gas giantJupiter, SaturnMostly hydrogen and helium, with small rocky/icy cores
Ice giantUranus, NeptuneLarger fraction of "ices" — water, ammonia, methane — beneath hydrogen-helium envelopes

The term "gas giant" was coined by science fiction writer James Blish in 1952. Astronomers increasingly prefer to distinguish the ice giants, whose interiors are dominated by heavier volatile compounds and whose magnetic fields are strongly tilted and offset, from the hydrogen-dominated Jupiter and Saturn. Despite the name, "ices" in planetary science refers to volatile compounds with freezing points above about 100 K, regardless of their actual physical state inside the planet, where they exist as hot, dense supercritical fluids.

Formation

In the standard core-accretion model, giant planets began as rocky and icy cores of roughly ten Earth masses that grew beyond the frost line of the young Solar System and then rapidly captured massive envelopes of hydrogen and helium from the protoplanetary disc before it dispersed. Their formation in the outer disc, and possible subsequent migration, shaped the architecture of the inner Solar System — a story told for the Moon itself in the giant-impact hypothesis of a colliding planetesimal.

Exoplanetary giants

Thousands of giant exoplanets are known, including "hot Jupiters" orbiting extremely close to their stars — a population with no Solar System analogue that revolutionised theories of planetary migration. Objects above about thirteen Jupiter masses can fuse deuterium and shade into brown dwarfs.
