Latent semantic analysis

Latent semantic analysis (LSA), also called latent semantic indexing (LSI), is a technique in natural language processing for analysing relationships between documents and the terms they contain. It constructs a term–document matrix, often weighted by tf–idf, and applies singular value decomposition (SVD) to reduce it to a low-rank approximation that captures underlying "latent" semantic structure.

Method

After SVD, terms and documents are represented as vectors in a space of a few hundred dimensions. Synonymous words used in similar contexts end up close together, addressing synonymy, while polysemy is handled only partially because each word receives a single representation. Similarity between texts is measured by cosine similarity of their vectors.

History and applications

LSA was patented in 1988 by Scott Deerwester, Susan Dumais, George Furnas, Thomas Landauer and Richard Harshman, and published in 1990. Applications include information retrieval, essay scoring (the Intelligent Essay Assessor), document classification and modelling human vocabulary acquisition and semantic memory.

Limitations

LSA assumes a Gaussian-like statistical model ill-suited to count data, motivating probabilistic successors such as pLSA and latent Dirichlet allocation. It also lacks word order and can be computationally expensive for very large corpora, though randomised SVD algorithms help.
