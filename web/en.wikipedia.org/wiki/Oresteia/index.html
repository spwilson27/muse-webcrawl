The Oresteia is a trilogy of Greek tragedies written by Aeschylus in the 5th century BC, concerning the murder of Agamemnon by his wife Clytemnestra, the murder of Clytemnestra by their son Orestes, the trial of Orestes, and the end of the curse on the House of Atreus. The trilogy — consisting of Agamemnon, The Libation Bearers and The Eumenides — won first prize at the Dionysia festival in 458 BC and is the only surviving trilogy of ancient Greek tragedy.

Agamemnon

A watchman awaits the beacon signal of Troy's fall. King Agamemnon returns home in triumph, bringing the captive prophetess Cassandra, who foresees the house's doom in vain. Queen Clytemnestra, nursing grievances — the sacrifice of their daughter Iphigenia and Agamemnon's concubine — welcomes him with feigned joy and murders both in the bath. She defends the killing to the chorus of Argive elders as justice for Iphigenia, and rules Argos with her lover Aegisthus.

The Libation Bearers

Years later, Orestes returns from exile at Apollo's command to avenge his father. Reunited with his sister Electra at Agamemnon's tomb, he enters the palace in disguise, kills Aegisthus and then, after a wrenching confrontation, his mother. As he flees he sees the Erinyes (Furies) rising to pursue him for matricide.

The Eumenides

Orestes seeks refuge at Delphi and then Athens, where the goddess Athena establishes a court — the Areopagus — to try him: the first homicide trial in myth. Apollo defends him; the Furies prosecute. The jury ties, and Athena's casting vote acquits. Athena persuades the Furies to accept honoured residence in Athens as the Eumenides, the "Kindly Ones", ending the cycle of blood vengeance and replacing it with civic law.

Themes

The trilogy traces the evolution of justice from private revenge to public law, contrasts older (matriarchal, chthonic) and newer (Olympian, patriarchal) orders, and has been read politically as a charter myth for Athenian legal institutions. Its influence on later literature — from Euripides and Seneca to Eugene O'Neill's Mourning Becomes Electra — is immense.
