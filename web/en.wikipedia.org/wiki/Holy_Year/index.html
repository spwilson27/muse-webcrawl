Holy Year

In the Roman Catholic Church, a Holy Year, or Jubilee Year, is a special year of forgiveness, reconciliation, and pilgrimage. During a Holy Year the pope grants a plenary indulgence to the faithful who meet the prescribed conditions, which traditionally include a pilgrimage to Rome and passing through the Holy Doors of the four major papal basilicas — St. Peter's, St. John Lateran, St. Mary Major, and St. Paul Outside the Walls — which are opened only for the jubilee.

History

The first Roman jubilee was proclaimed by Pope Boniface VIII for the year 1300, drawing enormous crowds of pilgrims to Rome; Dante and Giotto were among those in the city that year. Initially intended to be held every hundred years, the interval was shortened — to 50 years, then 33 (the years of Christ's life), and finally to every 25 years by Pope Paul II, so that each generation might experience one. Ordinary jubilees have since been held regularly at 25-year intervals, most recently the Great Jubilee of 2000.

In addition, popes may proclaim extraordinary Holy Years for special occasions: examples include the Holy Year of Redemption (1933 and 1983), marking anniversaries of the Crucifixion, and the Extraordinary Jubilee of Mercy proclaimed by Pope Francis for 2015–2016, during which Holy Doors were opened in cathedrals throughout the world — the first time the jubilee was celebrated globally rather than centered solely on Rome. The regular Jubilee of 2025, themed "Pilgrims of Hope," again drew millions of pilgrims to Rome.

Origins

The Catholic jubilee draws on the biblical Jubilee of Leviticus 25, in which every fiftieth year was a year of release: slaves were freed, debts remitted, and land returned — a model of restoration and mercy adapted by the medieval Church into spiritual form.
