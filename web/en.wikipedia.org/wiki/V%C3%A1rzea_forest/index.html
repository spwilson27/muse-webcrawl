Várzea forest

Region: Amazon Basin, South America

Flooding: Seasonal, by nutrient-rich "whitewater" rivers

Contrast: Igapó (blackwater flood forest)

A várzea forest is a seasonal floodplain forest inundated by whitewater rivers in the Amazon Basin of South America. During the annual flood pulse, water levels can rise by 10–15 metres, submerging the forest for up to seven months of the year and depositing nutrient-rich sediments from the Andes.

Ecology

Unlike the nutrient-poor blackwater igapó forests, várzea soils are fertile and renewed annually, supporting high productivity and distinctive plant communities adapted to prolonged submergence. Tree species show specialisations such as buoyant, fish-dispersed seeds; many Amazonian fish species enter the flooded forest to feed and spawn among the trees.

Human use

The várzea is the most densely settled floodplain environment in Amazonia: its fertile soils and rich fisheries support riverine (ribeirinho) communities practising small-scale agriculture, fishing and agroforestry. Because flood timing is shifting with climate change and upstream dam building, the várzea and its communities face growing ecological pressure.
