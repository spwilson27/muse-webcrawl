Butter tea
RegionTibet and the Himalayas
Main ingredientsTea leaves, yak butter, water, salt
Tibetan namepo cha (བོད་ཇ་)

Butter tea, also known as po cha ("Tibetan tea") in Tibetan, cha süma in Bhutanese, and gur gur cha in Ladakhi, is a drink of the people of the Himalayan regions of Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan and parts of India. Traditionally it is made from tea leaves, yak butter, water and salt; the result is a thick, salty, soup-like beverage that provides warmth and calories in the cold, high-altitude climate.

Preparation

Brick tea is boiled for a long time to make a strong concentrate, which is then churned with yak butter and salt in a tall wooden churn (a mdong mo) until emulsified. Modern households often use cow's milk butter and an electric blender. The tea is served in bowls, topped up frequently for guests as a matter of hospitality, and may be drunk dozens of times a day by nomads and monks.

Culture

Butter tea is central to Tibetan social life and religious offerings; it is mixed with roasted barley flour (tsampa) to make a staple dough, offered to deities in monasteries, and used to fuel butter lamps. Tea reached Tibet from China by the Tang dynasty, and the addition of butter and salt distinguishes the Tibetan preparation from Chinese practice. Similar salty butter teas are found among Mongolian (süütei tsai, with milk), Sherpa, and other Himalayan peoples.
