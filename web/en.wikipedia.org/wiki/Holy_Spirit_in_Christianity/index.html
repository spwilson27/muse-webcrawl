Holy Spirit in Christianity

In Christianity, the Holy Spirit is the third Person of the Trinity — God the Holy Spirit — distinct from God the Father and God the Son (Jesus Christ), yet of one substance with them. The Spirit is believed to inspire Scripture, sanctify believers, and empower the Church. Most Christian traditions affirm the doctrine as defined by the early ecumenical councils.

The dove, from the account of Jesus's baptism, is the most common symbol of the Holy Spirit.

Biblical basis

In the Old Testament, the ruach (spirit/breath) of God creates, inspires prophets and anoints kings. In the New Testament the Holy Spirit descends on Jesus at his baptism in the form of a dove, is promised by Jesus as the Paraclete (advocate/comforter) in John's Gospel, and descends on the apostles at Pentecost in wind and tongues of fire (Acts 2), marking the birth of the Church. Paul's letters describe the Spirit's fruits (love, joy, peace — Galatians 5) and gifts (1 Corinthians 12), and the Spirit's indwelling of believers.

Doctrine

The Council of Constantinople (381) expanded the Nicene Creed to confess the Spirit as "the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son is adored and glorified". The Western church's later addition of Filioque ("and the Son") to the creed became a major point of division with Eastern Orthodoxy — one of the causes of the Great Schism of 1054. The study of the Holy Spirit is called pneumatology.

Role and gifts

Christians traditionally ascribe to the Spirit the inspiration of Scripture, regeneration and sanctification of believers, the distribution of spiritual gifts (charisms), intercession in prayer, and guidance of the Church into truth. Sacramental traditions link the Spirit especially with baptism and confirmation/chrismation. The seven gifts of the Spirit (from Isaiah 11) — wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety and fear of the Lord — are enumerated in Catholic teaching.

Pentecostal and charismatic movements

From the early 20th century, Pentecostalism emphasised a distinct "baptism in the Holy Spirit" evidenced by speaking in tongues, prophecy and healing; the charismatic renewal later spread these emphases through mainline Protestant and Catholic churches. Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity now counts hundreds of millions of adherents worldwide, making the experience of the Spirit central to much of global Christianity.
