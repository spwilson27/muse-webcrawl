Pearl hunting

Pearl hunting, also known historically as pearling, is the activity of recovering pearls from wild molluscs, usually oysters or mussels, in the sea or fresh water. Pearl hunting used to be prevalent in the Persian Gulf region and Japan, and also occurred in other regions including the coasts of Venezuela, Panama and northern Australia.

History

Pearl fisheries in the Persian Gulf — around Bahrain, Qatar, Kuwait and the Trucial Coast (now the United Arab Emirates) — were for centuries a mainstay of regional economies. During the summer season, divers descended from dhows without breathing apparatus, wearing a nose clip and carrying a basket, making dozens of dives a day. The industry collapsed in the 1930s with the advent of Japanese cultured pearls and the Great Depression, shortly before oil transformed the Gulf economies; the pearling heritage of sites such as Muharraq in Bahrain is now recognised by UNESCO.

Techniques

Traditional pearl divers were free divers who weighted themselves with a stone to descend quickly and were hauled up by rope at the end of each dive. In Japan, the ama — predominantly women — dived for pearl oysters, abalone and other shellfish. In Australia's Torres Strait and Broome, the industry turned to helmeted standard diving dress and relied heavily on Aboriginal, Japanese, and Malay labour, at great human cost.

Decline

The development of cultured pearls in Japan by Mikimoto Kōkichi and others in the early twentieth century, together with overfishing of natural beds, largely ended wild pearl hunting as a major industry, though small-scale fisheries persist, notably for mother-of-pearl shell and in freshwater mussel fisheries.
