Balcerowicz Plan

The Balcerowicz Plan (Polish: Plan Balcerowicza), also called "shock therapy," was a programme of rapid economic transition from a centrally planned economy to a market economy implemented in Poland after the fall of communism. It was designed by Finance Minister and Deputy Prime Minister Leszek Balcerowicz in the government of Tadeusz Mazowiecki, advised by economists including Jeffrey Sachs, and took effect on 1 January 1990.

Measures

The plan combined macroeconomic stabilization with sweeping liberalization. Price controls were lifted almost overnight; subsidies were cut and the budget tightened; the złoty was devalued and made internally convertible at a fixed peg to the dollar; foreign trade was liberalized; state enterprises were subjected to hard budget constraints ("commercialization") and later privatized; and a punitive tax (the popiwek) restrained wage growth in the state sector to break the inflationary spiral. Poland also negotiated substantial reduction of its foreign debt with Western creditors.

Effects and debate

The plan ended shortages and hyperinflation — inflation fell from nearly 600% in 1989–90 to manageable levels within a few years — but at the cost of a sharp transitional recession: official output fell by about 11.6% in 1990 and 7% in 1991, and unemployment rose from effectively zero to over 16%. Growth resumed in 1992, earlier than in most post-communist states, and Poland became one of Europe's fastest-growing economies. The plan remains politically contested in Poland: supporters credit it with Poland's long-run success, while critics argue its social costs were excessive and that gradual alternatives were possible.
