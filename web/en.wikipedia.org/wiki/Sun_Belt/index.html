Sun Belt

The Sun Belt stretches across the southern third of the continental United States

The Sun Belt is a region of the United States generally considered to stretch across the Southeast and Southwest. Various definitions keep the region below the 36th parallel north. The term gained currency in the 1970s, promoted by political analyst Kevin Phillips in his 1969 book The Emerging Republican Majority, to describe the warm, economically booming belt running from Florida and Georgia through Texas, Arizona and Southern California.

Characteristics

The Sun Belt's growth after the Second World War was driven by a warm climate, cheap land and energy, right-to-work laws, military and aerospace spending, and — decisively — the spread of air conditioning, which made year-round life and work in hot climates practical. Retirees, defence industries, technology firms and immigrants reshaped states such as Florida, Texas, Arizona, Georgia and North Carolina, while the industrial Rust Belt of the Northeast and Midwest lost population and political weight.

Political significance

Population shifts translated directly into congressional seats and electoral votes reapportioned toward the South and West after each census. The Sun Belt became the heartland of the modern Republican coalition and later a fiercely contested battleground: states like Arizona, Georgia, Nevada and North Carolina turned competitive in the 21st century as metropolitan areas diversified. Major Sun Belt metros include Houston, Phoenix, Dallas–Fort Worth, Atlanta, Miami, Las Vegas and San Antonio.

Challenges

Rapid growth has strained water supplies in the desert Southwest, increased exposure to hurricanes and extreme heat, and produced sprawling low-density development. Debates over water rights on the Colorado River and the sustainability of cities such as Phoenix and Las Vegas are central policy questions for the region.
