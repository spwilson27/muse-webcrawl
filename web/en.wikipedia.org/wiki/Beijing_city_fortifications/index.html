The Beijing city fortifications were the walls, gates, towers, and moats that defended Beijing from the Ming dynasty until the mid-twentieth century. The system comprised the rectangular Inner City wall, built on Yuan foundations in the early 1400s, and the Outer City wall, added in 1553 to protect the southern suburbs — together giving old Beijing its characteristic "convex" (凸) plan. Almost all of the walls were demolished between the 1950s and 1970s, but several gates and towers survive.

Layout and construction

The Inner City wall ran about 24 kilometres (15 mi) with nine gates, each crowned by a gate tower and an arrow tower, and a wide moat beyond. Walls were massive: an earth core faced inside and out with brick, standing about 12–15 metres high and wide enough at the top for troops and carts. The Outer City added seven gates along roughly 14 kilometres of wall. The Forbidden City and Imperial City formed nested enclosures within. Famous gates included Zhengyangmen (Qianmen) to the south, Chongwenmen, Xuanwumen, Deshengmen to the north, and Chaoyangmen to the east.

Demolition

After 1949, preservation advocates such as architect Liang Sicheng proposed keeping the walls as a linear park, but the walls were judged obstacles to traffic and symbols of feudalism. Demolition proceeded piecemeal through the 1950s and accelerated during subway construction in the late 1960s: Line 2 of the Beijing Subway runs beneath the Inner City wall's former course, and the Second Ring Road follows both wall lines. Bricks were widely reused in construction.

Survivors and preservation

Surviving structures include the Zhengyangmen gate tower and arrow tower, the Deshengmen arrow tower, the Southeast Corner Tower, the watchtower at Dongbianmen, and a restored 1.5-kilometre stretch of Ming wall preserved as the Ming City Wall Relics Park near Beijing railway station. In the twenty-first century, public sentiment has shifted toward regret over the loss, and gates such as Yongdingmen have been rebuilt in replica.
