Libration

In astronomy, libration is the apparent oscillating motion of a satellite as seen from the body it orbits — most famously, the slow wobble of the Moon that allows observers on Earth to see slightly more than half of its surface over time. Although the Moon is tidally locked and rotates at the same average rate at which it revolves, several effects combine to reveal about 59 percent of the lunar surface in total.

Types of lunar libration

Libration in longitude results from the Moon's elliptical orbit: it rotates at a constant rate but moves faster or slower along its orbit, so the face turned towards Earth shifts east–west by up to about 7.9°. Libration in latitude arises because the Moon's axis is tilted slightly (about 6.7°) to its orbital plane, alternately exposing regions beyond the north and south poles. Diurnal (parallactic) libration, about 1°, comes from observing the Moon from different points on Earth's surface at moonrise and moonset. Physical libration is a small genuine wobble caused by gravitational torques from Earth.

Other examples

Libration also affects other tidally locked moons and Mercury, whose 3:2 spin–orbit resonance produces analogous effects. The word is also used for the motion of Trojan asteroids and quasi-satellites around Lagrange points (libration points).
