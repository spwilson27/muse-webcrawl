Slow-wave sleep

Slow-wave sleep (SWS), often referred to as deep sleep, consists of stage three of non-rapid eye movement sleep. It lasts between 70 and 90 minutes in the first sleep cycle and is characterised by high-amplitude, low-frequency delta waves on the electroencephalogram (EEG), slow or absent eye movements, and reduced muscle tone.

Characteristics

During slow-wave sleep there is a substantial decline in brain activity compared with wakefulness, accompanied by decreases in heart rate, blood pressure and metabolic rate. Growth hormone is secreted in large pulses during this stage, and it is thought to be important for physical restoration and for the consolidation of declarative memories. People awakened from SWS often feel groggy and disoriented, a phenomenon known as sleep inertia.

Amount across the lifespan

Children spend a large proportion of the night in slow-wave sleep; the amount declines through adolescence and continues to fall with ageing, and may be greatly reduced in older adults. Most SWS occurs in the first third of the night, whereas REM sleep predominates towards morning.

Disorders and parasomnias

The NREM parasomnias — sleepwalking, sleep terrors and confusional arousals — arise from slow-wave sleep, typically in the first part of the night. Sleep deprivation selectively increases the intensity and amount of subsequent SWS, a rebound effect reflecting homeostatic sleep pressure.
