The AKS primality test is a deterministic primality-proving algorithm created and published by Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal, and Nitin Saxena of the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur on 6 August 2002, in a paper titled "PRIMES is in P". It was the first primality test to be simultaneously general, polynomial-time, deterministic, and unconditional — not dependent on unproven hypotheses such as the generalised Riemann hypothesis.

Concept

The test rests on the theorem that an integer n ≥ 2 is prime if and only if the polynomial congruence (X + a)n ≡ Xn + a (mod n) holds for some a coprime to n. Because checking the full congruence is too slow, AKS reduces the polynomials modulo Xr − 1 for a carefully chosen small r, checks a bounded set of values of a, and additionally rules out small factors and perfect powers.

Significance

The result settled a decades-old problem: earlier methods were either probabilistic (Miller–Rabin), deterministic but super-polynomial for general inputs, or polynomial only under unproven assumptions (Miller's test under GRH) or for restricted inputs. AKS proved that primality testing lies in the complexity class P. The authors received the 2006 Gödel Prize and the 2006 Fulkerson Prize for the work.

Practice

Despite its theoretical importance, AKS is not used in practice for large numbers: refined variants (notably the Lenstra–Pomerance improvement) still cannot compete with ECPP and APR-CL for proving primality, or with Miller–Rabin/Baillie–PSW probable-prime tests used by cryptographic software.
