Dancing mania

Engraving by Hendrik Hondius showing dancing mania sufferers.

Dancing mania (also known as dancing plague, choreomania, St. John's Dance, tarantism and St. Vitus's Dance) was a social phenomenon that may have primarily occurred in mainland Europe between the 14th and 17th centuries. It involved groups of people dancing erratically, sometimes thousands at a time, occasionally for days, apparently to exhaustion. In some accounts, dancers collapsed or died from heart attacks, strokes, or exhaustion.

Outbreaks

One of the earliest known outbreaks was in the 7th century, and the phenomenon reappeared across Europe. The best-documented episode was the dancing plague of 1518 in Strasbourg, in the Holy Roman Empire, where a woman began dancing in July 1518 and was soon joined by hundreds of others; the dancing continued for weeks. Other major outbreaks occurred in 1374 in the Rhineland and in 1518's surrounding region.

Reactions

Contemporaries believed the mania to be caused by demons, overheated blood, or the curse of a saint. Authorities frequently attempted to cure the afflicted by encouraging more dancing and providing musicians, on the theory that the mania would be danced out — a response that likely worsened the 1518 outbreak. Shrines to St. Vitus became pilgrimage destinations for those seeking relief.

Possible causes

Modern explanations include mass psychogenic illness under conditions of extreme stress, religious ecstasy, and ergot poisoning from the fungus Claviceps purpurea growing on rye — though ergot poisoning typically restricts blood flow and would make sustained dancing difficult. The phenomenon is generally regarded today as an example of mass hysteria shaped by the religious culture of the period.
