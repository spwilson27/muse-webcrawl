Nagarjuna (c. 150 – c. 250 CE) was an Indian Mahāyāna Buddhist philosopher, founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school, and one of the most influential thinkers in the history of Buddhism. Tradition associates him with the monastery of Nalanda and with a South Indian king; his name combines nāga (the serpent beings of Indian mythology, said to have entrusted him with hidden scriptures) and arjuna.

Thought

His masterwork, the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, systematically argues that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) of intrinsic, independent existence (svabhāva): everything arises dependently (pratītyasamutpāda), and emptiness is itself that dependent arising, not a nothingness behind things. His method proceeds by prasaṅga — drawing out the contradictions in opponents' positions — and by the tetralemma, which analyses claims as true, false, both, or neither. He famously equated the dependently arisen world (saṃsāra) with liberation (Nirvana): there is not the slightest difference between them.

Influence

Madhyamaka became foundational for Tibetan Buddhism and influential in East Asian schools such as Tiantai, Huayan and Zen. Nagarjuna's works, preserved in Sanskrit fragments and in Chinese and Tibetan translation, remain the starting point for philosophical study in much of the Mahāyāna world, and he is venerated almost as a second Buddha in Tibet.
