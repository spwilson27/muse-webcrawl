Case series

A case series describes a group of patients with a similar diagnosis or treatment.

In medical research, a case series is a descriptive study that follows a group of patients who share a diagnosis, exposure, or treatment and reports their characteristics and outcomes. Unlike a case report, which describes a single patient, a case series aggregates several or many cases, but it typically has no comparison or control group.

Design and uses

Case series may be consecutive or non-consecutive depending on whether all eligible patients in a period are included, and they can be assembled prospectively or retrospectively from records. They are useful for describing new diseases, rare conditions, novel procedures, and possible adverse effects, and they often generate hypotheses for later controlled studies.

Limitations

Because there is no control group, a case series cannot by itself establish that a treatment caused an outcome; selection bias, reporting bias, and confounding are common concerns. In hierarchies of evidence, case series rank below randomized trials and cohort studies for questions of treatment efficacy, though they may be the best available evidence for rare events.
