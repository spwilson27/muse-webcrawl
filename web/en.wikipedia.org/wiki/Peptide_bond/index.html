Peptide bond

A peptide bond is a covalent chemical bond linking two consecutive amino acid monomers along a peptide or protein chain. It is an amide bond formed between the carboxyl group (–COOH) of one amino acid and the amino group (–NH2) of the next, with the release of a water molecule — a dehydration synthesis (condensation) reaction.

Chemical properties

Resonance between the carbonyl oxygen and the nitrogen lone pair gives the peptide C–N bond partial double-bond character (roughly 40%). As a result the bond is shorter than a typical C–N single bond, rotation around it is strongly restricted, and the six atoms of the peptide group (C, O, N, H and the two flanking Cα atoms) lie in a single rigid plane. The bond is almost always in the trans configuration, in which successive Cα atoms are on opposite sides; cis peptide bonds are rare and occur mainly before proline residues.

Formation and hydrolysis

In cells, peptide bonds are formed by the ribosome, whose peptidyl transferase centre (a ribozyme) catalyses the reaction during translation. Outside the cell, forming a peptide bond is thermodynamically unfavourable and in synthesis requires activation of the carboxyl group. Peptide bonds can be hydrolysed by proteases, or slowly by acid or base; despite thermodynamic instability toward hydrolysis, the uncatalysed half-life of a peptide bond in water is on the order of hundreds of years, making proteins kinetically stable.

Spectroscopy and detection

Peptide bonds absorb ultraviolet light strongly near 190–230 nm, a property used to quantify protein concentration. The biuret reaction, in which Cu2+ ions form a violet complex with peptide bonds in alkaline solution, is a classic qualitative test for proteins.
