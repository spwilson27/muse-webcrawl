Baltimore classification

Baltimore classification
Proposed byDavid Baltimore
Year1971
BasisViral genome type and route to mRNA synthesis
Groups7 (I–VII)

The Baltimore classification is a system used to classify viruses based on the type of their genome (DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded, positive- or negative-sense) and their method of replication — in particular, the route by which viral messenger RNA (mRNA) is produced. It was devised by Nobel laureate David Baltimore in 1971 and remains widely used alongside the taxonomy of the ICTV.

The seven groups

  • Group I: double-stranded DNA viruses (e.g. herpesviruses, poxviruses, adenoviruses) — mRNA made by host-like transcription.
  • Group II: single-stranded DNA viruses (e.g. parvoviruses) — a double-stranded intermediate is formed first.
  • Group III: double-stranded RNA viruses (e.g. reoviruses, rotaviruses) — mRNA transcribed from the genome by a viral polymerase carried in the particle.
  • Group IV: positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (e.g. picornaviruses, coronaviruses, flaviviruses) — the genome itself serves as mRNA.
  • Group V: negative-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (e.g. influenza, rabies, Ebola) — mRNA is transcribed from the negative-sense genome.
  • Group VI: single-stranded RNA viruses replicating via a DNA intermediate (retroviruses, e.g. HIV) — reverse transcription produces DNA that integrates into the host genome.
  • Group VII: double-stranded DNA viruses replicating via an RNA intermediate (pararetroviruses, e.g. hepatitis B virus).

Significance

Because every virus must produce mRNA that host ribosomes can translate, the classification predicts a virus's replication strategy, the enzymes it must carry or encode (such as RNA-dependent RNA polymerase or reverse transcriptase), and potential targets for antiviral drugs. Baltimore shared the 1975 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work on tumour viruses related to this scheme.
