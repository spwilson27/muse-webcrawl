Cortical blindness

Cortical blindness is the total or partial loss of vision in a normal-appearing eye caused by damage to the brain's occipital cortex. Unlike blindness from damage to the eyes or optic nerves, in cortical blindness the eyes and pupillary reflexes remain intact; the deficit lies in the visual cortex, where visual information is processed. It is also termed cerebral visual impairment when partial.

Causes

The most common cause is stroke involving the posterior cerebral arteries, which supply the occipital lobes. Other causes include hypoxic–ischemic injury (for example after cardiac arrest), traumatic brain injury, infections, tumours, and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. In children, perinatal hypoxia and periventricular leukomalacia are leading causes.

Features

Patients have loss of vision with normal pupillary light responses and a normal fundus examination. A distinctive associated phenomenon is Anton syndrome, in which patients deny their blindness and confabulate visual experiences. Some patients retain blindsight — the ability to respond to visual stimuli without conscious awareness — attributed to pathways bypassing the primary visual cortex.

Prognosis

Recovery depends on the cause and extent of injury; vision often improves partially in the weeks after a stroke, while bilateral occipital infarction may cause permanent loss. Management focuses on treating the underlying cause, rehabilitation and compensatory strategies.
