Paul Berg (30 June 1926 – 15 February 2023) was an American biochemist and professor at Stanford University. In 1980 he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Walter Gilbert and Frederick Sanger; Berg received half of the prize "for his fundamental studies of the biochemistry of nucleic acids, with particular regard to recombinant-DNA." His 1972 construction of the first recombinant DNA molecule opened the era of genetic engineering, and his subsequent leadership in debating its safety shaped the governance of modern biotechnology.

Early life and education

Berg was born in Brooklyn, New York, the son of Russian Jewish immigrants. He studied biochemistry at Pennsylvania State University (graduating in 1948 after wartime service in the U.S. Navy) and earned his PhD from Case Western Reserve University in 1952, working on the enzymatic synthesis of nucleotides. After postdoctoral work in Copenhagen and at Washington University in St. Louis with Arthur Kornberg, he joined Stanford's new biochemistry department in 1959, where he remained for his career.

Recombinant DNA

In the early 1970s Berg's group, including graduate student Janet Mertz, developed methods for cutting DNA with restriction enzymes and joining molecules from different sources. In 1972 they joined DNA from the monkey virus SV40 with DNA from bacteriophage lambda, producing the first recombinant DNA molecule in a test tube. Concerned about the hazards of introducing viral DNA into bacteria, Berg voluntarily deferred the next step of the experiment — a decision that led to the 1974 "Berg letter," a moratorium call signed by leading molecular biologists, and the landmark Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA in 1975, where scientists drafted safety guidelines later adopted by the National Institutes of Health.

Later career

Berg continued work on gene expression, RNA splicing and viral genomes, helped found the biotechnology company DNAX, and served as director of Stanford's Beckman Center for Molecular and Genetic Medicine from 1985 to 2000. He remained a prominent voice on science policy, stem cell research and the ethics of genetic technology until his death at 96.
