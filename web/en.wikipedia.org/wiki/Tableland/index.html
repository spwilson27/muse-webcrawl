Tableland

TypeUpland region / plateau
UsageGeographical term, especially in Australia
Related termsPlateau, mesa, table mountain, downs
ExamplesAtherton Tableland; New England Tableland; Central Tablelands (NSW)

A tableland is a broad, elevated area of land with a generally flat top — in effect a plateau, often bounded by distinct slopes or escarpments. The term is used most characteristically in Australian geography, where several upland districts along the Great Dividing Range are formally named Tablelands, and in older British usage as a general synonym for a plateau region.

Usage

Unlike "plateau", which is a technical geomorphological term for any elevated flat surface, "tableland" tends to denote a settled upland district of moderate height — rolling, fertile country raised above the surrounding lowlands rather than a dramatic landform. Australia's named tablelands include the Atherton Tableland of Far North Queensland, famous for dairy farming and rainforest; the New England Tableland of New South Wales, a high grazing country around Armidale; and the Central, Southern and Northern Tablelands regions of New South Wales, a formal administrative-geographic division of the state.

Formation

Tablelands typically form where resistant rock layers, basalt flows or uplifted peneplains protect a surface from erosion while surrounding country is worn down, or where block faulting and warping along a continental margin raise a strip of country — as along Australia's Great Escarpment — leaving level uplands behind the scarp.

In other contexts

The word appears in place names and literature elsewhere — for instance the "tableland" of central Ceylon in British colonial writing — and in Newfoundland, The Tablelands of Gros Morne National Park are a famous barren plateau of exposed mantle rock (peridotite), though there the formation is a specific named massif rather than a generic district.
