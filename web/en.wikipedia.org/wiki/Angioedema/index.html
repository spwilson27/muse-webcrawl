Angioedema

Angioedema
Specialty: Emergency medicine, immunology
Symptoms: Swelling of face, lips, tongue, throat, limbs, bowel
Causes: Allergy, ACE inhibitors, C1-inhibitor deficiency
Treatment: Antihistamines, corticosteroids, epinephrine; C1-inhibitor concentrate, icatibant (hereditary form)
Angioedema causes rapid swelling of the deeper layers of the skin and mucous membranes.

Angioedema is an area of swelling (edema) of the lower layer of skin and tissue just under the skin or mucous membranes. The swelling may occur in the face, tongue, larynx, abdomen, or arms and legs. It is similar to hives (urticaria), but hives affect the upper dermis while angioedema involves deeper tissues; the two frequently occur together. Swelling of the airway can be life-threatening.

Causes

Angioedema is commonly divided into histamine-mediated and bradykinin-mediated forms. Allergic (histamine-mediated) angioedema occurs with food, drug, or insect-sting allergies and as part of anaphylaxis. ACE-inhibitor drugs are a frequent bradykinin-mediated cause. Hereditary angioedema results from deficiency or dysfunction of C1 esterase inhibitor, usually inherited in an autosomal-dominant pattern from mutations in the SERPING1 gene; attacks cause recurrent swelling, severe abdominal pain from bowel-wall edema, and potentially fatal laryngeal swelling, and are not accompanied by hives.

Treatment

Securing the airway is the priority. Histamine-mediated cases are treated with antihistamines, corticosteroids, and epinephrine if severe. Bradykinin-mediated attacks respond poorly to those drugs and are treated with C1-inhibitor concentrate, the bradykinin-receptor blocker icatibant, or the kallikrein inhibitor ecallantide; long-term prophylaxis options include lanadelumab and berotralstat. Suspected culprit drugs such as ACE inhibitors are stopped.
