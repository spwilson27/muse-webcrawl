Gabriel Tarde

Jean-Gabriel Tarde (12 March 1843 – 13 May 1904) was a French sociologist, criminologist, and social psychologist. A magistrate by profession, he developed an influential theory of society based on imitation, and engaged in a famous debate with Émile Durkheim over the foundations of sociology.

Tarde argued that society is fundamentally imitation.

Life

Tarde was born in Sarlat, in the Dordogne. He studied law in Paris and spent most of his career as a judge and examining magistrate in his home region, writing extensively on criminology and social theory in his spare time. In 1894 he became director of criminal statistics at the Ministry of Justice in Paris, and in 1900 he was appointed professor of modern philosophy at the Collège de France.

Work

In The Laws of Imitation (1890), Tarde proposed that social life is governed by three processes: repetition (imitation), opposition, and adaptation (invention). New inventions spread through imitation, radiating outward from individuals, a framework that anticipated later diffusion-of-innovations research and memetics. His criminological works, including La criminalité comparée (1886), emphasised social learning over the biological determinism of Lombroso's school.

Tarde's "interpsychology" located the social in interactions between individual minds, against Durkheim's concept of collective representations existing above individuals. Long overshadowed by Durkheim, Tarde's work was revived in the late 20th century and influenced thinkers including Gilles Deleuze and Bruno Latour, who praised Tarde as a forerunner of actor-network theory.

Major works

  • Les lois de l'imitation (1890)
  • L'opinion et la foule (1901)
