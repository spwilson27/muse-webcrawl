An active electronically scanned array (AESA) is a type of phased-array radar whose antenna consists of many small, solid-state transmit/receive (T/R) modules, each able to generate and receive its own signal. By controlling the phase of the signal at each element, the radar beam can be steered electronically in microseconds without moving the antenna, and multiple beams can be formed for different tasks nearly simultaneously.

Advantages

Compared with mechanically scanned radars and with passive electronically scanned arrays (PESA), in which a single transmitter feeds many radiating elements, an AESA offers:

  • Agility — beams can be redirected almost instantaneously, interleaving air search, tracking, and ground mapping.
  • Reliability and graceful degradation — failure of individual modules only slightly reduces performance, unlike failure of a single central transmitter.
  • Low probability of intercept — energy can be spread across frequencies and directions, making the radar harder to detect.
  • Resistance to jamming — adaptive beamforming can place nulls toward jammers.

Applications

AESA radars are standard on modern fighter aircraft — such as the AN/APG-77 on the F-22 and the AN/APG-81 on the F-35 — and increasingly on naval vessels, where gallium nitride (GaN) modules have improved power and efficiency, as in the AN/SPY-6 air and missile defence radar. They are also used in airborne early warning, ground-based air defence, weather monitoring, and automotive radar.

History

Phased-array principles date to the Second World War, and large ground-based arrays appeared during the Cold War. Practical airborne AESA awaited affordable solid-state T/R modules, arriving operationally in the 1990s and 2000s, beginning with systems such as Japan's J/APG-1 for the F-2 fighter.
