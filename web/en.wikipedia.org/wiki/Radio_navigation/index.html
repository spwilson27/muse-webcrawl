Radio navigation

Radio navigation or radionavigation is the application of radio frequencies to determine a position on the Earth's surface, used by ships and aircraft. Radio location systems allow navigation in all weather and over long distances, and formed the basis of air and sea navigation for much of the 20th century before being largely superseded by satellite navigation.

Principles

Systems work by measuring one or more properties of signals from transmitters at known locations: the bearing (direction) of the signal, the difference in arrival time of signals from paired stations (hyperbolic navigation), or the round-trip time of a pulse (as in radar and distance measuring equipment). A position is fixed by combining two or more such measurements.

Historical systems

Early systems included radio direction finding and non-directional beacons (NDB) used with automatic direction finders (ADF). The Second World War produced Gee and its successors, and the long-range hyperbolic system LORAN, later modernised as LORAN-C. Post-war aviation adopted VOR (VHF omnidirectional range) and DME (distance measuring equipment) as standard short-range aids, together with instrument landing systems (ILS) for approach guidance. Omega provided worldwide coverage at very low frequency until 1997.

Satellite navigation

Global navigation satellite systems (GNSS) such as GPS, GLONASS, Galileo and BeiDou apply radio navigation principles from orbit, measuring signal travel times from satellites to compute positions to within metres. Despite this, ground-based radio aids are retained as backups in aviation and maritime navigation.
