Emigration

Emigration is the act of leaving one's resident country with the intention of settling elsewhere, permanently or for a long period. It is the outward counterpart of immigration: the same movement that constitutes emigration from the country of origin constitutes immigration into the destination country. People who emigrate are called emigrants or émigrés when political exile is implied.

Causes

Emigration is driven by "push" and "pull" factors. Push factors include war, persecution, famine, unemployment, political instability and natural disaster. Pull factors include better employment opportunities, higher wages, political and religious freedom, education, and family reunification. Historically, mass emigrations include the departure of Europeans for the Americas and Australasia in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the Irish emigration during and after the Great Famine, and the Jewish emigration from Europe before and during the Second World War.

Effects

Emigration can relieve population pressure and unemployment in the sending country and generate remittances, but it may also drain skilled workers — the so-called "brain drain". Destination countries gain labour and cultural diversity but must manage integration. Diaspora communities formed by emigrants often maintain strong cultural and economic ties to their homelands.

Legal aspects

The freedom to emigrate is recognised in Article 13 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which provides that everyone has the right to leave any country, including their own. Some states historically restricted emigration, notably the Soviet Union and other Eastern Bloc countries, whose exit-visa regimes made would-be emigrants — such as the Soviet "refuseniks" — a prominent human-rights issue.
