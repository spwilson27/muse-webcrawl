Henry Martyn Leland (February 16, 1843 – March 26, 1932) was an American machinist, inventor, engineer, and automotive entrepreneur. He founded the two premier American luxury automotive marques, Cadillac and Lincoln. He was also a leading figure in the campaign for precision manufacturing and the interchangeability of parts in the automobile industry.

Early life and career

Leland was born in Danville, Vermont, the youngest child in a farming family. He learned the machinist's trade at the Brown & Sharpe Manufacturing Company in Providence, Rhode Island, working on sewing machines, and later at the Colt Armory in Hartford, Connecticut, where he absorbed the discipline of armory practice — the manufacture of parts to such close tolerances that any part would fit any assembly. During the American Civil War he worked at the Springfield Armory.

In 1890 Leland moved to Detroit and founded Leland & Faulconer Manufacturing, a machine shop producing engines and transmissions. The firm built the single-cylinder engine used in the curved-dash Oldsmobile, and Leland's improvements substantially raised its output. When the directors of the Henry Ford Company parted ways with Ford in 1902, they called in Leland to appraise the plant; instead he persuaded them to reorganize using his engine, and the company was renamed the Cadillac Automobile Company, after the founder of Detroit, Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac.

Cadillac and precision manufacturing

Under Leland, Cadillac became famous for precision. In 1908 Cadillac won the Dewar Trophy in England after three cars were disassembled, their parts scrambled, and the cars reassembled from the mixed parts and driven — a celebrated demonstration of full interchangeability. Cadillac introduced the electric self-starter (developed by Charles Kettering) in 1912, eliminating the dangerous hand crank, and in 1915 marketed the first mass-produced V8 engine. General Motors bought Cadillac in 1909, and Leland stayed on as its head until 1917, when he left after a dispute with William C. Durant over producing Liberty aircraft engines for the First World War effort.

Lincoln

Leland and his son Wilfred formed the Lincoln Motor Company in 1917 to build Liberty V12 aircraft engines. After the war the company turned to luxury automobiles, launching the Lincoln Model L in 1920. Postwar recession and a disputed tax assessment forced the company into receivership, and in 1922 it was purchased by Henry Ford's Ford Motor Company, where Edsel Ford developed it into Cadillac's great rival. Leland remained president for a time but was dismissed in June 1922 after clashing with Ford.

Death and legacy

Leland died in Detroit in 1932. He is remembered as "the Master of Precision" and one of the fathers of the modern luxury car; both Cadillac and Lincoln endure as flagship marques of General Motors and Ford respectively.
