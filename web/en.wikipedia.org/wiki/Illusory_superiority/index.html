Illusory superiority

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: better-than-average effect diagram]

Illusory superiority is a cognitive bias whereby a person overestimates their own qualities and abilities relative to other people. It is also known as the better-than-average effect, superiority bias, and — after the fictional Minnesota town where "all the children are above average" — the Lake Wobegon effect. The term was coined by Van Yperen and Buunk in 1991.

Findings

The effect has been demonstrated across many domains. In a classic 1981 study by Ola Svenson, large majorities of both Swedish and American drivers rated themselves as more skilful and less risky than the median driver — a statistical impossibility for most of them. Similar results appear for intelligence, memory, health, work performance and teaching ability, and in surveys of college students, prisoners, academics and business managers.

Explanations

Proposed explanations include selective recruitment of evidence (people recall their own successes more readily), egocentrism (weighing one's own performance more heavily than others'), focalism, and the mental noise of judging others from limited information. The effect reverses for tasks perceived as very difficult — the worse-than-average effect — and is related to the Dunning–Kruger effect, optimism bias and self-serving bias.
