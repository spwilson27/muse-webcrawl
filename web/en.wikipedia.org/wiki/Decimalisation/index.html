Decimalisation

[Image: Public information poster explaining the new decimal coins before Decimal Day, 1971]

Decimalisation or decimalization (see spelling differences) is the conversion of a system of currency or of weights and measures to units that are related by powers of 10. Most countries have decimalised their currencies, dividing the main unit into 100 subunits; the most notable historical case was the replacement of the British and Irish £sd system—pounds, shillings, and pence, with 240 pence to the pound—by a pound divided into 100 new pence.

Decimal Day

The United Kingdom and Ireland decimalised on 15 February 1971, "Decimal Day". The transition was planned for years by the Decimal Currency Board, with new 5p, 10p, and 50p coins issued in advance and a large public information campaign. The pound itself kept its value; a shilling became 5 new pence, and the old penny became 0.4 new pence. Australia (1966), New Zealand (1967), and South Africa (1961) had earlier switched from pounds to decimal dollars or rand.

History

Russia under Peter the Great was the first country to adopt a decimal currency, in 1704, with the rouble divided into 100 kopecks. The United States adopted decimal coinage with the dollar in 1792, and revolutionary France introduced the franc of 100 centimes in 1795. By the late 20th century, nearly all currencies were decimal; rare non-decimal exceptions historically included currencies divided unevenly, such as the Malagasy ariary (divided into five iraimbilanja), an unusual non-power-of-ten subdivision.
