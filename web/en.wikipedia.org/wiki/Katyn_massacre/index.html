The Katyn massacre was a series of mass executions of nearly 22,000 Polish military and police officers, prisoners of war, and members of the intelligentsia carried out by the Soviet secret police, the NKVD, in April and May 1940. The massacre takes its name from the Katyn forest in western Russia, where some of the largest mass graves were first discovered by German forces in 1943.

Background

Under the secret protocol of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, the Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September 1939, taking some 250,000 Polish prisoners. Most enlisted men were released or handed over, but around 15,000 officers were kept in three special camps at Kozelsk, Starobelsk, and Ostashkov, alongside thousands of police officers and civilian prisoners held elsewhere. On 5 March 1940, NKVD chief Lavrentiy Beria proposed to Joseph Stalin the execution of the officer prisoners and thousands of other detainees; the Politburo approved the proposal the same day.

The executions

Beginning in early April 1940, the prisoners were transported in groups to execution sites. Officers from Kozelsk were shot in the Katyn forest near Smolensk; those from Starobelsk near Kharkiv; and those from Ostashkov near Kalinin (Tver). Police and civilians were murdered at additional sites, including Bykivnia near Kyiv. Victims were typically shot in the back of the head with German-manufactured pistols and buried in mass graves. The Polish surgeon and general Józef Kozłowski was among the few whose bodies were later identified; in total roughly 21,800–22,000 people were killed.

Discovery and the "Katyn lie"

Nazi Germany announced the discovery of the Katyn graves in April 1943 and blamed the Soviet Union; Moscow in turn blamed the Germans, claiming the murders occurred in 1941. The revelation ruptured relations between the Soviet Union and the Polish government-in-exile. The Western Allies, unwilling to jeopardise the alliance with Stalin, publicly accepted the Soviet version, and Soviet prosecutors attempted unsuccessfully to pin Katyn on Germany at the Nuremberg trials. For decades the official Soviet position—the "Katyn lie"—was maintained across the Eastern Bloc.

Acknowledgement

In April 1990, during glasnost, Mikhail Gorbachev formally admitted NKVD responsibility and expressed "profound regret"; in 1992 Boris Yeltsin's government handed Poland copies of the execution orders, including Beria's 5 March 1940 memorandum. In 2010 the Russian State Duma adopted a statement acknowledging that the crime was committed on the direct orders of Stalin and other Soviet officials. Katyn remains a potent symbol in Polish national memory and in Polish–Russian relations.
