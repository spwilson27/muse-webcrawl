Guthrum (Old Norse: Guðrum; died c. 890) was a Danish Viking king who led the Great Heathen Army's successors in their war against the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Wessex, and who, after his defeat by Alfred the Great at the Battle of Edington in 878, accepted baptism and became king of East Anglia under the Christian name Æthelstan. He is regarded as the founder of the Danelaw's settled order in eastern England.

War with Alfred

Guthrum emerged as a leader of the Danish "summer army" that reinforced the Great Heathen Army in 871. In January 878 he launched a midwinter surprise attack on Chippenham that nearly destroyed the West Saxon court, driving Alfred into hiding in the Somerset marshes at Athelney. Alfred rallied his forces and defeated Guthrum decisively at Edington (Ethandun) in May 878; the besieged Danes surrendered after a fortnight.

Treaty and kingship

By the Treaty of Wedmore (878), Guthrum was baptised with Alfred as his godfather, withdrew from Wessex, and took up rule in East Anglia, minting coins as Æthelstan. A later agreement — the Treaty of Alfred and Guthrum (c. 886–890) — fixed the boundary between English and Danish law along the Thames, the Lea, and Watling Street, and regularised relations between the two peoples. Unlike his earlier campaigns, Guthrum's decade as a settled king was largely peaceful; he died around 890 and was buried, by tradition, at Hadleigh in Suffolk.

Legacy

Guthrum personifies the transition of the Vikings in England from raiders to rulers: within two generations his Danish settlers were integrated into the emerging kingdom of England, though Scandinavian place-names and customs in the Danelaw endured.
