California effect

The California effect is the shift of a large, wealthy jurisdiction's environmental and consumer regulations toward stricter standards that then spread to other jurisdictions, as producers adopt the strict rules across their whole output rather than maintaining separate product lines. The term was coined by political scientist David Vogel in his book Trading Up (1995).

Mechanism

Because California is one of the largest consumer markets in the world, manufacturers of cars, chemicals and consumer goods often find it cheaper to comply with California's standards everywhere. Other U.S. states may also adopt California's rules directly — under the Clean Air Act, states may choose California's vehicle emission standards instead of the federal ones — and foreign producers conform in order to keep access to the market.

Examples

Notable examples include California's motor vehicle emission and greenhouse-gas standards, its Proposition 65 chemical-warning law, appliance efficiency rules, and privacy legislation whose practices were adopted nationally. A European analogue, in which EU rules spread worldwide through market size, is known as the Brussels effect.

Criticism

Critics argue the effect lets one jurisdiction effectively set national policy without national deliberation, and can raise costs; supporters reply that it overcomes races to the bottom and raises standards where federal action is stalled.
