Cooperative

[Image: members of an agricultural cooperative loading produce together]

A cooperative (co-op) is an autonomous association of persons united voluntarily to meet common economic, social or cultural needs through a jointly owned and democratically controlled enterprise. Members — who may be consumers, workers, producers or residents — own the business, typically on a one-member-one-vote basis, and share its surpluses.

Forms and scale

Forms include consumer co-ops (retail societies descended from the Rochdale Pioneers of 1844, whose Rochdale Principles still guide the movement), worker cooperatives (the Mondragon federation in Spain's Basque Country is the largest), agricultural co-ops (Fonterra, dairy and wine co-ops), credit unions and housing cooperatives. The International Co-operative Alliance (1895) co-ordinates a movement claiming a billion members worldwide. Economists study co-ops as an alternative governance form within a Market economy: advantages in trust, local knowledge and member commitment against difficulties raising capital and making fast decisions; their survival rates compare surprisingly well with conventional firms in several studies.
