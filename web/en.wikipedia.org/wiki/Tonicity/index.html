Tonicity

FieldCell biology, physiology
CategoriesHypertonic, hypotonic, isotonic
Related conceptOsmolarity; osmotic pressure

In biology, tonicity is a measure of the effective osmotic pressure gradient of two solutions separated by a selectively permeable membrane. Unlike osmolarity, which counts all dissolved solutes, tonicity considers only solutes that cannot freely cross the membrane ("effective osmoles"), because only these determine the direction of water movement and hence changes in cell volume.

Categories

A hypertonic solution has a greater concentration of effective solutes than the cell interior, so water leaves the cell, which shrinks (crenation in red blood cells; plasmolysis in walled plant cells). A hypotonic solution has a lower effective solute concentration; water enters the cell, which swells and may lyse. An isotonic solution produces no net water movement, and cells retain their normal shape. Human plasma is about 285–295 mOsm/kg; 0.9% sodium chloride ("normal saline") is approximately isotonic with blood, whereas 5% dextrose is isotonic in the bag but behaves hypotonically once the glucose is metabolised.

Significance

Tonicity underlies the management of intravenous fluids, the preservation of blood products, the action of osmotherapy for raised intracranial pressure, and everyday phenomena from the crisping of lettuce in water to food preservation by salting. Cells without walls regulate volume with ion pumps such as Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, and specialised organs — kidneys in vertebrates, contractile vacuoles in protists — defend tonicity at the organism scale.
