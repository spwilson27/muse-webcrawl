Deontology

Deontology
FieldNormative ethics
Core claimMorality is a matter of duties and rules; some acts are required or forbidden regardless of consequences
Key figureImmanuel Kant
[Image: An engraved stone plaque of the Ten Commandments, emblem of duty-based morality]

Deontology (from Greek deon, duty) is the normative ethical position that morality is determined by duties and rules: some actions — lying, killing the innocent — are wrong in themselves, even when they would produce better outcomes. Its paradigm is Immanuel Kant's ethics of the Categorical imperative, where persons must always be treated as ends, never merely as means.

Forms and debates

Deontological constraints structure law and rights-talk (rights as 'trumps', Nozick); the view must answer what to do when duties conflict (W. D. Ross proposed prima facie duties weighed by judgement) and the 'paradox of deontology' — why not violate a constraint to minimise identical violations by others. Divine-command theory is a theological deontology; contractualism (Scanlon) grounds duties in justifiability to others.
