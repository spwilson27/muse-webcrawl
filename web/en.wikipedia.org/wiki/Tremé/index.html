Tremé (French pronunciation: [tʁeme]) is a neighborhood in the city of New Orleans, Louisiana. Founded in the late 18th century, it is one of the oldest neighborhoods in the city, and was the site of the earliest substantial settlement of free people of color in the United States. It is often considered the oldest African-American neighborhood in the country and a vital center of the city's African-American and Creole culture, especially music.

History

The land was part of a plantation owned by Claude Tremé, subdivided and sold beginning in the 1790s. Many buyers were free people of color and newly arrived refugees from Saint-Domingue (Haiti). Its Congo Square (now within Louis Armstrong Park) was the 19th-century gathering place where enslaved Africans were permitted to congregate on Sundays to trade, play music and dance — gatherings widely regarded as foundational to the development of jazz.

Culture

Tremé is home to landmarks including the St. Augustine Church (established 1841 by free people of color), the Backstreet Cultural Museum, the New Orleans African American Museum, and Louis Armstrong Park. The neighborhood is famous for brass band traditions, second line parades, and the Mardi Gras Indians. In the 1960s, construction of the elevated Interstate 10 expressway over North Claiborne Avenue destroyed much of a thriving commercial corridor — a lasting symbol of harmful urban renewal. The neighborhood also gave its name to the HBO television series Treme (2010–2013), which dramatized its post-Hurricane Katrina recovery.
