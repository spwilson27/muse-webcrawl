The Peshtigo Fire was a large forest fire on 8 October 1871, in northeastern Wisconsin, United States, that burned around 1.2 million acres and killed an estimated 1,500 to 2,500 people. It is the deadliest wildfire in American history. The fire destroyed the town of Peshtigo entirely, along with a dozen other communities, but it was overshadowed in the press by the Great Chicago Fire, which began the same night some 250 miles to the south.

Background and course

The summer of 1871 was exceptionally dry in the Great Lakes region. Land clearing for farms and railroads, logging slash, and small burns were widespread. On the evening of 8 October, a strong low-pressure system brought high winds that fanned the scattered fires into a firestorm — survivors described a tornado-like wall of flame, radiant heat igniting buildings across streets, and temperatures sufficient to melt metal. In Peshtigo, residents fled to the Peshtigo River, immersing themselves for hours; even so, hundreds died in the town alone, many unidentifiable and buried in a mass grave.

Aftermath

Relief efforts for Peshtigo competed with those for Chicago. The town was rebuilt, and a wooden church tabernacle carried through the fire by its pastor became a celebrated relic; the Peshtigo Fire Museum, housed in a former church, preserves artefacts and a cemetery memorial to the unknown dead. Historians have speculated — without solid evidence — that fragments of Comet Biela might have ignited the simultaneous fires in Wisconsin, Michigan and Chicago; meteorologists attribute them instead to weather, drought and human-set fires.
