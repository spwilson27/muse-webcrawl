Chivalry

Knights at a medieval tournament

Chivalry, or the chivalric code, is an informal and varying code of conduct developed in Europe between 1170 and 1220. It was associated with the medieval Christian institution of knighthood; knights' and gentlemen's behaviours were governed by chivalrous social codes. The term derives from the Old French chevalerie, from chevalier ("horseman", "knight").

The code

The chivalric code combined martial virtues — prowess, courage, loyalty to one's lord, and largesse — with Christian virtues such as mercy, humility, and protection of the weak, and later with the ideals of courtly love toward noblewomen. Contemporary formulations varied; modern summaries often cite the "Ten Commandments of Chivalry" drawn from Léon Gautier's 1891 study, including defending the Church, obeying one's superiors, keeping one's word, and being generous to all.

Development and decline

Chivalry was celebrated in the literature of the High Middle Ages: the chansons de geste, Arthurian romance (in which Lancelot and Gawain embodied contrasting models), and troubadour lyric. From the 14th century, as professional infantry and gunpowder warfare eroded the knight's battlefield dominance, chivalry became increasingly ceremonial, expressed in orders of knighthood, heraldry, and tournaments, while its ethical vocabulary survived into later ideals of the gentleman and the officer.
