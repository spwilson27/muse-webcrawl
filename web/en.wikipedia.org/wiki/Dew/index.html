Dew

Dew is water in the form of droplets that appears on thin, exposed objects in the morning or evening due to condensation. As the exposed surface cools by radiating its heat, atmospheric moisture condenses at a rate greater than that at which it can evaporate, resulting in the formation of water droplets. When temperatures are low enough, dew takes the form of ice; this form is called frost.

Dew droplets beading on grass after a clear, calm night.

Formation

Dew forms when a surface loses heat by infrared radiation faster than it receives it, cooling to the dew point of the surrounding air. Ideal conditions are clear skies, little wind, and moist air near the ground. Because grass, leaves, car roofs and other thin objects cool quickly and are poorly connected to the ground's stored heat, they collect dew most readily. Dew does not "fall" like rain; it condenses in place, though a small contribution can come from moisture rising from warm soil.

Significance

Although dew yields only small amounts of water, it is an important moisture source for plants, insects and small animals in arid and Mediterranean climates, and some organisms (such as the Namib desert beetle) harvest dew or fog for survival. Dew on foliage can promote fungal disease in crops, while heavy dew was traditionally read as a sign of fair weather, since it forms under clear, calm conditions.

Dew point and measurement

The dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure, for saturation and condensation to occur; it is a standard measure of atmospheric humidity. Dew can be estimated with drosometers and is reported in agrometeorology for disease forecasting.
