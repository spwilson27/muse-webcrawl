Joyous Entry

A Joyous Entry (Dutch: Blijde Inkomste; French: Joyeuse Entrée) was the ceremonial formal entry of a ruler into a city in medieval and early modern Brabant and Flanders, typically accompanied by festivities, pageantry, and the swearing of an oath to uphold the city's liberties. The term is especially attached to the Joyous Entry Charter of 1356, the constitutional document granted to the Duchy of Brabant whose guarantees limited ducal power for centuries.

The Charter of 1356

On the death of Duke John III of Brabant in 1355, his daughter Joanna succeeded him. To secure recognition against rival claimants, Joanna and her husband Wenceslaus of Luxembourg entered Brussels and on 3 January 1356 swore to a charter by which the dukes promised to maintain the integrity of the duchy, to make no war, treaties, or coinage changes without consent of the towns and nobility, to appoint only native-born officials, and to govern according to local law. Most remarkably, the charter stated that if the duke violated its terms, subjects were released from their obedience until the wrong was righted — a medieval right of resistance that made the document a celebrated symbol of constitutional liberty in the Low Countries.

Ceremonial entries

Each new duke of Brabant thereafter swore to the charter at his or her Joyous Entry into the principal cities, amid elaborate processions, triumphal arches, tableaux vivants, and speeches by the guilds. Similar entries marked accessions and state visits throughout the Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands; the entries of Charles V, Philip II (1549), and the Archdukes Albert and Isabella (1599) were famous spectacles recorded in festival books, with Rubens designing the decorations for the entry of Cardinal-Infante Ferdinand into Antwerp in 1635.

Later history

The charter was invoked in the Brabant Revolution against Joseph II in 1789, whose reforms were denounced as violations of the Joyous Entry. The tradition survives in modern Belgium, where kings swear to the constitution and make ceremonial "joyous entries" into provincial capitals after their accession.
