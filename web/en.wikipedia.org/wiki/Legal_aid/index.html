Legal aid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legal aid is the provision of assistance to people who are unable to afford legal representation and access to the court system. It operationalises equality before the law: without it, formal rights are hollow for those who cannot pay. Legal aid covers advice, assistance and representation in criminal and civil matters, funded by states through salaried services, fee-paid private lawyers ("judicare"), clinics and pro bono obligations.

Systems and strains

The modern British scheme (Legal Aid and Advice Act 1949) became a pillar of the post-war welfare state, later narrowed by means and merits tests and by the LASPO cuts of 2012 in England and Wales, which removed most private family, housing, welfare and immigration advice from scope — followed by documented rises in unrepresented litigants ("litigants in person") and "legal aid deserts". International standards (the UN Principles and Guidelines on Access to Legal Aid, 2012) call for early, independent and adequately funded systems; criminal legal aid is additionally anchored in fair-trial treaties (ECHR Article 6(3)(c)). Research generally finds early legal help cost-effective by preventing escalation into courts, eviction and state costs.
