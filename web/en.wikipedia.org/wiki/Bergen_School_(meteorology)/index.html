The Bergen School of Meteorology was a group of meteorologists centred on the Geophysical Institute in Bergen, Norway, led by Vilhelm Bjerknes, which between about 1917 and the 1930s revolutionised weather forecasting. The school developed the theory of weather fronts and the polar front model of the mid-latitude cyclone, and established the practice — now universal — of analysing weather maps in terms of air masses and frontal surfaces.

Origins

Vilhelm Bjerknes, founder of modern theoretical meteorology, moved to Bergen in 1917 to direct geophysics there and, at the Norwegian government's request, to organise a weather service for western Norway. Wartime shortages of food and fuel made accurate forecasts urgent for fishermen and farmers. With few upper-air observations available, the Bergen group maintained an unusually dense network of surface stations and squeezed far more from surface data than conventional practice.

Fronts and the cyclone model

In 1918 Bjerknes's son Jacob noticed that surface winds converged along lines resembling battle fronts — the term "front" was deliberately borrowed from the military language of the First World War. In the landmark 1919 paper "On the Structure of Moving Cyclones", Jacob Bjerknes described the warm front, cold front and warm sector of a typical depression. With Halvor Solberg and Tor Bergeron he extended this into the polar front theory: mid-latitude cyclones form as waves on the boundary between polar and tropical air, grow, occlude and decay through a characteristic life cycle. Bergeron's 1928 dissertation added a three-dimensional analysis of air masses and fronts, and his later work on precipitation physics (the Bergeron process) grew from the school's emphasis on cloud processes.

Methods and influence

The Bergen School's "inductive" method demanded detailed, physically coherent synoptic analysis rather than formulaic rules, and its training produced a diaspora of influential scientists: Carl-Gustaf Rossby and Erik Palmén carried the methods to the United States and Finland, and frontal analysis became the standard operating method of weather services worldwide from the 1930s. The school's ideas underpin the Norwegian cyclone model still taught to forecasters today, even though modern numerical prediction has superseded its purely diagnostic technique.
