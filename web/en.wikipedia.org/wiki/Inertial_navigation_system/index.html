Inertial navigation system

An inertial navigation system (INS) is a navigation device that uses motion sensors (accelerometers), rotation sensors (gyroscopes) and a computer to continuously calculate by dead reckoning the position, orientation and velocity of a moving object without the need for external references. INS are used on vehicles such as aircraft, spacecraft, submarines, missiles and ships, and in miniaturised form in smartphones and game controllers. Their main drawback is that errors accumulate over time, so they are often combined with other navigation aids such as GPS.

Principles

The core of an INS is an inertial measurement unit (IMU) containing three accelerometers and three gyroscopes mounted on orthogonal axes. The gyroscopes track orientation; the accelerometers measure specific force, from which gravity is subtracted and the remainder integrated once to give velocity and twice to give position. Because position comes from a double integration, small constant sensor biases grow into large position errors — roughly quadratically (for a constant acceleration bias) with time — hence long-endurance systems require extremely accurate sensors or periodic external fixes.

Gimballed and strapdown systems

Early systems mounted the IMU on a mechanically gimballed platform stabilised to remain aligned with a fixed frame. Modern systems are usually strapdown: sensors are fixed to the vehicle body and the computer performs the coordinate transformations numerically, using quaternion or direction-cosine algorithms. Strapdown designs dominate because they are smaller, cheaper and more reliable. Sensor technologies range from spinning-mass and ring laser gyroscopes and fibre-optic gyros to low-cost MEMS devices.

History

Inertial guidance was pioneered in rocketry: the German V-2 used simple gyroscopic guidance, and post-war work by Charles Stark Draper's laboratory at MIT produced the guidance for the Titan and Polaris missiles and the Apollo spacecraft. Airliners adopted INS in the late 1960s (beginning with the Boeing 747 era), greatly improving oceanic navigation before satellite navigation became available.

Applications

INS remains essential where external signals are unavailable or untrusted: submarine navigation, spacecraft attitude determination, guided weapons, surveying, borehole drilling, autonomous vehicles and pedestrian navigation. Sensor fusion (for example with a Kalman filter blending GPS and INS data) is the standard remedy for drift.
