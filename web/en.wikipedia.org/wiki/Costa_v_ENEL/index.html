Costa v ENEL

Flaminio Costa v E.N.E.L.
CaseCase 6/64
Decided15 July 1964
CourtEuropean Court of Justice
KeywordsPrimacy (supremacy) of EU law

Flaminio Costa v E.N.E.L. [1964] ECR 585, Case 6/64, is a landmark judgment of the European Court of Justice establishing the primacy (or supremacy) of European Community law over the laws of the member states. The Court held that member states had definitively limited their sovereign rights by creating the Community, and that a law enacted by a member state after the Treaty of Rome could not prevail over Community law. Together with Van Gend en Loos (1963), which established direct effect, Costa forms one of the constitutional foundations of the European legal order.

Facts

Flaminio Costa, a lawyer and shareholder in the Milan electricity company Edisonvolta, refused to pay an electricity bill of 1,925 lire issued by ENEL, the newly nationalised Italian electricity monopoly created by a statute of 1962. Costa argued before the Milan small-claims court (the giudice conciliatore) that the nationalisation statute breached the Treaty of Rome, including its competition and state-monopoly provisions. The Milan court made a preliminary reference to the European Court of Justice. The Italian government argued that the reference was inadmissible because the national court was obliged to apply the later Italian statute in any event, under the ordinary principle that a later law overrides an earlier one (lex posterior derogat legi priori).

Judgment

The Court rejected Italy's arguments. It reasoned that "[b]y contrast with ordinary international treaties, the EEC Treaty has created its own legal system which, on the entry into force of the Treaty, became an integral part of the legal systems of the Member States and which their courts are bound to apply." By creating a Community of unlimited duration with its own institutions, personality and legal capacity, the member states had "limited their sovereign rights, albeit within limited fields, and have thus created a body of law which binds both their nationals and themselves". It followed, the Court held, that "[t]he law stemming from the Treaty, an independent source of law, could not, because of its special and original nature, be overridden by domestic legal provisions, however framed, without being deprived of its character as Community law and without the legal basis of the Community itself being called into question." A subsequent unilateral act incompatible with the Community system could therefore not prevail.

Significance

The primacy principle was confirmed and extended in later cases: Internationale Handelsgesellschaft (1970) held that EU law prevails even over fundamental rights provisions of national constitutions (while developing EU-level rights protection), and Simmenthal (1978) held that every national court must immediately disapply national law conflicting with directly effective EU law, without waiting for its legislature or constitutional court. National constitutional courts accepted primacy only conditionally — Germany's Federal Constitutional Court in the Solange line of cases and its 2020 PSPP judgment, which controversially declared an EU act and a Court of Justice judgment ultra vires, illustrate the continuing tension. The primacy principle was recorded in a declaration annexed to the Treaty of Lisbon rather than in the treaty text itself.
