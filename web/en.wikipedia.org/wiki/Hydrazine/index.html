Hydrazine

Hydrazine is an inorganic compound with the chemical formula N2H4. It is a simple pnictogen hydride, a colourless, flammable liquid with an ammonia-like odour. Hydrazine is highly toxic and dangerously unstable unless handled in solution as hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O).

Properties and structure

Hydrazine consists of two NH2 groups joined by a single N–N bond; each nitrogen is pyramidal. It is miscible with water, melts at about 2 °C and boils at about 114 °C. It is a weak base and a powerful reducing agent, and its combustion to nitrogen and water releases considerable energy.

Production and uses

Industrial production uses processes such as the Olin Raschig process, in which ammonia is oxidised by sodium hypochlorite via chloramine. Hydrazine's largest uses are as a chemical intermediate — in agricultural chemicals, pharmaceuticals, blowing agents for polymer foams, and as an oxygen scavenger in boiler water treatment. Hydrazine and its derivatives are important rocket propellants: hydrazine itself is used as a monopropellant over catalysts in spacecraft thrusters, and unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH) and monomethylhydrazine (MMH) serve as hypergolic fuels with nitrogen tetroxide. Hydrazine powered the German Me 163 rocket fighter of the Second World War (as part of C-Stoff) and remains common in satellites and probes.

Safety

Hydrazine is corrosive, a skin and respiratory sensitiser, and a suspected carcinogen; exposure limits are set very low, and propellant handling requires protective "SCAPE" suits. Its decomposition is catalysed by many metals, and anhydrous hydrazine can detonate.
