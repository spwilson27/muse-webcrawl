Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831) was a German philosopher and a major figure in German idealism. His comprehensive system sought to account for the whole of reality and history as the unfolding self-realisation of Spirit (Geist) through a dialectical process in which contradictions are overcome and preserved in higher unities (Aufhebung). His account of the master–slave dialectic, alienation, and the historicity of consciousness has been profoundly influential across philosophy, theology, political theory, and aesthetics.

Life

Educated at the Tübinger Stift alongside Hölderlin and Schelling, Hegel worked as a tutor and then lecturer at Jena, where he completed The Phenomenology of Spirit (1807) as Napoleon's troops entered the city. After posts at Nuremberg and Heidelberg, he took the chair at the University of Berlin in 1818, lecturing on logic, philosophy of right, history, art, and religion until his death, probably from cholera, in 1831.

Thought

The Phenomenology traces consciousness from sense-certainty through self-consciousness — the famous master–slave (lordship and bondage) dialectic — to reason, spirit, and absolute knowing. The Science of Logic presents his ontology of concepts, and the Philosophy of Right (1820) develops his theory of ethical life (Sittlichkeit) across family, civil society, and the state. Hegel saw philosophy as "its own time apprehended in thoughts" and held that "the rational is actual, and the actual is rational."

Legacy

After his death his followers split into Right and Left (Young) Hegelians; the latter included Karl Marx, who famously claimed to turn Hegel's dialectic "right side up" into historical materialism. Hegel's influence extends through Kierkegaard, British idealism, pragmatism, existentialism, and contemporary work by thinkers such as Charles Taylor and Robert Brandom, though critics from Schopenhauer to Popper have attacked his system as obscure or authoritarian.
