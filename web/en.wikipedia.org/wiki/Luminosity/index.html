Luminosity

In astronomy, luminosity is the total amount of electromagnetic energy emitted per unit of time by a star, galaxy or other astronomical object. In SI units it is measured in watts; in astronomy it is often expressed in units of the solar luminosity, L☉ ≈ 3.828 × 1026 W. As a measure of radiant power, luminosity is distinct from a source's apparent brightness, which also depends on its distance.

Relation to magnitude

Luminosity is connected to absolute magnitude by a logarithmic relation: a difference of five magnitudes corresponds to a factor of 100 in luminosity. The Sun has an absolute bolometric magnitude of about +4.74. Luminosity can be derived from a measured flux and a known distance via the inverse-square law, L = 4πd2f.

Stellar luminosity

For a star radiating as a black body, luminosity follows the Stefan–Boltzmann law, L = 4πR2σTeff4, so it depends on the star's radius and effective temperature. For main-sequence stars luminosity scales steeply with mass, roughly L ∝ M3.5, which is why massive stars are short-lived. Observed stellar luminosities range from about 10−4 L☉ for the faintest red dwarfs to several million L☉ for the most luminous supergiants, bounded by the Eddington limit at which radiation pressure would blow the star apart.

Other uses

In particle physics, luminosity measures the collision rate potential of an accelerator beam; in scattering theory and photometry the word is also used for related quantities, and "bolometric luminosity" denotes power integrated over all wavelengths.
