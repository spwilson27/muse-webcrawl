Median lethal dose

In toxicology, the median lethal dose, LD50 (abbreviation for "lethal dose, 50%"), is a measure of the lethal dose of a toxin, radiation or pathogen. The value is the dose required to kill half the members of a tested population after a specified test duration.

LD50 figures are frequently used as a general indicator of a substance's acute toxicity: a lower LD50 indicates a more lethal substance. Values are usually expressed in milligrams of substance per kilogram of body weight of the test animal, most often rats or mice, and by route of exposure (oral, dermal, inhalation).

History and criticism

The measure was introduced by J. W. Trevan in 1927. Animal LD50 tests have been criticised on ethical grounds and for limited predictive value for humans, and regulators have progressively adopted alternatives such as the fixed-dose procedure and in-vitro methods; the classic test guideline was deleted by the OECD in 2002.

Related measures

Related quantities include LC50 (median lethal concentration), TD50 (median toxic dose) and ED50 (median effective dose). The ratio of toxic to effective dose gives the therapeutic index.
