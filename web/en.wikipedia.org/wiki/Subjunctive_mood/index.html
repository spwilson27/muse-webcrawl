Subjunctive mood

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The subjunctive is a grammatical mood found in many languages, used to express various states of unreality such as doubt, possibility, necessity, desire, wishes, and statements contrary to fact. It contrasts with the indicative mood, which presents statements as factual. The subjunctive is highly developed in the Romance languages, where it is required in many subordinate clauses, and survives in a reduced form in English.

In English

Modern English has a limited subjunctive, mostly visible in the verb be and in the absence of third-person singular -s. Its principal uses are:

The present subjunctive appears in formal subordinate clauses after verbs of demand, recommendation, or necessity: "I insist that he be present"; "It is essential that she leave now." This usage remains standard in American English and more variable in British English, where should is often used instead.

The irrealis were appears in counterfactual wishes and conditions: "I wish I were taller"; "If I were you." Fossilised subjunctive forms survive in set phrases such as "God save the King", "be that as it may", "come what may", and "suffice it to say".

In other languages

In Spanish, French, Italian, and Portuguese, the subjunctive is a fully conjugated set of forms used after expressions of emotion, doubt, desire, and impersonal judgement, and its mastery is a major stage in second-language learning. Latin made extensive use of the subjunctive in subordinate clauses of purpose, result, and indirect command, a system from which the Romance uses partly descend. German has two subjunctive forms (Konjunktiv I and II), the latter commonly used for hypothetical statements and polite requests.
