Laparoscopy

Laparoscopy (from Ancient Greek λαπάρα (lapára) 'flank, side' and σκοπέω (skopéō) 'to see') is an operation performed in the abdomen or pelvis using small incisions (usually 0.5–1.5 cm) with the aid of a camera, and is the prototypical example of minimally invasive surgery. The laparoscope is a long fibre-optic camera system that allows the surgeon to view the operative field on a monitor. Operations performed with laparoscopy include appendectomy, cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal), hernia repair, and many gynaecological and colorectal procedures.

Procedure

The abdomen is usually insufflated with carbon dioxide gas to create a pneumoperitoneum, lifting the abdominal wall away from the internal organs to create a working space. Access is gained with a Veress needle or a direct (Hasson) entry, after which hollow ports called trocars are inserted. The laparoscope is passed through one port, and long instruments — graspers, scissors, electrocautery hooks, clip appliers and staplers — through the others. The surgeon operates while watching a video monitor. Single-incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS) and robot-assisted laparoscopy are modern variants.

History

Georg Kelling performed the first laparoscopic procedure in dogs in 1901, and Hans Christian Jacobaeus reported the first human cases in 1910. For decades the technique was used mainly diagnostically and in gynaecology; Raoul Palmer in Paris and Kurt Semm in Germany developed operative laparoscopy in the mid-20th century, Semm performing the first laparoscopic appendectomy in 1980. The laparoscopic cholecystectomy, performed by Erich Mühe in 1985 and popularised by Philippe Mouret and others in 1987–88, ignited the worldwide adoption of minimally invasive surgery.

Advantages and disadvantages

Compared with open surgery (laparotomy), laparoscopy generally offers less postoperative pain, smaller scars, reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stays and faster return to normal activities, with lower rates of wound infection and incisional hernia. Disadvantages include a longer learning curve, loss of direct tactile feedback and depth perception (partly offset by 3D camera systems), longer operating times for some procedures, and risks specific to the approach such as injury during blind entry, gas embolism and subcutaneous emphysema. Some situations — severe adhesions, uncontrolled bleeding, very large masses — may require conversion to open surgery.

Applications

  • General surgery: cholecystectomy, appendectomy, fundoplication, bariatric surgery, colectomy
  • Gynaecology: treatment of endometriosis, ovarian cystectomy, hysterectomy, tubal surgery
  • Urology: nephrectomy, prostatectomy
  • Diagnosis: staging of cancers, investigation of chronic abdominal pain and infertility
