Ficus religiosa

Ficus religiosa
Common names: sacred fig, bodhi tree, peepal, pipal, ashvattha
Family: Moraceae
Genus: Ficus
Native range: Indian subcontinent and Indochina

Ficus religiosa or sacred fig is a species of fig native to the Indian subcontinent and Indochina that belongs to Moraceae, the fig or mulberry family. It is also known as the bodhi tree, peepal tree, pipal tree, or ashvattha tree (in India and Nepal). The sacred fig is considered to have a religious significance in the three major religions that originated on the Indian subcontinent, Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism. Hindu and Jain ascetics consider the species to be sacred and often meditate under it. Gautama Buddha is believed to have attained enlightenment while meditating underneath a sacred fig — the famous Bodhi Tree at Bodh Gaya — and the species is the state tree of several Indian states.

Description

Ficus religiosa is a large, dry-season deciduous or semi-evergreen tree up to 30 metres (98 ft) tall with a trunk diameter of up to 3 metres (9.8 ft). The leaves are cordate (heart-shaped) in shape with a distinctive extended "drip tip"; they are 10–17 cm long and 8–12 cm broad, on a slender petiole 6–10 cm long, and shimmer and rustle in the slightest breeze. The small figs are 1–1.5 cm in diameter, green ripening to purple. Like other figs, it is pollinated by a species-specific fig wasp. The tree often begins life as an epiphyte, germinating in the crevice of a host tree or wall, and can live for many centuries.

Religious significance

In Buddhism, the Bodhi Tree at the Mahabodhi Temple in Bodh Gaya is held to be a direct descendant of the tree under which the Buddha attained enlightenment around the 5th century BCE; a sapling carried to Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE became the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi at Anuradhapura, the oldest living human-planted tree in the world with a known planting date (288 BCE). In Hinduism the tree is associated with Vishnu and with the trimurti, and its leaves and shade are used in worship; it appears in the Bhagavad Gita as the eternal ashvattha, the tree of life with roots above and branches below. Jains revere the species as the tree under which several tirthankaras attained omniscience.

Cultivation and uses

The sacred fig is widely planted as a shade and temple tree throughout South and Southeast Asia and as an ornamental in warm climates elsewhere. In traditional medicine, including Ayurveda, its bark, leaves, and figs have been used for a wide range of ailments. Because of its aggressive roots it is unsuitable for planting near buildings and pavements.
