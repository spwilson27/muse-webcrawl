Lex mercatoria

The lex mercatoria (from Latin for "merchant law"), often called the Law Merchant, is the body of commercial law used by merchants throughout Europe during the medieval period. It consisted of customary practices, usages, and rules that merchants applied to their dealings and enforced in their own courts, largely independent of national or feudal law.

Historical development

From the 11th century, the revival of long-distance trade — at the Champagne fairs, in the ports of the Mediterranean, and in the towns of the Hanseatic League — generated a shared commercial culture. Special courts, such as the English courts of piepowder (pieds poudrés, "dusty feet") at fairs and markets, resolved disputes quickly according to merchant custom rather than the slow formalities of the common law. The law merchant emphasised freedom of contract, good faith, negotiability of instruments, and speedy, equitable resolution.

Absorption into national law

From the 17th and 18th centuries, the law merchant was progressively incorporated into national legal systems: in England, Lord Mansfield integrated commercial custom into the common law through decisions on bills of exchange, insurance, and shipping; in France, Colbert's Ordonnance de commerce (1673) codified it. Doctrines of negotiable instruments, maritime law, and letters of credit descend from this tradition.

The "new lex mercatoria"

Modern scholars debate whether a revived, transnational body of commercial law — built from trade usages, standard contracts, the UNIDROIT Principles, the CISG, and international commercial arbitration — constitutes a new lex mercatoria operating beyond any single state. Critics question whether such rules form a true legal system or merely serve as interpretive aids and contract terms.
