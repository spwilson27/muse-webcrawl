Law of total probability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In probability theory, the law of total probability (also known as the law of total expectation's discrete counterpart and the method of conditioning) is a fundamental rule relating marginal probabilities to conditional probabilities. It expresses the total probability of an outcome that can be realised via several distinct events.

Statement

If {B1, …, Bn} is a finite or countably infinite partition of the sample space — that is, the events are pairwise disjoint and their union is the whole sample space — and each event Bi has nonzero probability, then for any event A of the same probability space:

P(A) = Σi P(A | Bi) P(Bi)

Applications

The law is frequently used to compute an unconditional probability by "conditioning" on which member of a partition occurs — for instance, the overall probability that a randomly manufactured item is defective, computed machine by machine. Combined with Bayes' theorem, it supplies the denominator in Bayesian updating. Its analogue for expected values is the law of total expectation.
