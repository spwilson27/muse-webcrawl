Cnut (Old Norse: Knútr; c. 990 – 12 November 1035), commonly known as Cnut the Great, was King of England, Denmark, and Norway — the ruler of the so-called North Sea Empire. The son of the Danish king Sweyn Forkbeard, Cnut completed the Danish conquest of England and ruled as a Christian monarch, lawgiver, and patron of the Church for nearly two decades.

Conquest of England

Cnut accompanied his father's invasion of England in 1013, and after Sweyn's death in 1014 he returned with a great fleet in 1015. Following the death of King Æthelred and the hard-fought campaign against Edmund Ironside, the kingdom was partitioned by treaty; when Edmund died in November 1016, Cnut became king of all England. He consolidated power by executing or exiling English magnates, marrying Æthelred's widow Emma of Normandy, dividing England into great earldoms (Wessex, Mercia, Northumbria, East Anglia), and paying off his invasion fleet with a huge geld.

King of three realms

Cnut succeeded his brother Harald as king of Denmark in 1018/1019 and in 1028 conquered Norway, installing his son Svein and his concubine Ælfgifu as regents — a rule that provoked rebellion and ended shortly after his death. In England he issued comprehensive law codes (drafted with Archbishop Wulfstan), endowed churches, made a pilgrimage to Rome in 1027 for the coronation of Emperor Conrad II, and styled himself "king of all England and of Denmark and of the Norwegians and of some of the Swedes".

Legacy and legend

Cnut died at Shaftesbury in 1035 and was buried at Winchester. His empire disintegrated among his sons Harold Harefoot, Harthacnut, and Svein. The famous story of Cnut commanding the tide — actually told of him demonstrating to flattering courtiers that kings have no power over nature — was first recorded by Henry of Huntingdon in the twelfth century.
