Natural deduction

Originators: Gerhard Gentzen, Stanisław Jaśkowski (1934)
Field: Proof theory, mathematical logic
Key feature: Introduction and elimination rules for each connective

Natural deduction is a kind of proof calculus for formal logic in which proofs are built from assumptions using inference rules that closely mirror informal mathematical reasoning. It was introduced independently in 1934 by Gerhard Gentzen and Stanisław Jaśkowski.

The system

For each logical connective there are two kinds of rules: introduction rules, which show how to prove a statement whose main connective it is (for example, to prove A → B, assume A and derive B), and elimination rules, which show how to use such a statement (from A → B and A, conclude B — modus ponens). Assumptions can be "discharged": a temporary assumption made to prove an implication or to reach a contradiction is no longer needed once the conclusion is drawn, giving natural deduction its characteristic tree-like derivations with scoped subproofs.

Significance

Gentzen designed natural deduction as a tool for studying consistency, and soon after developed the sequent calculus, for which he proved the cut-elimination theorem. Under the Curry–Howard correspondence, natural deduction proofs correspond to typed lambda-calculus terms, making the system fundamental in theoretical computer science; it is also the basis of the presentation of logic in influential textbooks such as Prawitz's Natural Deduction (1965) and of the Fitch-style proof systems widely taught to students.
