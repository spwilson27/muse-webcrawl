Takaaki Kajita

Takaaki Kajita (born 9 March 1959) is a Japanese physicist and the director of the Institute for Cosmic Ray Research at the University of Tokyo. He shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics with Arthur B. McDonald "for the discovery of neutrino oscillations, which shows that neutrinos have mass". Kajita presented the Super-Kamiokande collaboration's landmark 1998 result demonstrating that muon neutrinos produced in the atmosphere change flavour as they travel, resolving the atmospheric neutrino anomaly.

Biography

Kajita was born in Higashimatsuyama, Saitama Prefecture, and studied physics at Saitama University, graduating in 1981. He received his doctorate from the University of Tokyo in 1986, in the group of Masatoshi Koshiba, and worked on the Kamiokande proton-decay detector in the Kamioka mine. He joined the Institute for Cosmic Ray Research in 1988 and became its director in 2008. He has also led the KAGRA gravitational-wave observatory project — which connects his work to the wider search for the gravitational wave background and other signals.

Neutrino oscillations

The Kamiokande and later Super-Kamiokande detectors recorded fewer muon neutrinos from cosmic-ray interactions in the atmosphere than expected — the "atmospheric neutrino anomaly". In 1998 Kajita presented Super-Kamiokande data showing that the deficit depended on the distance the neutrinos had travelled through the Earth, exactly as expected if muon neutrinos oscillate into tau neutrinos en route. Oscillation requires neutrinos to have nonzero mass, contradicting the original Standard Model and providing the first evidence of physics beyond it. The discovery was recognised with the 2015 Nobel Prize; Kajita had earlier received, with Yoji Totsuka, the 2007–2008 Bruno Rossi Prize for the achievement.
