Devolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Devolution is the statutory delegation of powers from the central government of a sovereign state to govern at a subnational level, such as a regional, state or local level. It is a form of administrative decentralisation. Devolved territories have the power to make legislation relevant to the area; thus, in contrast to a federation, where the powers of the units are constitutionally guaranteed, devolved powers remain in law the property of the centre, which could in theory revoke them.

The best-known modern example is devolution within the United Kingdom: since 1999 Scotland has had its own Parliament, Wales a Senedd, and Northern Ireland an Assembly, each with varying legislative competences, while England has no separate parliament — the asymmetry that produces the “West Lothian question” about MPs from devolved nations voting on English matters. Spain's “State of Autonomies” and Italy's regional reforms are similarly devolutionary in structure, though constitutionally entrenched to differing degrees.

Devolution and federalism

In a federation, sovereignty is constitutionally divided and neither level may unilaterally abolish the other; devolution, by contrast, proceeds from a unitary state whose parliament remains sovereign — in the British formula, “power devolved is power retained”. In practice, political realities may make revocation unthinkable, and conventions (such as the Sewel convention that Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without consent) give devolution a quasi-federal character.

Purposes

Devolution is adopted to bring government closer to citizens, to accommodate distinct national or regional identities within a single state, to improve policy responsiveness, and sometimes to defuse separatist pressures. Its critics point to uneven services between territories, duplicated bureaucracies, and the possibility that devolution becomes a stepping stone to secession rather than an alternative to it.
