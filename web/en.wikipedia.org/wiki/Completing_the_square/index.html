Completing the square

In elementary algebra, completing the square is a technique for converting a quadratic polynomial of the form ax2 + bx + c to the form a(x − h)2 + k for some values of h and k. In other words, it rewrites the polynomial so that it contains a perfect square trinomial plus a constant. The technique, whose name reflects its geometric origins in Greek and Islamic mathematics, is used to solve quadratic equations, derive the quadratic formula, find the vertex of a parabola, and evaluate integrals in calculus.

Procedure

For a monic quadratic x2 + bx + c, take half the coefficient of x, square it, and add and subtract it:

x2 + bx + c = (x + b/2)2 + c − (b/2)2

For example, x2 + 6x + 5 = (x + 3)2 − 4. To solve an equation set in this form, one isolates the squared term and takes square roots. For a general leading coefficient a ≠ 1, each term is first divided by a.

Applications

Setting ax2 + bx + c = 0 and completing the square yields the quadratic formula x = (−b ± √(b2 − 4ac))/2a. In analytic geometry the same manipulation puts the equations of circles, ellipses and hyperbolas into standard form, revealing their centres and radii. In calculus it simplifies integrals containing irreducible quadratics, and in probability it is used to derive properties of the normal distribution.

History

Babylonian mathematicians solved quadratic problems by essentially this method nearly four thousand years ago. The geometric interpretation — literally completing a square figure with a missing corner — was developed by Euclid and systematised by the Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi in his ninth-century treatise on algebra.
