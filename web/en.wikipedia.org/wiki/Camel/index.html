Camel

The dromedary camel, the most numerous camel species.

A camel (from Latin camelus) is an even-toed ungulate of the genus Camelus, distinguished by fatty deposits known as "humps" on its back. The three surviving species are the dromedary (one hump), which accounts for about 94% of the world's camels; the domestic Bactrian camel (two humps); and the critically endangered wild Bactrian camel. Camels have been domesticated for thousands of years and are used as working animals for transport, milk, meat and wool across Asia and Africa.

Adaptations to the desert

Camels are famously adapted to arid environments. They can tolerate losing more than 30% of their body water, concentrate their urine, and allow their body temperature to fluctuate through the day to reduce sweating. The hump stores fat — not water — which can be metabolised for energy. Long eyelashes, sealable nostrils and broad, padded feet protect against blowing sand and soft ground. A camel can drink over 100 litres of water in minutes when it becomes available.

Distribution and use

Domestic camels are concentrated in the Horn of Africa, the Middle East and Central and South Asia; a large feral population also exists in the Australian outback, descended from animals imported in the nineteenth century. The dromedary is ridden, raced, and farmed for milk, which is a staple food for many desert peoples. Historically, camel caravans carried goods across the Sahara and the Silk Road, earning the camel the epithet "ship of the desert".

Biology

Camels are ruminating herbivores with a three-chambered stomach. Uniquely among mammals, their red blood cells are oval. They also produce unusual single-domain antibodies ("nanobodies") that are valuable in biotechnology. Average lifespan is 40 to 50 years.
