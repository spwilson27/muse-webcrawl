Tanis (Greek: Τάνις; Egyptian: Ḏꜥnt; modern San el-Hagar) is an ancient Egyptian city in the northeastern Nile Delta. It rose to prominence in the Third Intermediate Period, when it served as the capital of the kings of the 21st and 22nd Dynasties, and it is celebrated today for the intact royal burials discovered there in 1939–1940 — the most important find of pharaonic treasure after Tutankhamun's tomb.

History

Although the site was occupied earlier, Tanis was built up in the late New Kingdom and became the northern capital after the decline of Pi-Ramesses, whose monuments — obelisks, colossi, and inscribed blocks — were transported to Tanis in enormous quantities, long misleading scholars into identifying Tanis with Pi-Ramesses itself. Its great temple enclosure, dedicated to Amun with Mut and Khonsu, was modelled on Karnak. The city remained significant into the Ptolemaic period before silting of the Pelusiac branch of the Nile and shifting trade routes led to its decline.

The royal necropolis

In 1939–1940 the French archaeologist Pierre Montet uncovered within the temple precinct a series of underground tombs of 21st- and 22nd-Dynasty kings and officials, several unplundered. The burial of Psusennes I yielded a solid silver coffin, a gold funerary mask, and rich jewellery; other tombs belonged to Amenemope, Shoshenq II (buried with a spectacular falcon-headed silver coffin), and the general Wendjebauendjed. Coming on the eve of the Second World War, the discovery received less publicity than its importance warranted. The treasures are now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

In popular culture

Tanis is familiar to film audiences as the legendary resting place of the Ark of the Covenant in Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981), though the film's "lost city" narrative is fictional.
