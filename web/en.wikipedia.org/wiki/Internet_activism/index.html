Internet activism

Internet activism uses networked communication technologies to organize and spread campaigns.

Internet activism, also known as hacktivism in some contexts, cyberactivism, digital campaigning, and online activism, involves the use of electronic communication technologies such as social media, email, and podcasts for various forms of activism to enable faster and more effective communication by citizen movements, the delivery of particular information to large and specific audiences, and coordination of campaigns.

The Internet has become a key tool for political and social movements since the mid-1990s, when early email lists and websites were used by groups such as the Zapatista Army of National Liberation in Mexico to draw international solidarity. With the growth of Web 2.0 platforms, movements could organize mass protests with little central infrastructure, share video evidence in real time, and raise funds through crowdfunding.

History

One of the earliest recognized examples of Internet activism was the campaign in support of the Zapatista uprising in Chiapas in 1994, when supporters used email and websites to publicize the movement's communiqués worldwide. In 1999, the anti-globalization protests surrounding the World Trade Organization Ministerial Conference in Seattle were coordinated extensively online, helping give rise to the Independent Media Center (Indymedia) network.

The 2000s saw the rise of online petition platforms, blog-driven campaigns, and early uses of text messaging to organize demonstrations, including the 2001 EDSA II protests in the Philippines. By the early 2010s, social networks such as Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube played prominent roles in the Arab Spring uprisings, the Occupy movement, and numerous national protest waves.

Forms

Common forms of Internet activism include:

  • Online petitions and letter-writing tools, which lower the cost of contacting representatives.
  • Hashtag activism, in which a shared phrase aggregates posts around an issue, as with #MeToo.
  • Crowdfunding and mutual aid, raising money for causes, legal defense, and disaster relief.
  • Digital civil disobedience, including virtual sit-ins and distributed denial-of-service protest actions.
  • Culture jamming and memetic campaigns, using remix and humor to spread political messages.

Notable movements

The Arab Spring of 2010–2012 demonstrated how mobile connectivity could help coordinate mass protest, though scholars debate the causal weight of social media relative to on-the-ground organization. The Occupy Wall Street movement spread from New York to hundreds of cities in 2011 through online networks. Later campaigns, including Black Lives Matter and the global climate strikes associated with Fridays for Future, relied heavily on digital organizing.

Criticism and debate

Critics have coined the term "slacktivism" for low-effort online gestures that may substitute for deeper engagement. Researchers also note risks including state surveillance, platform censorship, coordinated disinformation, and "astroturfing" by governments and corporations. Authoritarian states have responded with Internet shutdowns, firewalls, and laws targeting online speech.
