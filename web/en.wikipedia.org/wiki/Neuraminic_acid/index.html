Neuraminic acid

Neuraminic acid (systematic name 5-amino-3,5-dideoxy-D-glycero-D-galacto-non-2-ulosonic acid) is an acidic amino sugar with a backbone formed by nine carbon atoms. Although 5-amino-3,5-dideoxy-nonulosonic acids are not naturally occurring, they are the parent compounds of the family of more than 50 naturally occurring derivatives collectively known as the sialic acids. The name "neuraminic acid" was introduced by the German scientist E. Klenk in 1941, in reference to the brain lipids from which it was first isolated.

Derivatives

The most common sialic acid in humans is N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac), in which the amino group carries an acetyl substituent. Other organisms produce variants such as N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc), which humans cannot synthesize because of an inactivating mutation in the CMAH gene, and 2-keto-3-deoxy-D-glycero-D-galacto-nononic acid (KDN). Further diversity arises from O-acetylation and other substitutions on the hydroxyl groups.

Biological role

Sialic acids typically occupy the terminal positions of glycan chains on glycoproteins and glycolipids (gangliosides) at cell surfaces, where they mediate cell–cell recognition, modulate immune responses through Siglec receptors, and serve as binding sites for pathogens: influenza viruses attach via their hemagglutinin to sialic acids, and the viral neuraminidase cleaves them during release.
