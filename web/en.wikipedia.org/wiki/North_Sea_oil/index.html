North Sea oil

North Sea oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons comprising liquid petroleum and natural gas produced from reservoirs beneath the North Sea. Petroleum exploration began in the 1960s; major discoveries followed, notably Norway's Ekofisk field (1969) and the British Forties and Brent fields (1970–71). Production began in 1975 and peaked around 1999–2000. The oil price benchmark Brent Crude takes its name from the Brent field.

Sectors

The North Sea is divided into national sectors along median lines between the United Kingdom, Norway, Denmark, Germany and the Netherlands. The British and Norwegian sectors hold the great majority of reserves. Major early fields included Ekofisk, Statfjord, Forties, Brent and Ninian; later developments included large Norwegian fields such as Troll (mainly gas) and Johan Sverdrup.

Impact

North Sea revenues transformed the economies of Norway — which saved much of the income in its sovereign wealth fund — and the United Kingdom, where oil income supported the Exchequer during the 1980s. Aberdeen became the centre of the British offshore industry. The sector also saw disasters, most notably the Piper Alpha explosion of 1988, which killed 167 men and led to major safety reforms. Production has declined from its peak, and the industry now faces decommissioning and questions over its future in the energy transition.
