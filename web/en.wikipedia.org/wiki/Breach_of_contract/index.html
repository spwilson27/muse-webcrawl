Breach of contract

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Breach of contract is the failure, without legal excuse, of a party to perform a promise that forms all or part of a contract. Breach may be actual (performance is defective, late, or refused when due) or anticipatory (a party repudiates the contract in advance, allowing the other to treat it as terminated immediately or to wait and insist on performance).

The consequences depend on the importance of the term broken. In English law, breach of a condition (a major term) entitles the innocent party to terminate and claim damages; breach of a warranty (a minor term) gives damages only; “innominate” terms are judged by the seriousness of the breach's consequences (Hongkong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki, 1962). Damages aim to compensate the expectation interest, bounded by causation, remoteness and mitigation; specific performance is available only where damages are inadequate and usually not for personal services. American law similarly distinguishes material from minor breach.

Excuses

Performance may be excused by impossibility, impracticability or frustration of purpose (contracts discharged when a supervening event destroys their foundation, as where a hired hall burns down — Taylor v Caldwell, 1863, or a coronation procession is cancelled — Krell v Henry, 1903). Force-majeure clauses allocate such risks expressly, a practice that expanded after the supply shocks of the COVID-19 pandemic.
