Gatehouse

A gatehouse is a type of fortified gateway structure, typically the principal entrance to a castle, walled town, abbey, or country house. Because the entrance was the weakest point of a fortification, gatehouses were often its strongest and most elaborate element, combining defences such as twin towers, portcullises, drawbridges, murder holes, and guardrooms with chambers for officials and, later, comfortable lodgings.

Development

Simple gate towers evolved in the 12th and 13th centuries into massive twin-towered gatehouses, exemplified by the Edwardian castles of Wales such as Harlech and Beaumaris, and by town gates like the Westgate at Canterbury. In later medieval England, gatehouses at colleges, monasteries, and manor houses emphasized status over defence, with ornate heraldic façades — as at Hampton Court and Layer Marney. In Tudor and Stuart great houses, the gatehouse became a ceremonial entrance lodge. Gatehouses also appeared at institutions such as prisons, workhouses, and cemeteries, and the term is used for small entrance buildings at modern gated communities and industrial sites.
