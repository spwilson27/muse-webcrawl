Forestry in the United Kingdom

Forestry in the United Kingdom concerns the management of the country's woodlands, which cover about 3.25 million hectares — roughly 13% of the total land area, one of the lowest proportions in Europe. Scotland holds the majority of the woodland area and of commercial conifer plantations.

History

Britain's native woodland was progressively cleared from prehistoric times for agriculture, fuel, and shipbuilding; by 1900 only about 5% of the land was wooded. Timber shortages during the First World War led to the Forestry Act 1919 and the creation of the Forestry Commission, which planted extensive conifer forests such as Kielder and Thetford. Private forestry expanded with tax incentives in the postwar decades, sometimes controversially, as in the planting of the Flow Country peatlands of northern Scotland, later halted for conservation reasons.

Present day

Forestry policy is devolved to England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. The UK Forestry Standard sets the framework for sustainable management. Sitka spruce is the dominant commercial species, accounting for around half of the conifer area, while broadleaves make up a growing share of new planting driven by climate-change mitigation targets, biodiversity, and recreation. The UK remains a major net importer of timber, and recent governments have set tree-planting targets of tens of thousands of hectares per year to support net-zero goals.
