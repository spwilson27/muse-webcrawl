Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (FLSA) is a United States federal labour law that created the right to a minimum wage, "time-and-a-half" overtime pay for hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek, and prohibitions on oppressive child labour. Signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt as part of the New Deal, it took effect in October 1938 and remains the cornerstone of U.S. wage-and-hour law, administered by the Wage and Hour Division of the Department of Labor.

Provisions

The Act initially set the federal minimum wage at 25 cents per hour, capped the standard workweek at 44 hours (falling to 40 by 1940), and required overtime at one-and-a-half times regular pay. It banned most employment of children under 16 and hazardous work under 18. Amendments over the decades raised the minimum wage (to $7.25 in 2009), extended coverage to retail, service and public-sector workers, and in 1963 added equal-pay provisions.

Exemptions and litigation

The FLSA exempts many executive, administrative and professional employees from overtime, a boundary repeatedly contested in rulemaking and court cases. It does not require paid leave, breaks, or severance. Many states set higher minimum wages than the federal floor.

Significance

The Act largely eliminated sweatshop conditions and child labour in interstate industry and established the 40-hour week as an American norm.
