Riccardo Giacconi (October 6, 1931 – December 9, 2018) was an Italian-American astrophysicist who laid the foundations of X-ray astronomy. In 2002 he received the Nobel Prize in Physics "for pioneering contributions to astrophysics, which have led to the discovery of cosmic X-ray sources."

Early life and education

Giacconi was born in Genoa, Italy, and grew up in Milan. He studied physics at the University of Milan, earning his doctorate in 1954 with research on cosmic rays. In 1956 he emigrated to the United States, working first at Indiana University and then at Princeton University before joining American Science and Engineering (AS&E) in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1959.

X-ray astronomy

At AS&E, Giacconi led a small team that built rocket-borne X-ray detectors. On June 12, 1962, a sounding rocket flight intended to detect X-rays from the Moon instead discovered the first cosmic X-ray source outside the Solar System, Scorpius X-1, along with a diffuse X-ray background — a result that effectively created the field of X-ray astronomy.

He went on to lead the development of the Uhuru satellite (launched 1970), the first dedicated X-ray astronomy observatory, which catalogued hundreds of X-ray sources and provided strong evidence for stellar-mass black holes in binary systems such as Cygnus X-1. He later directed the Einstein Observatory (HEAO-2, launched 1978), the first imaging X-ray telescope, and championed the Advanced X-ray Astrophysics Facility, launched in 1999 as the Chandra X-ray Observatory.

Later career

Giacconi moved to the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics in 1973. In 1981 he became the first director of the Space Telescope Science Institute, which operates the Hubble Space Telescope, and served there until 1993. From 1993 to 2002 he was director general of the European Southern Observatory, overseeing construction of the Very Large Telescope in Chile. He subsequently served as president of Associated Universities, Inc.

Honours

Besides the Nobel Prize, Giacconi received the Wolf Prize in Physics (1987), the National Medal of Science (2003), and numerous other honours. The asteroid 3371 Giacconi is named after him.
