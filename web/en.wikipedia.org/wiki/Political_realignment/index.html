Political realignment

Successive party systems in United States political history

A political realignment is a durable shift in the coalitions supporting political parties, typically accompanied by a change in the dominant issues of political conflict and in which party holds power. The concept is central to the study of American political development, where scholars such as V. O. Key Jr. and Walter Dean Burnham described periodic "critical elections" that inaugurate new party systems lasting for decades.

Critical elections and party systems

Key's theory of critical elections held that certain elections produce sharp, enduring redirections of voter loyalties, as large groups of voters permanently shift their allegiance in response to new crises or issues. Burnham elaborated the idea into a theory of recurring realignments roughly every thirty years, each resolving tensions left by the previous regime. In the United States, commonly cited realigning elections include 1800, 1828, 1860, 1896, and 1932, the last inaugurating the New Deal coalition that dominated American politics for a generation.

Mechanisms

Realignments may occur abruptly in a single election or gradually through "secular realignment"—a slow, election-by-election drift of groups from one party to another, as with the movement of white Southern voters from the Democratic to the Republican Party across the second half of the twentieth century. Generational replacement, in which new voters enter the electorate with different loyalties, is another important mechanism.

Debate

Some political scientists question whether realignments remain a useful concept in an era of candidate-centered politics and dealignment, in which voters hold weaker party attachments overall. Others argue that polarisation since the 1990s represents a new form of long-term coalition change, even if it did not arrive through a single critical election.
