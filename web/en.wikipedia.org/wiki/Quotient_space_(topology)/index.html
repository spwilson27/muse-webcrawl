Quotient space (topology)

In topology, a quotient space is a topological space formed from another space by identifying points according to an equivalence relation. The construction is one of the standard ways to build new spaces from old ones: points that are declared equivalent are treated as a single point in the new space.

If X is a topological space and ~ is an equivalence relation on X, the quotient set X/~ carries the quotient topology. A subset of the quotient is open exactly when its preimage under the natural projection map is open in X. This makes the projection continuous and gives the quotient the finest topology with that property.

Examples

A circle can be obtained from the unit interval by identifying its two endpoints. A torus can be formed from a square by identifying opposite edges in pairs. More complicated surfaces and orbit spaces are often described in the same language, because the identifications encode how pieces are glued together.

Properties

Quotient constructions need care: a quotient of a Hausdorff space need not be Hausdorff, and compactness or connectedness can behave differently depending on the equivalence relation. The quotient topology is nevertheless fundamental in algebraic topology, geometry and the theory of fiber bundles, where spaces are frequently assembled from simpler pieces.
