Overview

Chinese painting is one of the oldest continuous artistic traditions in the world. It is practised with brush, ink and mineral or vegetable pigments on paper or silk, and traditionally grouped with calligraphy — which shares its tools and training — as the highest of the arts in imperial China, cultivated by scholar-officials as well as professional court painters.

Styles and history

The major genres are landscape (shanshui, 'mountain-water'), figure painting, and bird-and-flower painting. Monumental landscape reached a pinnacle in the Northern Song dynasty with masters such as Fan Kuan and Guo Xi; the Yuan dynasty literati painters, including Zhao Mengfu and the Four Masters, prized personal expression and calligraphic brushwork over realism. Later landmarks include the Ming dynasty Wu School of Suzhou, the individualists Shitao and Bada Shanren at the dawn of the Qing, and the synthesis with Western techniques in the twentieth century by artists such as Xu Beihong and Zhang Daqian.

Techniques

Painting is mounted as handscrolls, hanging scrolls, album leaves or fans. Core methods include outline and 'boneless' washes, dry-brush texture strokes (cun) that model rocks and trees, and the expressive ink play of xieyi ('writing ideas') as opposed to the meticulous gongbi style. Seals and colophons added by artists, collectors and connoisseurs become part of the work's surface and history.
