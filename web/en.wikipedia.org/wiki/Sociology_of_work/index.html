Sociology of work

The sociology of work (also called industrial sociology in its earlier form) is the study of work, employment and workplaces as social phenomena: how jobs are organised, how occupations and professions form, how workers experience and resist management, and how paid work relates to gender, class, technology and the rest of life.

Development

Classical foundations come from Marx's analysis of alienation, Durkheim's division of labour and Weber's account of bureaucracy. In the twentieth century the field drew on the Hawthorne studies, the human relations school, theories of scientific management (Taylorism) and its critics — notably Harry Braverman's Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974), which argued that management deskills labour. Michael Burawoy's ethnographies of shop-floor "consent" and Arlie Hochschild's concept of emotional labour extended the agenda.

Contemporary themes

Current research addresses precarious and gig-economy work, remote and platform work, automation and artificial intelligence, global supply chains, unpaid care and domestic labour, work–life balance, occupational identity and the future of professions. Methods range from workplace ethnography to labour-force surveys and comparative institutional analysis.
