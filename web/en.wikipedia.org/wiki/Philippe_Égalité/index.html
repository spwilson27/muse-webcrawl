Philippe Égalité

Louis Philippe Joseph d'Orléans
Born: 13 April 1747, Château de Saint-Cloud, France
Died: 6 November 1793 (aged 46), Paris, France (executed)
Titles: Duke of Chartres; Duke of Orléans (from 1785)
Spouse: Louise Marie Adélaïde de Bourbon
Issue: Louis Philippe I, King of the French
House: Orléans

Louis Philippe Joseph d'Orléans (13 April 1747 – 6 November 1793), commonly known as Philippe Égalité, was a French prince of the blood and a leading figure of the early French Revolution. As Duke of Orléans he was one of the richest men in France and a cousin of Louis XVI, yet he cultivated popularity by supporting reform and, later, the Revolution itself.

Early life

Born at Saint-Cloud, he inherited the dukedom of Orléans in 1785. His career in the French navy ended after the indecisive Battle of Ushant (1778), and his hostility toward the court of Marie Antoinette — and his exclusion from further command — pushed him into opposition. He made the Palais-Royal in Paris a centre of fashionable and political life, opening its galleries to the public.

Revolution

Elected to the Estates-General of 1789, Orléans sided with the Third Estate and joined the National Assembly. In 1792 he renounced his titles and adopted the name Philippe Égalité ("Philip Equality"), and was elected to the National Convention. His vote for the death of Louis XVI in January 1793 scandalised royalists and did not save him: after his son, the young Duke of Chartres (the future King Louis Philippe), defected to the Austrians with General Dumouriez in April 1793, Philippe Égalité was arrested, tried by the Revolutionary Tribunal and guillotined in Paris on 6 November 1793.

Legacy

Despite his revolutionary name and vote, he was widely suspected of aspiring to the throne himself. His son eventually became King of the French in 1830, founding the July Monarchy. Philippe Égalité remains one of the most controversial princes of the Revolution — celebrated by some as a reformer, condemned by others as an ambitious opportunist.
