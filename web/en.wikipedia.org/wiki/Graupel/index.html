Graupel

Graupel (/ˈɡraʊpəl/; German: [ˈɡʁaʊ̯pl̩]), also called soft hail, hominy snow, or snow pellets, is precipitation that forms when supercooled water droplets collect and freeze on falling snowflakes, forming balls of crisp, opaque rime of diameter 2–5 mm (0.08–0.20 in).

Formation

Graupel grows through the process of riming: as a snow crystal falls through a cloud containing supercooled droplets, the droplets freeze on contact, coating the crystal and filling in its spaces. With continued accretion the original six-sided shape of the crystal is obscured, and the resulting pellet is conical, spherical or irregular, with a soft, opaque white appearance. Graupel falls faster than snowflakes and is more fragile than hail, lacking hail's layered ice structure and hard, translucent shell.

Properties and reporting

Graupel pellets are soft and crumbly to the touch and shatter on hitting a hard surface, distinguishing them from hail and from ice pellets (sleet). In aviation and surface weather reports, graupel and small hail share the METAR present-weather code GS. Graupel is most common in winter thundersnow and in convective showers in cold seasons near coasts, and it can coat the ground like a layer of small beads that are noted for making surfaces slippery.
