A germ cell is any cell of a multicellular organism that gives rise to the gametes — the sperm and egg cells that fuse during fertilisation in sexual reproduction. Germ cells together form the germ line, which is set apart from the somatic cells that make up the rest of the body; mutations in germ cells, unlike somatic mutations, can be passed to offspring.

Development

In many animals, primordial germ cells are specified early in embryonic development, either by inherited cytoplasmic determinants (germ plasm) in the egg or by induction from neighbouring cells. In humans, primordial germ cells appear in the wall of the yolk sac around the fourth week and migrate to the developing gonads, where they become oogonia or spermatogonia. Germ cells divide by mitosis and then undergo meiosis to produce haploid gametes, each carrying one set of chromosomes.

Clinical significance

Cancers arising from germ cells are called germ cell tumours; they most often occur in the testes and ovaries — such as seminomas and teratomas — but can also appear outside the gonads along the body's midline. Defects in germ cell development can cause infertility.
