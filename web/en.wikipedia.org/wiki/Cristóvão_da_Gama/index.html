Cristóvão da Gama (c. 1516 – 29 August 1542) was a Portuguese nobleman and soldier, the fourth son of the explorer Vasco da Gama. He is best known for commanding the Portuguese military expedition that intervened in Ethiopia (Abyssinia) in 1541–1542 during the Ethiopian–Adal War, in support of the Emperor Gelawdewos against the forces of Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Ahmad Gragn").

Expedition to Ethiopia

In 1541 the governor of Portuguese India, Estêvão da Gama (Cristóvão's elder brother), sent 400 musketeers under Cristóvão from Massawa into the Ethiopian highlands in response to appeals for help from the Ethiopian court, whose empire had been overrun by Adal forces since 1529. Miguel de Castanhoso, a member of the expedition, left a detailed narrative of the campaign.

Campaign and death

The Portuguese won victories at the Battle of Jarte (April 1542) and in subsequent engagements, where their firearms proved effective. In August 1542, at the Battle of Wofla, Cristóvão's smaller force was defeated; he was captured wounded, and after refusing to convert to Islam he was executed on Ahmad Gragn's orders on 29 August 1542. His death was avenged the following year: in February 1543 the combined Ethiopian–Portuguese force defeated the Adal army at the Battle of Wayna Daga, where Ahmad Gragn was killed — tradition holding that he was shot by a Portuguese musketeer.

Legacy

Cristóvão da Gama became a celebrated figure in Portuguese chronicles, compared by some writers to the heroes of chivalric romance, and his Ethiopian campaign is treated as a turning point that preserved the Christian Ethiopian empire. The expedition is narrated in Castanhoso's account and in João de Barros's Décadas.
