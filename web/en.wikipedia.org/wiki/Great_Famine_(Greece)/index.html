Great Famine (Greece)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Famine (Greek: Μεγάλος Λιμός) was a period of mass starvation in Greece during the Axis occupation of the Second World War, reaching its worst in the winter of 1941–42. Demographic estimates of excess deaths vary, but figures between roughly 250,000 and 450,000 — several percent of the pre-war population — are commonly cited, with Athens and Piraeus and the Aegean islands hit hardest.

Causes

Greece was occupied and partitioned by Germany, Italy, and Bulgaria following the invasion of April 1941. The famine resulted from a combination of factors:

  • Requisitioning of food, livestock, and transport by the occupation forces, and occupation costs that fuelled hyperinflation.
  • The Allied naval blockade, which cut off Greece's customary grain imports, on which the country depended.
  • The collapse of distribution networks, hoarding, and a rampant black market that priced food beyond the reach of urban wage-earners.
  • A poor domestic harvest and the Bulgarian annexation of the grain-producing regions of eastern Macedonia and Thrace.

Relief

International pressure led to a partial lifting of the blockade for relief shipments, administered chiefly by the Swedish and Swiss Red Cross from 1942. Ships carrying Canadian and other wheat, organised under the Greek War Relief Association and later the joint relief commission, substantially reduced mortality from the summer of 1942, though shortages and deaths continued for the remainder of the occupation.

Legacy

The famine left deep marks on Greek society and politics: it contributed to the growth of the EAM resistance movement, which organised food distribution in liberated areas, and it remains a central episode in Greek memory of the occupation. Mortality peaked in Athens in the winter of 1941–42, when thousands of bodies were collected daily from the streets.
