Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti Sijzi (Persian: معین‌الدین حسن چشتی; c. 1143 – c. 1236), known as Moinuddin Chishti and by his honorific Khwaja Gharib Nawaz ("Master, Benefactor of the Poor"), was a Persian Islamic scholar and Sufi saint who introduced and established the Chishti Order of Sufism in the Indian subcontinent. He settled in Ajmer, in present-day Rajasthan, in the 1190s, and is credited with spreading Islam in India through preaching, charity and personal example. His shrine at Ajmer remains one of the most visited Sufi pilgrimage sites in the world, drawing devotees of many faiths.

Early life and travels

According to the hagiographies, Moinuddin was born in Sistan (in some accounts his family came from Chisht, in present-day Afghanistan, the village that gave the order its name). Orphaned young, he renounced his inheritance — a garden and a mill — distributed it to the poor, and travelled in search of knowledge and spiritual guidance, studying in Samarkand and Bukhara. At Baghdad (or in Nishapur) he became the disciple of Khwaja Usman Harooni, whom he served for many years before receiving the khirqa and authorisation to teach. His journeys took him through Persia and Central Asia, and tradition associates him with meetings with saints such as Abdul Qadir Gilani and Najmuddin Kubra.

Settlement at Ajmer

Around 1191–92, near the time of the second battle of Tarain and the establishment of Muslim rule in northern India, Moinuddin reached Ajmer, then ruled by the Chauhan king Prithviraj III. There he established his khanqah (hospice), teaching a message of devotion to God, service to humanity, generosity, and peaceful coexistence; his langar (free kitchen) fed rich and poor alike. His disciples carried the order across India: Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki in Delhi, Baba Farid in Punjab, and through them Nizamuddin Auliya and the Chishti network that shaped South Asian Islam.

Teachings

Moinuddin's teaching, preserved in later compilations of his sayings, emphasised love of God expressed through service to creation, liberality ("a generous man is a friend of God"), detachment from worldly power — the early Chishtis famously kept their distance from royal courts — and the cultivation of the heart through prayer, remembrance (dhikr) and listening to devotional poetry and music (sama).

Death and shrine

He died at Ajmer, traditionally in 1236 (633 AH); his annual urs (death-anniversary festival) is celebrated in the Islamic month of Rajab. The Ajmer Sharif Dargah complex — with his marble tomb, mosques endowed by Mughal emperors including Akbar and Shah Jahan, and two enormous cauldrons (degs) for cooking food for pilgrims — became a site of imperial and popular pilgrimage. Akbar walked barefoot to the shrine in gratitude for the birth of a son, and the dargah continues to attract millions of visitors of all religions each year.
