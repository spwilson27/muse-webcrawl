Cognitivism (ethics)

Cognitivism is the meta-ethical view that ethical sentences express propositions and therefore can be true or false (they are truth-apt). Cognitivists further maintain that these propositions express beliefs about objective features of the world, or about how one ought to act. It contrasts with non-cognitivism, which holds that moral utterances are not propositions at all but expressions of attitudes, emotions or prescriptions.

Overview

On the cognitivist view, a sentence like "Murder is wrong" functions like the sentence "Murder is common": both purport to describe the world and can be assessed for truth. Cognitivism does not on its own decide which moral claims are true; that depends on the accompanying normative and metaphysical theory. Forms of cognitivism include moral realism, which holds that there are objective moral facts, and the error theory associated with J. L. Mackie, which accepts that moral sentences are truth-apt but argues that they are systematically false because there are no objective values.

Cognitivism and motivation

A central debate concerns moral motivation. Moral internalists argue that sincere moral judgement necessarily motivates, which sits uneasily with the cognitivist picture of moral judgement as belief, since beliefs alone are often held to be motivationally inert (the Humean theory of motivation). Externalists deny the necessary connection. Cognitivists have responded in various ways, including appeal to desires that rational agents necessarily have, or to the idea that moral beliefs are a special class of motivational states.

History

Cognitivism was the dominant assumption of early analytic ethics, defended in different forms by G. E. Moore and W. D. Ross. It was challenged in the mid-twentieth century by emotivists such as A. J. Ayer and Charles Stevenson, and continues to be a central position in contemporary meta-ethics.
