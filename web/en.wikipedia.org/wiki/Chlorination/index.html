Chlorination

Chlorination is the process of adding chlorine or chlorine compounds such as sodium hypochlorite to water. This method is used to kill bacteria, viruses and other microbes in water. In particular, chlorination is used to prevent the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera, dysentery, and typhoid.

History

Chlorine was first used as a disinfectant in the mid-19th century. John Snow's work on the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in London demonstrated that contaminated water spread disease, though chemical disinfection came later. Continuous chlorination of a public water supply was pioneered in Maidstone, England in 1897 after a typhoid outbreak, and in the United States beginning in 1908 in Jersey City, New Jersey. By the mid-20th century chlorination had dramatically reduced waterborne disease in the developed world.

Chemistry

When chlorine gas is added to water it hydrolyses to form hypochlorous acid (HOCl) and hypochlorite ion (OCl−), collectively called free available chlorine. Hypochlorous acid is the more effective disinfectant. Chlorine also reacts with ammonia and organic nitrogen compounds in water to form chloramines, which act as slower, longer-lasting disinfectants. The amount of chlorine consumed by these reactions is the chlorine demand; dosing must satisfy the demand and leave a protective residual.

Methods

Chlorination may use chlorine gas, sodium hypochlorite solution (liquid bleach), or calcium hypochlorite granules and tablets. Shock chlorination applies a high concentration to disinfect wells and new mains. Breakpoint chlorination adds enough chlorine to oxidise all ammonia, beyond which free chlorine residual rises proportionally with dose.

Health and safety concerns

Chlorine reacts with naturally occurring organic matter to form disinfection by-products such as trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids, some of which are regulated because of potential carcinogenicity. Alternatives or supplements include chloramination, ozonation, and ultraviolet disinfection, though chlorine remains valued because it leaves a residual that protects water throughout the distribution system.

Other uses

Chlorination is used in swimming pools, wastewater treatment, food processing, and as an industrial oxidising and bleaching agent. In organic chemistry, chlorination also refers to reactions that introduce chlorine atoms into molecules.
