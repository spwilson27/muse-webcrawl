Ship prefix

A ship prefix is a combination of letters, usually abbreviations, used in front of the name of a civilian or naval ship that has historically formed the full name of the vessel (for example, RMS Titanic or USS Enterprise). Naval prefixes came into use in the 18th and 19th centuries as a shorthand for the ship's status or propulsion, and many countries have their own conventions for naval and merchant vessels. Prefixes are not properly part of the registered name of a vessel, and their use is a matter of convention rather than law.

Common merchant prefixes

Historically, merchant ship prefixes indicated the means of propulsion: SS (steamship) was the generic prefix for screw-driven steamers, with variants such as PS (paddle steamer), TS (turbine ship), and later MS or MV (motor ship/motor vessel) for diesel-powered vessels, RV (research vessel), FV (fishing vessel), and MY (motor yacht) or SY (sailing yacht). The prestigious prefix RMS (Royal Mail Ship) was borne by ocean liners contracted to carry mail for the British Royal Mail, including the Titanic, Lusitania and the Queen Mary liners. As steam gave way to diesel, the plain prefix MV became standard for merchant vessels.

Naval prefixes

Navies assign prefixes by state and service: the Royal Navy uses HMS (His/Her Majesty's Ship), the United States Navy uses USS (United States Ship) for commissioned warships and USNS for civilian-crewed auxiliaries, and other examples include HMCS (Canada), HMAS (Australia), INS (India), JS (Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force), and FGS historically for German federal ships (in English contexts). Some navies — notably the French Marine Nationale — use no prefix at all. Submarines may carry special designations such as HM Submarine or hull pennant numbers. Prefixes also change with political history: imperial, royal, and republican variants have succeeded one another in many fleets.
