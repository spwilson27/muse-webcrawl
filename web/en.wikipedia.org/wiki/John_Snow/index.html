John Snow (15 March 1813 – 16 June 1858) was an English physician and a leader in the development of anaesthesia and medical hygiene. He is considered a founder of modern epidemiology, in part because of his work in tracing the source of a cholera outbreak in Soho, London, in 1854, which he identified as water contaminated from the Broad Street pump.

Early life and anaesthesia

Snow was born in York, the eldest son of a labourer, and was apprenticed to a surgeon at 14. He moved to London in 1836, qualified as a physician, and gained an MD from the University of London in 1844. When ether and chloroform anaesthesia arrived in Britain, Snow made himself their leading scientific practitioner, studying dosage and designing inhalers; he twice administered chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth (1853 and 1857), helping to make obstetric anaesthesia respectable.

Cholera and the Broad Street pump

Against the prevailing "miasma" theory, Snow argued in On the Mode of Communication of Cholera (1849; greatly expanded 1855) that cholera was spread by a self-reproducing agent swallowed in water contaminated with the evacuations of the sick. During the Soho outbreak of August–September 1854, which killed more than 600 people in a few streets, Snow plotted the deaths on a dot map and showed they clustered around the public water pump in Broad Street (now Broadwick Street). He persuaded the parish authorities to remove the pump handle on 8 September 1854; the outbreak was already waning, but the investigation became the classic case study of epidemiological reasoning.

Snow also conducted a celebrated "natural experiment" comparing London households supplied by the Lambeth Water Company (which had moved its intake upstream) with those supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company (still drawing sewage-laden Thames water downstream), finding a roughly fourteen-fold difference in cholera mortality.

Death and legacy

Snow died of a stroke in 1858, aged 45, having suffered ill health possibly hastened by his experiments with anaesthetic gases. His work was vindicated after his death, and a replica pump with a memorial stands on Broadwick Street in Soho. The John Snow Society and the journal-published "John Snow" lectures commemorate him; in 2003 he was voted the greatest physician of all time in a poll of British doctors.
