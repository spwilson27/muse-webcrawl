The Crown of Saint Wenceslas (Czech: Svatováclavská koruna) is the central piece of the Bohemian Crown Jewels, made in 1347 for the coronation of Charles IV as King of Bohemia. Charles dedicated the crown to Saint Wenceslaus, the patron saint of Bohemia, decreeing that it should rest permanently on the saint's skull at St. Vitus Cathedral and be lent to kings only for their coronations.

Description

The crown is made of exceptionally pure gold and decorated with some of the largest precious stones of any European crown: 19 sapphires, 44 spinels (several long described as rubies), 30 emeralds, and 20 pearls. It has four lily-shaped half-arches topped by a cross said to contain a thorn from Christ's crown of thorns. It weighs nearly two and a half kilograms.

History and tradition

Charles IV stipulated severe curses against anyone who removed or sold the crown's stones. The jewel was used at Bohemian coronations for centuries; the last coronation was that of Ferdinand I of Austria (as Ferdinand V of Bohemia) in 1836. According to legend, any usurper who places the crown on his head will die within a year — a tale attached in popular retelling to Reinhard Heydrich, the Nazi governor of Prague, who was assassinated in 1942.

Custody

The crown jewels are kept in the Crown Chamber of St. Vitus Cathedral behind a door with seven locks, whose keys are held by seven Czech state and church officials (including the President and the Prime Minister); all seven must assemble to open it. The jewels are displayed publicly only on rare special occasions.
