Fiscal policy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fiscal policy is the use of government revenue collection (taxation) and expenditure (spending) to influence a country's economy. The modern doctrine descends from Keynes's General Theory (1936): because private demand can fall short of full-employment output, government deficits can and should expand demand in slumps (and surpluses restrain it in booms) — a doctrine institutionalised after 1945 in employment acts, automatic stabilisers (taxes falling and benefits rising without new legislation) and discretionary stimulus packages.

Debates

Practice oscillates with theory and crisis: the post-war Keynesian consensus; monetarist and supply-side reactions (the Laffer-curve era, Ricardian-equivalence objections that rational households save tax cuts against future taxes); austerity programmes after the euro crisis, contested by multiplier research (the IMF's 2012 reassessment); pandemic-era mega-stimulus and the inflation that followed; and industrial-policy fiscal activism in the 2020s. The accounting frame — deficit versus debt, primary balances, r-versus-g arithmetic, debt ceilings and fiscal rules (EU stability pact, debt brakes) — is where economics meets constitutional politics, and where the "fiscal space" of governments expands and vanishes with market mood.
