Half-life

Half-life
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

A half-life is the time required for a quantity to reduce to half of its initial value. The term is most commonly used for radioactive decay: after one half-life, half of the atoms in a sample of a radionuclide will (on average) have decayed; after two half-lives, a quarter remain, and so on.

Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years: polonium-214 decays in microseconds, carbon-14 in about 5,730 years (the basis of radiocarbon dating), and uranium-238 in about 4.5 billion years. The concept also appears in pharmacology (drug elimination), chemistry and other exponential-decay processes. Crucially, decay is random for individual atoms but statistically predictable for large numbers, and the half-life does not depend on temperature, pressure or chemical state.

Mathematics

If N₀ atoms are present, the number remaining after time t is N(t) = N₀·(1/2)^(t/t½). The half-life is related to the decay constant λ by t½ = ln 2 / λ, and to the mean lifetime τ by t½ = τ·ln 2.
