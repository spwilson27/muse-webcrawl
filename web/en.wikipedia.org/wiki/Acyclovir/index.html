Acyclovir

Acyclovir (ACV), also spelled aciclovir and sold under the brand name Zovirax among others, is an antiviral medication used primarily for the treatment of herpes simplex virus infections, chickenpox and shingles. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism

Acyclovir is a nucleoside analogue of guanosine. It is converted to its active triphosphate form preferentially in virus-infected cells — the first phosphorylation step is performed by the viral enzyme thymidine kinase — and then acts as a chain terminator of viral DNA synthesis, halting replication of the virus with relatively low toxicity to uninfected cells.

History

The drug was developed in the 1970s at Burroughs Wellcome by Howard Schaeffer and colleagues, working in the research environment of Gertrude Elion and George Hitchings, and was approved for medical use in 1981. Its development is regarded as a landmark in rational drug design and selective antiviral therapy. The related prodrug valacyclovir offers improved oral bioavailability.
