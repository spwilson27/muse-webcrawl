The Tachibana clan (橘氏, Tachibana-uji) was one of the most powerful kuge (court aristocracy) families in early Japanese history. Founded in the Nara period, the clan rose to prominence at the imperial court during the 8th and 9th centuries, rivalling the Fujiwara clan for influence before gradually declining in the Heian period.

Origins

The clan descended from Prince Katsuragi, a son of Emperor Bidatsu. The Tachibana name was first granted in 708 to Agata-no-Inukai no Michiyo, the wife of Prince Minu, by Empress Genmei, taking its name from the tachibana, a type of Japanese citrus fruit. Michiyo's sons by Prince Minu, Katsuragi (later Tachibana no Moroe) and Sai, were granted the surname Tachibana in 736, formally leaving the imperial family to found the clan.

Prominence

Tachibana no Moroe (684–757) rose to become Minister of the Left and effectively led the government in the mid-8th century. His son Tachibana no Naramaro was implicated in a failed plot against the Fujiwara in 757 and died in disgrace. The clan produced numerous senior courtiers, scholars, and poets, including Tachibana no Hayanari, a celebrated calligrapher counted among the Sanpitsu (Three Brushes) of the early Heian period, and Tachibana no Kachiko, consort of Emperor Saga.

Decline

During the Heian period the Tachibana were progressively eclipsed by the Fujiwara northern house, which monopolised the regency. Nevertheless, the Tachibana remained one of the four great surnames (Genpeitōkitsu) alongside the Minamoto, Taira, and Fujiwara. In later centuries, a number of samurai families, including a Tachibana clan of Kyushu who held Yanagawa Domain in the Edo period, claimed descent from the courtly line, though the connection is debated.
