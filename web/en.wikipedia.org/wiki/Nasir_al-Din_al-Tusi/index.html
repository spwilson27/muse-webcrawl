Nasir al-Din al-Tusi

Nasir al-Din al-Tusi
Born: 18 February 1201, Tus, Persia
Died: 26 June 1274, Baghdad
Known for: Tusi couple, trigonometry, Maragheh observatory

Khawaja Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Tusi (1201–1274), better known as Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, was a Persian polymath — astronomer, mathematician, philosopher, theologian, physician and architect — regarded as one of the greatest scholars of the Islamic Golden Age. He wrote more than 150 works in Arabic and Persian.

Astronomy

After the Mongol conquest, Tusi persuaded Hülegü Khan to fund the great observatory at Maragheh (begun 1259), where he compiled the Zij-i Ilkhani star tables. He criticised Ptolemy's models and devised the "Tusi couple" — a pair of circles generating linear oscillation — to repair them; the same device appears in Copernicus's work, suggesting transmission of the idea to Europe.

Mathematics and philosophy

Tusi's Treatise on the Quadrilateral established trigonometry as an independent discipline, giving the first systematic statement of the law of sines for plane and spherical triangles. His commentary on Euclid presented a notable discussion of the parallel postulate. His Akhlaq-i Nasiri (Nasirean Ethics) became the most influential Persian work on ethics, and he defended Avicennan philosophy against al-Ghazali's criticisms.
