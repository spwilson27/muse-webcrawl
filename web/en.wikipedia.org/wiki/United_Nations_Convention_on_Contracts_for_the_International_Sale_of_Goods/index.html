The United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG), sometimes known as the Vienna Convention, is a multilateral treaty that establishes a uniform framework for international commerce. Signed in 1980 and in force since 1 January 1988, it has been ratified by around 97 states representing the great majority of world trade, making it one of the most successful uniform commercial law treaties.

Scope and content

The CISG applies automatically to contracts for the sale of goods between parties whose places of business are in different Contracting States, unless the parties exclude it. It governs the formation of the contract and the rights and obligations of buyer and seller — including conformity of the goods, remedies for breach, the passing of risk and damages — but not the validity of the contract or the proprietary effects of the sale. Consumer sales and certain goods, such as ships, aircraft and electricity, fall outside its scope.

Notable features

The Convention dispenses with a strict writing requirement, permits acceptance by conduct, and departs from the common-law mirror-image rule by treating replies with additional or different terms as acceptances unless the terms materially alter the offer. Although the United Kingdom is not a party, most other major trading nations — including the United States, China, Germany, France and Japan — are.
