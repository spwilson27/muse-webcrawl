Almagest

The Almagest is a 2nd-century mathematical and astronomical treatise by the Alexandrian astronomer Claudius Ptolemy, written in Greek around AD 150. It presents a complete geocentric model of the motions of the Sun, Moon, planets, and stars, together with a catalogue of over a thousand stars, and remained the authoritative astronomy text in the Byzantine, Islamic, and Latin worlds for more than 1,400 years.

Title and transmission

Ptolemy called the work the Mathēmatikē Syntaxis ("Mathematical Treatise"). Its later Greek epithet hē megistē ("the greatest") passed into Arabic as al-majisṭī and thence into Latin as Almagestum. The text was translated into Arabic in 9th-century Baghdad — notably by Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq and Thābit ibn Qurra — and into Latin from Arabic by Gerard of Cremona in 12th-century Toledo; a Greek-to-Latin version followed in Sicily.

Contents

The thirteen books lay out spherical astronomy and chord-table trigonometry; the theory of the Sun; the Moon's complex motion with its evection; eclipses; a catalogue of 1,022 stars in 48 constellations; and the planetary models, in which each planet moves on an epicycle whose centre travels on a deferent circle, displaced by the equant point — a device that sacrificed uniform circular motion for predictive accuracy. The Almagest's cosmology placed a spherical, stationary Earth at the centre of the universe.

Influence

Ptolemy's system was refined by Islamic astronomers and transmitted to Europe, where it underpinned astronomy until Copernicus's De revolutionibus (1543) and Kepler's elliptical astronomy displaced it. The star catalogue's names and magnitudes shaped Western astronomy permanently.
