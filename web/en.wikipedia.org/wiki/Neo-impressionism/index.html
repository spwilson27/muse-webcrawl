Neo-Impressionism

Neo-Impressionism is an art movement founded by Georges Seurat and Paul Signac in the mid-1880s. Building on Impressionism, its painters applied scientific theories of colour and optics — especially simultaneous contrast — by placing small, distinct touches of pure colour side by side so that they blend in the viewer's eye rather than on the palette. The technique is called Pointillism or Divisionism (Chromoluminarism). The term "Neo-Impressionism" was coined by the critic Félix Fénéon in 1886.

History

Seurat's monumental A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–86) announced the movement at the final Impressionist exhibition and the Salon des Indépendants of 1886, which Seurat, Signac, and others had founded in 1884 after rejection by the official Salon. The painters drew on the colour theories of Michel Eugène Chevreul, Ogden Rood, and Charles Henry. After Seurat's early death in 1891, Signac became the movement's leader and theorist, publishing D'Eugène Delacroix au Néo-Impressionnisme (1899).

Artists and ideas

Key figures included Camille Pissarro (who worked in the style from 1885 to about 1890), Henri-Edmond Cross, Théo van Rysselberghe, Maximilien Luce, and the young Henri Matisse, whose Luxe, Calme et Volupté (1904) carried divisionism toward Fauvism. Several Neo-Impressionists, including Signac, Cross, and Luce, held anarchist sympathies, seeing in the harmonious, scientifically ordered canvas an image of an ideal society. Their landscapes and scenes of labour and leisure often carried utopian overtones.

Technique

Painters avoided mixing pigments (which dulls colour) and instead juxtaposed small dots, dashes, or blocks of unmixed colour. At a distance the eye fuses them — "optical mixture" — producing luminosity greater, they believed, than conventional mixing. Compositions were carefully planned with preparatory drawings and oil sketches, in deliberate contrast to the spontaneity prized by the Impressionists.

Legacy

Neo-Impressionism influenced Fauvism, Orphism, early abstraction, and modern colour theory in art education, and Seurat's and Signac's works remain icons of post-Impressionist painting.
