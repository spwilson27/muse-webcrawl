Utility pole

A utility pole is a column or post, typically of wood, used to support overhead power lines and various other public utilities, such as electrical cable, fibre-optic cable, and related equipment such as transformers and street lights. It may also be called a telephone pole, telegraph pole, power pole, or hydro pole depending on region and use.

Structure and use

A typical distribution pole carries, from top to bottom: crossarms bearing the three phases of a primary distribution circuit (often 4–35 kV), a pole-mounted transformer stepping power down to secondary voltages, secondary service drops to individual premises, and, in the lower "communications space", telephone, cable television, and fibre-optic lines. Poles are set directly in the ground and may be stabilised by guy wires anchored to the earth. Standard North American poles are about 11–18 metres (35–60 ft) tall, classified by strength in standards such as ANSI O5.1.

Materials and treatment

Most poles are pressure-treated wood — southern yellow pine in the United States, Douglas fir and lodgepole pine in the west — impregnated with preservatives such as chromated copper arsenate (CCA), creosote, or pentachlorophenol to resist rot and insects; service lives of 25–50 years are typical. Poles are also made of steel, concrete, and fibre-reinforced composites, chosen for high-load, long-span, or termite-prone environments.

History and issues

Poles spread with the telegraph in the 1840s and then with telephone and electric power networks; Samuel Morse's lines followed the Baltimore–Washington corridor after experiments with buried cable failed. Modern concerns include storm damage and wildfire ignition risks from overhead lines, aesthetic objections that motivate "undergrounding" of utilities, pole-attachment disputes between utilities and broadband providers, and hazards to wildlife — poles provide perches and nesting sites for birds such as ospreys and eagles.
