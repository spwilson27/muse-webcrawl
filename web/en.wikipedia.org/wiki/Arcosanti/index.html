Arcosanti is an experimental town in Yavapai County, central Arizona, United States. Begun in 1970 by the Italian-American architect Paolo Soleri (1919–2013), it is intended as a demonstration of his concept of arcology — the fusion of architecture and ecology — proposing dense, three-dimensional, pedestrian cities as an alternative to urban sprawl.

Design

The master plan envisioned a compact city of about 5,000 people on a mesa above the Agua Fria River, occupying only a small fraction of its 860-acre (348 ha) preserve. Buildings cluster into large structures with passive solar features: south-facing apses that collect winter sun, a greenhouse belt supplying heat and food, and silt-cast concrete vaults and domes built with earth-forming techniques developed at Soleri's earlier Cosanti studio in Scottsdale. A completed city would minimize land use, energy consumption, and dependence on automobiles.

Construction

Construction has proceeded slowly, financed largely by the sale of Soleri's bronze and ceramic windbells and by workshop tuition. Thousands of volunteers and students have taken part in building campaigns since 1970. Completed elements include the Ceramics Apse, the East and West housing complexes, the Vaults, an amphitheatre, and a swimming pool; only a small fraction — often estimated at 3–5 percent — of the proposed city has been realized, and the resident population has typically numbered in the dozens.

Legacy

Although unfinished, Arcosanti has influenced thinking about sustainable urbanism, earth architecture, and intentional communities, and it remains an active site for workshops, performances, and the Cosanti Foundation's educational work. Critics note the tension between its utopian ambitions and its slow, incomplete materialization, while supporters regard it as a long-term laboratory whose value lies in experiment rather than completion.
