Archimedes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A classical bust of Archimedes with beard, looking thoughtful]

Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287 – c. 212 BC) was an Ancient Greek mathematician, physicist, engineer, astronomer, and inventor from the city of Syracuse in Sicily. Although few details of his life are known, he is regarded as one of the leading scientists in classical antiquity and among the greatest mathematicians of all time.

Archimedes anticipated modern calculus and analysis with his method of exhaustion, computing areas and volumes (the sphere's volume is 2/3 that of its circumscribing cylinder — a result he prized), and gave an approximation of π between 3+10/71 and 3+1/7. His On Floating Bodies founded hydrostatics (Archimedes' principle — 'Eureka!').

Inventions and war machines

He is credited with the Archimedes screw for raising water, compound pulleys, and war engines defending Syracuse against Rome — catapults, cranes that lifted ships (the Claw of Archimedes), and (less credibly) burning mirrors concentrating sunlight.

He was killed during the Roman capture of Syracuse in 212 BC, despite orders that he not be harmed, reportedly while absorbed in a geometric diagram ('Do not disturb my circles'). Cicero later located and restored his tomb, marked by the sphere and cylinder.

Mathematical legacy

Recovered works (the Archimedes Palimpsest, read by imaging in the 2000s) revealed his 'Method' of mechanical reasoning with indivisibles, a genuine precursor of integration. His writings influenced Galileo, Newton, and Leibniz profoundly.
