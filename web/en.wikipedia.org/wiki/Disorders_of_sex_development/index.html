SpecialtyMedical genetics, endocrinology

Disorders of sex development (DSDs), also known as differences in sex development, are congenital conditions in which development of chromosomal, gonadal, or anatomic sex is atypical. The terminology was proposed in 2006 at a consensus conference in Chicago, replacing older terms such as "intersex", "hermaphroditism" and "sex reversal" in medical usage, though terminology remains debated by clinicians, patients and advocacy groups.

Classification

The Chicago consensus classification organises DSDs by karyotype:

  • Sex chromosome DSD — including Turner syndrome (45,X and variants), Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), 45,X/46,XY mosaicism, and 46,XX/46,XY chimerism.
  • 46,XY DSD — disorders of testicular development, disorders of androgen synthesis or action (including complete and partial androgen insensitivity syndrome), and other conditions affecting male development.
  • 46,XX DSD — disorders of ovarian development, androgen excess (most commonly congenital adrenal hyperplasia due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency), and other conditions.

Causes and management

Causes include variants in genes controlling gonadal development and hormone pathways (such as SRY, NR5A1, AR and CYP21A2), and disruptions of hormone synthesis or response. Management is typically multidisciplinary, involving endocrinology, genetics, urology or gynaecology, and psychological support. Historical practice favoured early "normalising" genital surgery in infancy; this has become controversial, with many medical bodies and human-rights organisations urging that non-urgent irreversible procedures be deferred until the individual can participate in decisions.

Terminology debate

Some people with these conditions prefer "intersex" or "differences of sex development" and object to "disorder" as stigmatising; others value the medical clarity of DSD. Prevalence estimates vary widely depending on which conditions are counted, from about 1 in 4,500–5,500 births for markedly atypical genitalia to higher figures under broader definitions.
