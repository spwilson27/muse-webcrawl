Fourier-transform spectroscopy

Fourier-transform spectroscopy is a measurement technique whereby spectra are collected by measuring the coherence of an electromagnetic or other radiation source over time, using time-domain measurements that are then converted to a spectrum by a Fourier transform. The best-known form is Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), which measures how well a sample absorbs light at each infrared wavelength.

Principle

Most Fourier-transform spectrometers are based on the Michelson interferometer: light is split into two beams that travel different path lengths and recombine, producing an interference pattern (an interferogram) as one mirror moves. The Fourier transform of the interferogram yields the spectrum. Because all wavelengths are measured simultaneously (the Fellgett advantage) and throughput is high (the Jacquinot advantage), FTIR offers much better signal-to-noise ratios and speed than dispersive instruments.

Applications

FTIR is a workhorse of analytical chemistry, used to identify organic compounds, polymers, pharmaceuticals and pollutants, and in forensic analysis. Related Fourier-transform techniques are used in NMR spectroscopy (pulsed Fourier-transform NMR, the basis of MRI), mass spectrometry (FT-ICR), and astronomy.
