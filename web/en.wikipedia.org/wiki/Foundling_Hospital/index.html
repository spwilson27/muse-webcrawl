Foundling Hospital

The Foundling Hospital in London, England, was founded in 1739 by the philanthropic sea captain Thomas Coram. It was a children's home established for the "education and maintenance of exposed and deserted young children". The word "hospital" was used in its more general sense of a place of hospitality.

History

Coram campaigned for seventeen years before receiving a royal charter from George II in 1739. The first children were admitted in 1741 to a house in Hatton Garden; the famous purpose-built hospital in Bloomsbury, with its two wings around a courtyard, opened in stages from 1745. Mothers left tokens — coins, scraps of fabric, ribbons — with their babies so that a child could be identified if a parent later returned to claim it.

Art and music

The hospital became one of London's most fashionable charities and a pioneer of the public art gallery: William Hogarth, a founding governor, donated paintings and persuaded fellow artists to do the same. George Frideric Handel gave benefit performances of Messiah in the hospital chapel from 1750 and bequeathed a fair copy of the score.

Later years

The hospital moved out of London to Redhill and then Berkhamsted in the twentieth century; the Bloomsbury buildings were demolished in the 1920s, though part of the site became Coram's Fields, a children's park. The charity survives as Coram, and the Foundling Museum opened in 2004.
