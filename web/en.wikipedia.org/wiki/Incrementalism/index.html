Incrementalism

Incrementalism is a method of working by adding to a project using many small incremental changes instead of a few (extensively planned) large jumps. In public policy, incrementalism refers to the tendency for policies to change gradually, through small adjustments to existing programmes, rather than through comprehensive rational reform.

Lindblom and muddling through

Political scientist Charles E. Lindblom introduced the concept in his 1959 article 'The Science of Muddling Through', contrasting the 'branch method' of successive limited comparisons — building on past policy, comparing a restricted range of alternatives, and adjusting goals and means together — with the 'root method' of comprehensive rational analysis. Lindblom argued bounded rationality, disagreement and practical constraints make incrementalism both descriptive of real policy-making and often desirable.

Applications and criticism

Incrementalism has been applied to budgeting (Aaron Wildavsky), organisational change, software and law. Critics argue it can entrench the status quo, neglect long-term or systemic problems such as climate change, and undervalue innovation; supporters reply it reduces risk, allows learning from feedback, and fits the policy cycle. Related ideas include reformism in politics.
