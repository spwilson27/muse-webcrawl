Constitution of 1917

The Constituent Congress at Querétaro produced the world's first constitution to enshrine social rights.

The Political Constitution of the United Mexican States of 1917 (Constitución Política de los Estados Unidos Mexicanos) is the current constitution of Mexico. It was promulgated on 5 February 1917 by the Constituent Congress convened at Querétaro by Venustiano Carranza, First Chief of the Constitutionalist Army, during the Mexican Revolution. It replaced the liberal Constitution of 1857 and is celebrated as the first constitution in the world to include extensive social rights.

Key provisions

  • Article 3 — free, secular public education, barring religious instruction in schools and clergy from primary teaching (later softened by amendment).
  • Article 27 — national ownership of land, water and subsoil resources (including petroleum); restoration and redistribution of communal ejido lands; restrictions on foreign and church land ownership. This underpinned the 1938 oil expropriation under Lázaro Cárdenas.
  • Article 123 — comprehensive labour code: the eight-hour day, minimum wage, right to strike and organise, protections for women and children.
  • Articles 129–130 — strict separation of church and state, denial of legal personality to churches, and restrictions on clergy (partly liberalised in 1992).

The constitution also established a federal republic with a strong presidency, a bicameral congress, no presidential re-election, and the amparo writ for protecting individual rights.

Legacy

Though frequently amended — hundreds of times — and unevenly enforced, the 1917 constitution remains the legal frame of the Mexican state and a foundational symbol of the Revolution. Its centenary in 2017 was marked by ceremonies at Querétaro. Its social articles influenced later constitutions in Latin America and beyond.
