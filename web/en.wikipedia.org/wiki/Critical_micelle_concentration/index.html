Critical micelle concentration

In colloid and surface chemistry, the critical micelle concentration (CMC) is defined as the concentration of surfactants above which micelles form spontaneously and all additional surfactant added to the system will form micelles. Below the CMC, surfactant molecules are dispersed mainly as individual molecules (monomers) and accumulate at interfaces, lowering surface tension; above it, thermodynamic equilibrium favours aggregation into micelles — roughly spherical assemblies with hydrophobic tails pointing inward and hydrophilic heads outward in aqueous solution.

Behaviour around the CMC

Many physical properties of a surfactant solution change abruptly near the CMC, which is how it is measured: surface tension falls steeply with concentration and then plateaus, while turbidity, osmotic pressure, conductivity (for ionic surfactants) and solubilisation capacity show characteristic breaks in their concentration dependence.

Factors affecting the CMC

The CMC decreases as the hydrophobic tail length increases (roughly halving for each added methylene group for many ionic surfactants), is lower for nonionic than for ionic surfactants, and for ionic surfactants falls with added electrolyte, which screens head-group repulsion. Temperature effects are often modest and non-monotonic. Typical values range from around 8 mmol/L for sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) in pure water at 25 °C to micromolar values for long-chain or double-tailed surfactants.

Applications

The CMC is central to detergency, emulsification, drug delivery (micellar solubilisation), enhanced oil recovery and membrane-protein biochemistry, where detergents are used above their CMC to solubilise membranes.
