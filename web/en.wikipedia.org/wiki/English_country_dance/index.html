English country dance is a form of social folk dance which originated in Renaissance England, and was the root of the related traditions of contra dance and Scottish country dance. It is danced to music in a variety of metres using simple walking steps, and is characterised by figures performed by couples in sets — most commonly longways sets "for as many as will".

History

The form coalesced in the late 16th century from a mixture of courtly and country dances. Its canonical source is John Playford's The English Dancing Master (1651), which published melodies and instructions for over a hundred dances; the collection went through many editions into the 18th century. Country dancing was a staple of assemblies and balls throughout the Georgian era and features prominently in the novels of Jane Austen.

Revival

In the early 20th century Cecil Sharp collected and reinterpreted Playford's dances, founding the English Folk Dance Society (later the English Folk Dance and Song Society). The modern revival emphasises graceful, flowing figures danced to baroque and traditional tunes, often with a caller prompting the figures.

Figures

Common figures include the hey, cast off, lead through, poussette, rights and lefts, and the figure-eight. Dances are usually progressive: couples move up or down the set so that all dance with all.
