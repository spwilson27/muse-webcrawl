Californium is a synthetic chemical element with the symbol Cf and atomic number 98. It is an actinide and the sixth transuranium element to be synthesised. It was first produced in 1950 at the Lawrence Radiation Laboratory (now Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory) at the University of California, Berkeley, by Stanley G. Thompson, Kenneth Street Jr., Albert Ghiorso, and Glenn T. Seaborg, who bombarded curium-242 with alpha particles in a cyclotron. It was named for California and its university.

Properties

Californium is a silvery-white, moderately soft metal that tarnishes slowly in air. It has no stable isotopes; the longest-lived, californium-251, has a half-life of 898 years, while the most useful, californium-252, has a half-life of 2.645 years and an infamous intensity of radioactivity: one microgram emits 2.3 million neutrons per minute.

Applications

252Cf is one of the strongest known neutron emitters and is used as a portable neutron source: to start up nuclear reactors, in neutron activation analysis and neutron moisture gauges, in scanners that inspect aircraft components and luggage, and in some experimental cancer treatments (brachytherapy). Californium is produced in only a few facilities, principally Oak Ridge National Laboratory in the United States and the Research Institute of Atomic Reactors in Russia, and gram quantities command very high prices.

Hazards

Californium is extremely radioactive and a serious radiological hazard. It accumulates in skeletal tissue, where its radiation disrupts red blood cell production, and it is dangerous both as an external source and if ingested or inhaled. Handling requires heavy shielding and remote manipulation.
