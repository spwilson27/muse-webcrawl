John Edward Redmond (1 September 1856 – 6 March 1918) was an Irish nationalist politician, barrister, and MP in the House of Commons of the United Kingdom. He was best known as leader of the moderate Irish Parliamentary Party (IPP) from 1900 until his death in 1918, and for his achievement of the Government of Ireland Act 1914, which granted Ireland Home Rule within the United Kingdom (though it was suspended for the duration of the First World War).

Early life

Redmond was born at Ballytrent, County Wexford, into a Catholic gentry family with a long political tradition. He was educated at Clongowes Wood College and Trinity College Dublin, though he left without taking a degree. He worked for a time in the House of Commons as a clerk and spent several years in Australia, where he married Eleanor Dalton in 1883. Called to the Irish Bar in 1886, he combined a legal career with politics.

Parliamentary career

Redmond was first elected to Parliament in 1881 as MP for New Ross, in the by-election that followed his father's death. A loyal follower of Charles Stewart Parnell, he became one of Parnell's most trusted lieutenants. When the party split in 1890 over Parnell's leadership, Redmond led the smaller Parnellite faction through a decade in the political wilderness, keeping faith with Parnell's constitutional methods.

Leadership of the reunited party

In 1900 the two factions reunited and Redmond became chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party. Skilled in parliamentary tactics, he rebuilt the party's discipline and finances, raising funds extensively in the United States and Australia. After the general elections of 1910 the IPP held the balance of power at Westminster, and Redmond extracted from H. H. Asquith's Liberal government a commitment to a third Home Rule Bill. The Parliament Act 1911 removed the House of Lords' veto, and in September 1914 the Government of Ireland Act passed into law — the summit of Redmond's career — though its operation was suspended until the end of the war and special provision for Ulster remained unresolved.

First World War and decline

At the outbreak of the First World War Redmond pledged Ireland's support for the Allied cause, hoping to unite nationalist and unionist Ireland and to secure the implementation of Home Rule. His speech at Woodenbridge in September 1914 urged the Irish Volunteers to enlist, splitting the Volunteer movement. The Easter Rising of 1916, the execution of its leaders, and the government's handling of the crisis destroyed the political centre ground on which Redmond stood. Sinn Féin, advocating complete independence, won a string of by-elections. Redmond's health failed and he died in London on 6 March 1918. Within months the IPP was swept aside in the general election of December 1918.

Legacy

Redmond is remembered as the heir of Parnell and the last great leader of constitutional Irish nationalism. His strategy of achieving self-government by parliamentary means came within reach of success in 1914, but was overtaken by war and revolution. Redmond Park and the Redmond Bridge in Wexford commemorate his name.
