Stevie Wonder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Stevie Wonder at the keyboard in the 1970s, braided hair and tinted glasses, in the years of Innervisions]

Stevland Hardaway Morris (born Stevland Hardaway Judkins, 13 May 1950), known by his stage name Stevie Wonder, is an American singer-songwriter, musician and record producer, a child prodigy who became one of the most creative and beloved musicians of the twentieth century. Blind since infancy, he signed to Motown at 11 as 'Little Stevie Wonder', topped the charts at 13 with 'Fingertips', and in the 1970s seized artistic control to produce a run of albums — Talking Book (1972), Innervisions (1973), Fulfillingness' First Finale (1974), Songs in the Key of Life (1976) — that stand among popular music's highest achievements.

His honours include 25 Grammy Awards, an Academy Award ('I Just Called to Say I Love You', 1984), the Presidential Medal of Freedom (2014) and the campaign, crowned in 1983, that made Martin Luther King Jr.'s birthday a US national holiday.

Motown prodigy to auteur

Born in Saginaw, Michigan, Wonder taught himself piano, harmonica and drums in childhood. Motown's Berry Gordy signed him in 1961; the live 'Fingertips' made him a star, and 'Uptight (Everything's Alright)' (1965) and a run of hits established him. At 21 he renegotiated for creative control — the deal that let the seventies albums happen, their synthesizer orchestras (built with TONTO's Malcolm Cecil and Robert Margouleff) reinventing soul's sound.

Later career

A near-fatal car accident in 1973 (he was in a coma for days) deepened the spirituality of Innervisions, finished after his recovery. The eighties brought 'Ebony and Ivory' (with Paul McCartney), the King holiday campaign and tireless benefit work; subsequent decades saw fewer albums, constant honour, and tours that function as national celebrations.
