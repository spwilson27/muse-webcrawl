Theropithecus is a genus of primates in the family Cercopithecidae (Old World monkeys), containing a single living species, the gelada (Theropithecus gelada) of the Ethiopian highlands, and several extinct species. It is closely related to baboons of the genus Papio, from which it split roughly four million years ago.

The living gelada is distinguished from true baboons by its diet, which consists overwhelmingly of grasses — it is the only living primate that is primarily a grazer — and by the bare patch of skin on its chest, which in males is bright red and hourglass-shaped and serves as a signal of status and reproductive condition.

Extinct species

The fossil record shows that Theropithecus was once much more diverse and widespread than it is today. Extinct species include T. oswaldi, a very large form found at sites across Africa and as far afield as Spain and India, as well as T. brumpti and T. quadratirostris. These extinct forms were among the most abundant primates in the Pliocene and Pleistocene fossil assemblages of eastern Africa, and their remains are frequently found alongside those of early hominins at sites such as Olduvai Gorge and Koobi Fora.

The large extinct species appear to have been terrestrial grazers occupying open grassland, a niche that contracted as climates changed, leaving the modern gelada confined to high-altitude grasslands of the Ethiopian Plateau.

Taxonomy

The genus was erected by Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire in 1843. Molecular and morphological studies place Theropithecus within the tribe Papionini, as the sister group to Papio or to a clade containing Papio and Lophocebus.
