Predatory pricing

Predatory pricing is a pricing strategy in which a firm with market power sets prices so low — typically below an appropriate measure of its own costs — that rival firms cannot compete profitably and are driven from the market or deterred from entering. Once competition is eliminated, the predator is expected to raise prices to monopoly levels and recoup its earlier losses. Predatory pricing is illegal under the competition laws of many jurisdictions, including United States antitrust law and European Union competition law.

Legal tests

Modern courts generally require two elements, following the US Supreme Court's decision in Brooke Group Ltd. v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp. (1993): pricing below an appropriate measure of cost, and a dangerous probability that the predator will recoup its investment in below-cost prices. The influential Areeda–Turner test proposed average variable cost as a proxy for marginal cost. In the EU, the AKZO test treats prices below average variable cost as presumptively abusive, and prices below average total cost as abusive when part of a plan to eliminate a competitor.

Economic debate

Economists associated with the Chicago School have argued that successful predation is rare and often irrational, since the predator loses money on every sale, must outlast its prey, and faces new entry if it raises prices afterwards. Others point to game-theoretic models — reputation effects, financial constraints on prey, and multi-market contact — under which predation can be a rational strategy. Distinguishing predatory pricing from vigorous legitimate price competition remains one of the hardest problems in competition policy. See also antitrust and dumping.
