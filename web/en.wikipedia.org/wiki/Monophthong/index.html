Monophthong

A monophthong (from Greek monophthongos, from μόνος monos, "single" and φθόγγος phthongos, "sound") is a pure vowel sound; one whose articulation at both beginning and end is relatively fixed, and which does not move towards a new position in the vowel space. In the International Phonetic Alphabet, monophthongs are transcribed with a single vowel symbol, such as [iː], [e], [a], or [u].

Monophthongs contrast with diphthongs, in which the vowel quality glides from one position to another within a single syllable (as in English boy [bɔɪ]), and with triphthongs, which glide through two such transitions. A pure vowel is sometimes called a "glide-free" vowel.

In English

English vowel systems vary by dialect in how many vowels are monophthongal. Received Pronunciation is traditionally analysed with twelve monophthongs, including the long vowels /iː ɑː ɔː uː ɜː/ and the short vowels /ɪ e æ ʌ ɒ ʊ ə/. In General American, several vowels written as single letters are actually slightly diphthongised, such as the "long a" /eɪ/ and "long o" /oʊ/, while in Scottish English the corresponding vowels are generally pure monophthongs. In many dialects of the southern United States, the diphthong /aɪ/ is monophthongised to [aː] (Southern drawl).

Monophthongization

Monophthongization is a sound change by which a diphthong becomes a monophthong — for example, the smoothing of Middle English diphthongs in various modern dialects, or the monophthongisation of Ancient Greek diphthongs such as ει and ου in later Greek. The reverse process is diphthongization (or vowel breaking).
