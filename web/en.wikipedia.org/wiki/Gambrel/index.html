Gambrel

A gambrel or gambrel roof is a usually symmetrical two-sided roof with two slopes on each side. The upper slope is positioned at a shallow angle, while the lower slope is steep. The design provides the advantages of a sloped roof while maximising headroom inside the building's upper level and shortening what would otherwise be a tall roof.

Etymology

The term gambrel comes from the medieval Latin gamba, "horse's leg": the shape of the roof recalls the hind leg of a horse, whose upper portion (the hock) bends at a similar angle. The word was also used for a stick used by butchers to hang slaughtered animals by their legs.

Origins and use

Roofs of this form appear in the Netherlands and England in the 17th century and were carried to North America, where the gambrel became the signature roof of Dutch Colonial houses and of the classic American barn. The steep lower slope nearly vertical at the eaves gives a barn's hayloft far more usable volume than a simple gable roof, which made the gambrel the standard for dairy and livestock barns across the United States in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

The gambrel is related to the European mansard roof; unlike a mansard, a gambrel usually has gable ends rather than slopes on all four sides, and its two slopes meet at an angle rather than a curve.

Examples

Notable early American examples include the gambrel roof of the First Harrison Gray Otis House in Boston (1796). Gambrel-roofed barns of red-painted wood are an iconic image of rural America, and the form remains popular for sheds, garages and kit-built barns.
