Urban poverty

Informal settlements house a large share of the world's urban poor.

Urban poverty is poverty concentrated in urban areas, affecting residents of cities and towns who lack adequate income, housing, services, and security. While global poverty was once predominantly rural, rapid urbanization — especially in Africa and Asia — has shifted its geography: the World Bank and UN-Habitat estimate that more than a billion people live in slums and informal settlements, and the urban share of the poor continues to rise.

Characteristics

Urban poverty differs from rural poverty in its dependence on cash income: food, rent, water, and transport must be purchased, making the urban poor highly vulnerable to price shocks and job loss. Other features include insecure tenure and eviction risk, overcrowded and unsanitary housing, exposure to pollution and flooding, informal and precarious work, and food insecurity despite proximity to markets. Poverty measures based on income alone may understate these deprivations, prompting multidimensional indices that include housing quality, water and sanitation, and education.

Causes

Drivers include rural-to-urban migration outpacing formal job creation, deindustrialization and informalization of labor markets, unaffordable housing and speculative land markets, austerity and weak municipal finance, and segregation by race, caste, or migration status. In wealthy countries, urban poverty is associated with concentrated disadvantage in segregated neighborhoods, housing costs, and the working poor.

Responses

Policy responses include slum upgrading and tenure regularization, social housing, conditional cash transfers (originated in Latin American cities), basic-service extension, public transport subsidies, and community-driven development. Critics caution against forced evictions and "beautification" clearances that displace the poor, and debate whether place-based or people-based programs work better.
