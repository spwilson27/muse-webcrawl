Rationalization (psychology)

In psychology, rationalization is a defense mechanism in which a person constructs a logical, socially acceptable explanation for behaviour, feelings, or outcomes that were actually driven by less acceptable or unconscious motives. The explanation is generated after the fact and protects the individual from anxiety, guilt, or loss of self-esteem.

Psychoanalytic origins

The concept was introduced into psychoanalysis by Ernest Jones in 1908 and developed by Sigmund Freud and Anna Freud within the catalogue of ego defense mechanisms. Classic illustrations include Aesop's "sour grapes" fable — devaluing what one cannot obtain — and its opposite, "sweet lemon", in which an unwanted outcome is reframed as desirable.

Examples

Common examples include a student who fails an examination blaming unfair marking rather than lack of preparation, or a person who, having made an expensive purchase, exaggerates its benefits and dismisses its flaws. Rationalization overlaps with cognitive dissonance reduction and with post-hoc justification observed in experimental social psychology.

Clinical significance

Used occasionally, rationalization is a normal part of coping. Persistently relied upon, it can block insight and maintain maladaptive behaviour, and it is a target of exploration in psychotherapy, where patients are helped to recognize the feelings the explanation conceals.
