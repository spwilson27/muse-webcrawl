Tintype

A tintype, also known as a melainotype or ferrotype, is a photograph made by creating a direct positive on a thin sheet of metal coated with a dark lacquer or enamel and used as the support for the photographic emulsion. The process was introduced by Hannibal Goodwin and Adolphe-Alexandre Martin in the 1850s and became extremely popular during the 1860s and 1870s, particularly in the United States during and after the American Civil War.

History

The tintype process was first described by Adolphe-Alexandre Martin in France in 1853. In the United States, Hannibal Goodwin and Victor Griswold developed and marketed the process in 1856. Because tintypes were inexpensive, quick to produce, and durable, they rapidly displaced the more costly ambrotype for portrait photography. Itinerant photographers set up at fairs, carnivals, and army camps, and Civil War soldiers commonly had tintype portraits made to send home to their families. Tintypes remained common into the early twentieth century, especially as novelty portraits at tourist attractions.

Process

A tintype is made on a thin sheet of iron coated with collodion containing silver halides. After exposure in the camera, the plate is developed, fixed, washed, and dried, producing a positive image in a matter of minutes. Because the image is an underexposed negative viewed against the dark backing of the plate and lacquer, it appears as a positive. The finished photograph is a mirror image of the subject. Tintypes were often hand-colored with pigments and placed in paper sleeves or small albums; miniature "gem" tintypes were cut from a single plate exposed through a multi-lens camera.

Characteristics

Tintypes are lightweight, opaque, and do not require a negative file for reproduction, which made them cheap—often costing only a few cents. Their durability means that many nineteenth-century tintypes survive today, providing a rich visual record of ordinary people of the era. The support is iron rather than tin, despite the name.
