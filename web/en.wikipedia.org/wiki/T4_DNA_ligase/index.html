T4 DNA ligase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

T4 DNA ligase is an enzyme purified from bacteriophage T4 that catalyses the formation of a phosphodiester bond between juxtaposed 5′ phosphate and 3′ hydroxyl termini in duplex DNA or RNA. It is the most widely used DNA ligase in molecular biology laboratories, principally for joining DNA fragments together during molecular cloning.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds in three steps using ATP as a cofactor. First, the enzyme reacts with ATP to form a covalent enzyme–AMP intermediate, with release of pyrophosphate. The AMP group is then transferred to the 5′-phosphate at the nick or end junction, and finally the 3′-hydroxyl attacks this activated phosphate, forming the phosphodiester bond and releasing AMP.

Substrates and properties

Unlike the E. coli DNA ligase, which is largely restricted to sealing nicks and joining cohesive ("sticky") ends, T4 DNA ligase can also join blunt-ended DNA fragments, albeit much less efficiently; this activity can be enhanced by macromolecular crowding agents such as polyethylene glycol. The enzyme also ligates DNA ends to RNA in hybrid duplexes to a limited extent, and single-stranded nicks in duplex substrates are its most efficient substrate. Typical ligation reactions are run at 16 °C overnight or at room temperature for shorter periods, balancing enzyme activity against the annealing stability of short overhangs.

Applications

T4 DNA ligase is a routine reagent in recombinant DNA work: inserting restriction-digested fragments into plasmid vectors, assembling synthetic genes, adaptor ligation in sequencing library preparation, and site-directed mutagenesis protocols. Commercial preparations are standardised in Weiss units of activity.
