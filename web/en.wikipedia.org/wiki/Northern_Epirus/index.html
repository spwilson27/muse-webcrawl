Northern Epirus

Northern Epirus (Greek: Βόρεια Ήπειρος, Vóreia Ípiros; Albanian: Epiri i Veriut) is a term used for the northern part of the historical region of Epirus, which today lies within the borders of Albania. The southern part of Epirus lies in Greece. The area is home to a significant ethnic Greek minority, alongside Albanians, Aromanians and others.

Geography

Northern Epirus corresponds approximately to the modern Albanian counties of Gjirokastër, Vlorë and Berat in the south of the country. Important towns include Gjirokastër, Sarandë, Delvinë, Himarë and Permet. The terrain is mountainous, descending to the Ionian Sea coast along the Albanian Riviera.

History

In antiquity the region was inhabited by Greek tribes such as the Chaonians and formed part of the kingdom of Epirus. It later passed through Roman, Byzantine and Ottoman rule. During the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913, Greek forces occupied the area, but the Protocol of Florence (1913) assigned it to the newly created Albanian state.

In 1914 the local Greek population proclaimed the Autonomous Republic of Northern Epirus, centred on Gjirokastër (Argyrokastro), which was briefly recognised by the Protocol of Corfu before the outbreak of the First World War led to its dissolution. Greece again occupied the region during the Greco-Italian War of 1940–1941, but it was restored to Albania after the Second World War.

Demographics and minority issues

The size of the Greek minority in Albania has long been disputed between the two countries, with Albanian census figures lower than Greek estimates. Under the communist regime of Enver Hoxha, a "minority zone" of 99 villages was officially designated where Greek-language education was permitted. Relations between Greece and Albania were affected by the issue for decades; a formal state of war between the two countries, dating from 1940, was only lifted in 1987.
