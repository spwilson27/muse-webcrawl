The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, informally known as the LDS Church or Mormon Church, is a restorationist Christian church headquartered in Salt Lake City, Utah. It is the largest denomination in the Latter Day Saint movement founded by Joseph Smith during the early 19th-century Second Great Awakening.

History

Joseph Smith said that in 1820 he received a vision of God the Father and Jesus Christ (the First Vision), and that in 1823 the angel Moroni directed him to golden plates from which he translated the Book of Mormon, published in 1830. The church was organized on April 6, 1830. Early members gathered in Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois, where Smith was killed by a mob in 1844. Under Brigham Young, most members migrated west, arriving in the Salt Lake Valley in 1847. The church practiced plural marriage until the 1890 Manifesto ended the practice. In 1978 the church extended priesthood ordination to men of African descent.

Beliefs and practices

Latter-day Saints believe in God the Father, Jesus Christ, and the Holy Ghost as three distinct beings united in purpose. They accept the Bible and regard the Book of Mormon as another testament of Jesus Christ, along with the Doctrine and Covenants and the Pearl of Great Price. Distinctive practices include baptism by immersion, temple worship and sealings, family history work, missionary service by young adults, adherence to the Word of Wisdom health code, tithing, and a lay ministry led by a president regarded as a prophet.

Organization

The church is led by the First Presidency and the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles. Local congregations are called wards and branches, grouped into stakes. As of recent reports the church claims more than 17 million members worldwide, with temples operating in many countries.
