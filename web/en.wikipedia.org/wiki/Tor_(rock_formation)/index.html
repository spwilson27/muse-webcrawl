Tor (rock formation)

Tor
TypeRock outcrop / landform
FormationWeathering and erosion of jointed bedrock
Typical rocksGranite, also sandstone, dolerite, schist
Notable examplesDartmoor, Bodmin Moor, Pennines, Matobo Hills

A tor is a large, free-standing rock outcrop that rises abruptly from the smooth and gentle slopes of a rounded hill summit or ridge crest. Tors are most commonly associated with granite landscapes, notably the moorlands of south-west England, but they occur in many rock types and on every continent.

Formation

The origin of tors was long debated. The classic two-stage hypothesis, associated with David Linton, proposes that deep chemical weathering along joints in the bedrock, under a warmer past climate, produced rounded corestones surrounded by decomposed rock (growan or grus). Later erosion — especially periglacial solifluction during the Ice Ages — stripped away the loose material, leaving the resistant corestones stacked as castellated outcrops. Alternative views emphasise differential weathering and frost action alone. Tors typically display horizontal and vertical jointing that gives them a stacked, blocky appearance.

Distribution and examples

Famous tors include Haytor and Great Staple Tor on Dartmoor, the Cheesewring on Bodmin Moor, and Brimham Rocks in Yorkshire (millstone grit). Granite tors also form the summit features of the Cairngorms in Scotland, the Matobo Hills of Zimbabwe, and parts of the Korean and Chinese granite landscapes, where similar forms are sometimes called inselbergs when they rise from plains.

Etymology

The word tor derives from an Old Welsh word for a bulge or belly, and survives in many place-names in Wales and the West Country, including Glastonbury Tor.
