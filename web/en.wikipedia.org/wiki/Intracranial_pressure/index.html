Intracranial pressure

Intracranial pressure (ICP) is the pressure exerted by fluids — principally cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) — inside the skull and on the brain tissue. In a healthy adult lying down it is normally about 7–15 mmHg. Because the skull is a rigid container, the Monro–Kellie doctrine states that the combined volume of brain, blood and CSF is constant, so an increase in one component must be offset by a decrease in another or the pressure rises.

Raised intracranial pressure

Sustained elevation of ICP (intracranial hypertension) is a medical emergency. Causes include traumatic brain injury, tumours, haemorrhage, hydrocephalus, meningitis and cerebral oedema. Warning signs include headache, vomiting, altered consciousness, pupillary changes and the Cushing reflex (hypertension, bradycardia and irregular respiration). Untreated, raised ICP reduces cerebral perfusion and can cause brain herniation and death.

Measurement and management

ICP can be measured with an external ventricular drain (the reference standard) or intraparenchymal probes; lumbar puncture manometry is used in selected conditions but is dangerous when a mass lesion is present. Management involves head elevation, sedation, osmotic therapy with mannitol or hypertonic saline, CSF drainage, control of fever and seizures, and in refractory cases decompressive craniectomy. A related condition, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, produces raised pressure without a mass lesion and typically affects young women with obesity, causing headache and visual symptoms.
