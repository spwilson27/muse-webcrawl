Ramsey theory, named after the British mathematician and philosopher Frank P. Ramsey, is a branch of combinatorics that focuses on the appearance of order in large structures. Problems in Ramsey theory typically ask a question of the form: "How many elements of some structure must there be to guarantee that a particular property will hold?" The field is often summarized by the motto that complete disorder is impossible: in any sufficiently large structure, some pattern must emerge.

Ramsey's theorem

The founding result, proved by Ramsey in 1930 in a paper on mathematical logic, states that for any given numbers of colours and sizes there is a number R so large that every colouring of the edges of a complete graph on R vertices contains a monochromatic complete subgraph of the prescribed size. The smallest such numbers are called Ramsey numbers; for example R(3, 3) = 6, which yields the classic party problem: in any group of six people, there are three who mutually know each other or three who mutually do not. Only a handful of Ramsey numbers are known exactly, and computing them is famously difficult — Paul Erdős remarked that if aliens demanded R(5, 5) humanity might find it, but if they demanded R(6, 6) humanity should attempt to destroy the aliens.

Other results

The theory includes van der Waerden's theorem on monochromatic arithmetic progressions, Schur's theorem on solutions to x + y = z in one colour, the Hales–Jewett theorem, and Graham's number, which arose from a Ramsey-type problem. Ramsey theory has applications in number theory, geometry, ergodic theory, theoretical computer science, and information theory.
