Strike-slip fault

In geology, a strike-slip fault (also called a wrench fault, transverse fault, or lateral fault) is a fault surface that is usually vertical or nearly so, across which the rocks on either side have moved mostly horizontally. If the block opposite an observer looking across the fault moves to the right, the fault is termed right-lateral (dextral); if it moves to the left, the fault is termed left-lateral (sinistral).

Strike-slip faults form where the principal stresses are horizontal, typically at transform plate boundaries or within continents deforming in response to distant plate motions. Large strike-slip systems are called transcurrent faults.

Examples

Famous strike-slip faults include the San Andreas Fault in California (right-lateral), the North Anatolian Fault in Turkey (right-lateral), the Dead Sea Transform (left-lateral), the Alpine Fault in New Zealand, and the Altyn Tagh fault in China, one of the largest continental strike-slip structures on Earth.

Structures

Strike-slip zones commonly develop characteristic structures such as pull-apart basins at releasing bends, pressure ridges at restraining bends, flower structures in cross-section, and en echelon folds and fractures. Motion combining strike-slip with normal or reverse components produces oblique-slip faults; the terms transtension and transpression describe these combined regimes.
