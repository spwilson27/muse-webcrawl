Johannes Stark (15 April 1874 – 21 June 1957) was a German physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1919 "for his discovery of the Doppler effect in canal rays and the splitting of spectral lines in electric fields". The splitting phenomenon bears his name as the Stark effect. He later became infamous as a leading proponent of the nationalist, anti-Semitic "Deutsche Physik" movement under the Nazi regime.

Scientific career

Stark studied at the University of Munich and held professorships at Hanover, Aachen, Greifswald and Würzburg. In 1905 he demonstrated the Doppler effect in canal rays (positive ion beams), and in 1913 he discovered that a strong electric field splits spectral lines — work announced almost simultaneously with the Italian physicist Antonino Lo Surdo. With the Nobel money he established a private laboratory. A combative personality, Stark quarrelled with much of the physics establishment and left academic life in the early 1920s for business ventures.

Deutsche Physik and the Nazi era

Stark joined the Nazi Party in 1930 and, with fellow Nobel laureate Philipp Lenard, championed "Aryan physics", denouncing relativity and quantum mechanics as "Jewish" science and attacking theorists such as Werner Heisenberg in party newspapers. The regime rewarded him with the presidency of the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt (1933) and of the German Research Association, but his influence waned as pragmatic officials sidelined the movement. After the war a denazification court sentenced him in 1947 to four years in a labour camp as a "major offender", a sentence later suspended. He died in 1957.
