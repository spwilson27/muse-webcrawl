Phreatomagmatic eruption

Phreatomagmatic eruption
Cause: Magma interacting explosively with external water
Products: Fine ash, base surges, accretionary lapilli
Typical landforms: Maars, tuff rings, tuff cones
Examples: Surtsey (1963), Taal, Ukinrek maars (1977), Lake Nyos crater formation

Phreatomagmatic eruptions are volcanic eruptions driven by the explosive interaction of magma with external water — groundwater, lake or sea water, or ice. When water flashes to steam on contact with magma it expands enormously, fragmenting the magma into very fine ash and greatly increasing the explosivity of the eruption beyond that of the magma's own dissolved gases.

Characteristics

Phreatomagmatic deposits are typically well-bedded, fine-grained and rich in fragmented country rock. The eruptions produce base surges — fast-moving, ground-hugging clouds of steam, ash and rock — along with accretionary lapilli and bomb sags. Where eruptions occur in shallow water or saturated ground they blast out broad, low-rimmed craters called maars, often later occupied by lakes, ringed by tuff rings. Surtsey, Iceland (1963–67), was phreatomagmatic while sea water had access to the vent, then became effusive once the island sealed the vent off. The 1977 Ukinrek eruption in Alaska formed two maars in ten days. Sustained magma–water interaction also shaped many of the maar-diatreme volcanoes of the Eifel and the Cameroon Volcanic Line, including the crater of Lake Nyos.

Comparison

Phreatomagmatic eruptions are distinguished from purely phreatic eruptions, in which water is heated by magma but no fresh magma is itself ejected, and from magmatic eruptions driven only by exsolved volcanic gases. In practice many eruptions pass through phreatomagmatic phases as water availability changes.
