Diatreme

Diatreme
Type: Volcanic pipe / vent structure
Formed by: Gas-rich explosive eruption (phreatomagmatic or magmatic)
Fill: Volcanic breccia, kimberlite, lamproite
Associated landform: Maar crater at the surface

A diatreme is a volcanic pipe formed by a gas-rich explosive eruption. Where rising magma — very often kimberlite — encounters groundwater or otherwise releases gas violently, the explosion excavates a conical, carrot-shaped funnel through the crust, typically hundreds of metres to more than a kilometre deep, filled with broken rock (breccia) derived from both the magma and the shattered country rock.

Structure

A typical diatreme system has three parts: a shallow crater at the surface (often occupied by a maar lake, such as Lake Nyos in Cameroon), the tapering diatreme zone filled with tuffisite and breccia, and a deeper hypabyssal root zone of coherent igneous rock grading into dikes. Erosion may strip away the crater and expose the diatreme itself, as in many of the diamond-bearing kimberlite pipes of South Africa, Canada and Siberia.

Economic importance

Diatremes are economically significant because kimberlite diatremes are the primary source of mined diamonds: the ascending kimberlite magma samples the deep mantle and can carry diamonds to the surface rapidly enough to preserve them. Famous examples include the Kimberley pipes of South Africa and the Ekati and Diavik pipes of Canada's Northwest Territories. Similar volcanic pipes host the famous maar lakes of the Eifel in Germany and the gas-charged crater lakes of the Cameroon Volcanic Line.
