Meromictic lake

Meromictic lake
Upper layer: Mixolimnion (circulates)
Boundary: Chemocline
Lower layer: Monimolimnion (stagnant, anoxic)
Examples: Lake Nyos, Lake Kivu, Lake Tanganyika, Lake Cadagno, Black Sea (largest example)

A meromictic lake is a lake whose water layers do not completely mix. Unlike most lakes, which turn over seasonally (holomictic lakes), a meromictic lake retains a permanent density stratification: a circulating upper layer, the mixolimnion, overlies a stagnant, denser bottom layer, the monimolimnion, separated by a sharp chemical gradient called the chemocline.

Causes

Meromixis arises when deep water is made denser by dissolved salts or gases (crenogenic, ectogenic or biogenic meromixis) and when a lake is deep relative to its surface area, sheltered from wind, or shaded, so mixing energy cannot overcome the density gradient. The bottom layer typically becomes anoxic and accumulates dissolved carbon dioxide, methane, hydrogen sulfide and nutrients over centuries.

Hazards and examples

Gas-charged meromictic crater lakes can be deadly: Lake Monoun (1984) and Lake Nyos (1986) in Cameroon suffered limnic eruptions in which overturn released huge volumes of dissolved CO₂ that asphyxiated people and animals in surrounding valleys. Lake Kivu, between Rwanda and the DR Congo, holds vast quantities of dissolved CO₂ and methane, the latter now extracted for power generation. Other notable meromictic lakes include Lake Tanganyika, Switzerland's Lake Cadagno, Soap Lake in Washington, and the Black Sea, the world's largest meromictic water body.
