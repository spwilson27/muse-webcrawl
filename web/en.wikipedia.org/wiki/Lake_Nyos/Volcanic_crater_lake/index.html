Volcanic crater lake
Formation: Water filling a volcanic crater, caldera or maar
Notable examples: Crater Lake (Oregon), Lake Toba, Lake Nyos, Kawah Ijen, Lake Taupo, Kelimutu

A volcanic crater lake is a lake that forms within a volcanic depression — a summit crater, a collapse caldera, or a broad explosion crater (maar) — after eruptive activity subsides and the basin fills with rain, groundwater or meltwater. Crater lakes range from deep, cold and nearly pure (Crater Lake in Oregon, about 594 m deep) to hot, colourful and extremely acidic.

Chemistry and hazards

Because many crater lakes sit directly above active volcanic conduits, their waters interact with volcanic gases and hydrothermal fluids. Some, such as Kawah Ijen in Indonesia, are among the most acidic natural waters on Earth (pH below 0.5). Gas-rich lakes can store enormous quantities of dissolved carbon dioxide in their depths: at Lake Nyos, Cameroon, a limnic eruption in 1986 released this gas catastrophically, killing about 1,746 people. Changes in a crater lake's temperature, colour and level are closely monitored as indicators of volcanic unrest, since heating or displacement of a lake can precede eruptions and generate destructive lahars, as at Ruapehu in New Zealand.

Notable examples

Famous crater lakes include Crater Lake (Mount Mazama, Oregon), the caldera Lake Toba in Indonesia — the largest volcanic lake in the world — Lake Taupo in New Zealand, the tri-coloured lakes of Kelimutu in Flores, Lake Batur in Bali, and the maar lakes of the Eifel in Germany. Several support distinctive ecosystems isolated from other waters, and some, including Nyos, Monoun and Kivu, are managed with degassing or monitoring programmes because of the gas hazard.
