Maar

A maar is a broad, low-relief volcanic crater caused by a phreatomagmatic eruption — an explosion that occurs when rising magma comes into contact with groundwater. The resulting crater typically lies below the surrounding ground surface and often fills with water to form a shallow crater lake. The word comes from the German Maar, used for the volcanic lakes of the Eifel region, and ultimately from Latin mare ("sea").

Characteristics

Maars range from a few hundred metres to several kilometres across and are surrounded by a low rim of ejected debris (a tuff ring). Beneath the crater floor a carrot-shaped diatreme — a pipe filled with breccia — extends into the subsurface. Maars are the second most common volcanic landform on land; famous examples include the Eifel maars in Germany, the maar lakes of the Cameroon volcanic line (such as Lake Nyos and Lake Monoun), the Hopi Buttes of Arizona, and Soda Lakes in Nevada.

Significance

Many maar lakes are important scientific archives: their undisturbed sediments record past climates (as at Lagerstätte-type fossil sites such as the Messel Pit, itself a maar lake deposit), and in Cameroon the deep, gas-charged maars Nyos and Monoun became infamous for deadly limnic eruptions.
