Limnic eruption

Also known asLake overturn
Gas involvedPrimarily carbon dioxide (CO₂)
Known eventsLake Monoun (1984); Lake Nyos (1986)
At-risk lakesNyos, Monoun (Cameroon); Kivu (DRC/Rwanda)

A limnic eruption, also known as a lake overturn, is a rare natural disaster in which dissolved carbon dioxide suddenly erupts from the deep waters of a lake, forming a gas cloud capable of suffocating wildlife, livestock, and humans. Such eruptions may also displace lake water to form a tsunami in the lake.

Mechanism

In certain volcanic crater lakes, CO₂ seeps into the bottom water from magma below and dissolves under high pressure, producing a stably stratified (meromictic) lake whose deep layer becomes saturated with gas. A trigger such as a landslide, earthquake, or heavy rainfall can disturb the stratification; as deep water rises, pressure falls, gas comes out of solution in a runaway "soda-bottle" effect, and a vast volume of CO₂ bursts out. The gas, denser than air, hugs the ground and displaces breathable air in valleys and settlements.

Recorded events

Only two events are confirmed. On 15 August 1984, Lake Monoun in Cameroon erupted, killing 37 people. On 21 August 1986, Lake Nyos, also in Cameroon, released an estimated 1.6 million tonnes of CO₂, which travelled up to 25 km and suffocated 1,746 people and thousands of livestock — one of the deadliest natural-gas disasters on record. Scientists have also identified an ancient mass extinction near Lake Kivu in geological time suggesting prehistoric eruptions there.

Prevention

Degassing pipes installed at Lakes Nyos (2001) and Monoun (2003) siphon deep water to the surface in self-sustaining fountains, slowly lowering gas concentrations. At Lake Kivu, large-scale methane extraction for power generation pursues the same end.
