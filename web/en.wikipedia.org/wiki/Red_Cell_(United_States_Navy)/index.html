Red Cell (officially Naval Security Force Exercise and Evaluation Teams, OPNAV N34) was a United States Navy unit responsible in the 1980s and early 1990s for testing the security of naval installations in a red team role. Its members — largely drawn from the Navy SEALs — attempted to infiltrate bases, "steal" weapons and equipment, plant mock explosive devices, and even take hostages, in order to expose vulnerabilities in base security.

History

The unit was created in 1984 at the instigation of Vice Admiral James A. Lyons and placed under the command of Commander Richard Marcinko, the founding commander of SEAL Team Six. Red Cell reported striking successes: its operators claimed to have penetrated highly sensitive facilities, including nuclear submarine bases, and on one occasion to have placed simulated bombs near Air Force One. Their unannounced exercises caused alarm and resentment among base commands, and simulated violence against captured personnel drew controversy.

Marcinko's flamboyant style and conflicts with the naval establishment eventually brought the unit under scrutiny; he was convicted in 1990 of conspiracy to defraud the government in a procurement matter and served prison time. Red Cell was disbanded in 1992. Marcinko later popularised the unit in his bestselling autobiography Rogue Warrior (1992).

Legacy

Red Cell is frequently cited as a defining example of red teaming in security practice, and its story informed later debates about realistic security testing, the ethics of unannounced exercises, and the institutional friction such units generate.
