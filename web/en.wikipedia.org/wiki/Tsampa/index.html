Tsampa (Tibetan: རྩམ་པ་, Wylie: rtsam pa) is a staple food of Tibet and the Himalayan region, consisting of roasted flour, usually of barley (nas rtsam), though sometimes of wheat or other grains. Because it is made from flour that has already been roasted, tsampa needs no further cooking, which makes it an ideal food for nomads, travellers, and mountaineers in a region where fuel is scarce.

Preparation and use

To eat tsampa, a few spoonfuls of the flour are placed in a bowl, and butter tea (po cha) is poured over it. The mixture is kneaded by hand into small dough balls or a paste and eaten directly, often with dried cheese (chura), tsampa being one of the few Tibetan foods eaten without chopsticks. It may also be made into a porridge, mixed with tea, water, or even beer, and sweetened variations exist. Tossed into the air as an offering, tsampa also has ritual uses: handfuls of the flour are thrown at weddings and funerals, and it is offered to deities at New Year (Losar) celebrations.

Cultural significance

Tsampa is so central to Tibetan identity that Tibetans are sometimes jokingly called "tsampa-eaters" (tsampapa). Barley is one of the few grains that ripens reliably at the high altitudes of the Tibetan Plateau, and tsampa's keeping qualities and energy density suited both nomadic herders and caravan trade. In the modern era it is eaten throughout Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan, and Tibetan communities in exile, and similar roasted-flour preparations exist across Central Asia and Mongolia.
