Animal sacrifice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Animal sacrifice is the ritual killing of animals as part of a religious ceremony, typically as an offering to a deity or deities, to maintain or restore a relationship between human beings and the sacred. The practice is attested in cultures worldwide from prehistory onward, and it continues today in several religions, including Hinduism, Islam, and some Afro-Caribbean and indigenous traditions.

Ancient practice

In ancient Greece, sacrifice was the central act of public religion: animals were garlanded, led to an altar, and slaughtered, with portions burned for the gods and the rest shared in a communal feast. Roman practice was similar and included large state ceremonies. The Hebrew Bible prescribes an extensive system of sacrifices at the Temple in Jerusalem — burnt offerings, sin offerings, and peace offerings — which ceased with the Temple's destruction in 70 CE. Vedic religion in India centred on fire sacrifices (yajna), some of which, such as the horse sacrifice (ashvamedha), marked royal power.

Contemporary practice

Animal sacrifice remains part of Islamic observance at Eid al-Adha, when sheep, goats, or cattle are slaughtered to commemorate Abraham's willingness to sacrifice his son and the meat is distributed to the poor. Sacrifices also occur in Hindu festivals in parts of India and Nepal, in the Yoruba-derived religions of the Americas, and in some indigenous and folk traditions. In China, offerings of cooked animals feature in ancestor worship and festivals.

Interpretation and controversy

Scholars have interpreted sacrifice variously as gift-giving to gods, communion through a shared meal, a means of channelling violence, or a system for distributing meat. The practice is contested in modern societies, where animal welfare laws sometimes conflict with claims of religious freedom; several European countries regulate or restrict ritual slaughter, and court cases in the United States have addressed the rights of practitioners of Santería.
