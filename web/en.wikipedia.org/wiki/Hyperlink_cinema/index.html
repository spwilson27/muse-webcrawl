Hyperlink cinema

Hyperlink cinema is a term coined by author Alissa Quart, who used it in her review of the film Happy Endings (2005) for the journal Film Comment. Hyperlink films are characterised by multiple storylines, large ensemble casts, and narratives that interweave and eventually intersect, often through chance encounters between characters. The style is named after the hyperlink, reflecting the way the viewer can jump between loosely connected narrative threads.

Characteristics

Hyperlink films typically feature:

  • Several protagonists of roughly equal narrative weight, rather than a single hero;
  • Storylines that run in parallel and cross through coincidence, shared locations, or minor connecting characters;
  • A mosaic or network structure in which the connections between stories may be revealed late in the film, prompting the audience to reinterpret earlier scenes;
  • Themes of interconnectedness, fate, globalisation, and the ripple effects of individual actions.

History

Precursors include multi-strand films such as Robert Altman's Nashville (1975) and Short Cuts (1993). The style became prominent in the 2000s with films such as Magnolia (1999), Traffic (2000), 21 Grams (2003), Crash (2004), Syriana (2005), and Babel (2006), the latter three associated with director Alejandro González Iñárritu and screenwriter Guillermo Arriaga. International examples include Amores perros (2000) and Code Unknown (2000).

Reception and criticism

Critics have praised hyperlink films for their ambition and their portrait of an interconnected world, while others criticise the mode for contrived coincidences and for reducing characters to thematic functions. The structure has influenced television series with ensemble, multi-timeline narratives.
