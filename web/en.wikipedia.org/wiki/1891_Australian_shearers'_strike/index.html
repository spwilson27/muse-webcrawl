1891 Australian shearers' strike

1891 shearers' strike
DateJanuary – June 1891
LocationCentral Queensland, Australia (centred on Barcaldine)
CausesPastoralists' attempts to employ non-union labour and cut shearers' conditions
ResultStrike defeated; union leaders imprisoned; momentum toward the formation of the Australian Labor Party

The 1891 Australian shearers' strike was one of the first major industrial disputes in Australian history and a defining event in the development of the Australian labour movement. Confronting pastoralists who sought to break the power of the newly formed shearers' unions, thousands of shearers in central Queensland went on strike in early 1891. The strike was ultimately defeated, but its political legacy — including the election of labour representatives and the founding mythology of the Australian Labor Party — proved enduring.

Background

During the 1880s, shearers organised into unions, notably the Australian Shearers' Union founded in 1886 under figures such as William Spence, and won agreements guaranteeing union rates and conditions in the woolsheds. Facing falling wool prices and the economic depression beginning in 1890, the pastoralists combined in associations determined to assert "freedom of contract" — the right to employ non-union labour at whatever rates they chose. The confrontation came to a head during the 1891 shearing season in Queensland.

The strike

The strike began in January 1891 when pastoralists at stations in central Queensland attempted to impose non-union agreements. Shearers struck and established large camps, most famously at Barcaldine, where strikers organised under strict discipline with elected committees, pickets and mounted patrols. The colonial government of Queensland responded by deploying police and military forces, and armed escorts protected non-union labour brought in to shear the sheep. Tensions produced episodes of violence, including the burning of woolsheds. Under the Tree of Knowledge at Barcaldine, strikers held meetings that later became central to Labor Party tradition.

Defeat and trials

By mid-1891 the strike collapsed as union funds ran out and stations completed shearing with non-union labour. Thirteen strike leaders were arrested and tried at Rockhampton for conspiracy and sedition; they were convicted and sentenced to three years' hard labour on St Helena Island prison in Moreton Bay. The defeat, following the failed maritime strike of 1890, convinced many unionists that industrial action alone was insufficient and that labour needed direct parliamentary representation.

Legacy

The strike is widely regarded as a founding moment of the Australian Labor Party: labour electoral leagues formed in its wake, and in 1892 Thomas Glassey and others entered colonial parliaments; in 1899 Queensland briefly had the world's first parliamentary labour government under Anderson Dawson. Barcaldine's Tree of Knowledge — poisoned in 2006 but preserved as a memorial — remains a symbol of the movement, and the strike's story is retold in Australian labour history, literature and song.
