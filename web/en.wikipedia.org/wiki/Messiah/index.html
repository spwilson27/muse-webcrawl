Messiah

In Abrahamic religions, a messiah or messias (Hebrew: מָשִׁיחַ, romanized: māšīaḥ; Greek: μεσσίας, messías; Arabic: مسيح, masīḥ) is a saviour or liberator of a group of people. The concepts of mashiach, messianism, and of a Messianic Age originated in Judaism, and in the Hebrew Bible, in which a mashiach is a king or High Priest traditionally anointed with holy anointing oil.

In Judaism, Ha-mashiach (המשיח, "the Messiah") often refers to the future Jewish king from the Davidic line, who is expected to be the future redeemer of the Jewish people and of humanity. In Christianity, Jesus is regarded as the saviour and christ (Greek: χριστός, khristós, "anointed one") — translated, "Messiah". In Islam, Jesus (Arabic: عيسى, romanized: ʿĪsā) is also regarded as the al-Masīḥ, and is prophesied to return to defeat the false messiah (Al-Masih ad-Dajjal) before Judgment Day.

Etymology

Messiah is an English transliteration of the Hebrew word māšīaḥ (מָשִׁיחַ), meaning "anointed one", derived from the verb "to apply oil or ointment". The Greek translation in the Septuagint is christós (χριστός), from which the English word "Christ" derives. In ancient Israel, anointing with oil was the ceremony by which kings and priests were consecrated to their office.

In Judaism

The Hebrew Bible uses mashiach for anointed kings (such as Saul and David), priests, and even the Persian king Cyrus, whom Isaiah calls God's anointed. In later Jewish tradition, the Messiah became a specific eschatological figure: a descendant of David who will rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem, gather the exiles of Israel, and usher in an age of peace. Mainstream Orthodox Judaism includes the coming of the Messiah among Maimonides' Thirteen Principles of faith. Jewish messianic movements have arisen repeatedly through history, around figures such as Simon bar Kokhba and, in the 17th century, Sabbatai Zevi.

In Christianity

Christians believe Jesus of Nazareth is the Messiah prophesied in the Hebrew scriptures. The New Testament presents him as saviour, Son of God, and the one who will return at the Second Coming to fulfil remaining messianic prophecies, including the resurrection of the dead and the Last Judgment. Christian theology of the Messiah is developed in the field of Christology.

In Islam

The Quran calls Jesus al-Masīḥ eleven times. Islamic tradition holds that Jesus, the son of Mary, will return in the end times, descend at Damascus, defeat the Dajjal (the false messiah), and rule justly. Sunni and Shia traditions differ on the relationship between Jesus' return and the appearance of the Mahdi, a separate redeemer figure in Islamic eschatology.

Other traditions

Messiah-like figures appear in other religions: the Zoroastrian Saoshyant, the Hindu Kalki avatar, the Buddhist Maitreya, and the Taoist Li Hong. The Rastafari movement regards the Ethiopian emperor Haile Selassie I as the Messiah returned.
