Sialic acid

Sialic acids are a class of alpha-keto acid sugars with a nine-carbon backbone. They are typically found at the outermost ends of glycan chains on cell surfaces and soluble glycoproteins, where their negative charge and terminal position give them central roles in cell recognition, adhesion, and signalling. The best-known member is N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac), which predominates in humans; the family takes its name from saliva (Greek sialon), from whose mucin the substance was first isolated. More broadly the term also covers the related compound KDN.

Biochemistry

Sialic acids are biosynthesised in the cytosol and nucleus, activated as CMP-sialic acid, and transferred onto glycoconjugates by sialyltransferases in the Golgi apparatus. Enzymes called sialidases (neuraminidases) cleave them. Microorganisms display or exploit sialic acids in many ways: influenza viruses bind host-cell sialic acids via hemagglutinin and release progeny virions via viral neuraminidase — the target of the drugs oseltamivir (Tamiflu) and zanamivir — and many bacteria coat themselves in sialic acid to mimic host tissue and evade complement.

Roles in humans

Humans, unlike most other mammals, cannot synthesise N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc) because of an inactivating mutation in the CMAH gene, a notable biochemical difference between humans and other great apes. Sialic acids are abundant in the brain in gangliosides and in polysialic acid chains on NCAM important for neural development. They serve as ligands for Siglec receptors on immune cells, regulating inflammation, and they determine the half-life of circulating glycoproteins: loss of terminal sialic acid exposes galactose that triggers clearance by the hepatic asialoglycoprotein receptor.

Disorders

Defects in sialic acid metabolism cause rare diseases including Salla disease and infantile sialic acid storage disorder (defects of the lysosomal transporter sialin), sialuria (overproduction due to feedback-insensitive UDP-GlcNAc 2-epimerase), and GNE myopathy (hereditary inclusion body myopathy), a muscle-wasting disease caused by mutations in the bifunctional enzyme that initiates sialic acid synthesis.
