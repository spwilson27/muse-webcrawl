Distortion (optics)

In geometric optics, distortion is a deviation from rectilinear projection — the projection in which straight lines in a scene remain straight in the image. It is an optical aberration in which magnification varies with distance from the optical axis, so that the image is warped without being blurred. Distortion does not reduce the sharpness of the image; it displaces image points from their ideal positions.

Types

  • Barrel distortion: magnification decreases with distance from the axis; straight lines bow outwards, as in a barrel. Common in wide-angle and fisheye lenses.
  • Pincushion distortion: magnification increases with distance from the axis; lines bow inwards. Common in telephoto lenses.
  • Mustache (moustache) distortion: a complex combination, barrel-like in the centre and pincushion-like at the edges, producing wavy lines.

Causes and correction

Distortion arises because different parts of a lens system magnify the image by different amounts, and is strongly affected by the position of the aperture stop. It can be minimised by symmetric lens designs and by placing the stop appropriately. In digital photography it is routinely corrected in software using lens profiles. Severe distortion is deliberately embraced in fisheye lenses, which sacrifice rectilinearity for an extremely wide field of view.
