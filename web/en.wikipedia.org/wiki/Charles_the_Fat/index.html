Charles III (839 – 13 January 888), also known as Charles the Fat, was the emperor of the Carolingian Empire from 881 to 887. A member of the Carolingian dynasty, Charles was the youngest son of Louis the German and Hemma. He briefly reunited the empire — the last time the entire Carolingian realm (apart from southern Italy) would be under one ruler — but his inability to defend it against Viking raids, compounded by recurring illness, led to his deposition in 887 and the permanent fragmentation of the empire.

Rise to power

In the division of East Francia following his father's death in 876, Charles received Alemannia (Swabia) and Rhaetia. In 879 his brother Carloman abdicated Italy to him, and Charles was crowned King of Italy. On 12 February 881 he was crowned emperor in Rome by Pope John VIII. When his nephew Carloman II of West Francia died in 884, the West Frankish nobles invited Charles to rule, and the whole empire was again united under a single Carolingian.

Reign and Viking invasions

Charles's reign was dominated by Viking attacks. His response at the Siege of Asselt (882), where he bought off the Viking leader Godfrid with tribute and a marriage alliance, drew criticism. During the great Siege of Paris (885–886), Charles arrived with an army but chose to pay the Vikings 700 pounds of silver to withdraw into Burgundy rather than fight, outraging his subjects. Plagued by an illness that may have been epilepsy, and by a failed campaign, he was deposed by an assembly at Tribur in November 887, which elected his illegitimate nephew Arnulf of Carinthia in the east; the west soon chose Odo of Paris. Charles died weeks later, in January 888, at Neidingen.

Assessment

Traditional historiography treated Charles as indolent and incompetent — the nickname "the Fat" itself may be a later invention. More recent scholarship notes that much of his reputation rests on hostile sources such as the Annals of Fulda, and that he was a reasonably active ruler undermined by illness and by the structural disintegration of the Carolingian world after his death.
