British brass band

A British brass band is a musical ensemble comprising a standardised range of brass and percussion instruments. The modern form of the band, with its fixed instrumentation of cornets, flugelhorn, tenor horns, baritones, euphoniums, trombones, basses (tubas) and percussion, developed in the industrial districts of northern England and the Midlands during the nineteenth century, and banding remains closely associated with mining, mill and manufacturing communities.

Instrumentation

The standard competition band fields 25 brass players plus percussion:

  • Soprano cornet (E♭), solo cornets, repiano cornet, second and third cornets
  • Flugelhorn (B♭)
  • Solo, first and second tenor horns (E♭)
  • First and second baritones, first and second euphoniums
  • Solo, second and bass trombones
  • E♭ and B♭ basses (tubas)
  • Percussion section

With the exception of the trombones, all instruments read treble clef, a convention that allows players to move between instruments within the band.

History

Brass bands flourished during the Industrial Revolution, when employers in collieries, textile mills and factories sponsored works bands as a wholesome recreation for employees. The standardisation of instrumentation was driven by instrument makers such as Distin and by the rise of contesting. The first recorded brass band contests took place in the 1850s, and the British Open Championships, founded in 1853, and the National Championships of Great Britain, founded in 1900, remain the movement's premier events.

Contesting

Bands are graded into sections (Championship, First, Second, Third and Fourth) and compete on set test pieces, with promotion and relegation between sections. Famous championship bands include Black Dyke, Grimethorpe Colliery, Brighouse and Rastrick, Cory and Foden's. The Whit Friday march contests in Saddleworth and Tameside draw hundreds of bands each year.
