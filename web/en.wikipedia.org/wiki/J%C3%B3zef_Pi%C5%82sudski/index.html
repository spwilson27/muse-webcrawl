Józef Klemens Piłsudski (5 December 1867 – 12 May 1935) was a Polish statesman who served as the Chief of State (1918–1922) and First Marshal of Poland (from 1920). He is widely considered responsible for Poland regaining its independence in 1918, after 123 years of partitions by Russia, Prussia, and Austria.

Early life

Piłsudski was born into a Polish-Lithuanian noble family at Zułów, in the Vilna Governorate of the Russian Empire. As a young man he became involved in Polish independence movements and joined the Polish Socialist Party (PPS). In 1887 he was arrested by Tsarist authorities on charges of plotting to assassinate Tsar Alexander III and was exiled to Siberia for five years.

World War I and independence

During the First World War, Piłsudski commanded the Polish Legions fighting alongside Austria-Hungary and Germany against Russia, expecting that the defeat of the partitioning powers would open the way to independence. In 1917 he refused to swear loyalty to the Central Powers and was imprisoned at Magdeburg. Released in November 1918, he returned to Warsaw and became provisional head of the newly independent Polish state.

Polish–Soviet War

Piłsudski led Polish forces in the Polish–Soviet War of 1919–1921. His forces won the decisive Battle of Warsaw in August 1920, sometimes called the "Miracle on the Vistula," halting the westward advance of the Red Army. Poland's eastern borders were settled by the Treaty of Riga in 1921.

Later years

After retiring from politics in 1923, Piłsudski returned to power in the May Coup of 1926 and remained the dominant figure in Polish politics until his death in 1935, serving several times as Minister of Military Affairs and twice as Prime Minister. He promoted the concept of Intermarium, a federation of Central and Eastern European states, and pursued a policy of balancing relations between Germany and the Soviet Union.
