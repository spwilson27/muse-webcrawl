Claus process

Claus process
Type: Gas desulfurization
Patented: 1883 (Carl Friedrich Claus)
Products: Elemental sulfur
Typical recovery: 95–99% (with tail-gas treatment, >99.9%)

The Claus process is the most significant gas desulfurizing process, recovering elemental sulfur from the gaseous hydrogen sulfide contained in raw natural gas and from the by-product gases derived in refining crude oil and other industrial processes. The process was patented in 1883 by the German chemist Carl Friedrich Claus.

Chemistry

The overall reaction converts hydrogen sulfide to elemental sulfur and water:

2 H2S + O2 → 2 S + 2 H2O

The process proceeds in two steps. In the thermal stage, a portion of the hydrogen sulfide feed is burned with air in a reaction furnace at temperatures above 850 °C, producing sulfur dioxide. In the catalytic stage, the remaining hydrogen sulfide reacts with the sulfur dioxide over an alumina or titania catalyst:

2 H2S + SO2 → 3 S + 2 H2O

Process description

The multi-step process recovers sulfur from hydrogen sulfide gas (the "acid gas") produced in refineries and natural gas processing plants. After the thermal stage, the gas passes through two or three catalytic converter beds, each followed by a sulfur condenser; reheating between stages shifts the equilibrium and drives conversion to roughly 95–97%. Additional "SuperClaus" or tail-gas treatment units can raise total recovery above 99.9%.

Applications and significance

The sulfur produced is a major industrial feedstock, principally for sulfuric acid manufacture. Because hydrogen sulfide is highly toxic and its combustion product sulfur dioxide causes acid rain, the Claus process is essential to meeting environmental emission standards worldwide. Global sulfur production from Claus plants amounts to tens of millions of tonnes per year.
