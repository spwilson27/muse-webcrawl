Getting Things Done (GTD) is a personal productivity system created by David Allen and described in his 2001 book Getting Things Done: The Art of Stress-Free Productivity. Its central premise is that a person's mind is for having ideas, not holding them: by moving every task, reminder and commitment into a trusted external system, one achieves a "mind like water" state in which attention can be given fully to the task at hand.

The workflow

GTD prescribes five stages: capture everything that has your attention into inboxes; clarify what each item means and whether it is actionable (applying the "two-minute rule" — if it takes less than two minutes, do it now); organise outcomes into lists of next actions grouped by context, projects, waiting-for items and a calendar; reflect through regular reviews, especially a weekly review; and engage, choosing what to do based on context, time, energy and priority. Multi-step outcomes are treated as "projects", each with a defined next physical action.

Reception

The book became a bestseller and the method acquired a large following among knowledge workers and software developers, with countless applications designed around it. Critics have noted that the system demands significant upkeep and suits personal task management better than collaborative work, but its vocabulary — inbox zero, next actions, weekly review — has passed into general use.
