King of Rome

King of Rome (Latin: Rex Romae) was a title used in several distinct historical contexts: for the monarchs of the Roman Kingdom before the founding of the Roman Republic in 509 BC; in the Holy Roman Empire, for a designated heir to the imperial throne; and by Napoleon Bonaparte, who gave the title to his son.


Romulus, legendary first King of Rome

Roman Kingdom

According to Roman tradition, Rome was founded in 753 BC by Romulus, the first of seven kings. The traditional list of the kings of Rome is: Romulus, Numa Pompilius, Tullus Hostilius, Ancus Marcius, Lucius Tarquinius Priscus, Servius Tullius, and Lucius Tarquinius Superbus (Tarquinius the Proud). The kings were not purely hereditary monarchs; the office combined military, judicial, and religious authority, and later kings were elected by the Curiate Assembly, subject to the interrex and senatorial confirmation. The last king, Tarquin the Proud, was expelled in 509 BC, after which Rome became a republic and the title of king acquired strong negative connotations; Roman law thereafter made aspiring to kingship (adfectatio regni) a capital crime.

Holy Roman Empire

In the Holy Roman Empire, King of the Romans (Latin: Rex Romanorum; German: Römisch-deutscher König) was the title given to the elected successor of the emperor, chosen by the prince-electors during the emperor's lifetime or after an interregnum. One crowned emperor, the King of the Romans expected to succeed automatically upon the emperor's death. The title was used from the reign of Henry II onward and was borne by most designated heirs until the Empire's dissolution in 1806. Notable holders who never became emperor include Conrad III (initially) and Henry (VII).

Napoleonic use

On 17 February 1811, Napoleon I issued a decree providing that his expected child, if a son, would bear the title King of Rome (French: Roi de Rome). Napoleon Francis Joseph Charles Bonaparte, born 20 March 1811 to Napoleon and Marie Louise of Austria, held the title from birth. The title evoked the status of a crown prince and the historical King of the Romans, deliberately connecting Napoleon's dynasty to both ancient Rome and the Holy Roman Empire he had dissolved. After Napoleon's fall in 1814, the child lived in Austria as the Duke of Reichstadt and died in 1832.
