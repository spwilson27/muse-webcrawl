Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease after the Norwegian physician Gerhard Armauer Hansen, is a chronic infectious disease caused mainly by the bacterium Mycobacterium leprae. It chiefly affects the skin, the peripheral nerves, the mucous membranes of the upper respiratory tract, and the eyes. Leprosy is curable with multidrug therapy, and treatment in the early stages prevents the disabilities for which the disease has been feared since antiquity.

Signs, symptoms, and classification

The earliest sign is often a pale or reddish skin patch with definite loss of sensation. Because M. leprae prefers cooler tissues and grows within Schwann cells, nerve damage is central: untreated disease produces weakness, clawed hands, foot drop, loss of fingers and toes through repeated unnoticed injury, and blindness. The disease spans a spectrum governed by the host's immune response, classified by the Ridley–Jopling system from tuberculoid leprosy (strong cell-mediated immunity, few well-demarcated lesions, few bacteria) through borderline forms to lepromatous leprosy (weak cell-mediated immunity, widespread symmetric lesions teeming with bacteria, thickened skin and nodules). The WHO simplifies this into paucibacillary and multibacillary categories for treatment purposes.

Transmission and cause

Transmission is thought to occur mainly through prolonged close contact with untreated multibacillary cases, via droplets from the nose and mouth; most exposed people never develop disease, and leprosy is only weakly contagious. The incubation period averages three to five years and can reach two decades. In the southern United States, nine-banded armadillos carry M. leprae and are a documented source of occasional human infections. A second causative species, M. lepromatosis, identified in 2008, is associated particularly with diffuse lepromatous leprosy in Mexico and the Caribbean.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is primarily clinical — a skin lesion with sensory loss, a thickened peripheral nerve, or acid-fast bacilli on a skin smear or biopsy. Since the 1980s the World Health Organization has recommended multidrug therapy (MDT) combining rifampicin, dapsone, and clofazimine, supplied free of charge worldwide; six to twelve months of treatment renders the patient non-infectious and cures the infection. Acute immunological episodes called leprosy reactions — type 1 (reversal) reactions and type 2 (erythema nodosum leprosum) — can cause severe nerve damage even during treatment and are managed with corticosteroids and other anti-inflammatory drugs.

Epidemiology and history

Some 200,000 new cases are still reported each year, concentrated in India, Brazil, and Indonesia. Leprosy has afflicted humanity for millennia and carried intense stigma: medieval Europe maintained thousands of leper houses, and in many places people with the disease were compelled to live apart, a legacy of exclusion that persisted in leprosaria into the twentieth century in countries from Japan to the United States (Carville, Louisiana; Kalaupapa, Hawaii). Hansen identified the bacillus in 1873 — one of the first bacteria linked to a human disease. Global prevalence has fallen by more than 95% since MDT became widespread, and WHO strategy now targets interruption of transmission and zero disability in children; stigma and discrimination against affected people remain the chief obstacles, and advocates prefer the name "Hansen's disease" for that reason.
