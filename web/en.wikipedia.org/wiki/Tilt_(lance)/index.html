Tilt (lance)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the context of medieval and early modern jousting, a tilt is a contest in which two mounted knights, armed with lances, charge at one another and attempt to unhorse their opponent or break their lance on the opponent's shield or helm. The word also gives its name to the tilt barrier (or simply "the tilt"), the long wooden fence that ran down the centre of the lists to separate the two riders and prevent collisions between the horses.

The tilt barrier

Early jousts were run without a barrier, in the open field, which risked the horses colliding head-on. The tilt barrier — originally a stretched length of cloth, later a solid wooden fence about chest-high to the horses — was introduced in the 14th and 15th centuries and became standard in the great tournaments of Burgundy, France, Germany, and Tudor England. Each rider charged along the left side of the barrier with the lance couched across the horse's neck, angled over the barrier toward the opponent. The barrier made the sport safer and more predictable, and the specialized armour developed for the tilt, such as the heavy German Stechzeug, became correspondingly specialized and massive.

Usage in language

The jousting tilt survives in several English expressions. To tilt at windmills, from the episode in Don Quixote in which the hero charges windmills he mistakes for giants, means to attack imaginary enemies. A person "at full tilt" is moving at full speed, as a knight charged in the tilt. The phrase "tilting" also appears in pinball terminology, an unrelated later borrowing describing shaking the machine.

Modern revival

Tilting is still practised in historical re-enactment and competitive modern jousting, in which riders use balsa-tipped lances and score points for strikes and broken lances. The Danish town of Sønderborg and other European venues hold ring-riding (ringridning) events, a related sport in which riders attempt to spear a small ring with a lance at the gallop.
