Mohawk (Kanienʼkéha, "[language] of the Flint Place") is an Iroquoian language spoken by the Mohawk people (Kanienʼkehá꞉ka) in communities in southeastern Canada and northern New York State. It is the most widely spoken of the Northern Iroquoian languages, though it is considered endangered, with most fluent speakers over middle age. Intensive revitalisation programmes, including immersion schools, have produced a growing number of second-language speakers.

Mohawk is written with a Latin-based alphabet using a glottal stop marker (ʼ).

History

The Mohawk were the easternmost nation of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy). Contact with Dutch, French, and English colonists brought trade, missionary activity, and early written records of the language, including translations of religious texts in the 17th and 18th centuries. In the 19th and 20th centuries, government residential and boarding schools suppressed Indigenous languages, sharply reducing intergenerational transmission of Mohawk.

Phonology and grammar

Mohawk has a relatively small consonant inventory but contrasts vowel length and nasalised vowels. It is a polysynthetic language: complex words are built from many morphemes, and a single verb word can express what requires a whole sentence in English. Word order is relatively free and is driven largely by information structure — what is new or emphasised — rather than by fixed grammatical roles. Nouns are frequently incorporated into verbs.

Dialects

Three main dialects are recognised: Western (Ohswé꞉ken and Wáhta), Central (Ahkwesáhsne), and Eastern (Kahnawà꞉ke and Kanehsatà꞉ke). They differ principally in the pronunciation of certain consonant clusters and in some vocabulary.

Revitalisation

Community-led efforts include the Akwesasne Freedom School (founded 1979), immersion programmes at Kahnawà꞉ke, adult immersion courses, and the standardised orthography adopted in 1993 following a conference at Kahnawà꞉ke. Mohawk-language radio, curricula, and digital resources support new speakers.
