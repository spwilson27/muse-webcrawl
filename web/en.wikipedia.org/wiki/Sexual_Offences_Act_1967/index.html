The Sexual Offences Act 1967 (c. 60) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom which decriminalised homosexual acts in private between two men over the age of 21 in England and Wales. It was a landmark in the legal history of LGBT rights in Britain, though it applied restrictive conditions and did not extend to Scotland (until 1980), Northern Ireland (until 1982), the armed forces, or the merchant navy.

Background

The Act implemented the central recommendation of the Wolfenden Report (1957), which had concluded that consensual homosexual behaviour between adults in private should not be a criminal offence. After earlier bills failed, Labour MP Leo Abse reintroduced the measure as a private member's bill in 1966, with government support from Home Secretary Roy Jenkins and the Lord Chancellor, Lord Gardiner, who steered it through the House of Lords.

Provisions

The Act provided that a homosexual act in private between two consenting men aged 21 or over was not an offence. "In private" was narrowly defined: an act was not private if more than two persons were present, or if it took place in a public lavatory. The age of consent was set higher (21) than for heterosexual acts (16), an inequality reduced to 18 in 1994 and equalised at 16 in 2000. Prosecutions for offences such as gross indecency in fact rose in the years immediately after the Act.

Legacy

The Act is regarded as a milestone of the liberal reforms of the 1960s. Its remaining discriminatory provisions were progressively repealed, and in 2017 "Alan Turing law" pardons addressed historic convictions under the older laws the Act had partially replaced.
