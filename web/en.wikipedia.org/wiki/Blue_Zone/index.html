Blue zone

[Hero image: elderly residents of Okinawa, Japan — one of the original blue zones — sharing a traditional meal]

Blue zones are regions in the world where people are claimed to live, or have lived, measurably longer lives than average, with unusually high proportions of centenarians. The concept grew out of demographic work by Gianni Pes and Michel Poulain, who identified a cluster of exceptional longevity in the Barbagia region of Sardinia and marked it in blue ink on a map. It was popularised by Dan Buettner in a 2005 National Geographic cover story and best-selling books.

The five regions

  • Ogliastra / Barbagia, Sardinia, Italy — mountain villages with an unusually high ratio of male centenarians.
  • Okinawa, Japan — long noted for elderly women's longevity; traditional diet rich in sweet potatoes and vegetables.
  • Nicoya Peninsula, Costa Rica — lower middle-age mortality and many male centenarians.
  • Icaria, Greece — Aegean island where a high share of residents reach their 90s.
  • Loma Linda, California, USA — a community of Seventh-day Adventists whose lifestyle is associated with longer life.

Common characteristics

Buettner summarised shared lifestyle factors as the "Power 9": regular natural physical activity, a sense of purpose, stress reduction, moderate caloric intake (the Okinawan hara hachi bu), a largely plant-based diet, moderate wine consumption, engagement in spirituality, family priority, and close social networks. Later work by Buettner extended the idea to urban policy through the "Blue Zones Project" for community health.

Criticism

The concept has been criticised on demographic grounds: claims of extreme longevity may partly reflect poor or fraudulent age records, pension fraud, and errors in historical registration in some regions, and researchers such as Saul Justin Newman have argued that supercentenarian clusters correlate with record-keeping errors and poverty. Okinawa's longevity advantage has also narrowed markedly in younger generations.
