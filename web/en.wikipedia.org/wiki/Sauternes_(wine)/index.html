Sauternes is a French sweet wine from the region of the same name in the Graves section in Bordeaux. Sauternes is made from Sémillon, Sauvignon blanc, and Muscadelle grapes that have been affected by Botrytis cinerea, also known as noble rot. This causes the grapes to become partially raisined, resulting in concentrated and distinctly flavoured wines.

Production

The Sauternes appellation covers the communes of Sauternes, Barsac, Bommes, Fargues and Preignac, south of the city of Bordeaux along the Garonne and its tributary the Ciron. Autumn mists from the cold Ciron meeting the warmer Garonne encourage noble rot; pickers pass through the vineyards many times (tries), selecting only botrytised berries. Yields are tiny and the wine is aged in oak, producing a golden, honeyed wine with flavours of apricot, peach, honeycomb and marmalade, balanced by high acidity, capable of ageing for decades.

Classification and famous estates

In the Bordeaux Wine Official Classification of 1855, requested by Napoleon III, Sauternes estates were ranked separately from the Médoc reds. Château d'Yquem of Sauternes was the only estate rated Premier Cru Supérieur, a class of its own; eleven estates were classed as Premiers Crus and fifteen as Deuxièmes Crus. Barsac estates may use either the Barsac or the Sauternes appellation.

Food pairing

Sauternes is classically paired with foie gras, Roquefort and other blue cheeses, and fruit-based desserts, though drier styles and older vintages are also served as apéritifs.
