Richter scale

The Richter scale — more precisely the local magnitude scale, ML — is a measure of the strength of earthquakes developed by Charles F. Richter in 1935, in collaboration with Beno Gutenberg, at the California Institute of Technology.

Definition

Magnitude is defined as the logarithm to base 10 of the maximum trace amplitude, measured in micrometres, recorded by a standard Wood-Anderson torsion seismometer at a distance of 100 kilometres from the epicentre, with corrections applied for other distances. Because the scale is logarithmic, each whole-number step corresponds to about a 31.6-fold increase in energy release and a tenfold increase in measured amplitude.

Use and successors

The Richter scale was designed for shallow earthquakes in Southern California and saturates for very large events. Seismologists today principally use the moment magnitude scale (Mw), which is based on seismic moment and works over a wider range; however, "Richter scale" remains a popular synonym for earthquake magnitude in media reports.
