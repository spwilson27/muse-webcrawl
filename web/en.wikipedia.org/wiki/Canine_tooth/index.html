In mammalian oral anatomy, the canine teeth, also called cuspids, dog teeth, eye teeth, vampire teeth, or fangs, are the relatively long, pointed teeth. In humans there are four canine teeth, one in each quadrant of the mouth, situated between the incisors and the premolars. The upper canines are sometimes called eye teeth because of their position below the eyes.

Function

Canines are adapted for piercing and tearing food, and in many mammals — especially males — they are enlarged for display, fighting and defence. Human canines are much reduced compared with those of other primates but remain the longest-rooted teeth, important for guiding jaw movement ("canine guidance") and for supporting facial structure.

Development and variation

The permanent upper canines erupt around age 11–12 and are the teeth most commonly impacted after third molars. Sexual dimorphism in canine size is pronounced in many primates; in humans the difference is modest. In folklore and fiction, elongated canines are associated with vampires.
