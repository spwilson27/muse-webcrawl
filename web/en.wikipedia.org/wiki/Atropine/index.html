Atropine

Atropine is a tropane alkaloid and anticholinergic drug naturally occurring in a number of plants of the nightshade family, including deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna), jimsonweed (Datura stramonium), and mandrake. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

IUPAC name: (RS)-(8-methyl-8-azabicyclo[3.2.1]oct-3-yl) 3-hydroxy-2-phenylpropanoate

Formula: C17H23NO3

Routes: Oral, intravenous, intramuscular, ophthalmic, rectal

Class: Antimuscarinic (anticholinergic)

Medical uses

Atropine is used to treat certain types of nerve agent and pesticide poisonings (organophosphate poisoning), as well as some types of slow heart rate (bradycardia), and to decrease saliva production during surgery. It is typically given intravenously or by injection into a muscle. Eye drops are used to dilate the pupil (mydriasis) and to treat uveitis and certain refractive conditions in children. Atropine works by blocking muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, antagonizing the effects of the parasympathetic nervous system.

Side effects

Common side effects include dry mouth, blurred vision, sensitivity to light, urinary retention, constipation, and an increased heart rate. The classic signs of atropine toxicity are summarized as "hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, and mad as a hatter." It is generally considered unsafe in pregnancy although evidence is limited.

History

Extracts of belladonna were used in medicine and cosmetics for centuries—the name belladonna derives from the Italian for "beautiful lady," after the practice of using the juice to enlarge the pupils. Atropine was first isolated in crystalline form in the 1830s, and its chemical synthesis was achieved by Richard Willstätter in 1901.
