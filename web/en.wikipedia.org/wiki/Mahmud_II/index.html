Mahmud II

Mahmud II (20 July 1785 – 1 July 1839) was the 30th Sultan of the Ottoman Empire from 1808 until his death in 1839. He is remembered as a major reformer who centralised government, modernised the army and administration, and set the empire on the course of the Tanzimat reforms carried out under his sons.

Early reign

Mahmud came to the throne amid the turmoil following the deposition of Mustafa IV, surviving an attempt on his life during the palace coup of 1808 in which his brother was killed. In his early years he depended on powerful provincial notables (ayan) and signed the Sened-i İttifak (Charter of Alliance) of 1808, which recognised their privileges; he subsequently spent years curbing their power and reasserting the authority of the centre. His reign saw the Greek War of Independence (1821–29), which ended with Greek independence, and wars with Russia and with Muhammad Ali of Egypt.

The Auspicious Incident and reforms

In June 1826 Mahmud abolished the Janissary corps in the event known as the Auspicious Incident (Vaka-i Hayriye), in which the Janissaries who revolted against his new army were destroyed in Istanbul. Freed of this obstacle, he created a modern conscript army, reorganised ministries on European lines, founded new schools including medical and military academies, introduced a postal service and quarantine system, reformed dress (mandating the fez for officials), and took the first Ottoman census in 1831. He also centralised the administration of pious endowments (waqfs) and began publishing an official gazette, Takvim-i Vekayi.

Death and legacy

Mahmud II died in 1839, just as a new crisis with Muhammad Ali of Egypt was culminating, and was succeeded by his son Abdülmecid I. His reforms laid the institutional foundations for the Tanzimat era of Ottoman modernisation.
