Series and parallel circuits

Series and parallel circuits are the two fundamental ways of connecting components in an electrical network. In a series circuit, components are connected end-to-end so that the same electric current flows through each of them. In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two points, so the same voltage appears across each branch while the current divides among them.

Series circuits

In a series connection, the total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances: Rtotal = R1 + R2 + ... The current is identical everywhere in the loop, and the total voltage equals the sum of the voltage drops across each component, in accordance with Kirchhoff's voltage law. If one component fails open, the entire circuit stops working.

Parallel circuits

In a parallel connection, the reciprocal of the total resistance equals the sum of the reciprocals of the branch resistances: 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... The voltage across each branch is the same, and the total current is the sum of the branch currents, per Kirchhoff's current law. Components can fail independently without interrupting the others, which is why household wiring is arranged in parallel.

Combinations

Most practical circuits combine series and parallel sub-networks. Such networks can be analysed by repeatedly reducing parallel groups and series groups to equivalent resistances until a single equivalent value remains.
