Enforcement Acts

The Enforcement Acts were three bills that were passed by the United States Congress between 1870 and 1871 during the Reconstruction era to combat attacks upon the suffrage rights of African Americans from groups like the Ku Klux Klan. The acts protected the voting rights granted by the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments and authorised the federal government to intervene when states failed to act.

Federal enforcement protected African American voters during Reconstruction.

The acts

The First Enforcement Act (1870) prohibited state officials from discriminating in voter registration and made interference with voting a federal crime. The Second Enforcement Act (1871) provided for federal supervisors of elections. The Third Enforcement Act (1871), commonly called the Ku Klux Klan Act, made violent conspiracies against civil rights a federal offence and empowered the President to suspend habeas corpus and use the military to suppress them — powers President Ulysses S. Grant used against the Klan in South Carolina.

Legacy

The prosecutions under the acts broke the first Ku Klux Klan as an organised force. After Reconstruction ended, the acts fell into disuse, but sections of the Ku Klux Klan Act survive in modern law, notably 42 U.S.C. § 1983, a cornerstone of civil rights litigation.
