Propaganda model

The propaganda model is a conceptual model in political economy advanced by Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky in their book Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media (1988). It argues that the mass media of nominally democratic societies function as propaganda systems: without formal censorship, news content is shaped by market forces and institutional structures so that it systematically serves powerful interests and marginalises dissent.

The five filters of the propaganda model

The five filters

Herman and Chomsky propose that raw news passes through five filters before publication, jointly determining what becomes news:

  1. Size, ownership, and profit orientation: dominant media firms are large, profit-seeking corporations owned by and interlocked with other wealthy interests.
  2. Advertising: because advertising is the primary revenue source, media must attract affluent audiences and avoid content offensive to advertisers.
  3. Sourcing: media depend on a steady flow of information from government and corporate sources, which subsidise news production and can punish outlets by withholding access.
  4. Flak: negative responses — complaints, lawsuits, official pressure — discipline media that stray from accepted lines.
  5. Anti-communism and fear: a dominant ideology (anti-communism in the Cold War, later generalised to enemies such as "terrorism") supplies a dichotomy between worthy and unworthy victims and frames permissible debate.

Evidence and applications

The model is tested not by theory alone but by paired case studies: for example, the extensive, indignant coverage of the murder of the Polish priest Jerzy Popiełuszko by a hostile regime contrasted with the muted coverage of murders of religious workers in U.S.-aligned El Salvador; and the legitimation of elections in client states versus the delegitimation of elections in enemy states. Later editions and other researchers applied the model to coverage of wars, climate change, and the financial crisis.

Criticism

Critics argue that the model underplays journalistic professionalism and audience agency, that contrary examples (investigative exposés of government wrongdoing) are common, and that the filters can be unfalsifiable. Defenders reply that the model predicts tendencies rather than uniform outcomes and that its case-study predictions have repeatedly been borne out.
