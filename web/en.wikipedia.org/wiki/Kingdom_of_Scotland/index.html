The Kingdom of Scotland (Scottish Gaelic: Rìoghachd na h-Alba; Scots: Kinrick o Scotland) was a sovereign state in north-west Europe, traditionally said to have been founded in 843 with the unification of the Scots of Dalriada and the Picts under Kenneth MacAlpin. It endured for more than eight centuries, expanding to roughly the borders of modern Scotland, before uniting with the Kingdom of England under the Acts of Union 1707 to form the Kingdom of Great Britain.

Early kingdom

The early kingdom, known as Alba, was a Gaelic-speaking realm centred north of the Forth. Its kings fought and intermarried with the Britons of Strathclyde, the Angles of Lothian and Norse settlers in the north and west. Malcolm II's victory at Carham (1018) secured Lothian, and by the reign of Alexander I Scotland's southern border approximated today's. Under David I (r. 1124–1153) the kingdom was transformed by feudal tenures, burghs, sheriffs, coinage and reformed monastic orders — the "Davidian Revolution" — while its kings gradually absorbed Strathclyde, Galloway, Moray and, by treaty in 1266 and purchase in 1472, the Hebrides, Orkney and Shetland from Norway.

Wars of Independence

The death of Alexander III (1286) and of his heir Margaret, Maid of Norway (1290), triggered a succession crisis and Edward I of England's attempted domination. Resistance under William Wallace and Robert the Bruce — victor at Bannockburn in 1314 — restored independence, asserted in the Declaration of Arbroath (1320) and recognised by England in 1328. Later medieval Scotland, ruled by the Stewart dynasty from 1371, combined a cultured Renaissance court with chronic noble feuding and conflict with England, culminating in James IV's death at Flodden in 1513.

Reformation and union of crowns

The Scottish Reformation of 1560 made the kingdom Protestant, though Mary, Queen of Scots' Catholicism, deposition (1567) and execution in England (1587) dominated her reign. Her son James VI inherited the English throne in 1603 (the Union of the Crowns) and thereafter the kingdoms shared a monarch while remaining separate states. Scotland's resistance to Charles I's religious policies sparked the Bishops' Wars and helped ignite the Wars of the Three Kingdoms; Scotland endured Cromwellian occupation in the 1650s, the Restoration, the "Killing Time" persecution of Covenanters, and the Revolution of 1688–89, which brought a Presbyterian settlement and, soon after, Jacobite risings.

Union 1707

Economic pressure — including the ruinous failure of the Darien scheme (1698–1700) — and English political leverage produced the Treaty of Union. The Acts of Union 1706/07, effective 1 May 1707, merged the Scottish and English parliaments into the Parliament of Great Britain, while Scotland preserved its legal system, established church, universities and burgh institutions.
