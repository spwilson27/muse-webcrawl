Pyrrhonism is a school of philosophical scepticism founded by Pyrrho of Elis in the 4th century BCE. Pyrrhonists suspend judgement (epoché) about all non-evident matters, arguing that for every claim an equally persuasive counter-claim can be found, and they report that this suspension leads, unexpectedly, to tranquillity of mind (Ataraxia). Unlike the Academic sceptics, who were often read as asserting that nothing can be known, Pyrrhonists avoid even that assertion.

History

Pyrrho wrote nothing; his thought was recorded by his disciple Timon of Phlius. The school was revived in the 1st century BCE by Aenesidemus, who formulated the Ten Modes — sets of arguments showing how appearances vary with the perceiver, circumstances and customs. Our fullest source is Sextus Empiricus (2nd–3rd century CE), whose Outlines of Pyrrhonism and Against the Mathematicians preserve the sceptical arguments that, rediscovered in the Renaissance, deeply influenced Montaigne, Descartes and Hume.

Arguments and practice

The later tradition organised the modes into the Five Modes attributed to Agrippa — dispute, infinite regress, relativity, hypothesis and circularity — designed to show that any justification offered for a belief itself requires justification. The Pyrrhonist nevertheless lives and acts according to appearances, following nature, the passions, laws and customs, and the arts, without assenting to dogmatic claims about how things are in themselves.
