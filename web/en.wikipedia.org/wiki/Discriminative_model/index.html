A discriminative model learns a boundary separating classes directly.

Discriminative models, also called conditional models, are a class of statistical and machine-learning models used for classification and regression. Rather than modelling how the data were generated, they model the conditional probability distribution P(Y | X) of the target variable Y given observed inputs X — or simply learn a direct mapping from inputs to a predicted class.

Contrast with generative models

A generative model learns the joint distribution P(X, Y), typically by modelling the class-conditional densities P(X | Y) together with class priors P(Y); predictions then follow from Bayes' rule. Naive Bayes and Gaussian discriminant analysis are generative. Because a generative model captures the full data distribution, it can also synthesise new samples and handle missing data gracefully, but it spends modelling effort on aspects of X that may be irrelevant to the classification task.

Typical examples

Logistic regression, support vector machines, decision trees and random forests, conditional random fields, and neural networks trained with cross-entropy loss are all ordinarily used as discriminative models: each is optimised to separate the classes, and none attempts to model the distribution of the inputs themselves.

Trade-offs

Classical results (for example, comparisons of naive Bayes and logistic regression) suggest generative classifiers can approach their (higher) asymptotic error faster with little data, while discriminative classifiers typically achieve lower error when training data are plentiful, since they focus capacity on the decision boundary. The distinction also shapes semi-supervised learning, where unlabelled data inform generative models naturally.
