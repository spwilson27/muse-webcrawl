Marcus Licinius Crassus

Born: c. 115 BC
Died: 53 BC, near Carrhae
Nationality: Roman
Office: Consul (70, 55 BC); triumvir
Known for: Defeating Spartacus; member of the First Triumvirate; death at Carrhae

Marcus Licinius Crassus (c. 115 – 53 BC) was a Roman general and statesman, the richest man in Rome, who played a leading role in the transformation of the Roman Republic into the Roman Empire. He formed the so-called First Triumvirate with Pompey and Julius Caesar.

Wealth

Crassus amassed an immense fortune through property speculation — famously buying burning and fire-damaged houses at low prices with the help of his private fire brigade — as well as mines, slave trading, and the proscriptions of Sulla, under whom he had fought in the civil wars and at the Battle of the Colline Gate (82 BC).

Spartacus and the triumvirate

In 72–71 BC Crassus was given command against the slave revolt of Spartacus, reviving decimation to stiffen discipline and ultimately crushing the rebellion, crucifying 6,000 captured slaves along the Appian Way. He shared the consulship with Pompey in 70 BC and with him again in 55 BC during the triumvirate years.

Death at Carrhae

Seeking military glory to match his partners, Crassus invaded Parthia in 54 BC. At the Battle of Carrhae (53 BC) his army was destroyed by Parthian horse archers under Surena; Crassus was killed during a parley, and later legend claimed the Parthians poured molten gold down his throat in mockery of his greed. His death destabilised the triumvirate and hastened the civil war between Caesar and Pompey.
