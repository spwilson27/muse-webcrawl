Hatsumōde

Hatsumōde (初詣) is the first Shinto shrine — or, commonly, Buddhist temple — visit of the Japanese New Year. Typically taking place during the first three days of January (sanganichi), it is one of the most widely observed customs in Japan, drawing tens of millions of worshippers who pray for health, safety, and good fortune in the coming year and purchase new protective amulets.

Customs

Worshippers typically visit a shrine on New Year's Eve or in the first days of January. Old amulets (omamori) from the previous year are returned to the shrine to be ritually burned, and new ones are bought for the year ahead. Visitors toss a coin into the offering box, bow twice, clap twice, pray, and bow once more. Many draw omikuji — paper fortune slips — and buy lucky charms such as hamaya (evil-destroying arrows). Sweet sake (amazake) and warm drinks are often served, and bells are rung at temples at midnight on New Year's Eve (joya no kane).

History

The custom descends from toshigomori, the practice of staying awake all night on New Year's Eve at the shrine of the local tutelary deity (ujigami), and from ehōmairi, visiting a shrine located in the year's auspicious direction. In the Meiji period, the growth of railways allowed mass excursions to famous shrines, turning hatsumōde into the popular custom it remains today.

Popular sites

The busiest sites each draw several million visitors over the first three days of January. They regularly include Meiji Shrine in Tokyo, Fushimi Inari Taisha in Kyoto, Naritasan Shinshō-ji in Chiba, Kawasaki Daishi in Kanagawa, and Sumiyoshi Taisha in Osaka. Police manage enormous crowds at these locations every year.
