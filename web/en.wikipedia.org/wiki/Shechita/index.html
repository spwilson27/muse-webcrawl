Shechita

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shechita (Hebrew: שחיטה) is the Jewish ritual method of slaughtering animals for food, required so that meat is kosher. It is performed by a specially trained slaughterer, a shochet, who severs the trachea, oesophagus, and the major blood vessels of the neck with a single, swift cut using an extremely sharp, smooth knife called a chalaf, producing rapid blood loss and unconsciousness.

Requirements

The laws of shechita derive from the Torah and are elaborated in the Talmud and the Shulchan Aruch. The knife must be perfectly smooth — a nick renders the animal non-kosher — and is inspected before and after use. Five acts invalidate the slaughter, including pausing during the cut, pressing the knife, or cutting outside the prescribed region of the neck. After slaughter, the carcass is inspected (bedikah), particularly the lungs for adhesions, and certain fats, blood vessels, and the sciatic nerve are removed (nikkur); blood is drained and salted out (melicha) before the meat may be eaten.

Debate and regulation

Because shechita is traditionally performed without prior stunning, it is debated in animal-welfare discussions, and some European countries require stunning before or immediately after the cut or have banned unstunned slaughter, while others protect religious slaughter in law. Jewish authorities maintain that the method is humane when correctly performed and have opposed restrictions as infringements of religious freedom. Comparable debate surrounds Islamic dhabiha slaughter.
