Zeta function

A zeta function is, in the broadest sense, a function of the form of an infinite series resembling the Riemann zeta function, ζ(s) = Σ n−s. Mathematicians have defined dozens of zeta functions across number theory, geometry, dynamical systems and mathematical physics; most encode, in their analytic behaviour, the arithmetic or spectral structure of the object from which they are built — typically through an Euler product over primes, closed orbits, or eigenvalues.

Major examples

The prototype is the Riemann zeta function, whose non-trivial zeros are the subject of the Riemann hypothesis. Its generalisations include the Dirichlet zeta and L-functions attached to characters and modular forms; the Dedekind zeta function of a number field, which counts ideals and has an Euler factor for every prime ideal; the Hasse–Weil zeta function of an algebraic variety, whose special cases underlie the Weil conjectures; and the Artin–Mazur and Ruelle zeta functions of dynamical systems, which count periodic orbits. In spectral theory, zeta functions of operators — defined from eigenvalues — underpin zeta-function regularisation, used by physicists to assign values to divergent determinants and sums.

Common features

Despite their diversity, zeta functions share a family resemblance: a Dirichlet series or product converging in some half-plane, a meromorphic continuation beyond it, often a functional equation relating s to some dual value, and special values or zeros carrying deep information. The pole of ζ(s) at s = 1 encodes the distribution of the primes; analogues elsewhere encode class numbers, ranks of elliptic curves, or the topology of a manifold.

Other zeta functions

The term also appears in combinatorics and lattice theory (the zeta function of a partially ordered set, whose inversion is the Möbius function of incidence algebras), in fractal geometry (complex dimensions and geometric zeta functions of fractal strings), and in graph theory (the Ihara zeta function of a graph, an analogue of the Selberg zeta function). The local zeta function of a variety over a finite field, the subject of the Weil conjectures proved by Deligne, counts points over finite fields and is a rational function — one of the great triumphs of twentieth-century arithmetic geometry.
