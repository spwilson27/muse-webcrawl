Damping

In physical systems, damping is the process by which an oscillation decreases in amplitude over time, as the system's energy is dissipated, typically into heat through friction or resistance. Damping is essential in engineering: it prevents destructive vibrations in structures and vehicles, while instruments such as shock absorbers and seismometers are designed around controlled damping.

The damped oscillator

The standard model is the damped harmonic oscillator, described by

m·ẍ + c·ẋ + k·x = 0

where m is mass, k the spring constant and c the viscous damping coefficient. The behaviour is governed by the damping ratio ζ = c / (2√(mk)):

  • Underdamped (ζ < 1): the system oscillates with exponentially decaying amplitude.
  • Critically damped (ζ = 1): the system returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible without oscillating.
  • Overdamped (ζ > 1): the system returns to equilibrium slowly, without oscillating.

Applications

Automotive shock absorbers are tuned near critical damping so wheels regain road contact quickly after a bump. Buildings in earthquake zones use tuned mass dampers — enormous pendulums or blocks, such as the 660-tonne damper in Taipei 101 — to absorb sway. In electrical engineering, damping in RLC circuits and in control systems is described by the same mathematics, and the Q factor measures how lightly a resonant system is damped.
