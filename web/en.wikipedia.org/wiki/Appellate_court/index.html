Appellate court

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An appellate court, commonly called a court of appeals, is a court with the power to review the judgment of a lower court or tribunal. Appeal differs from trial: appellate courts decide on the record below, take no new evidence (save exceptionally), and review for errors of law, findings of fact unsupported by evidence, and abuse of discretion, applying standards of review of varying deference. Their remedies include affirming, reversing, modifying and remanding for further proceedings.

Appeal structures vary: the United States maintains intermediate courts of appeals (thirteen federal circuits; state systems modelled similarly) beneath courts of last resort, with further review largely discretionary (certiorari); England and Wales route appeals to the Court of Appeal and onward, on points of law of general public importance, to the Supreme Court; civil-law systems traditionally allowed broader re-examination (appel in France rehearing fact and law, with cassation above for law only). Appellate courts make precedent: their published opinions are the main medium of Case law development, and their panels' composition and doctrines of deference (to legislatures, agencies and trial courts) are central questions of judicial politics.
