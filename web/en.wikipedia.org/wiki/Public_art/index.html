Public art is art created for, and sited in, publicly accessible places—streets, squares, parks, transit systems, and the exteriors and lobbies of civic buildings. Unlike art in museums, public art addresses an audience that has not chosen to visit an exhibition, and it is typically commissioned by governments, developers or community bodies rather than made for the market. The term is usually reserved for works produced under official or semi-official processes in the modern period, though its ancestors include the triumphal columns, equestrian statues, fountains and religious images that have occupied civic space since antiquity. Public art ranges from permanent monuments and memorials to temporary installations, murals, sound and light works, land art, and participatory "new genre public art" in which the work consists of social engagement itself.

History

Rulers have always used public imagery to project authority: Roman imperial fora, medieval cathedral sculpture, Renaissance civic statues such as Michelangelo's David (placed outside Florence's Palazzo della Signoria), and the absolutist monuments of Louis XIV set the pattern of the commissioned civic monument. The 19th century democratised and multiplied the genre—public subscription raised statues of national heroes across Europe and America (many of them, especially Confederate and colonial monuments, contested today)—while world's fairs and urban beautification (the City Beautiful movement) integrated art into planning. After 1945 the modern commissioning system took shape: the United States' General Services Administration "Art in Architecture" programme (from 1963) and the many municipal percent-for-art ordinances (Philadelphia first, in 1959) reserved a fraction—typically 1 percent—of public construction budgets for art, a model adopted worldwide.

From the 1960s, artists reacted against the "plop art" of abstract sculptures dropped into plazas: site-specific art (Richard Serra's Tilted Arc, 1981, whose removal after a public hearing in 1989 remains the field's defining controversy), environmental and land art, Maya Lin's Vietnam Veterans Memorial (1982)—a minimalist black granite wall whose initial unpopularity turned to canonical acclaim—and feminist and community practices recast what public art could be. Mexican muralism of the 1920s–30s (Rivera, Orozco, Siqueiros) had earlier demonstrated a politically engaged, narrative public art on a monumental scale, and its legacy persists in the community mural movements of Los Angeles, Belfast, Derry and elsewhere.

Forms and functions

Public art serves commemoration (war memorials, monuments to persons and events), place-making and civic identity (Antony Gormley's Angel of the North, 1998; Anish Kapoor's Cloud Gate, 2006), wayfinding, protest and counter-memory (the Stolpersteine stumbling-stones set into pavements across Europe; temporary Fourth Plinth works in London's Trafalgar Square), and social practice (Suzanne Lacy's large-scale performances; Theaster Gates's neighbourhood projects in Chicago). Because its audience is involuntary and its patron is often the state, public art is a perennial site of democratic argument: debates over the Confederate monuments in the United States, over colonial statues such as that of Cecil Rhodes at Oxford and Cape Town (#RhodesMustFall), and over who is commemorated at all (women and minorities being historically almost absent from civic statuary) are conducted through public art, sometimes ending in official removal and sometimes in iconoclasm. Conservation is a further challenge: outdoor works face weather, vandalism and redevelopment, and artists' moral rights (such as under the U.S. Visual Artists Rights Act) have collided with owners' property rights in prominent cases.

Commissioning

Modern commissions are usually awarded through competitions or panels involving artists, curators, architects and community representatives, with budgets covering fabrication, engineering, installation and maintenance endowments. Temporary programmes—biennales, festivals, plinth commissions and light festivals—have grown alongside permanent works, and unofficial public art (graffiti and street art, yarn bombing, guerrilla memorials) occupies an ambiguous position between vandalism and acclaim: Banksy's murals, once prosecuted, are now protected behind Perspex by the same authorities.
