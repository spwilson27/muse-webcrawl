Aphrodite of Knidos

Artist: Praxiteles
Year: 4th century BC
Medium: Parian marble (original, lost)
Subject: Aphrodite

The Aphrodite of Knidos (or Cnidus) was an Ancient Greek sculpture of the goddess Aphrodite created by the sculptor Praxiteles of Athens around the 4th century BC. It is one of the first life-sized representations of the nude female form in Greek history, and displayed alternative versions of clothed Aphrodite that Praxiteles created.

Description and history

According to Pliny the Elder, Praxiteles produced two statues of Aphrodite, one draped and one nude. The city of Kos bought the draped version, while the nude statue was purchased by the city of Knidos in Caria, where it was installed in an open temple that allowed it to be viewed from all sides. The goddess is shown preparing for her ritual bath, her drapery slipping onto a hydria beside her, one hand modestly shielding herself.

The statue became one of the most famous works of art in antiquity, drawing travellers to Knidos and inspiring numerous copies and variations — including the Venus de' Medici and the Capitoline Venus types. The original is lost; its appearance is known from Roman copies and from coins of Knidos.

Significance

The Knidian Aphrodite marked a turning point in Greek art: the first monumental female nude by a named master, it established a canon of female beauty that dominated Western art for centuries and remains a touchstone for discussions of the gaze, modesty, and eroticism in classical sculpture.
