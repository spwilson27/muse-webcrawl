Themistocles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Herm bust of Themistocles, Roman copy of a 5th-century original]

Themistocles (c. 524 – 459 BC) was an Athenian politician and general, the architect of Athens' naval power and of its survival in the Persian Wars. A 'new man' who rose through the young democracy, he persuaded the Assembly (482 BC) to spend the Laurion silver strike on 200 triremes — the fleet that, under his strategy, won at Salamis (480 BC) after he had manoeuvred both the Greek allies and Xerxes into fighting in the straits. After the war he rebuilt Athens' walls and the Piraeus fortifications against Spartan wishes; ostracised around 471 BC, he ended his life, in the war's great irony, as a Persian governor of Magnesia in Asia Minor.

Architect of the sea-power

Themistocles read Athens' future at sea earlier than any contemporary: he developed the Piraeus as the city's port, championed the thetes (the poorest citizens) who rowed the fleet and thereby deepened the democracy, and in 480 BC stage-managed the campaign — the evacuation of Athens, the 'wooden walls' oracle interpreted as the fleet, the secret message to Xerxes — that produced Salamis. Honoured at Sparta itself after the war, he pushed through the Long Walls plan in embryo and Athens' post-war fortification despite Spartan protests.

Fall and exile

His arrogance, wealth and rumoured Medism made enemies; ostracised (c. 471 BC), he was then implicated in the treason of the Spartan regent Pausanias and fled — through Argos, Corcyra, Epirus — to the court of Artaxerxes I, who granted him Magnesia and two other cities 'for bread, wine and relish'. There he learned Persian and died about 459 BC (Thucydides rejects the story that he poisoned himself rather than serve against Greece). Athens' saviour dying a Persian pensioner remains antiquity's favourite meditation on the fickleness of democracies.
