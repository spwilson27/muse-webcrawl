Volcanic crater

A volcanic crater is an approximately circular depression in the ground caused by volcanic activity. It is typically a bowl-shaped basin within which occurs a vent, or vents, that emit lava, tephra and gases. A crater may be large in dimension and sometimes of great depth. During certain types of explosive eruptions, a volcano's magma chamber may empty enough for an area above it to subside, forming a type of larger depression known as a caldera.

The summit crater of a stratovolcano, with a vent at its centre.

Formation

Craters form either by the accumulation of erupted material around a vent — building a raised rim — or by explosive excavation and collapse. Small craters along fissures are called pit craters; craters flooded by groundwater explosions are termed maars. Many craters partially fill with rainwater, forming crater lakes such as Lake Toba in Indonesia or Crater Lake in Oregon.

Examples

  • Halemaʻumaʻu crater, Kīlauea, Hawaii
  • The summit caldera of Mount Fuji, Japan
  • Irazú Volcano craters, Costa Rica
