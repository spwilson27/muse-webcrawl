Mesocyclone

A mesocyclone is a meso-gamma mesoscale region of rotation (vortex), typically around 2 to 10 km (1.2 to 6.2 mi) in diameter, within a convective storm. Most commonly the term refers to the mid-level rotation of a supercell thunderstorm, in which the mesocyclone is the rotating updraft that sustains the storm and from which tornadoes may descend.

A supercell whose updraft forms a mesocyclone

Formation

Mesocyclones form in environments with strong vertical wind shear. Horizontal vorticity generated by the shear is tilted into the vertical by a developing thunderstorm updraft, producing rotation on either side of the updraft; the cyclonic (counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere) member is ingested and stretched, intensifying into a mesocyclone. Dynamic pressure effects associated with the rotating updraft — a mid-level pressure minimum that accelerates air upward from below — help maintain the storm's longevity and can draw the rotation downward toward the surface, where further stretching beneath the updraft may tighten into a tornado.

Identification

On weather radar, a mesocyclone appears as a tight couplet of inbound and outbound velocities in Doppler velocity data, and often correlates with reflectivity features such as a hook echo and a bounded weak echo region. The US National Weather Service uses mesocyclone detection algorithms on NEXRAD data to assist in issuing tornado warnings. Visually, a well-developed mesocyclone is often marked by a lowered, rotating wall cloud beneath the rain-free base of the supercell.
