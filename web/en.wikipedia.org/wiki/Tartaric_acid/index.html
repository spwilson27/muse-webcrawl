Tartaric acid

Formula: C4H6O6

Molar mass: 150.09 g/mol

Appearance: White crystalline powder

Melting point: 169 °C (L-form)

E number: E334

Tartaric acid is a white, crystalline organic acid that occurs naturally in many fruits, most notably in grapes, bananas, tamarinds, and citrus. It is a diprotic aldaric acid with the formula C4H6O6. Its salt, potassium bitartrate — commonly known as cream of tartar — develops in wine casks during fermentation.

Stereochemistry

Tartaric acid is a classic object in the history of stereochemistry. It has two chiral carbon atoms and exists as a pair of enantiomers, the naturally occurring L-(+)-tartaric acid and its mirror image D-(−)-tartaric acid, plus an achiral meso form with an internal plane of symmetry. In 1848 Louis Pasteur manually separated crystals of sodium ammonium tartrate into left- and right-handed forms, providing the first demonstration of molecular chirality.

Occurrence and production

Tartaric acid is the principal acid of grapes and wine; "wine diamonds" of potassium bitartrate sometimes crystallize in bottled wine. Commercially, the natural L-enantiomer is produced from the by-products of winemaking (lees and argol). Synthetic routes yield racemic mixtures.

Uses

As a food additive (E334), tartaric acid is used as an antioxidant and acidulant, imparting a sour taste to wines, candies, and soft drinks. Cream of tartar is used in baking (to stabilize beaten egg whites and, with sodium bicarbonate, in baking powder), and tartaric acid and its salts (tartrates) serve in pharmaceuticals, tanning, and as chelating and reducing agents, e.g., in Fehling's solution (Rochelle salt, potassium sodium tartrate).
