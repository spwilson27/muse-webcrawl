Negotiation

[Image: Two parties seated across a table in discussion, illustrating a negotiation]

Negotiation is a dialogue between two or more parties intended to reach a beneficial outcome over one or more issues where a conflict exists with respect to those issues. It occurs in business, diplomacy, law, labour relations and everyday life, and aims to produce an agreement that the parties prefer to the alternatives available if no agreement is reached.

Approaches

Two broad orientations are commonly distinguished. Distributive negotiation (or "win–lose" bargaining) treats the negotiation as dividing a fixed amount of value, with each side seeking the largest possible share. Integrative negotiation (or "win–win" bargaining) seeks to expand the value available — for example by trading concessions on issues the parties value differently — so that both sides can gain. In practice most negotiations contain both elements.

The influential book Getting to Yes (1981) by Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton popularised principled negotiation, advising negotiators to separate the people from the problem, focus on interests rather than positions, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria.

Key concepts

BATNA — the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — is the course of action a party will take if talks fail; a party's bargaining power depends heavily on the attractiveness of its BATNA. Related concepts include the reservation price (the least favourable terms a party will accept), the zone of possible agreement (the overlap between the parties' acceptable ranges), and anchoring, the effect of the first offer on the eventual outcome. Preparation, information gathering, and understanding the other side's interests and constraints are widely regarded as decisive.

Process and tactics

Negotiations typically proceed through preparation, opening, information exchange, bargaining and closing/implementation. Tactics range from cooperative problem-solving to pressure tactics such as deadlines, take-it-or-leave-it offers, and strategic concessions. Ethical debates surround deceptive tactics such as bluffing about one's alternatives. Communication style, culture, emotion and power differences all influence outcomes; multi-party and multi-issue negotiations add coalition dynamics. Where talks stall, third parties may assist through mediation or arbitration (see Conflict resolution).

Fields of application

Negotiation is central to international diplomacy and treaty-making, collective bargaining between employers and unions, commercial contracts and mergers, plea bargaining in criminal law, and hostage and crisis negotiation. It is taught as a core skill in law, business and public policy schools, and studied in game theory and behavioural economics, where experiments examine fairness, reciprocity and cognitive biases in bargaining.
