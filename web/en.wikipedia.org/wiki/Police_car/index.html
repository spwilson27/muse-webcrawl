Police car

A marked police patrol car.

A police car is a ground vehicle used by police and other law enforcement agencies for transportation during patrols and responses to calls for service. A type of emergency vehicle, police cars are used for patrols, response, and occasionally pursuit of suspects, and are equipped with specialised equipment such as emergency lights, sirens, radios, and sometimes prisoner transport compartments.

Types

Police cars range from standard patrol vehicles to specialised units:

  • Patrol cars — the general-purpose marked vehicles used for routine patrol and first response.
  • Unmarked cars — vehicles without external markings, used by detectives and for covert operations.
  • Interceptor and pursuit vehicles — high-performance cars used for traffic enforcement and pursuits.
  • K-9 units — modified vehicles for transporting police dogs.
  • SUVs and trucks — increasingly common, used for carrying equipment and all-weather patrol.

Equipment

Typical equipment includes light bars or beacons, sirens, two-way radios or mobile data terminals, in-car video systems, speed detection devices (radar or lidar), first aid kits, and a partition between the front and rear seats. Many modern police cars feature automatic number-plate recognition (ANPR) cameras.

Livery

Marked police cars carry high-visibility liveries intended to deter crime and reassure the public. In the United Kingdom, the reflective yellow-and-blue Battenburg markings are standard; in the United States, black-and-white schemes are traditional in some jurisdictions, though liveries vary widely.

History

The first police car is often credited to Akron, Ohio, in 1899 — a battery-powered wagon used to transport prisoners. Motorised patrol expanded rapidly in the early twentieth century, and by the post-war era purpose-built "police package" versions of production cars, such as the Ford Crown Victoria Police Interceptor, became standard in North America.
