Hearts and minds (term)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

"Winning hearts and minds" is a concept in counterinsurgency warfare denoting efforts to persuade a population — emotionally ("hearts") and intellectually ("minds") — to support a government or occupying force rather than an insurgency. Instead of relying on military force alone, the approach uses security provision, governance, development aid and propaganda to win popular allegiance, on the theory that insurgencies depend on the population for recruits, supplies and concealment.

Origins

The pairing is older than its military usage: John Adams wrote in 1818 that the American Revolution "was effected before the War commenced... in the minds and hearts of the people." In its modern counterinsurgency sense the phrase is associated with Sir Gerald Templer, British High Commissioner during the Malayan Emergency, who said in 1952 that the answer lay "not in pouring more troops into the jungle, but in the hearts and minds of the people."

Vietnam and after

The phrase became inseparable from the Vietnam War, where US and South Vietnamese "pacification" programmes — including the CORDS programme from 1967 — tried to secure villages and deliver services. The failure of the war made "hearts and minds" a byword, in some quarters, for naive or cynical slogans at odds with brutal practice. The concept was revived in the 2000s in the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, where the US Army's 2006 counterinsurgency manual (FM 3-24, associated with General David Petraeus) again placed population security and legitimacy at the centre of strategy, with mixed results.

Wider usage

Beyond war, the phrase is now used generally for any campaign to gain public support — in politics, policing, marketing and organisational change — usually with the implication of winning both emotional commitment and rational assent.
