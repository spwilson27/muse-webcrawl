Academic grading in the United States

In the United States, academic grading commonly uses a five-letter scale — A, B, C, D and F — where A denotes the highest achievement and F failure. Most schools convert letter grades to grade points and average them to produce a grade point average (GPA) on a 0.0–4.0 scale.

Many institutions add plus and minus grades (e.g. B+ = 3.3), and many high schools weight honours and Advanced Placement courses, producing weighted GPAs above 4.0. There is no national standard; scales vary between school districts and universities.

History

American grading evolved from descriptive evaluations at colleges such as Yale in the eighteenth century through numerical scales in the nineteenth century to the letter system popularised in the early twentieth century. Mount Holyoke College is often credited with an early A–F scale in 1897.

Issues

Recurring debates concern grade inflation — average GPAs have risen substantially since the 1960s — the comparability of grades across schools, and alternatives such as narrative evaluations, standards-based grading and pass/fail options.
