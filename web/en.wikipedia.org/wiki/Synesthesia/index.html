Synesthesia

FieldNeurology, psychology

Synesthesia (American English) or synaesthesia (British English) is a perceptual phenomenon in which stimulation of one sensory or cognitive pathway leads to involuntary experiences in a second sensory or cognitive pathway. People with synesthesia, called synesthetes, might perceive letters or numbers as inherently coloured (grapheme–colour synesthesia, the most common form), "see" sounds as shapes or colours, taste words, or perceive sequences such as days of the week as arranged in spatial patterns around the body (sequence–space synesthesia).

Letters as perceived by a grapheme–colour synesthete.

Characteristics

Genuine synesthetic experiences are automatic, involuntary, consistent over time (a synesthete's letter colours remain stable for years, a fact used to test authenticity), and typically unidirectional — hearing a sound evokes a colour, but seeing the colour does not evoke the sound. Experiences may be "projected" into external space or "associated" in the mind's eye. Synesthesia runs in families and appears to have a genetic component, though the exact mechanisms are debated; leading hypotheses involve increased cross-connectivity between brain regions or disinhibited feedback between them.

Research history

Synesthesia was studied in the late 19th century by figures including Francis Galton, but fell out of favour during the behaviourist era because it relied on subjective reports. It returned to mainstream neuroscience in the 1980s and 1990s through the work of researchers such as Richard Cytowic, Simon Baron-Cohen and V. S. Ramachandran, who developed objective consistency tests and neuroimaging studies. Synesthesia is generally not considered a disorder: most synesthetes report neutral or positive attitudes to their experiences, and synesthesia is associated in some studies with enhanced memory for material related to the synesthetic associations.

Art and culture

Synesthetic metaphors are common in literature ("loud colours", "sweet sounds"), and several artists and composers — among them Wassily Kandinsky, Olivier Messiaen, and (disputedly) Alexander Scriabin, who designed a colour organ for Prometheus: The Poem of Fire — drew on cross-sensory ideas. Modern musicians including Pharrell Williams have described having synesthesia.
