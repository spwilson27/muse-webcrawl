ROM hacking

ROM hacking is the process of modifying a ROM image of a video game, usually with the intent of creating fan-made content such as new levels, graphics, dialogue, translations, or gameplay mechanics, or of altering game data for research and preservation. ROM hacks are typically distributed as patches—such as IPS, UPS, or BPS files—which modify a legally obtained copy of the original game and are played using an emulator or on original hardware via a flash cartridge. The practice flourished from the 1990s onward around 8-bit and 16-bit consoles such as the NES, SNES, Game Boy, and Sega Genesis.

Techniques

Hacking ranges from simple edits made with dedicated level editors to full-scale reverse engineering of a game's code. Common activities include hex editing; editing tiles and sprites; modifying text with relative searching and table files; tracing assembly code in an emulator's debugger to understand the game engine; and inserting translated text—fan translations of games never released outside Japan were an early driving force of the scene. Tools document ROM "maps" of data structures, and experienced hackers sometimes produce near-total conversions of a game.

Notable examples and community

Well-known hacks range from difficulty mods and "randomizers"—which shuffle item or enemy placement, popular for games such as The Legend of Zelda and the Pokémon series—to large-scale projects such as new story campaigns built in editors for games like Super Mario World. Dedicated Pokémon hack communities have produced extensive original games using the third-generation engines. Fan translations made titles such as Mother 3 and early Fire Emblem games accessible to English-speaking audiences. Communities such as ROMhacking.net (which wound down its archive operations in 2024) long served as central repositories.

Legal status

The legality of ROM hacking varies by jurisdiction; distributing pre-patched ROMs generally infringes copyright, whereas patches containing only original modifications are treated by communities as a grey area, and users are expected to dump their own cartridges. Publishers have sometimes acted against prominent projects through takedown notices, while others tolerate the hobbyist scene.
