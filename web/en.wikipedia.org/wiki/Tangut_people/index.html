Tangut people

The Tanguts (Tangut: 𗼎𗾧; Chinese: 黨項, Dangxiang) were a Tibeto-Burman-speaking people of northwestern China who founded the Western Xia dynasty (1038–1227). Originating among the Qiangic peoples of the upper Yellow River and the Tibetan plateau margins, Tangut clans migrated into the Ordos region during the late Tang dynasty and grew into a major power under the imperial clan surnamed Tuoba (later Li and Weiming).

Western Xia

In 1038 Li Yuanhao proclaimed the Western Xia empire, controlling the Hexi Corridor and much of present-day Ningxia, Gansu and northern Shaanxi. The Tanguts created their own complex logographic script — nearly 6,000 characters — translated Confucian and Buddhist texts on a large scale, and held their own against the Song dynasty, the Khitan Liao and the Jurchen Jin.

Destruction and assimilation

The Mongol conquest of 1226–1227, completed in Genghis Khan's final campaign, devastated Western Xia and dispersed the Tanguts; over the following centuries they were absorbed into Han Chinese, Mongol and Tibetan populations, and the Tangut language died out, the last inscription being dated 1502. The language and script were recovered for scholarship after Pyotr Kozlov's 1908–1909 excavation of the abandoned city of Khara-Khoto yielded a vast library of Tangut texts.
