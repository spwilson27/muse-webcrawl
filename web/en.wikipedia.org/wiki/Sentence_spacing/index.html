Sentence spacing

One space versus two after a sentence-ending period.

Sentence spacing concerns the horizontal space inserted after a sentence-ending punctuation mark, most commonly a full stop (period), in typeset and typed text. Two conventions have competed: single sentence spacing, one space between sentences, and double sentence spacing, two spaces. Modern style guides overwhelmingly recommend a single space, and digital typesetting systems typically collapse multiple spaces automatically, but the double-space habit persists among many writers trained on typewriters.

History

Early printers used variable and often generous spacing after sentences. Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, English and French printers diverged: the wider spacing sometimes called "English spacing" used an em space after sentences, while "French spacing" used a uniform word space throughout. In metal type, compositors could insert spaces of different widths — em, en, and thin spaces — and sentence spacing was often larger than word spacing.

The introduction of the typewriter in the late nineteenth century removed this flexibility: monospace typefaces gave every character the same width, and typists were commonly taught to strike the space bar twice after a period to create a visible break between sentences. This "typewriter spacing" became the standard in typing instruction through much of the twentieth century.

The one-space convention

Professional typesetters using proportional fonts generally set a single word space between sentences, arguing that the period itself, followed by a capital letter, adequately marks the sentence boundary and that extra space creates distracting gaps or "rivers" in the text. By the late twentieth century, major style manuals — including the Chicago Manual of Style, the MLA Handbook, and later editions of the APA Publication Manual — converged on recommending one space. Desktop publishing software, word processors, and HTML all default to rendering text with single spacing; HTML collapses consecutive spaces unless special markup is used.

Studies and debate

Readability studies have produced mixed results. Some early experiments with monospaced text found small advantages for two spaces in comprehension or reading speed, while studies using proportional fonts generally found no significant difference. A widely reported 2018 study suggested a minor reading-speed benefit for two spaces with monospaced fonts, but its methods and generality were criticized. Typographers and style authorities generally maintain that with modern proportional type, one space is both conventional and sufficient.

The topic periodically attracts disproportionate public passion, with partisans of two spaces citing their schooling and the visual clarity of the larger break, and one-space advocates citing professional practice. Because most publishing pipelines normalize spacing automatically, the distinction is invisible in most professionally published text regardless of the author's habit.

Related conventions

Sentence spacing is distinct from word spacing, line spacing (leading), and paragraph indentation. In some languages and traditions, spacing conventions differ before punctuation marks as well: French typographic practice, for example, places a narrow space before certain punctuation such as the colon, semicolon, question mark, and exclamation mark in some contexts.
