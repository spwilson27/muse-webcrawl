Escape from Freedom is a book by the German-American psychoanalyst Erich Fromm, first published in the United States in 1941 by Farrar & Rinehart. In the United Kingdom it was published as The Fear of Freedom. It is regarded as one of the founding works of political psychology.

Summary

Fromm asks why millions of people in a modern, ostensibly free society were willing to surrender their freedom to authoritarian regimes such as Nazi Germany. His answer centres on the burden that individual freedom imposes. The historical process of individuation — from the medieval world, where the individual was bound in a fixed social order (what Fromm calls "primary ties"), through the Renaissance, Reformation, and capitalism — freed people from traditional bonds but left many feeling isolated, powerless, and anxious.

Mechanisms of escape

Fromm proposes three principal mechanisms by which people escape from the burden of freedom:

  • Authoritarianism — the tendency to surrender independence and submit to a leader, state, or movement; its two poles are masochism and sadism, united in the "authoritarian character".
  • Destructiveness — the elimination of the threatening external world through aggression directed at persons, peoples, or oneself.
  • Automaton conformity — ceasing to be oneself and adopting the personality offered by cultural patterns, becoming indistinguishable from others.

Positive freedom

Against these escapes Fromm sets "positive freedom": the spontaneous activity of the total, integrated personality, expressed in love and productive work, which unites the individual with the world without surrendering individuality. He argues that democracy can survive only if it moves toward such positive freedom.

Reception and influence

The book made Fromm internationally known and remains a classic analysis of the social psychology of fascism, influencing later work on the authoritarian personality. Critics have questioned its historical simplifications and its idealisation of the medieval world, but its core question — why freedom is experienced as a burden — continues to be widely cited.
