Wort is the sugary liquid extracted from mashed grain before fermentation.

Wort (pronounced /wɜːrt/) is the sweet liquid extracted during the brewing process in the production of beer and whisky. It is produced by steeping crushed malted grain — usually barley — in hot water in a vessel called a mash tun, allowing enzymes in the malt to convert the grain's starches into fermentable sugars. The resulting liquid contains maltose and other sugars, dextrins, proteins and, once hops are added and the wort is boiled, the bittering and aroma compounds of the hop. When yeast is added and fermentation takes place, wort becomes beer (or, in distilling, the "wash" that is distilled into whisky).

Production

Mashing typically holds the grain and water mixture at about 63–68 °C (145–155 °F) for an hour or more, during which amylase enzymes break down starch into sugars. The liquid wort is then drained from the grain bed in a step called lautering, with the grains rinsed ("sparged") to recover remaining sugars. In brewing, the wort is next boiled, usually for about an hour, with hops added at intervals for bitterness, flavour and aroma; boiling sterilises the wort, concentrates it and precipitates proteins. After cooling, the clear wort is transferred to a fermenter and "pitched" with yeast.

Measurement

Brewers characterise wort by its sugar concentration, measured as specific gravity (the "original gravity") with a hydrometer or refractometer, or in degrees Plato or Balling. The difference between original and final gravity indicates how much sugar the yeast consumed and hence the alcohol content of the finished beer. Wort composition — its proportion of fermentable to unfermentable sugars — largely determines a beer's body, sweetness and strength.

Other meanings

The word derives from Old English wyrt, meaning a plant or herb, and survives in the names of many traditional medicinal plants, such as St John's wort, lungwort and mugwort. In older brewing usage, unfermented beer was itself called wort, and the term also appears in cheesemaking and in the "sweet wort" of malt vinegar production.
