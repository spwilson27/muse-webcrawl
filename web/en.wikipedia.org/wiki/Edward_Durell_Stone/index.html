Edward Durell Stone (March 9, 1902 – August 6, 1978) was an American architect known for the formal, highly decorative buildings he designed in the 1950s and 1960s. His best-known works include the United States Embassy in New Delhi, the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts in Washington, D.C., and the original Museum of Modern Art building in New York.

Career

Stone was born in Fayetteville, Arkansas, and studied at the University of Arkansas, Harvard University, and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, though he left without a degree. After winning the Rotch Travelling Scholarship in 1927 he spent two years in Europe. In the early 1930s he worked in the International Style, designing the Mandel House in Mount Kisco, New York, and — with Philip Goodwin — the Museum of Modern Art building (1939), a landmark of American modernism.

During the 1950s Stone turned against austere modernism and developed a personal "New Formalism," using screens, grilles, marble, and classical proportions. His United States Embassy in New Delhi (1954–1959), praised by Frank Lloyd Wright, made him a celebrity architect; he appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1958. Major commissions followed: the U.S. Pavilion at the Brussels World's Fair (1958), the Kennedy Center (opened 1971), the General Motors Building and the Museum of Television and Radio (Paley Center) in New York, and numerous university and corporate campuses, including the Stanford University Medical Center and the National Geographic Society headquarters.

Style and criticism

Stone's ornamented, romantic modernism was popular with clients and the public but was often disparaged by modernist critics as kitsch or "early Hilton." Later generations reassessed his work as an important alternative current within mid-century American architecture. He died in New York City in 1978.
