Dyskinesia

Dyskinesias arise from dysfunction in the basal ganglia circuits that regulate movement.

Dyskinesia refers to a category of movement disorders characterised by involuntary muscle movements, ranging from subtle fidgeting and tics to severe whole-body writhing (chorea and athetosis). The term literally means "difficulty in performing voluntary movements". Dyskinesias are commonly encountered as complications of neurological disease and of long-term treatment with dopaminergic or dopamine-blocking drugs.

Types and causes

Levodopa-induced dyskinesia develops in many patients with Parkinson's disease after years of levodopa therapy, appearing as choreiform movements at peak drug effect. Tardive dyskinesia is a potentially persistent syndrome of repetitive movements—often of the face, lips and tongue—caused by prolonged use of antipsychotic (dopamine-receptor blocking) medication. Dyskinesia also occurs in Huntington's disease, in Wilson's disease, and as paroxysmal dyskinesias, brief attacks triggered by movement, caffeine or stress, often with a genetic basis. In cardiology the term is also used for abnormal, paradoxical motion of a segment of the heart wall.

Treatment

Management depends on the cause: adjusting levodopa dose or delivery, adding amantadine or using deep brain stimulation in Parkinson's disease; and for tardive dyskinesia, VMAT2 inhibitors such as valbenazine and deutetrabenazine, approved specifically for that condition. Avoiding or minimising the offending drug, where possible, is the first step.
