Eastern Armenian (Armenian: արևելահայերեն, arevelahayeren) is one of the two standardised forms of modern Armenian, the other being Western Armenian. It is the official language of the Republic of Armenia and is spoken in Armenia and by Armenian communities in Russia, Georgia, Iran (where the older Yerevan-based standard also influenced usage) and other countries of the former Soviet Union. It is based on the dialect of Yerevan.

Development

Eastern Armenian emerged as a literary standard in the 19th century, centred on Yerevan and Tiflis (Tbilisi) and the Armenian communities of the Russian Empire; writers such as Khachatur Abovian, author of Wounds of Armenia (1841), pioneered the use of the modern vernacular (ashkharhabar) in place of Classical Armenian (grabar). Following the establishment of Soviet Armenia, a spelling reform in 1922 (modified in 1940) simplified the orthography; the resulting "reformed orthography" is used in Armenia today, while most Western Armenian speakers and the Armenian community in Iran retain the classical orthography.

Differences from Western Armenian

The most conspicuous difference is phonological: Eastern Armenian preserves the three-way contrast of stop consonants of Classical Armenian (voiced, voiceless unaspirated and aspirated), whereas Western Armenian shifted to a two-way contrast, so that letters transliterated b, d, g in the East are pronounced like p, t, k in the West. There are also differences in grammar (for example, in the formation of some verb tenses and cases), vocabulary borrowings (Russian and Persian loans in the East versus Turkish loans in the West) and the orthographic reform noted above. The two standards remain mutually intelligible to a considerable degree, especially in writing.
