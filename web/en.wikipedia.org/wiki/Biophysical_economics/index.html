Biophysical economics

Biophysical economics treats the economy as a system governed by the laws of thermodynamics.

Biophysical economics is a school of heterodox economics that applies the laws of thermodynamics and principles of ecology to economic systems. It holds that the economy is an open subsystem of the biosphere, dependent on flows of energy and materials, and therefore ultimately constrained by physical limits such as the availability of net energy and the entropy law.

History

The approach grew out of the work of Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen, whose 1971 book The Entropy Law and the Economic Process argued that neoclassical production theory ignored the qualitative degradation of energy and matter. Howard T. Odum developed energy systems analysis and the concept of emergy, while Charles A. S. Hall and colleagues formalized the measure of energy return on investment (EROI) in the 1980s. Biophysical economics overlaps strongly with ecological economics, though the latter gives greater weight to social and institutional questions.

Key concepts

Central concepts include net energy — the energy surplus remaining after the energy cost of obtaining energy — and EROI, the ratio of energy delivered to energy invested. A complex industrial society is thought to require an EROI of at least roughly 10:1 from its primary energy sources. The field also studies the relationship between energy consumption and economic output, the physical basis of money and debt, and the possibility that declining resource quality will constrain future growth.

Criticism

Mainstream economists criticize biophysical approaches for undervaluing technological substitution, price signals and the role of knowledge in decoupling output from material throughput. Proponents respond that substitution cannot repeal thermodynamics and that monetary measures systematically hide physical decline.
