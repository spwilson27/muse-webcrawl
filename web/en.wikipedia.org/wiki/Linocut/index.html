Linocut

Linocut, also known as lino print, lino printing or linoleum art, is a printmaking technique, a variant of woodcut in which a sheet of linoleum (sometimes mounted on a block of wood) is used for a relief surface. A design is cut into the linoleum surface with a sharp knife, V-shaped chisel or gouge, with the raised (uncarved) areas representing a reversal (mirror image) of the parts to be printed.

Technique

The linoleum sheet is inked with a roller (a brayer), and then impressed onto paper or fabric. Printing may be done by hand or with a printing press. Because linoleum lacks a directional grain, it permits broad, smooth cut areas and is softer and easier to carve than wood, though it cannot hold lines as fine and its edges crumble under high press pressure.

History

Linoleum was patented by Frederick Walton in the 1860s as a floor covering; artists adopted it for printmaking in the early 20th century. The technique was used by the German Die Brücke group, by Pablo Picasso and Henri Matisse, and became a staple of school art classes and of poster and propaganda art, including the celebrated British schools prints and the work of artists such as Sybil Andrews and Lill Tschudi of the Grosvenor School.

Reduction linocut

In the reduction method, associated with Picasso, a single block is progressively cut away and printed in successive colours, so the whole edition must be printed before the next stage of cutting destroys the previous layer.
