The Estonian Legion (Estonian: Eesti Leegion) was a military formation of the German Waffen-SS raised in German-occupied Estonia during the Second World War. Announced by the Estonian police chief Hjalmar Mäe on 28 August 1942, it recruited Estonian volunteers to fight on the Eastern Front against the Soviet Union. The Legion fought principally on the Velikiye Luki and Nevel sectors before it was enlarged in 1943–1944 into the 3rd Estonian SS Volunteer Brigade and then the 20th Waffen Grenadier Division of the SS (1st Estonian).

Formation

Estonia had been annexed by the Soviet Union in 1940 and occupied by Germany after the summer of 1941. Anti-communist sentiment, sharpened by the Soviet deportations of June 1941, made recruitment feasible. The Legion's first battalion, commanded by Alfons Rebane, completed training in Poland and was sent to the front near Velikiye Luki in the winter of 1942–43, fighting alongside the German 2nd SS Infantry Brigade.

Expansion

In May 1943 the Legion was expanded into the 3rd Estonian SS Volunteer Brigade, which fought around Nevel and was committed to anti-partisan operations. Following the German decision to mobilise Estonians more broadly, the brigade was used in January 1944 as the core of the 20th Waffen Grenadier Division of the SS (1st Estonian), which fought in the battles at Narva and the Sinimäed Hills during the Soviet summer offensive of 1944, before retreating into Germany and surrendering to Soviet forces in Czechoslovakia in May 1945.

Post-war assessments

The Estonian International Commission for the Investigation of Crimes Against Humanity concluded that the Legion itself, as a front-line formation, was not implicated in crimes against humanity, although some members had previously served in police formations that had taken part in atrocities. The subject remains politically sensitive in Estonia, where veterans have been commemorated by some as fighters against a renewed Soviet occupation, while Russia and others regard commemoration of Waffen-SS units as glorification of Nazism.
