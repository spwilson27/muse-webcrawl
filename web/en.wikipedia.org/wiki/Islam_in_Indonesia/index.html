Islam in Indonesia

Islam is the dominant religion in Indonesia, the country with the world's largest Muslim population. Roughly 87% of Indonesians identify as Muslim, corresponding to well over 230 million people. The vast majority are Sunni Muslims following the Shafi'i school of jurisprudence, with significant minorities of Shia and Ahmadi Muslims. Indonesia is officially a republic with a multi-religious character: the state ideology of Pancasila requires belief in one God but does not establish Islam as a state religion.

History

Arrival and spread

Islam was brought to the archipelago mainly by traders and Sufi missionaries from Arabia, India (especially Gujarat) and China from about the 13th century onward. Marco Polo reported a Muslim community at Perlak in northern Sumatra in 1292, and the gravestone of Sultan Malikussaleh of Samudera Pasai (died 1297) marks the first Muslim kingdom in the region. From coastal trading ports the faith spread along maritime routes to Java, where the nine saints known as the Wali Songo are credited with its propagation, and onward to Kalimantan, Sulawesi and the Maluku Islands, largely through trade and intermarriage rather than conquest.

Sultanates and colonial era

Muslim sultanates such as Demak, Banten, Mataram, Aceh and Ternate became the region's major powers. The Acehnese sultanate was a notable centre of Islamic scholarship in the 16th and 17th centuries. Under Dutch colonial rule, Islam provided a focus of identity and resistance, and in the early 20th century mass organisations were founded: Muhammadiyah (1912), a modernist reform movement, and Nahdlatul Ulama (1926), representing traditionalist scholars — today two of the largest independent Islamic organisations in the world.

Independence

At independence in 1945, proposals to make Indonesia an Islamic state (the Jakarta Charter) were set aside in favour of the Pancasila framework. A Ministry of Religious Affairs oversees religious life, and Islamic law applies chiefly in personal and family matters, except in Aceh, which under special autonomy implements a broader system of sharia by-laws.

Practice and society

Indonesian Islam is known for its blending with local customs (adat), reflected in traditions such as the slametan communal feast and the veneration of saints' tombs. Religious education is delivered through mosques, madrasas and the pesantren boarding schools run by Nahdlatul Ulama. The Indonesian Ulema Council (MUI) issues fatwas, and the state recognises Islamic holidays including Eid al-Fitr (Idul Fitri) and Eid al-Adha.

Demographics

Muslims form the majority in most provinces of Sumatra, Java, Kalimantan and Sulawesi. Significant non-Muslim minorities include Christians (concentrated in parts of eastern Indonesia and North Sumatra), Hindus (predominant in Bali) and Buddhists. Aceh is the most observant region, while urban Java hosts both modernist and traditionalist strongholds.
