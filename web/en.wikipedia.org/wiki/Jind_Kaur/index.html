Maharani Jind Kaur (1817 – 1 August 1863) was regent of the Sikh Empire from 1843 until 29 March 1847. She was the youngest wife of the first maharaja of the Sikh Empire, Ranjit Singh, and the mother of the last maharaja, Duleep Singh. She was renowned for her beauty, energy and strength of purpose, and was popularly known as Rani Jindan.

Regency

After Ranjit Singh's death in 1839 the empire descended into factional violence. When her five-year-old son Duleep Singh was proclaimed maharaja in 1843, Jind Kaur became regent and governed with considerable ability, reviving the council of regency and rallying the Khalsa army. The British regarded her as a formidable opponent — "the Messalina of the Punjab," in one official's phrase — during the First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46).

Downfall and exile

After the British victory, the Treaty of Bhyroval (1846) stripped her of power; in 1847 she was exiled to Sheikhupura and then Benares, separated from her son, who was taken into British guardianship. She escaped from Chunar fort in 1849, disguised as a servant, and fled to Nepal, where she lived under surveillance for a decade. In 1861 she was permitted to join Duleep Singh in England, where she died in Kensington in 1863. Her remains were later repatriated and, in 2024, her tombstone — long displayed in the V&A — featured in renewed public interest in her story.

Legacy

Jind Kaur has been reclaimed in modern times as a symbol of Sikh resistance to British annexation of the Punjab (1849) and of female political power in 19th-century India. She has been the subject of plays, novels and films.
