Discrete trial training (DTT) is a technique used by practitioners of applied behavior analysis (ABA) that was developed by Ole Ivar Løvaas at the University of California, Los Angeles. It is a structured teaching method particularly used in early intensive behavioural intervention for children with autism spectrum disorder, in which a skill is broken down into its most basic components and taught one step at a time through repeated, clearly delimited trials.

Structure of a trial

Each discrete trial consists of a sequence: the therapist presents a discriminative stimulus (an instruction or cue), the learner responds, and the therapist delivers a consequence — reinforcement for a correct response, or a prompt/correction for an incorrect one — followed by a brief inter-trial interval before the next trial begins. Data are recorded on each trial so progress can be measured and programs adjusted.

History and reception

Løvaas's 1987 study reported that a substantial proportion of children receiving intensive DTT-based intervention (around 40 hours per week) achieved educational placements in mainstream classrooms, results that made the method widely adopted but also debated; later research and meta-analyses have produced more mixed conclusions, and critics — including many autistic self-advocates — have raised concerns about the intensive and compliance-focused nature of traditional programmes. Modern practice often combines DTT with more naturalistic teaching approaches such as pivotal response treatment.
