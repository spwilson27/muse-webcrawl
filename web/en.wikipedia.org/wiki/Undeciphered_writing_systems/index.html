Undeciphered writing systems

A number of writing systems of the ancient world remain undeciphered: their signs are recorded, but their sound values or meanings cannot yet be read, usually because the underlying language is unknown, bilingual texts are lacking, or the surviving corpus is too small. Attempts at decipherment must be treated with caution, as the field attracts many unverified claims; compare the famous successes with Egyptian hieroglyphs (via the Rosetta Stone) and Linear B, decoded by Michael Ventris in 1952.

Major cases

Indus script. Some 400–600 distinct signs appear on seals and other objects from the Indus Valley Civilisation, but inscriptions average only about five signs, and no bilingual text exists; scholars dispute whether it encodes a full language. Linear A, used by the Minoans, shares many sign shapes with the deciphered Linear B, so approximate sound values can be assigned, but the language ("Minoan") remains unidentified. Rongorongo of Easter Island, carved on wooden tablets, may be a genuine independent invention of writing, but its small surviving corpus resists reading. Proto-Elamite tablets from ancient Iran number in the thousands yet remain largely unread, though progress continues on their numerical systems. Other undeciphered material includes Cretan hieroglyphs, the Byblos syllabary, the Phaistos Disc (a unique artefact of uncertain authenticity of interpretation), Etruscan's relatives such as Lemnian (Etruscan itself can be read phonetically but its language is only partly understood), and the Voynich manuscript, which may or may not be writing at all.

Requirements for decipherment

Successful decipherments generally need a sufficient corpus, knowledge of the underlying language or a close relative, and constraints from context—names, numbers, or parallel texts. Statistical and computational methods assist, but none has yet cracked the systems listed here to scholarly consensus.
