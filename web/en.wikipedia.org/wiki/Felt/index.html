Felt

Felt is a textile material produced by matting, condensing and pressing fibres together. Unlike woven or knitted cloth, felt is a nonwoven fabric: its fibres interlock through heat, moisture, friction and pressure rather than through a loom structure. It can be made from natural fibres such as wool, or from synthetic fibres such as acrylic.

Production

Animal hairs have tiny surface scales that catch on one another when agitated in warm, soapy water. In traditional wet felting, layers of carded wool are wetted with hot soapy water and rubbed or rolled until the fibres migrate and lock into a dense mat. Needle felting, a dry method, uses barbed needles to tangle fibres. Industrial felt is often made by needle-punching synthetic fibres or by fulling woven wool cloth.

History

Felting is among the oldest textile technologies, predating weaving; well-preserved felt artefacts from the Pazyryk burials date to the 5th century BC, and felt-making was central to the material culture of Central Asian nomads, who used it for tents (yurts), rugs, clothing and horse gear. In Europe felt was long essential to hat-making — a trade associated with mercury poisoning from the carroting process, hence the "mad hatter". Legend attributes the discovery of felting to St Clement, patron saint of feltmakers.

Uses

Felt is used in clothing and hats, crafts, musical instruments (piano hammers, dampers), machinery (gaskets, seals, polishing pads), acoustics, filtration, and traditional dwellings. It insulates well, absorbs sound and does not fray when cut.
