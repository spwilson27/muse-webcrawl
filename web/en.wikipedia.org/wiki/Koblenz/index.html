Koblenz

Koblenz (German: [ˈkoːblɛnts]; French: Coblence) is a city in the German state of Rhineland-Palatinate, situated at the confluence of the Rhine and Moselle rivers — the famous Deutsches Eck ("German Corner"). It is one of Germany's oldest cities, founded as a Roman military post around 8 BC, and celebrated its 2,000th anniversary in 1992.

History

Drusus established a fort here around 8 BC under the name Castellum apud Confluentes ("fort at the confluence"), from which the name Koblenz derives. The city later belonged to the Electorate of Trier; the electors resided in the Electoral Palace built in the 1780s. Koblenz was a centre of French émigrés during the Revolution and was itself annexed by France. After 1815 it passed to Prussia and was heavily fortified as part of the Festung Koblenz system, including the Ehrenbreitstein Fortress on the right bank of the Rhine.

The city was heavily bombed in the Second World War. At the Deutsches Eck stands a monumental equestrian statue of Kaiser Wilhelm I, first erected in 1897, destroyed in 1945 and re-erected in 1993. Koblenz lies within the Upper Middle Rhine Valley UNESCO World Heritage landscape and hosted the Bundesgartenschau (Federal Garden Show) in 2011, for which a cable car was built across the Rhine to Ehrenbreitstein.

Landmarks

Notable sights include the Basilica of St. Castor, the oldest church in the city, where the Treaty of Verdun was negotiated in 843; the Liebfrauenkirche and Florinskirche; Stolzenfels Castle to the south; and the Electoral Palace. Koblenz is also a seat of the Federal Archives (Bundesarchiv) of Germany.
