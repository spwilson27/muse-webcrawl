Glass wool is an insulating material made from fibres of glass arranged into a texture similar to wool. The process traps many small pockets of air between the glass fibres, and the resulting product has high thermal insulation and sound insulation properties. It is produced in rolls or in slabs, with different thermal and mechanical properties, and is one of the most widely used forms of insulation in buildings worldwide.

Manufacturing

Glass wool is manufactured by melting sand, soda ash, limestone and other raw materials — often with a large proportion of recycled glass cullet — in a furnace at temperatures around 1,400–1,500 °C. The molten glass is then spun into fine fibres, historically by a process patented by Games Slayter in the 1930s for Owens-Illinois, using centrifugal spinning or a steam/air blowing process. A binder, traditionally a phenol-formaldehyde resin (giving the familiar yellow colour), is sprayed onto the fibres; newer products use bio-based or acrylic binders. The mat is cured in an oven, compressed and cut into batts, rolls or rigid boards.

Properties and uses

The entrapped air gives glass wool a low thermal conductivity, typically 0.03 to 0.04 W/(m·K), making it effective for loft, wall cavity and pipe insulation. It also absorbs sound and is non-combustible, melting rather than burning. It is used in residential and commercial construction, in heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) ductwork, and as insulation in domestic appliances and vehicles.

Health and safety

Handling glass wool can irritate the skin, eyes and respiratory system, so gloves, eye protection and dust masks are recommended during installation. Modern glass wool fibres are biosoluble and are classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as not classifiable as to carcinogenicity to humans (Group 3); older concerns about respirable fibres led to reformulation of the products from the 1990s onward.
