Grant-in-aid

Grants-in-aid transfer funds between levels of government for specified purposes.

A grant-in-aid is a transfer of money from one level of government to another — or from a government to a private or non-profit organisation — to be used for a specific purpose. Because the funds are provided for designated ends and often under stated conditions, grants-in-aid differ from general revenue sharing.

Forms

Common forms include:

  • Categorical grants — for narrow purposes (e.g. highway construction, school lunches), typically with detailed conditions and reporting requirements.
  • Block grants — for broad functional areas (e.g. community development, public health), giving recipients more discretion in spending.
  • Matching grants — requiring the recipient to contribute a proportion of the cost, intended to stimulate local spending and commitment.
  • Formula vs. project grants — distributed by a statutory formula, or awarded competitively on application.

United States

In the United States, federal grants-in-aid to state and local governments grew substantially during the 20th century, especially under the New Deal and the Great Society programmes. They now fund major shares of Medicaid, transportation, education and welfare programmes and are a central mechanism of American fiscal federalism — and a recurring source of political debate over federal influence on state policy.

United Kingdom

In Britain, "grant-in-aid" is also the standard funding mechanism by which government departments finance arm's-length bodies (non-departmental public bodies), such as museums and research agencies, under a framework document setting out conditions of use.
