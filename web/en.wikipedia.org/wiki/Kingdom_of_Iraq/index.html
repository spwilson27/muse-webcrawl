The Kingdom of Iraq (Arabic: المملكة العراقية) was a Hashemite monarchy in the Middle East established in 1921 under a British mandate and lasting, after independence in 1932, until the revolution of 14 July 1958, when the monarchy was overthrown and a republic proclaimed.

Establishment under the mandate

Following the Ottoman Empire's defeat in the First World War and the Iraqi revolt of 1920, Britain combined the Ottoman vilayets of Baghdad, Basra, and (later) Mosul into a Class A mandate. At the Cairo Conference of 1921, Faisal ibn Husayn — son of the Sharif of Mecca and former king of Syria — was selected as king and confirmed by plebiscite. Faisal I was crowned in Baghdad in August 1921. Under the Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930, the mandate ended and Iraq joined the League of Nations as an independent state on 3 October 1932, though Britain retained air bases and strong influence.

Reigns of Ghazi and Faisal II

After Faisal I's death in 1933, his son Ghazi became king; Ghazi died in a car accident in 1939, leaving his four-year-old son Faisal II on the throne under the regency of Prince 'Abd al-Ilah. Politics in the period were dominated by the veteran statesman Nuri al-Said. In 1941 the pro-Axis government of Rashid Ali al-Gaylani briefly seized power, prompting the British invasion in the Anglo-Iraqi War and the restoration of the regent.

Postwar period and fall

Postwar Iraq saw urban unrest, including the 1948 Wathbah uprising against a renewed British treaty, while oil revenues transformed the state's finances; the Development Board channeled income into infrastructure. Iraq joined the Baghdad Pact in 1955, aligning with the West against regional nationalism. Growing opposition to the monarchy's foreign alignments and domestic inequalities culminated on 14 July 1958, when Free Officers led by Abd al-Karim Qasim and Abdul Salam Arif seized Baghdad. King Faisal II, the former regent 'Abd al-Ilah, and Nuri al-Said were killed, and the Republic of Iraq was proclaimed.
