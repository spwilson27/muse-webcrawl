Lutheranism is a major branch of Western Christianity that identifies with the theology of Martin Luther, the German monk and reformer whose challenge to the Catholic Church launched the Protestantism Reformation. Lutherans form the historically dominant churches of Germany and Scandinavia — several of them former or current state churches — and significant communities in North America, Africa and Asia.

Teachings

Lutheran doctrine is summarised in the Book of Concord (1580), including the Augsburg Confession of 1530. Its centre is justification by grace alone through faith alone: sinners are declared righteous for Christ's sake, apart from works. Scripture is the sole norm of doctrine. Lutherans retain a strongly sacramental piety: baptismal regeneration and the real presence of Christ's body and blood "in, with and under" the bread and wine of the Eucharist (sacramental union), rejecting both transubstantiation and a merely symbolic reading. Luther's Small and Large Catechisms remain standard teaching texts.

History and diversity

After Luther's death (1546), Lutheran territories consolidated under the Peace of Augsburg (1555) principle cuius regio, eius religio. Lutheran orthodoxy, then Pietism's devotional renewal, shaped German and Nordic culture; Lutheran missionaries and emigrants carried the tradition worldwide. Modern Lutheranism spans confessional bodies emphasising historic doctrine, and the large churches of the Lutheran World Federation (founded 1947), most of which ordain women and practise open communion with partner churches. The 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification with the Catholic Church formally resolved the central doctrinal dispute of the Reformation era.
