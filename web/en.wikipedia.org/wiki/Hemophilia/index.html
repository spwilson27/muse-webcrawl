Hemophilia (also spelled haemophilia) is a mostly inherited genetic disorder that impairs the body's ability to make blood clots, a process needed to stop bleeding. This results in people bleeding for a longer time after an injury, easy bruising, and an increased risk of bleeding inside joints or the brain. There are two main types: hemophilia A, which occurs due to low amounts of clotting factor VIII, and hemophilia B, which occurs due to low levels of clotting factor IX. A rarer third form, hemophilia C, results from deficiency of factor XI.

Cause and inheritance

Hemophilia A and B are inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern, so they occur almost always in males, while females are usually carriers — though carriers may have mild symptoms. Queen Victoria was a famous carrier of hemophilia B; the condition spread through the royal families of Europe via her descendants, affecting, among others, the Spanish and Russian royal houses (Tsarevich Alexei Nikolaevich of Russia was a notable sufferer). About a third of cases arise from new mutations with no family history. Severity depends on factor levels: severe disease (less than 1 per cent of normal) involves spontaneous bleeding, especially into joints (hemarthrosis), which over time causes disabling joint damage.

Treatment

Treatment consists of replacing the missing clotting factor, either on demand for bleeds or regularly as prophylaxis. Concentrates were historically made from donor plasma; in the 1980s many people with hemophilia were infected with HIV and hepatitis C through contaminated products, a major medical scandal. Modern recombinant factor products and non-factor therapies such as the monoclonal antibody emicizumab have greatly improved safety and convenience, and gene therapies for hemophilia A and B have been approved in the 2020s.
