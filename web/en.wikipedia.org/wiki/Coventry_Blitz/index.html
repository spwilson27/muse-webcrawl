Coventry Blitz

The Coventry Blitz was a series of bombing raids by the German Luftwaffe on the British city of Coventry during the Second World War. The most devastating attack took place on the night of 14–15 November 1940, when more than 500 bombers attacked the city in an operation codenamed Mondscheinsonate ("Moonlight Sonata").

The 14 November raid

Coventry was a major centre of the British motor and armaments industries, making it a natural target. Pathfinder aircraft marked the city with flares, guided by the X-Gerät radio navigation system, which British countermeasures failed to jam effectively that night. Over eleven hours, the bombers dropped hundreds of tons of high explosive and tens of thousands of incendiary bombs, starting fires that merged into a firestorm in the city centre.

Some 568 people were killed — estimates vary — and more than 4,000 homes and much of the city's industrial capacity were destroyed or damaged. The medieval St Michael's Cathedral was gutted, leaving only its tower, spire and outer walls. The scale of destruction led the German propaganda ministry to popularise the verb coventrieren — "to Coventrate", to annihilate a city from the air.

Aftermath and mythology

A persistent myth holds that Winston Churchill knew of the raid in advance from Ultra decrypts of Enigma traffic and allowed the city to be destroyed to protect the secret. Historians have largely rejected this: the intelligence available indicated an attack somewhere in the Midlands, and countermeasures were in fact attempted.

Further heavy raids followed in April 1941. After the war, Coventry rebuilt its centre, built a new cathedral beside the ruins of the old (consecrated in 1962), and became a symbol of post-war reconciliation, twinning with Kiel and Dresden.
