Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta

The Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (Pali; Sanskrit: Dharmacakrapravartana Sūtra), usually translated as the "Setting in Motion the Wheel of the Dhamma Sutta" or "Turning the Wheel of Dharma", is a Buddhist text that records the first teaching given by the Buddha after his enlightenment. It was delivered at the Deer Park at Isipatana (modern Sarnath), near Varanasi, to the five ascetics who had formerly been the Buddha's companions. The discourse sets out the Middle Way, the Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path.

Contents

The sutta opens with the Buddha rejecting two extremes to be avoided by one who has gone forth: indulgence in sensual pleasure, which is "low, vulgar, coarse, ignoble and unbeneficial", and devotion to self-mortification, which is "painful, ignoble and unbeneficial". Between them lies the Middle Way — the Noble Eightfold Path of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration — which leads to peace, insight and nirvana.

The discourse then expounds the Four Noble Truths: the truth of suffering (dukkha), the truth of its origin in craving, the truth of its cessation, and the truth of the path leading to its cessation. Each truth is presented in three phases — recognising the truth, understanding the task it implies, and knowing that the task has been accomplished — giving the "three turnings and twelve aspects" of the Buddha's realisation. The text closes by saying that, on hearing the teaching, the ascetic Koṇḍañña attained the "dustless, stainless Dhamma-eye", becoming the first stream-enterer, and that the devas proclaimed the turning of the wheel of Dhamma through the cosmos.

Significance

The sermon is traditionally dated to the full-moon day of the month of Āsāḷha, commemorated as Asalha Puja (Dhamma Day). The event marks the founding of the Buddhist saṅgha, and the sutta (preserved in the Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 56.11, with parallels in other canons) is among the most widely studied and chanted texts in Theravada Buddhism; its themes form the framework of the Buddhist path in all traditions.
