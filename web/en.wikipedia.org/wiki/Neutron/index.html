Neutron

The neutron is a subatomic particle with no electric charge and a mass slightly greater than that of a proton; together, neutrons and protons (nucleons) form the atomic nucleus. It was discovered by James Chadwick in 1932, resolving anomalies in atomic mass and spin.

Free neutrons are unstable, decaying into a proton, electron and antineutrino with a mean lifetime of about 15 minutes (half-life roughly 10 minutes; measured values differ slightly between methods — the 'neutron lifetime puzzle'). Neutrons are indispensable tools: they initiate fission in reactors and bombs, and neutron scattering probes the structure of materials and magnetic order. Because they are uncharged, neutrons penetrate matter deeply and interact only via the strong force and magnetism.

Discovery

Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker's beryllium radiation (1930) and Irène Joliot-Curie's experiments set up Chadwick's identification at Cambridge that the radiation was a neutral particle of about proton mass — work that won him the 1935 Nobel Prize and unlocked Fermi's neutron-bombardment programme and fission.
