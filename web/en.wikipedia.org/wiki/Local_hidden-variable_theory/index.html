Local hidden-variable theory

In the interpretation of quantum mechanics, a local hidden-variable theory is a hidden-variable theory that satisfies the principle of locality: outcomes at one location cannot be instantaneously influenced by events at a distant location. Such theories attempt to explain quantum correlations by supposing that particles carry predetermined properties ("hidden variables") not described by the quantum state.

The EPR argument

Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen argued in 1935 that quantum mechanics must be incomplete if reality is local, because measurements on entangled particles appear correlated over distance. The EPR paradox suggested hidden variables could restore a classical, locally causal picture.

Bell's theorem

In 1964 John Stewart Bell proved that any local hidden-variable theory must satisfy inequalities (Bell inequalities) that quantum mechanics predicts to be violated. Experiments beginning with those of Alain Aspect in the early 1980s, and culminating in loophole-free tests in 2015, consistently confirmed the quantum predictions, ruling out local hidden-variable theories (given the other assumptions, such as measurement independence).

Non-local alternatives

Hidden-variable theories that abandon locality, such as de Broglie–Bohm theory, remain compatible with experiment. The failure of local hidden variables is a central motivation for debates about realism, locality, and the meaning of quantum entanglement.
