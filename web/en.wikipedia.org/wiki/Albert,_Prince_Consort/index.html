Albert, Prince Consort (born Prince Franz August Karl Albert Emanuel of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha; 26 August 1819 – 14 December 1861) was the husband of Queen Victoria of the United Kingdom. Although he held no formal constitutional power, he became one of the most influential figures of the Victorian era, championing public education, science, industry and social reform.

Early life and marriage

Albert was born at Schloss Rosenau near Coburg, the younger son of Ernest I, Duke of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, and a first cousin of Victoria. The two married on 10 February 1840 at the Chapel Royal, St James's Palace. They had nine children, through whom Albert is an ancestor of many European monarchs; he is sometimes called the "grandfather of Europe" alongside Victoria as its grandmother.

Role and achievements

Albert gradually won the Queen's complete confidence and acted as her unofficial private secretary. He is best remembered for organising the Great Exhibition of 1851 in the Crystal Palace, whose profits funded the South Kensington museum quarter — the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum and the Natural History Museum — sometimes nicknamed "Albertopolis." He supported the abolition of slavery, army reform, and the expansion of university education as Chancellor of the University of Cambridge. In 1857 Victoria formally granted him the title Prince Consort.

Death and legacy

Albert died at Windsor Castle on 14 December 1861, aged 42, officially of typhoid fever (modern historians have suggested chronic conditions such as Crohn's disease or stomach cancer). Victoria wore mourning for the remaining forty years of her life. The Royal Albert Hall and the Albert Memorial in London commemorate him.
