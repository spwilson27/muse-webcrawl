The Théâtre du Grand-Guignol was a theatre in the Pigalle district of Paris (at 20 bis, rue Chaptal), which from its opening in 1897 until its closing in 1962 specialised in naturalistic horror shows. Its name is now used as a general term for graphic, amoral horror entertainment — "Grand Guignol" theatre.

Repertoire and technique

An evening's program alternated short horror plays with comedies (the "hot and cold showers" format). The horror pieces depicted graphic violence, madness, mutilation, and supernatural terror with meticulous stage effects: fake blood, trick props, and devices for simulated eye-gougings, acid attacks, and beheadings. Playwright André de Lorde, the theatre's most famous author, collaborated with psychologist Alfred Binet on plays set in asylums and prisons. A house doctor was reportedly kept on staff for audience members who fainted.

Key figures and decline

Director Max Maurey (1914–1944) cemented the theatre's fame; its star actress Paula Maxa was billed as "the most assassinated woman in the world," her characters dying on stage thousands of times. Audiences declined after the Second World War, as real atrocities dulled the appeal of stage horror and cinema took over the genre; the theatre closed in 1962, its last director remarking that they could never compete with Buchenwald.

Legacy

Grand Guignol influenced horror cinema, the London stage, and modern "splatter" and extreme theatre; revivals and homages continue internationally.
