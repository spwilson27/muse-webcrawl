Regulatory arbitrage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Regulatory arbitrage is the practice of taking advantage of differences or loopholes between regulatory systems in order to circumvent unfavourable regulation. It typically involves restructuring transactions, relocating activities to a different jurisdiction, or recharacterising the legal form of an activity so that it falls under a lighter regulatory regime, while its economic substance remains unchanged.

Forms

Arbitrage may occur between countries, as when financial institutions book business in jurisdictions with lower capital requirements or lighter supervision; between regulators within one country, as in the historical competition between United States state and federal bank charters; and between regulated and unregulated sectors, as when lending migrates from banks to the shadow banking system. In taxation, closely related strategies are known as tax arbitrage.

Consequences and responses

Regulatory arbitrage can undermine the effectiveness of regulation, concentrate risk in the least supervised parts of the financial system, and trigger a "race to the bottom" among jurisdictions competing for business — a dynamic widely discussed after the 2008 financial crisis in connection with investment banks, money market funds and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Policy responses include international harmonisation through bodies such as the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, substance-over-form doctrines, and consolidation of regulatory agencies.
