Biclustering

Biclustering, also called co-clustering or two-mode clustering, is a data mining technique that simultaneously clusters rows and columns of a matrix. Whereas ordinary clustering groups rows according to all columns, a bicluster is a submatrix — a subset of rows together with a subset of columns — within which the entries follow a coherent pattern. The technique was introduced for gene expression analysis by Yizong Cheng and George Church in 2000.

A bicluster is a coherent submatrix of rows and columns.

Motivation

In gene expression data, a set of genes may be co-expressed only under a subset of experimental conditions; global clustering over all conditions can miss such local patterns. Biclustering searches for these local structures, allowing genes and conditions to belong to several biclusters at once, or none.

Types of biclusters

Biclusters may exhibit constant values, constant row or column values, or coherently evolving values across rows and columns (additive or multiplicative coherence). Algorithms differ in whether biclusters may overlap and in the merit function — often a mean squared residue — used to judge coherence.

Algorithms

Well-known approaches include the Cheng–Church greedy search, spectral co-clustering, plaid models, the FABIA factor-analysis method, and order-preserving submatrix (OPSM) methods. Because the search problem is computationally hard in general, most practical methods are heuristic.

Applications

Beyond bioinformatics, biclustering is used in text mining (documents and words), recommender systems (users and items), and market-basket analysis.
