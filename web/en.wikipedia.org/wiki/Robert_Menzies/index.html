Sir Robert Gordon Menzies (20 December 1894 – 15 May 1978) was an Australian politician and lawyer who served as the 12th prime minister of Australia from 1939 to 1941 and again from 1949 to 1966. He held office for over 18 years in total — the longest of any Australian prime minister — and founded the Liberal Party of Australia, leading it to victory at seven federal elections.

Early life and first ministry

Menzies was born at Jeparit, Victoria, read law at the University of Melbourne, and became one of Australia's leading constitutional lawyers, arguing the landmark Engineers' Case (1920). He entered Victorian state politics, then federal parliament in 1934 as a member of the United Australia Party, becoming Attorney-General. In 1939 he succeeded Joseph Lyons as prime minister, leading Australia into the Second World War, but resigned in 1941 amid wartime criticism and loss of support.

The Liberal Party and second premiership

In opposition, Menzies rebuilt the non-Labor side of politics, founding the Liberal Party in 1944 around his appeal to "the forgotten people" — the middle class. He returned to office in 1949, defeating Ben Chifley's Labor government, and governed through the post-war boom: mass immigration, the Snowy Mountains Scheme, the ANZUS Treaty (1951), expanded universities, and the attempted (and referendally defeated) banning of the Communist Party in 1950–51. His government committed Australian troops to Korea, Malaya, and Vietnam, and maintained the White Australia policy while beginning its gradual dismantling.

Retirement and legacy

Menzies retired in January 1966, succeeded by Harold Holt, and was appointed Lord Warden of the Cinque Ports. He died in Melbourne in 1978. His era shaped modern Australia through economic prosperity, anti-communism, and a close alliance with the United States, while attracting lasting debate over civil liberties and social conservatism.
