Northwestern Hawaiian Islands

The Northwestern Hawaiian Islands, also called the Leeward Hawaiian Islands, are the small islands, atolls and reefs stretching for about 1,200 miles (1,930 km) north-west of the main Hawaiian Islands, from Nihoa to Kure Atoll. They are the older, eroded remnants of the Hawaiian–Emperor volcanic chain, and most are uninhabited. Together they form the Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument, one of the largest marine protected areas in the world.

Geography and ecology

Running north-west from Nihoa and Necker (Mokumanamana), the chain includes French Frigate Shoals, Gardner Pinnacles, Maro Reef, Laysan, Lisianski, Pearl and Hermes Atoll, Midway Atoll and Kure Atoll. Because they have been isolated for millions of years, the islands support extraordinary endemism: they are breeding grounds for most of the world's Laysan and black-footed albatrosses, the critically endangered Hawaiian monk seal, and green sea turtles, and home to endemic land birds such as the Laysan finch, Nihoa finch and Laysan duck.

History

Native Hawaiians voyaged to Nihoa and Necker, leaving stone terraces and shrines, and the islands hold deep cultural and genealogical significance — hence the name Papahānaumokuākea, from the earth-mother Papahānaumoku and sky-father Wākea. In the 19th century the islands were exploited for guano, seals and bird feathers, and Laysan's birds were devastated by introduced rabbits. Midway became a famous naval air base, site of the pivotal Battle of Midway in June 1942.

Protection

The chain was progressively protected as a bird reservation (1909), a wildlife refuge, and in 2006 as a national monument, expanded in 2016 to about 583,000 square miles (1.5 million km²), then the largest marine conservation area on Earth. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2010 for both natural and cultural values.
