Funerary art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medieval tomb effigies in a cathedral: funerary art preserves the image and status of the dead.

Funerary art is any work of art forming, or placed in, a repository for the remains of the dead. The term generally encompasses most art produced for the ritual of burial: tombs, monuments, grave goods, coffins and sarcophagi, funerary masks, memorial sculpture and architecture, and inscriptions. Much of the art that survives from ancient cultures is funerary art, because tombs were often sealed and protected, and because societies invested heavily in them.

History

The earliest known funerary goods accompany Neanderthal and early modern human burials — ochre, tools and ornaments. The monumental tradition begins with ancient Egypt: pyramids, mastabas, painted tomb chambers, canopic jars and the treasures of Tutankhamun were all designed for the afterlife. Etruscan and Roman sarcophagi carried elaborate relief sculpture; Roman funerary portraits and ash chests preserved individual likenesses.

In East Asia, the Terracotta Army guarded the tomb of China's first emperor, Qin Shi Huang, and Tang dynasty tombs were stocked with mingqi — spirit goods in ceramic. Mesoamerican cultures produced jade burial masks, as for the Maya king Pakal. In medieval Europe, tomb effigies (gisants) and chantry chapels dominated, and Renaissance artists such as Michelangelo produced tombs of monumental ambition (the Medici Chapel, the tomb of Julius II).

Functions

Funerary art serves overlapping purposes: to house and protect the body, to equip or honour the dead in an afterlife, to display the status and piety of the deceased and their family, to preserve memory for the living, and to mediate grief. Grave goods were often deliberately "killed" (broken or pierced) to release them for use in the next world.

Modern funerary art

From the 19th century, garden cemeteries such as Père Lachaise in Paris made funerary sculpture a public art form; war memorials and cenotaphs extended the tradition to collective remembrance. Contemporary practice ranges from elaborate Victorian-style monuments to minimalist memorials and digital memorialisation.
