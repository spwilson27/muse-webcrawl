The Battle of Maldon


Byrhtnoth, the English commander, commemorated at Maldon

"The Battle of Maldon" is the name conventionally given to a surviving 325-line fragment of Old English poetry describing the real Battle of Maldon of 991 AD, in which English forces under Ealdorman Byrhtnoth of Essex were defeated by Viking raiders on the shore of the River Blackwater in Essex.

Text and survival

The poem was composed by an unknown author shortly after the battle. The unique manuscript was in the Cotton Library and was destroyed by fire in 1731; the text survives because it had been transcribed, probably by John Elphinston, and published in 1726. The beginning and end of the poem are lost.

Contents

The fragment opens as the Viking host, drawn up across a tidal causeway, demands tribute. Byrhtnoth refuses, and — in a much-debated decision — allows the Vikings to cross to the mainland "for his ofermod" (often translated as pride or overconfidence), so that battle may be joined on equal terms. The English fight bravely; Byrhtnoth is killed, some men flee, but his loyal retainers resolve to die around their lord's body. The poem contains Byrhtwold's famous declaration: "Mind must be firmer, heart the more fierce, courage the greater, as our strength diminishes" (Hige sceal þē heardra, heorte þē cēnre, mōd sceal þē māre, þē ūre mægen lȳtlað).

Reception

The poem is regarded as one of the finest celebrations of the northern heroic ethos, though critics continue to debate whether it praises or criticises Byrhtnoth's decision. J. R. R. Tolkien wrote a verse drama sequel, The Homecoming of Beorhtnoth Beorhthelm's Son (1953), criticising the heroic ideal the poem embodies.
