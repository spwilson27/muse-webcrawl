Javanese script

The twenty basic characters (aksara) of the Javanese script

The Javanese script (Hanacaraka) is an abugida traditionally used to write the Javanese language, and historically also used for Sundanese, Madurese, and other languages of Java, Indonesia. It descends from the Old Kawi script, itself derived from the Pallava script of southern India.

Structure

The script has twenty basic consonant letters (aksara nglegena), each carrying an inherent /a/ vowel. The traditional ordering spells out a mnemonic legend — ha-na-ca-ra-ka ("there were two emissaries") — recounting the myth of Aji Saka and his two servants who killed each other in loyalty to their orders. Diacritics (sandhangan) modify vowels and mark final consonants; consonant clusters are written with subjoined forms (pasangan). Special honorific letters (aksara murda) and letters for foreign sounds complete the inventory.

History and use

The script developed by roughly the 17th century in the Mataram court culture and was used for literary manuscripts, chronicles (babad), and primbon divination texts, as well as for inscriptions on buildings. Under Dutch colonialism and after Indonesian independence, Latin script displaced it in daily life. It remains taught in Javanese schools, appears in signage at palaces and official buildings in Yogyakarta and Surakarta, and is encoded in Unicode (block U+A980–A9DF).
