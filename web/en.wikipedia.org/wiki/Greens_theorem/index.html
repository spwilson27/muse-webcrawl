Green's theorem

In physics and mathematics, Green's theorem gives the relationship between a line integral around a simple closed curve C and a double integral over the plane region D bounded by C. It is the two-dimensional special case of Stokes' theorem.

Statement of the theorem

Let C be a positively oriented, piecewise smooth, simple closed curve in a plane, and let D be the region bounded by C. If L and M are functions of (x, y) defined on an open region containing D and having continuous partial derivatives there, then

∮C (L dx + M dy) = ∬D (∂M/∂x − ∂L/∂y) dx dy

where the path of integration along C is anticlockwise. The theorem is named after George Green, who stated a similar result in his 1828 paper An Essay on the Application of Mathematical Analysis to the Theories of Electricity and Magnetism.

Proof when D is a simple region

If D is a simple region whose boundary consists of the curves C1, C2, C3, and C4, Green's theorem can be applied to each piece of D, and the contributions along shared interior boundaries cancel, yielding the result for the whole region. For general regions, D can be decomposed into a finite union of simple regions.

Relationship to other theorems

Green's theorem is a special case of the Kelvin–Stokes theorem, when applied to a region in the xy-plane. It is also related to the divergence theorem: in terms of a vector field F = (L, M), Green's theorem equates the circulation around C to the double integral of the curl over D. The flux form of Green's theorem relates the outward flux across C to the double integral of the divergence.

Applications

Green's theorem can be used to compute the area of a plane region: choosing L and M such that ∂M/∂x − ∂L/∂y = 1 gives the area as a line integral around the boundary. It also underlies the operation of the planimeter, a mechanical device for measuring areas. In fluid dynamics and electromagnetism, the theorem converts between circulation and local rotation of a field.
