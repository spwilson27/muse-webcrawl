Inner city

The term inner city has been used, especially in the United States, as a euphemism for lower-income residential districts in the downtown or city centre area. Sociologists sometimes use the term to apply to the impoverished area in the middle of a city, close to the central business district. In the United States the term came to be associated, often pejoratively, with predominantly African American neighbourhoods affected by poverty, disinvestment and the legacy of segregation.

Urban geography

In Ernest Burgess's concentric zone model of urban structure (1925), the inner city corresponds to the "zone in transition" surrounding the central business district, characterised by older housing, industry, and successive waves of immigrant settlement. As cities grew and transport improved, wealthier residents moved to suburbs, a process accelerated in the post-war United States by highway construction, redlining, and suburbanisation, leaving many inner-city districts with declining populations and services.

Regeneration and gentrification

From the late twentieth century, many inner cities experienced reinvestment, urban renewal and gentrification, bringing rising property values and new residents but also displacement of long-standing communities. In British usage, "inner city" refers more neutrally to the older districts close to the centre of a large city — such as Inner London — and appears in policy contexts concerning deprivation, housing and regeneration rather than carrying the same racial connotation as in the United States.

Usage

Because of its connotations, some writers and policymakers prefer terms such as "urban core", "downtown neighbourhoods" or specific place names. Nevertheless "inner city" remains common in journalism, sociology and programme names, such as inner-city schools and missions.
