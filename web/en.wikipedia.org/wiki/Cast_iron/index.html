Cast iron is a class of iron–carbon alloys with a carbon content of more than 2% (typically 2–4%), together with silicon and other elements. Its usefulness derives from its relatively low melting temperature and excellent fluidity when molten: cast iron was the first metal that could readily be melted and poured into moulds to form complex shapes, from ancient Chinese agricultural tools and vessels to bridge structures, engine blocks and cookware. Compared with steel (which has less carbon), most cast irons are hard but brittle, because their excess carbon forms graphite flakes or cementite within the microstructure.

History

Cast iron was first produced in China by about the 5th century BC, where blast furnaces generated temperatures sufficient to melt iron; Chinese founders cast tools, weapons, bells and statues for two thousand years before the technology reached Europe. In Europe, cast iron appeared in the late Middle Ages and spread with the blast furnace in the 15th–16th centuries. Abraham Darby's coke smelting at Coalbrookdale (1709) made cast iron cheap and abundant, fuelling the Industrial Revolution: the world's first cast-iron bridge was built over the Severn at Ironbridge in 1779, and cast iron framed mills, railings, pipes and machinery across the industrial world. In the 20th century, ductile (nodular) iron — invented in the 1940s by treating the melt with magnesium so graphite forms spheres — combined castability with steel-like toughness.

Types

  • Grey iron — the commonest type; graphite flakes give a grey fracture, good vibration damping and machinability. Used for engine blocks, machine beds, pipes and cookware.
  • White iron — carbon held as cementite; very hard and abrasion-resistant but brittle; used for wear surfaces and as feedstock for malleable iron.
  • Ductile (nodular/spheroidal graphite) iron — graphite nodules confer ductility and toughness; widely used for crankshafts, gears, pipes and automotive parts.
  • Malleable iron — heat-treated white iron with irregular graphite aggregates; used for fittings and hardware.

Properties and uses

Cast iron's low melting range (~1,150–1,200 °C, hundreds of degrees below pure iron), fluidity and low solidification shrinkage make it ideal for foundry work. It is strong in compression, wear-resistant and corrosion-resistant relative to mild steel, but most grades are brittle in tension and not forgeable or readily weldable. Modern uses include engine blocks and cylinder heads, brake discs, water and sewer pipes, machine tool frames, stoves, and seasoned cookware prized for heat retention.
