Ernest Starling

Ernest Henry Starling
Born: 17 April 1866, London, England
Died: 2 May 1927 (aged 61), Kingston, Jamaica
Alma mater: Guy's Hospital, London
Known for: Frank–Starling law of the heart; coining "hormone"; discovery of secretin

Ernest Henry Starling (17 April 1866 – 2 May 1927) was a British physiologist who made foundational contributions to the understanding of the heart, circulation and chemical signalling in the body. Working mainly at University College London, often with his brother-in-law William Bayliss, he discovered the hormone secretin and introduced the very word "hormone" into science.

Secretin and the hormone concept

In 1902 Bayliss and Starling showed that acid in the duodenum triggers pancreatic secretion even after all nerves to the pancreas are cut, and that an extract of the intestinal lining (which they named secretin) reproduces the effect when injected into the blood. This was the first clear demonstration of a blood-borne chemical messenger acting at a distance. In his 1905 Croonian Lecture, Starling proposed the term hormone (from the Greek for "I arouse to activity"), founding the discipline of endocrinology.

The heart and circulation

Starling's work on the isolated heart–lung preparation led to the Frank–Starling law of the heart (1914–1918): the stroke volume of the heart increases in response to an increase in the volume of blood filling it, because stretched cardiac muscle contracts more forcefully. He also formulated the Starling equation describing fluid movement across capillary walls, balancing hydrostatic pressure against the osmotic (oncotic) pressure of plasma proteins — a principle central to understanding oedema. His Principles of Human Physiology (1912) became a standard textbook.

Later life

Starling was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1899. During the First World War he worked on gas defence and food policy. He died in 1927 aboard ship in Kingston Harbour, Jamaica, and is buried in London.
