Computer-mediated communication

Computer-mediated communication (CMC) is any human communication that occurs through the use of two or more electronic devices. The term traditionally referred to communication occurring via computer networks — email, Usenet, bulletin boards, instant messaging, and chat rooms — and now encompasses texting, social media, video conferencing, and other mobile and networked forms. CMC is studied in communication studies, media studies, sociology, psychology, and human–computer interaction.

Forms

CMC can be synchronous (real-time chat, video calls) or asynchronous (email, forums, comments), one-to-one, one-to-many, or many-to-many, and may be text-based or multimodal. These properties — editability, persistence, searchability, and audience reach — distinguish CMC channels from face-to-face talk and shape their social dynamics.

Theories

Early "cues-filtered-out" approaches treated text-based CMC as impoverished because it lacks nonverbal cues, predicting impersonal or disinhibited behaviour (the online disinhibition effect). Subsequent theories complicated this view: social information processing theory (Joseph Walther) holds that users adapt to the medium over time and can form relationships as strong as offline ones; Walther's hyperpersonal model argues CMC can even exceed face-to-face intimacy because senders optimise self-presentation and receivers idealise partners. The social identity model of deindividuation effects (SIDE) emphasises how anonymity shifts salience toward group identities. Media richness and media synchronicity theories compare channels by their capacity to convey equivocal or complex messages.

Research topics

CMC research covers online communities and identity, language and discourse online (including emoji, memes, and new registers), deception and trust, computer-supported cooperative work and remote work, online education, telehealth, political communication and social movements, and the effects of CMC on relationships and well-being. As communication has migrated to smartphones and platforms, CMC has become a central, everyday mode of social life rather than a special case.
