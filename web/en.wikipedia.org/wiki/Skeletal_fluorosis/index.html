Skeletal fluorosis

Skeletal fluorosis is a bone disease caused by excessive accumulation of fluoride in the bones over a long period, leading to changes in bone structure that make bones denser but more brittle. It is a form of fluorosis, most commonly resulting from drinking groundwater with naturally high fluoride concentrations, and is endemic in parts of India, China, and East Africa, as well as in some industrial settings.

Causes

The condition develops after years of ingesting large amounts of fluoride, typically from well water drawn from fluoride-rich aquifers, from certain teas (such as brick tea), or from occupational exposure in industries such as aluminium smelting and phosphate fertiliser production. Fluoride substitutes for hydroxyl groups in bone mineral, forming fluorapatite and increasing bone density.

Symptoms and stages

Early stages may be asymptomatic or involve joint pain and stiffness. As the disease progresses, ligaments and tendons calcify, joints become restricted, and sufferers may develop spinal deformity, muscle wasting, and neurological symptoms from compression of the spinal cord. Advanced ("crippling") fluorosis can cause severe disability. Skeletal fluorosis is often preceded by dental fluorosis, mottling of the teeth that occurs when excess fluoride is ingested during childhood.

Prevention and treatment

There is no cure for established skeletal fluorosis, though progression can be halted by removing the source of excess fluoride. Prevention focuses on providing drinking water within recommended fluoride limits (the WHO guideline value is 1.5 mg/L), defluoridation of water supplies, and adequate nutrition, particularly calcium and vitamin C, which reduce fluoride absorption and severity.
