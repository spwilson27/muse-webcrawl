Nucleus basalis

The nucleus basalis, also known as the nucleus basalis of Meynert, is a group of neurons located in the substantia innominata of the basal forebrain. It is the principal source of cholinergic innervation to the cerebral cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus, and plays an important role in arousal, attention, learning, and memory.


Location of the nucleus basalis within the basal forebrain.

Anatomy

The nucleus takes its name from the German anatomist Theodor Meynert, who described it in the nineteenth century. Its large cholinergic neurons are scattered through the substantia innominata below the globus pallidus and anterior commissure. In the widely used nomenclature of Mesulam, the nucleus corresponds to the Ch4 cell group of the basal forebrain cholinergic system. Its axons project widely and diffusely across the entire cortical mantle.

Function

By releasing acetylcholine throughout the cortex, the nucleus basalis modulates cortical excitability and plasticity. Activity in the nucleus is associated with wakefulness and sustained attention, and experimental stimulation in animals can enhance learning and sharpen sensory representations. Together with the medial septal nucleus and the diagonal band of Broca, it forms the main cholinergic output of the basal forebrain.

Clinical significance

The nucleus basalis undergoes pronounced degeneration in Alzheimer's disease, with substantial loss of its cholinergic neurons. This loss contributed to the "cholinergic hypothesis" of Alzheimer's disease and underlies the use of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil, which partly compensate for reduced cortical acetylcholine. Degeneration of the nucleus is also seen in Parkinson's disease dementia and dementia with Lewy bodies, and deep brain stimulation of the nucleus has been investigated experimentally as a treatment for dementia.
