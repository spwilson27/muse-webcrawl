Income in the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: UK income distribution chart]

Income in the United Kingdom is measured principally by the Office for National Statistics (ONS) and the Department for Work and Pensions' Households Below Average Income (HBAI) series. Median gross annual earnings for full-time employees were about £35,000 in 2023 (Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings), while median household disposable income was roughly £34,500 for the financial year ending 2023, after taxes and benefits.

Distribution

UK income is unevenly spread. London and the South East have the highest median incomes; the North East, Wales and Northern Ireland the lowest. The top 1 per cent of earners receive a disproportionate share of total income, and the Gini coefficient for disposable income — roughly 0.35–0.36 in recent years — places the UK among the more unequal countries of western Europe, though less unequal than the United States.

Sources and policy

Income comes from employment and self-employment earnings, occupational and state pensions, welfare benefits (notably Universal Credit) and investments. Government policy shapes distribution through income tax, National Insurance, the National Living Wage (introduced as a higher minimum wage for older workers) and in-kind services such as the NHS and education. Relative poverty is commonly defined as below 60 per cent of median income, affecting roughly a fifth of the population after housing costs.
