Chiltern Hundreds

Full title: Crown Steward and Bailiff of the Chiltern Hundreds

Appointer: Chancellor of the Exchequer

Purpose: Procedural device for resignation from the House of Commons

The Chiltern Hundreds is an ancient administrative area in Buckinghamshire, England, consisting of the hundreds of Stoke, Desborough and Burnham. Since the 17th century it has been associated with a procedural device that allows a Member of Parliament (MP) in the House of Commons of the United Kingdom to effectively resign their seat.

Background

Members of the House of Commons are technically not permitted to resign their seats directly. However, an MP who accepts an "office of profit under the Crown" is disqualified from sitting in the Commons. By convention, two nominal offices — Crown Steward and Bailiff of the Chiltern Hundreds, and Crown Steward and Bailiff of the Manor of Northstead — are used for this purpose. Both are sinecures with no duties or salary of any substance.

Procedure

An MP wishing to resign applies to the Chancellor of the Exchequer for one of the stewardships. The appointments alternate between the Chiltern Hundreds and the Manor of Northstead so that two MPs can resign at the same time. Once appointed, the MP is disqualified and a by-election is held in their constituency. The stewardship is retained until another MP applies for it or it is otherwise vacated.

History

The Chiltern Hundreds were once notorious for lawlessness, and a Crown Steward was appointed in the 17th century to maintain order in the area — by then the office had already become a formality. The first MP to use the device to retire was John Pitt in 1751. It has since been used by hundreds of MPs, including, famously, resignations connected with political protest and scandal.
