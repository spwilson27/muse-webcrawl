In thermodynamics, free energy is the portion of a system's internal energy that is available to perform work at a given temperature. The concept bridges the first and second laws of thermodynamics: not all of a system's energy can be converted into useful work, because some must be expended as the "cost" of entropy. Two state functions are in general use.

Gibbs free energy

The Gibbs free energy G = H − TS, where H is enthalpy, T absolute temperature and S entropy, applies to systems at constant temperature and pressure — the usual conditions of chemistry and biology. The change in Gibbs energy, ΔG, determines spontaneity: a process is spontaneous if ΔG is negative, at equilibrium if ΔG is zero, and non-spontaneous if positive. ΔG also equals the maximum non-expansion work obtainable from the process, which is why it underlies electrochemistry and the energetics of metabolism.

Helmholtz free energy

The Helmholtz free energy A = U − TS, where U is internal energy, applies at constant temperature and volume. Its decrease equals the maximum total work extractable from a system held at constant temperature. In statistical mechanics the Helmholtz energy connects directly to the partition function, A = −kBT ln Z.

History and usage

The ideas were developed in the 1870s and 1880s by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Hermann von Helmholtz, building on the thermodynamics of Clausius and Kelvin. Today "free energy" calculations pervade chemistry (predicting reaction equilibria and cell potentials), materials science (phase stability) and biophysics (protein folding and ATP hydrolysis, which releases about 30.5 kJ/mol under standard conditions).

Other meanings

The phrase "free energy" is also used in pseudoscientific contexts to claim devices that produce energy from nothing, in violation of the laws of thermodynamics; such claims have never withstood controlled testing.
