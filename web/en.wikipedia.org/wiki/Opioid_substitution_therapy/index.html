Opioid substitution therapy (OST), also called opioid agonist therapy (OAT) or medication-assisted treatment, is a medical treatment for opioid use disorder in which a long-acting opioid medication is prescribed in place of the shorter-acting opioids a person has been using, such as heroin or illicit fentanyl. By stabilising opioid receptors, OST suppresses withdrawal symptoms and cravings, allowing patients to disengage from illicit drug use and its associated harms.

Agents

The two most widely used agents are methadone, a full opioid agonist usually dispensed daily under supervision at specialist clinics, and buprenorphine, a partial agonist with a ceiling effect on respiratory depression, often combined with naloxone (Suboxone) and available in long-acting depot and implant formulations. In some European countries and Canada, slow-release oral morphine and supervised injectable diamorphine (heroin-assisted treatment) are used for patients who do not respond to first-line agents.

Evidence and policy

OST is among the best-evidenced treatments in addiction medicine: remaining in treatment is associated with large reductions in all-cause and overdose mortality, reduced injecting and HIV/HCV transmission, and reduced crime. Methadone and buprenorphine are on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines. Despite the evidence, access remains restricted in many countries by regulation, stigma and limited clinic capacity, and coverage falls well short of need worldwide.
