Capital punishment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Capital punishment, also known as the death penalty, is the state-sanctioned practice of killing a person as a punishment for a crime. Historically carried out by methods from crucifixion and burning to hanging, beheading and the electric chair, modern executions use principally lethal injection, hanging, shooting and, in a few states, beheading. Roughly two-thirds of countries have abolished it in law or practice; retentionist states include China (believed to execute the most, with figures a state secret), Iran, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the United States and India, while the Council of Europe's zone is abolitionist by treaty.

History and arguments

Death was the standard penalty for serious crime across the ancient and early-modern world, often public and spectacular. Enlightenment critics (Beccaria, 1764) attacked its justice and utility; the nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw gradual abolition (Venezuela 1863 and Portugal 1867 were early), accelerating after 1945 and again after 1989, alongside wrongful-conviction revelations from DNA exonerations. Debates turn on deterrence (empirical evidence is inconclusive, according to major reviews), retribution, cost, racial and class disparities in application, the risk of executing the innocent, and whether the practice is inherently cruel — with public opinion divided and often crucial to retention.

International law

The ICCPR (1966) restricts the death penalty to the "most serious crimes" and forbids it for juveniles and pregnant women; its Second Optional Protocol (1989) commits parties to abolition. International and hybrid tribunals dropped the death penalty entirely, and extradition to retentionist states is commonly conditioned on assurances it will not be imposed or carried out.
