Bielefeld School

LocationBielefeld University, Germany
Period1970s–1980s (core phase)
Key figuresHans-Ulrich Wehler, Jürgen Kocka, Reinhart Koselleck
ApproachSocial history (Gesellschaftsgeschichte)

The Bielefeld School is a group of German historians based at Bielefeld University who, from the 1970s onward, promoted social history (Gesellschaftsgeschichte) as a new direction in German historiography. Its leading figures were Hans-Ulrich Wehler and Jürgen Kocka; the conceptual historian Reinhart Koselleck, also at Bielefeld, is often associated with the school though his work on Begriffsgeschichte followed a distinct path.

Method

The Bielefeld School advocated a "history of society" that treated history as a historical social science. Drawing on Max Weber, and to a lesser extent on Karl Marx and the Annales School, its practitioners emphasised social structures, economic processes, inequality, and class formation over the traditional focus on politics, diplomacy, and great individuals (Primat der Politik). Quantitative methods, sociological theory, and comparative approaches were central to the programme.

Sonderweg thesis

The school is closely associated with the Sonderweg thesis — the argument that Germany followed a "special path" to modernity, in which a failed bourgeois revolution and the persistence of pre-industrial elites produced conditions leading eventually to Nazism. Wehler's study of the German Empire, Das Deutsche Kaiserreich 1871–1918 (1973), exemplified the approach, as did his multi-volume Deutsche Gesellschaftsgeschichte. The thesis was sharply criticised by British historians David Blackbourn and Geoff Eley in The Peculiarities of German History (1984).

Legacy

The Bielefeld historians dominated West German historiography in the 1970s and 1980s and were central to the Historikerstreit (historians' dispute) of 1986–87. From the 1980s the school's structural approach was increasingly challenged by the history of everyday life (Alltagsgeschichte) and later by the cultural turn, though its influence on German social history remains substantial.
