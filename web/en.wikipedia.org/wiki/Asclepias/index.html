Asclepias is a genus of herbaceous, perennial flowering plants in the family Apocynaceae, known commonly as milkweeds because of the milky latex they exude when damaged. The genus contains over 200 species, distributed mainly in the Americas and southern Africa. Carl Linnaeus named the genus after Asclepius, the Greek god of medicine, because of the plants' traditional medicinal uses.

Description and pollination

Milkweeds have one of the most complex pollination mechanisms among flowering plants. Their pollen is packaged into waxy masses called pollinia, borne in pairs within elaborate star-shaped flowers with hoods and horns. An insect visitor slips a leg into a floral slit and withdraws it with pollinia attached, transferring them to the next flower. Many species contain cardiac glycosides (cardenolides) that make them toxic to most herbivores.

Ecology

Milkweeds are famous as the obligatory host plants of monarch butterfly caterpillars, which feed exclusively on the leaves and sequester the plants' cardenolides for their own defence, advertising their toxicity with bright warning colours. Other specialist insects include milkweed bugs, milkweed leaf beetles, and milkweed tussock moths. Declines in common milkweed (Asclepias syriaca) across North American farmland, linked to herbicide use, have been implicated in the decline of monarch populations, and planting milkweed is a widespread conservation recommendation.

Uses

Native American peoples used milkweeds for fibre, food (after processing to remove toxins), and medicine. The silky seed floss was used as a buoyant filling for life jackets during the Second World War and is still used in hypoallergenic bedding. Several species, such as butterfly weed (A. tuberosa) and swamp milkweed (A. incarnata), are popular garden plants for pollinators.
