Medieval university

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A medieval university was a corporation organized during the Middle Ages for the purposes of higher education. The first institutions generally considered to be universities were established in Italy, France, Spain, Portugal and England in the late 11th and the 12th centuries for the study of the arts and the higher disciplines of theology, law, and medicine. These universities evolved from much older Christian cathedral schools and monastic schools, and it is difficult to define the exact date on which they became true universities, though lists of medieval universities in Europe are conventionally dated to about 1050.

The word universitas originally applied only to the scholastic guilds — that is, the corporation of students and masters — within the school. During the High Middle Ages and beyond into the Late Middle Ages and early modern period, they spread across all of Europe. From the time of the Protestant Reformation onward, and especially in a European colonial context, universities eventually came to spread worldwide, although a medieval university is often still regarded as a distinctly European institution.

Precursors

The university is generally regarded as a formal institution that has its origin in the Medieval Christian setting. Prior to the founding of universities, European higher education took place for hundreds of years in Christian cathedral schools and monastic schools (Scholae monasticae), in which monks and nuns taught classes. Evidence of these immediate forerunners of the later university at many places dates back to the 6th century AD.

The first universities in Europe with a form of corporate/guild structure were the University of Bologna (1088), the University of Paris (c. 1150, later associated with the Sorbonne), and the University of Oxford (1167).

Establishment

The students and teachers of a medieval university were organized as a guild or corporation, the universitas magistrorum et scholarium. The university at Bologna began as a guild of students, who hired and paid the teachers; at Paris, by contrast, the masters formed the corporation. Universities sought charters from popes, emperors and kings granting privileges such as exemption from civil law, the right to strike (suspend lectures), and the ius ubique docendi — the right of a graduate to teach everywhere without further examination.

Characteristics

Medieval universities were degree-granting institutions with a fixed curriculum and a hierarchy of faculties: a lower faculty of arts and three higher faculties of theology, law, and medicine. Teaching was conducted in Latin, and the standard methods of instruction were the lecture (lectio) and the disputation (disputatio). Students typically entered at about fourteen to fifteen years of age and studied the seven liberal arts — grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy — before proceeding to a higher faculty.

Curriculum

The curriculum rested on authoritative texts: Aristotle's works for logic and natural philosophy (via Latin translations from Arabic and Greek), the Sentences of Peter Lombard for theology, Gratian's Decretum for canon law, and the Corpus Juris Civilis for civil law. Degrees progressed from bachelor to master or doctor, with the master of arts generally qualifying a scholar to teach in the arts faculty.

Legacy

Many structural features of the modern university — faculties, examinations, academic degrees, academic dress, and the ideal of academic freedom and institutional autonomy — descend directly from the medieval university. By 1500, more than eighty universities existed in Europe.
