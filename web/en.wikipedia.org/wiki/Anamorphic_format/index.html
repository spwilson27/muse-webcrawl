Anamorphic format

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anamorphic photography squeezes a widescreen image onto standard film.

The anamorphic format is a cinematography technique in which a widescreen picture is recorded on a standard film frame (or sensor) by optically squeezing the image horizontally, typically by a factor of two; an anamorphic projection lens then unsqueezes it to its full width. The classic implementation, CinemaScope, yields an aspect ratio of about 2.39:1.

History

The principle derives from Henri Chrétien's Hypergonar lens of the 1920s, adapted from tank periscopes. Twentieth Century Fox acquired the system and launched it as CinemaScope with The Robe (1953) to compete with television and rival widescreen processes such as Cinerama. Panavision became the dominant supplier of anamorphic lenses, and the format has remained a mainstay of feature filmmaking ever since.

Characteristics

Anamorphic photography has distinctive optical signatures: oval (elliptical) bokeh, shallower effective depth of field for a given field of view, horizontal lens flares, and characteristic distortion of out-of-focus highlights. It uses the full height of the 35 mm frame, giving finer grain than cropping spherical footage. Digital cinema cameras with 4:3 sensors revived interest in 2× anamorphic lenses, and 1.33× "anamorphic" adapters serve 16:9 sensors.
