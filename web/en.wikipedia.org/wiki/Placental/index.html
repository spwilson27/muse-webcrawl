Placentalia

Placental mammals (infraclass Placentalia /plæsənˈteɪliə/) are one of the three surviving divisions of the class Mammalia, the other two being Monotremata (the egg-laying monotremes) and Marsupialia (the marsupials). Placentalia is by far the largest and most diverse of the three groups, containing more than 5,000 living species — including humans — organised into some 21 orders. Placental mammals are partly distinguished by the presence of a placenta, an organ that forms during pregnancy and facilitates the exchange of nutrients and wastes between the blood supplies of mother and fetus, allowing a relatively long gestation inside the uterus.

Characteristics

Anatomically, placental mammals — like marsupials — lack the epipubic bones found in monotremes, and they possess a corpus callosum connecting the cerebral hemispheres (absent in other mammals). The female has a uterus and vagina; embryos develop within the uterus attached to the mother via a placenta derived from the same membranes (amnion, chorion and allantois) found in other amniotes. Gestation is prolonged compared with marsupials, and young are born at a comparatively advanced stage, though the degree varies enormously: rodents bear blind, hairless pups after a few weeks, while elephants gestate for nearly two years and deliver a well-developed calf.

Not all mammals with placentas are in Placentalia: marsupials also develop a short-lived yolk-sac placenta. The placental placenta is instead a long-lived chorioallantoic organ, and it is this sustained internal development that defines the group.

Evolution

Fossil evidence suggests the last common ancestor of living placentals lived in the Late Cretaceous, although molecular-clock studies often imply an earlier, mid-Cretaceous divergence among the major lineages. The oldest widely accepted placental fossil, Juramaia sinensis from China (~160 million years ago), is a stem-eutherian that pushes the origin of the wider group deep into the Jurassic. The great diversification of placental orders — the "explosive" radiation recorded by fossils just after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago — removed the dominant non-avian dinosaurs and opened ecological space that placental mammals rapidly filled, producing whales, bats, primates, rodents, carnivorans and ungulates.

Classification

Molecular phylogenetics divides Placentalia into four superorders:

  • Xenarthra — armadillos, sloths and anteaters (Americas).
  • Afrotheria — elephants, hyraxes, aardvarks, elephant shrews, tenrecs and manatees (largely African origin).
  • Euarchontoglires — rodents, lagomorphs, primates, colugos and tree shrews.
  • Laurasiatheria — bats, carnivorans, pangolins, odd- and even-toed ungulates, and eulipotyphlans (shrews, moles, hedgehogs).

Xenarthra and Afrotheria are often united as Atlantogenata, contrasted with Boreoeutheria (Euarchontoglires + Laurasiatheria), whose name reflects a northern-hemisphere origin. Rodentia is the largest order, with over 2,000 species, followed by bats (Chiroptera) with more than 1,400.

Relationship to humans

Humans are placental mammals of the order Primates, superorder Euarchontoglires. Placental mammals also supply most domesticated animals — cattle, pigs, sheep, horses, dogs and cats — and are the dominant large terrestrial animals on every continent except (historically) Australia, where marsupials predominated until human introductions.
