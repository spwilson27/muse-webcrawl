Māori people

The Māori are the indigenous Polynesian people of mainland New Zealand (Aotearoa). Their ancestors voyaged from East Polynesia and settled New Zealand in several waves of canoe (waka) journeys roughly between 1320 and 1350. Over centuries they developed a distinctive culture with its own language (te reo Māori), mythology, crafts and performing arts, and a society organised around kin groups — whānau (extended family), hapū (subtribes) and iwi (tribes).

History

Early Māori society adapted Polynesian traditions to a cooler climate, cultivating kūmara (sweet potato) and exploiting abundant game including moa. Europeans arrived from the late 18th century; in 1840 Māori chiefs and the British Crown signed the Treaty of Waitangi. Colonisation, land confiscation and introduced disease severely reduced the Māori population during the 19th century, reaching a low point of about 42,000 in 1896 before recovering.

Culture

Māori culture includes whakairo (carving), raranga (weaving), kapa haka (performing arts, including the haka), tā moko (tattooing) and a rich oral literature. The marae remains a focal point for communal and ceremonial life.

Modern era

From the 1970s a Māori cultural and political renaissance advanced language revitalisation — te reo Māori became an official language in 1987 — and Treaty-based settlements of historical grievances. Māori today form roughly 17–18% of New Zealand's population, with additional communities in Australia and elsewhere.
