Air traffic control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Air traffic control (ATC) is a service provided by ground-based air traffic controllers who direct aircraft on the ground and through controlled airspace, primarily to prevent collisions, organise and expedite traffic, and provide information and support to pilots. In most countries ATC is a state or corporatised state function operating to standards set by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), in English, the language of international aviation. The system's founding bargain — pilots follow controller instructions in controlled airspace, controllers keep aircraft separated by prescribed minima (typically 5 nautical miles horizontally en route, 3 near airports, or 1,000 feet vertically) — makes commercial flying statistically the safest form of travel.

Organisation and history

Control is divided among aerodrome (tower) controllers, approach/departure radar units, and en-route area centres handling high-altitude sectors; pilots are handed off from frequency to frequency across a flight (the world of flight made audible). Rules divide airspace into classes and flights into VFR (visual) and IFR (instrument, the airline norm). The system grew from flag-waving at 1920s aerodromes (Croydon's wooden tower is often cited as the first) through wartime radar to jet-age reform: the 1956 Grand Canyon mid-air collision, killing 128, shocked the United States into creating the Federal Aviation Administration and positive control of high airspace. Modernisation — satellite-based ADS-B surveillance, data-link clearances, free-route airspace in Europe, the long-running US NextGen and European SESAR programmes — chases capacity and efficiency against chronic controller shortages, a constraint thrown into relief whenever systems strain (and famously in 1981, when President Reagan fired 11,000 striking US controllers of PATCO). Drones and commercial spaceflight are the newest traffic to fit into the sky's oldest bargain.
