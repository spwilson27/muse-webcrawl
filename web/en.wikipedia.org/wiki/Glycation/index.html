Glycation


Glycation: the non-enzymatic bonding of a sugar to a protein or lipid.

Glycation is the covalent attachment of a sugar to a protein or lipid, occurring spontaneously without the controlling action of an enzyme. Typical sugars that participate in glycation are glucose, fructose and their derivatives. The term is used to describe the damaging, non-enzymatic process, in contrast to glycosylation, the enzyme-mediated, site-specific addition of sugars that is essential to normal protein function.

Chemistry

Glycation begins when the carbonyl group of a reducing sugar reacts with a free amino group of a protein — usually the ε-amino group of a lysine residue or an N-terminal amino acid — to form an unstable Schiff base. Over hours to days this rearranges into a more stable ketoamine, the Amadori product; in the case of glucose reacting with haemoglobin, the Amadori product is HbA1c, the glycated haemoglobin measured clinically to monitor long-term blood glucose control. Over weeks to years, Amadori products undergo further oxidation, dehydration and cross-linking reactions to form a heterogeneous group of compounds known as advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). The same chemistry, when it occurs in food during heating, is part of the Maillard reaction responsible for browning and flavour.

Biological effects

Because glycation is driven by sugar concentration and exposure time, it is accelerated in diabetes mellitus, and long-lived proteins such as collagen, lens crystallins and myelin accumulate AGEs over a lifetime. AGEs stiffen tissues by cross-linking proteins and act as ligands for the receptor RAGE, triggering inflammatory signalling; they are implicated in diabetic complications including retinopathy, nephropathy and atherosclerosis, and in normal ageing. The body has defences — notably the glyoxalase system, which detoxifies the reactive glycation precursor methylglyoxal — but these decline with age and metabolic disease. Dietary AGEs formed in high-heat cooking are also absorbed to a limited extent, and their contribution to human disease remains an active research question.
