The Commodore VIC-20 (known as the VC-20 in Germany and VIC-1001 in Japan) is an 8-bit home computer introduced by Commodore Business Machines in 1980. Marketed as "the friendly computer" and advertised by actor William Shatner, it was the first computer of any kind to sell one million units.

History

The VIC-20 was built around MOS Technology's VIC (Video Interface Chip), a low-cost colour graphics and sound chip originally designed for terminals and games consoles. Under Jack Tramiel, Commodore targeted a sub-$300 price; the machine launched in Japan in 1980 as the VIC-1001 and in North America in 1981 at US$299.95.

Hardware and software

The machine shipped with 5 KB of RAM, expandable via cartridges, and Commodore BASIC 2.0 in ROM. It used a television as a display and supported the Datasette cassette recorder, the VIC-1540/1541 disk drives, joysticks, and a modem. An extensive library of game and utility cartridges — including Gorf, Omega Race, and Scott Adams adventure games — helped drive sales.

Legacy

Production ended in January 1985 after roughly 2.5 million units, as the VIC-20 was succeeded by the Commodore 64. Linus Torvalds has cited a VIC-20 as the first computer he used. The machine remains popular with retro-computing hobbyists, and new software continues to appear for it.
