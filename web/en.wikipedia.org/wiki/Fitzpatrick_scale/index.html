Fitzpatrick scale

The Fitzpatrick scale (also Fitzpatrick skin typing test) is a numerical classification scheme for human skin colour and, more precisely, for the skin's response to ultraviolet (UV) radiation. It was developed in 1975 by American dermatologist Thomas B. Fitzpatrick of Harvard Medical School as a way to estimate how different types of skin respond to UV light, in order to set safe doses for phototherapy.

The scale

TypeTypical featuresResponse to sun exposure
IVery pale white skin, blue/green eyes, blond or red hairAlways burns, never tans
IIFair skin, blue or hazel eyes, blond or brown hairBurns easily, tans minimally
IIIDarker white skinSometimes burns mildly, tans uniformly
IVLight brown skinRarely burns, tans easily
VBrown skinVery rarely burns, tans deeply
VIDeeply pigmented dark brown to black skinNever burns

The original scale, based on interviews about sunburn and tanning history, had only types I–IV, describing white skin. Types V and VI were added later to cover brown and black skin. Because classification rests partly on self-reported burning and tanning behaviour, it does not map perfectly onto measured skin pigmentation.

Uses

The scale remains in routine use in dermatology to estimate the risk of sunburn and skin cancer, to choose laser and light-therapy settings, and to assess the risk of post-inflammatory pigmentation after cosmetic procedures. It has also been adopted outside medicine: emoji modifiers for skin tone introduced in 2015 were based on the Fitzpatrick scale, and it is used in research on bias in imaging technology, since camera exposure, pulse oximetry and facial recognition can perform unevenly across the skin-tone range the scale describes.

Criticism

Critics note that the scale was designed around UV response in lighter populations, compresses the diversity of darker skin into two categories, and is often misused as a proxy for race or ancestry. Alternative systems, such as the ten-point Monk Skin Tone scale developed for computer vision, aim to represent pigmentation more evenly.
