Grey wolf

The grey wolf (Canis lupus), also spelled gray wolf, is a large canine native to Eurasia and North America. It is the largest living member of the dog family, with males averaging 40 kg (88 lb). Wolves are distinguished from other Canis species by their broader snouts, shorter ears and longer bodies, and from domestic dogs — which descend from them — by a suite of skeletal and behavioural traits.

Behaviour and ecology

Wolves are highly social animals living in packs built around a breeding pair and their offspring. They communicate through howling, scent marking and body language, maintain large territories, and are apex predators that hunt mainly large hoofed animals such as deer, elk and moose, supplementing their diet with smaller prey and carrion. Their hunting can reshape ecosystems: the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 is a famous case study in trophic cascades.

Relationship with humans

Once the most widely distributed land mammal in the Northern Hemisphere, the wolf was eradicated from much of its range by hunting, trapping and habitat loss, driven by conflicts over livestock. Conservation and legal protection have allowed populations to recover in parts of Europe and North America, renewing debates between conservationists, hunters and farmers. The wolf looms large in mythology, folklore and literature, from the Norse Fenrir to the fables of Aesop and the story of Romulus and Remus.
