Antimalarial medication

Antimalarial medications, also known as antimalarials, are chemical compounds used to treat or prevent malaria, a parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium species and spread by Anopheles mosquitoes. Malaria affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide each year, and effective drug treatment is a cornerstone of control and elimination efforts.

Artemisinin-based combination therapies are the standard first-line treatment for falciparum malaria

Classification

Antimalarials may be grouped by their chemical structure and by the stage of the parasite life cycle they target. Blood schizonticides act on the asexual parasites in red blood cells and are used to treat clinical attacks; tissue schizonticides act on parasites in the liver; hypnozoiticides such as primaquine and tafenoquine kill the dormant hypnozoite forms of Plasmodium vivax and P. ovale responsible for relapses.

Major drug classes

Quinine and related compounds

Quinine, extracted from the bark of the cinchona tree, was the first effective treatment for malaria and remained the mainstay for centuries. Synthetic relatives include chloroquine, amodiaquine, mefloquine, piperaquine, and lumefantrine. Widespread resistance to chloroquine in P. falciparum during the twentieth century greatly reduced its usefulness in most regions.

Artemisinin derivatives

Artemisinin and its derivatives (artesunate, artemether, dihydroartemisinin) are derived from the plant Artemisia annua and were developed in China under Project 523; Tu Youyou received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2015 for this work. The World Health Organization recommends artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), such as artemether–lumefantrine and artesunate–amodiaquine, as first-line treatment for uncomplicated falciparum malaria. Intravenous artesunate is preferred for severe malaria. Partial artemisinin resistance has emerged in Southeast Asia and, more recently, parts of Africa.

Antifolates and antibiotics

Combinations such as sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine target the parasite's folate synthesis pathway. Certain antibiotics, including doxycycline and clindamycin, have antimalarial activity and are used in combination regimens or for prophylaxis.

Prophylaxis

Travelers to endemic regions may take prophylactic antimalarials such as atovaquone–proguanil, doxycycline, or mefloquine. In endemic populations, strategies include intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy and seasonal malaria chemoprevention for children.

Drug resistance

The evolution and spread of drug-resistant parasites is a central challenge. Resistance has compromised chloroquine, sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine, and mefloquine in many areas, and monitoring for resistance markers guides treatment policy.
