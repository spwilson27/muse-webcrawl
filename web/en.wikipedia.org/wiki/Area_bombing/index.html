Area bombing

Area bombing is a form of strategic bombing in which aircraft attack a general urban area rather than a specific military target, with the aim of destroying housing, industry dispersed within the city, infrastructure, and civilian morale. It is contrasted with precision bombing of discrete targets. The practice reached its greatest scale during the Second World War and was formalised for the Royal Air Force by the British Area Bombing Directive of February 1942.

Origins

Interwar theorists such as Giulio Douhet and Hugh Trenchard argued that bombing civilian populations would break an enemy's will to fight. Practical limits reinforced theory: the RAF's Butt Report of 1941 showed that only a fraction of night bombers got within miles of their targets, persuading Bomber Command under Arthur Harris to adopt area attacks on whole cities, using high-explosive and incendiary bombs aimed at working-class housing districts. The German Luftwaffe had already bombed Warsaw, Rotterdam, London, and Coventry in the Blitz.

Second World War campaigns

British night area bombing, later joined by American forces bombing by day, devastated German cities including Hamburg (Operation Gomorrah, July 1943, where a firestorm killed roughly 37,000 people), Dresden (February 1945), Cologne, Berlin, and the cities of the Ruhr. In the Pacific, the United States adopted area firebombing of Japanese cities from early 1945 under Curtis LeMay; the raid on Tokyo of 9–10 March 1945 was the deadliest air raid of the war, killing on the order of 90,000–100,000 people. Estimates of German civilian deaths from Allied bombing range from about 350,000 to 600,000.

Debate and legacy

Whether area bombing materially shortened the war, and whether it was morally or legally defensible, remains contested. Post-war surveys found that German war production rose into 1944 despite bombing, though the campaign diverted enormous resources to air defence and eventually wrecked Germany's transport and oil systems when re-targeted. The practice contributed to post-war prohibitions in international humanitarian law on indiscriminate attacks and attacks directed at civilian populations, codified in Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (1977). The memorialisation of raids such as Dresden remains politically sensitive in Britain and Germany.
