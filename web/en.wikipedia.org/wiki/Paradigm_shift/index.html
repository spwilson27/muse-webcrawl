Paradigm shift

A paradigm shift is a fundamental change in the basic concepts and experimental practices of a scientific discipline. The concept was introduced by the American physicist and philosopher Thomas S. Kuhn in his influential 1962 book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. According to Kuhn, science does not progress by steady accumulation alone; instead, periods of "normal science" within an accepted paradigm are punctuated by revolutions in which one paradigm is replaced by another that redefines the field's problems, standards, and worldview.

Mechanism

In Kuhn's model, anomalies — results that the reigning paradigm cannot explain — accumulate until confidence collapses into crisis. Competing frameworks emerge; the transition to a new paradigm is not purely a matter of proof, since rival paradigms may be incommensurable, judging success by different criteria. The shift is complete when the new paradigm wins over the community and becomes the basis for the next period of normal science.

Examples

Classic instances include the Copernican Revolution (geocentric to heliocentric astronomy), the replacement of Newtonian mechanics by relativity and quantum mechanics, the acceptance of plate tectonics in geology, the Darwinian revolution in biology, and the chemical revolution associated with Lavoisier's oxygen theory of combustion replacing phlogiston.

Use outside science

The phrase has spread far beyond philosophy of science into business, technology, and popular culture, where it is often used loosely for any dramatic change in assumptions or practice — a usage Kuhn's stricter concept does not always support, and one sometimes criticized as a buzzword.
