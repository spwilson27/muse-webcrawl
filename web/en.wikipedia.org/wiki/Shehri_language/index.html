Shehri, also known as Jibbali (from Arabic jabal, "mountain"), is a Modern South Arabian language spoken in the Dhofar Governorate of southern Oman, principally in the mountains behind the coastal city of Salalah and on the offshore Khuriya Muriya Islands. It is the most widely spoken of the Modern South Arabian languages, a small group of Semitic languages unrelated to Arabic that preserve features of the ancient tongues of the frankincense-producing kingdoms of southern Arabia.

Hero image: the Dhofar mountains during the khareef (monsoon), the traditional homeland of Shehri.

Classification and related languages

Shehri belongs to the Modern South Arabian branch of Semitic, together with Mehri, Soqotri, Harsusi, Bathari and Hobyot. Although often loosely described as "dialects" by Arabic speakers, these languages are not descended from Arabic and are not mutually intelligible with it. Shehri is the easternmost and most internally diverse member of the mainland group, with coastal and mountain varieties that differ noticeably.

Speakers and status

Shehri is the first language of tribal groups such as the Shahra and Qara of the Dhofar mountains and of some island communities. Speaker numbers are uncertain; estimates commonly range from about 25,000 to over 50,000. The language is unwritten in everyday life — Arabic is the language of schooling, government and media in Oman — and transmission to children has weakened in towns, though it remains vigorous in many mountain communities. Linguists classify Shehri as vulnerable/endangered, and it has attracted increasing documentation efforts, including a grammatical description by Janet Watson based on fieldwork with speakers of the eastern varieties.

Features

Like other Modern South Arabian languages, Shehri preserves consonants lost in Arabic, including lateral fricatives, and retains grammatical features such as a dual number and distinctive verbal patterns. It has a rich oral tradition of poetry, narrative and proverb, historically tied to the pastoral way of life of the Dhofari highlands — cattle herding, frankincense collection and the seasonal monsoon (khareef) that turns the mountains green.
