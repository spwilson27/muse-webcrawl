Siege of Maastricht (1673)

The siege of Maastricht of 1673, during the Franco-Dutch War, was the first siege directed in person by Louis XIV of France and the first in which the military engineer Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban applied his celebrated system of siege parallels. The Dutch-held fortress city of Maastricht fell in just thirteen days of open trenches (19–30 June 1673), a striking demonstration of methodical siege warfare, and the action is famous in popular culture as the place where the musketeer d'Artagnan — Charles de Batz de Castelmore — was killed.

Background

Having bypassed Maastricht during the invasion of the Netherlands in 1672, Louis determined to take the fortress — a Dutch enclave on the Meuse commanding routes into the Spanish Netherlands — in 1673. He brought an army of some 40,000 men and attended the siege personally with his court, an event stage-managed as a spectacle of royal power.

The siege

Vauban, given effective direction of the attack, opened trenches on the night of 17–18 June and pushed forward three parallel lines connected by zigzag approach trenches, allowing artillery and infantry to be concentrated safely against a chosen point of the enceinte while ricochet fire suppressed the defenders. Mining and massed batteries breached the outworks; a particularly bloody struggle was fought for a demi-lune covering the Tongeren gate, during which d'Artagnan, captain of the King's Musketeers, was shot dead on 25 June. The governor, Jacques de Fariaux, capitulated on 30 June, and the garrison evacuated under terms on 1 July.

Significance

Maastricht became the textbook debut of Vauban's "attack by parallels," which dominated siege warfare for two centuries. France held the city until the Treaty of Nijmegen (1678) returned it to the Dutch.
