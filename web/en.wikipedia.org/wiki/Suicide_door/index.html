Suicide door

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rear-hinged "suicide door" on a classic automobile.

A suicide door is the slang term for an automobile door that is hinged at its rear rather than the front. Such doors were originally used on horse-drawn carriages, but are rarely found on modern vehicles, primarily because they are perceived to be less safe than conventional front-hinged doors. They were common on cars manufactured in the first half of the 20th century.

History

Rear-hinged doors were widespread on early automobiles because they allowed easier entry and exit, particularly for rear passengers in formal cars. Well-known examples include the 1961–1969 Lincoln Continental, whose rear suicide doors became a signature feature, and the Ford Thunderbird four-door models of the late 1960s.

Safety concerns

The name derives from the perceived danger: if a rear-hinged door was accidentally opened, or its latch failed while the car was moving, the slipstream could tear the door fully open, and a passenger holding the handle could be dragged out. On early cars without seat belts and with weak latches this risk was real. Early designs also risked trapping occupants between door and body when entering or leaving.

Modern use

Modern safety regulations and improved designs — including doors that cannot open unless the front door is opened, electronic latches and sensors — have allowed limited revivals. Rear-hinged "coach doors" appear on Rolls-Royce models such as the Phantom and Ghost, while "freestyle" half-doors appeared on cars such as the Mazda RX-8, Toyota FJ Cruiser, and extended-cab pickup trucks. Manufacturers avoid the "suicide" term, using names like "coach doors" or "opposing doors" instead.
