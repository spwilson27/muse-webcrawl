The Defense of Marriage Act (DOMA) was a United States federal law passed by the 104th United States Congress and signed into law by President Bill Clinton on September 21, 1996. It defined marriage for federal purposes as the union of one man and one woman, and allowed states to refuse to recognize same-sex marriages granted under the laws of other states.

Provisions

Section 2 of DOMA provided that no state (or other political subdivision within the United States) was required to recognize a same-sex marriage from another state. Section 3 defined "marriage" and "spouse" for purposes of federal law as applying only to opposite-sex couples, thereby denying same-sex spouses more than a thousand federal rights and benefits tied to marital status.

Legal challenges and repeal

In United States v. Windsor (2013), the Supreme Court struck down Section 3 as unconstitutional. In Obergefell v. Hodges (2015), the Court held that same-sex couples have a constitutional right to marry nationwide, rendering Section 2 unenforceable. DOMA was formally repealed by the Respect for Marriage Act, signed into law on December 13, 2022.

Background

DOMA was enacted in anticipation that Hawaii's courts might legalize same-sex marriage following the 1993 Baehr v. Lewin decision. It passed both houses of Congress by large margins. Public opinion on same-sex marriage in the United States shifted substantially in the following decades, and Clinton and other original supporters later urged the law's repeal.
