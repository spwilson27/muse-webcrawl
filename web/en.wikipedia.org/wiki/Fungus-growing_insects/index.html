Fungus-growing insects

Fungus-growing insects are groups of insects that cultivate fungi as a food source in a mutualistic symbiosis broadly comparable to human agriculture. Fungiculture evolved independently in several insect lineages — most famously in the attine ants (including the leafcutter ants), in fungus-growing termites of the subfamily Macrotermitinae, and in ambrosia beetles — making insects the only animals besides humans known to practise true agriculture.

Attine ants

The roughly 250 species of attine ants of the New World tropics cultivate fungi of the family Lepiotaceae. Higher attines such as Atta and Acromyrmex cut fresh leaf fragments and carry them underground to feed their gardens; the fungus, in turn, is the colony's principal food. The symbiosis is ancient — molecular estimates place its origin around 50–60 million years ago — and involves additional partners, including antibiotic-producing bacteria (Pseudonocardia) carried on the ants' cuticle that suppress the parasitic fungus Escovopsis.

Fungus-growing termites

Termites of the subfamily Macrotermitinae, found across Africa and Asia, grow fungi of the genus Termitomyces on combs of chewed plant material inside their mounds. The fungus breaks down lignocellulose and enriches the food with nitrogen, allowing the termites to thrive on poor-quality plant litter. This symbiosis arose roughly 30 million years ago in African rainforests.

Ambrosia beetles

Ambrosia beetles of the weevil subfamilies Scolytinae and Platypodinae bore into dead or stressed trees and inoculate the tunnel walls with ambrosia fungi, which they carry in specialised organs called mycangia. Both larvae and adults feed on the fungi rather than the wood itself.

Significance

Fungus-growing insects are model systems for the evolution of mutualism, symbiont domestication, and division of labour. Their gardens face pathogens analogous to crop diseases, and the ants' use of antibiotics predates human medicine by tens of millions of years.
