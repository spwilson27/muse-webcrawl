Michelangelo Antonioni

Michelangelo Antonioni (29 September 1912 – 30 July 2007) was an Italian film director, screenwriter and editor. He is best known for his "trilogy on modernity and its discontents" — L'Avventura (1960), La Notte (1961) and L'Eclisse (1962) — and for the English-language films Blow-up (1966) and The Passenger (1975). Together with Federico Fellini and Ingmar Bergman, he reshaped postwar art cinema through an abstract style of long takes, elliptical narrative and visual composition, focusing on alienation in the modern world.

Career

Antonioni was born in Ferrara, studied economics at the University of Bologna, and worked as a film critic before assisting Marcel Carné and studying at the Centro Sperimentale in Rome. After documentaries and the neo-realist-influenced Story of a Love Affair (1950) and Le Amiche (1955), he achieved international fame with L'Avventura, which was booed at its Cannes premiere yet won the Jury Prize and became a landmark for its plotless narrative of a woman who vanishes during a yachting trip — a disappearance the film never explains. La Notte won the Golden Bear at Berlin, and Red Desert (1964), his first colour film, won the Golden Lion at Venice.

English-language films and later work

Under contract with Carlo Ponti and MGM, Antonioni made Blow-up (1966) in swinging London — a photographer believes he has recorded a murder — which won the Palme d'Or and became a major commercial success; the American counterculture film Zabriskie Point (1970) failed at the box office but gained cult status, and The Passenger (1975), with Jack Nicholson, was acclaimed. After a stroke in 1985 left him partially paralysed and unable to speak fluently, he continued directing with the assistance of Wim Wenders on Beyond the Clouds (1995). He received an Honorary Academy Award in 1995. He died in Rome on 30 July 2007 — the same day as Ingmar Bergman.
