Chad, officially the Republic of Chad, is a landlocked country at the crossroads of North and Central Africa. It is bordered by Libya to the north, Sudan to the east, the Central African Republic to the south, Cameroon and Nigeria to the southwest, and Niger to the west. Chad is the fifth-largest country in Africa, spanning Saharan desert in the north, the semi-arid Sahel in the centre, and savanna in the south. Lake Chad, in the west, after which the country is named, has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s — from about 25,000 km² to a fraction of that in recent decades, varying seasonally — squeezing the farmers, herders and fishers of four countries who depend on it.

History

The basin is anciently inhabited — the hominid fossil Sahelanthropus tchadensis ("Toumaï", about 7 million years old) was found in Chad's Djurab Desert. Medieval kingdoms such as Kanem-Bornu traded across the Sahara. France colonised Chad by 1920; independence in 1960 under François Tombalbaye began decades of civil wars, rebellions, the brutal Hissène Habré regime (1982–90; he was later convicted of crimes against humanity in a landmark African-led trial) and the long rule of Idriss Déby (1990–2021), who was killed fighting rebels in 2021 and succeeded by his son Mahamat Déby. Oil production since 2003 (piped to Cameroon) transformed state finances without broad development: Chad remains one of the world's poorest countries, hosting large refugee flows from Sudan and the Central African Republic.
