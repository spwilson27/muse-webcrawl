Modes of discourse

FieldRhetoric and composition
The four modesNarration, description, exposition, argumentation
Origin19th-century rhetoric, esp. Alexander Bain

The modes of discourse, also called rhetorical modes, are a traditional classification of the main kinds of written and spoken language, based on the writer's purpose and the way a text is organised. In the form taught in schools and composition courses since the 19th century, there are four modes: narration, description, exposition and argumentation (or persuasion). Most real texts mix modes, using, for example, narrative episodes inside an argument, but the classification remains a common organising framework in first-year composition and secondary-school writing instruction.

The four modes

Narration

Narrative writing tells a story or recounts a sequence of events, usually in chronological order and from a consistent point of view. Its structural elements include plot, setting, character and conflict. Narrative is used in fiction, memoir, journalism and anecdotal illustration within other modes.

Description

Descriptive writing portrays a person, place, object or scene through sensory detail — sight, sound, smell, taste and touch — organised by spatial order, order of impression or another pattern. Its aim is to create a dominant impression in the reader's mind.

Exposition

Expository writing explains, informs or clarifies. Its sub-patterns include definition, classification and division, comparison and contrast, cause and effect, process analysis and exemplification. Textbooks, manuals, encyclopedias and most technical writing are primarily expository.

Argumentation

Argumentative (persuasive) writing seeks to convince an audience of a claim, supporting it with reasons and evidence and anticipating counterarguments. It includes the classical appeals of ethos, pathos and logos described in Aristotle's Rhetoric, and structures such as the Rogerian argument and the Toulmin model.

History

The four-mode scheme is generally traced to the Scottish philosopher Alexander Bain, whose English Composition and Rhetoric (1866) classified discourse into description, narration, exposition and persuasion, and to the "modes" pedagogy that spread through American textbooks such as those of John Genung and Barrett Wendell in the late 19th century. Twentieth-century rhetoricians criticised the scheme for treating modes as properties of whole essays rather than of parts, and for ignoring audience and context; James Kinneavy's aims of discourse (expressive, referential, literary, persuasive) and other taxonomies offered alternatives. Nevertheless, the modes remain widely used in school curricula and standardised writing tests.
