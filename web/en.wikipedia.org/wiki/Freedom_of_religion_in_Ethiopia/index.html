Freedom of religion in Ethiopia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freedom of religion in Ethiopia is guaranteed by the country's 1995 constitution, which establishes a secular state, prohibits a state religion, and protects the freedom to hold or adopt any religion or belief and to manifest it in worship, observance, practice, and teaching. Ethiopia is religiously diverse: according to census and survey estimates, the largest faiths are Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity (about 43 percent of the population), Islam (about 34 percent), Protestant Christianity (about 19 percent), with smaller Catholic, traditional, and other communities.

Legal framework

Article 11 of the Constitution of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia separates state and religion and bars government interference in religious matters and religious interference in state affairs, while Article 27 guarantees freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, subject to limitations prescribed by law for public safety, order, health, and the rights of others. Religious organisations register with the government to obtain legal personality, and the state recognises religious holidays of the major faiths. Ethiopia has a long history of religious coexistence, symbolised by the first Hijra of early Muslims to the Christian kingdom of Axum in 615 CE.

Practice and concerns

In practice, observers such as the U.S. State Department's International Religious Freedom reports have generally found the government respectful of religious freedom, while noting occasional tensions: disputes over church and mosque land, episodes of communal violence in which places of worship were attacked — including attacks on mosques and churches during periods of political unrest — intra-church disputes within the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and restrictions applied under states of emergency during the Tigray war and other conflicts. Muslim communities have at times protested what they saw as state interference in the election of Islamic affairs councils, and some Protestant and minority groups have reported difficulties securing permits to build places of worship in areas dominated by another faith. Interfaith bodies such as the Inter-Religious Council of Ethiopia work to mediate such disputes.
