Stock footage

Stock footage, also called archive footage or library footage, is film or video footage that can be used again in other productions. It is typically maintained by libraries and licensing agencies and sold or licensed to filmmakers who need shots — establishing views of cities, historical events, wildlife, weather, crowd scenes — that would be impractical or impossible to shoot themselves.

History

Studios have reused footage since the silent era: battle scenes, stampedes, and city exteriors shot for one film routinely reappeared in others. Newsreel companies and television networks built vast archives during the twentieth century, and independent stock houses grew up alongside them. The advent of online digital libraries in the 1990s and 2000s — firms such as Getty Images, Shutterstock, and Pond5, alongside public archives like the Prelinger Archives and the Internet Archive — made stock footage searchable and downloadable worldwide, shifting the market from physical film reels to keyword-tagged digital clips.

Licensing models

Stock footage is generally supplied under two regimes. Rights-managed licences price a clip according to its intended use, medium, territory, and duration. Royalty-free licences (a term of art meaning a flat fee, not no fee) allow repeated use within the licence terms after a single payment. Editorial footage of news, sports, and historical events usually carries additional restrictions and may not be used in advertising or fictional contexts. Users must also secure releases for identifiable people and property appearing in commercial-use footage.

Uses

Stock footage is ubiquitous in documentaries, news packages, commercials, corporate video, and increasingly in low-budget features and online content. Historical productions rely on archival shots for period authenticity; nature programmes supplement their own filming with library material; and advertisers use generic lifestyle clips. Increasingly, drone aerials and time-lapses form a large share of library sales. Repeatedly reused clips can become famous in themselves, such as the Wilhelm scream in sound libraries or widely used shots of exploding buildings and charging elephants.

Criticism

Overuse of generic stock footage is sometimes criticised for making visual media feel interchangeable, and for the risk that an identical clip — or model — appears in competing advertisements. News organisations must be careful that archive footage is labelled as such so viewers do not mistake it for current events.
