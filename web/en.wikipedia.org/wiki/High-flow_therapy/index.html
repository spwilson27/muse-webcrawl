High-flow therapy

High-flow therapy, also called heated humidified high-flow nasal cannula therapy (HFNC or HHHFNC), is a method of respiratory support in which a heated, humidified mixture of air and oxygen is delivered through large-bore nasal prongs at flow rates exceeding the patient's inspiratory demand, commonly 20–60 litres per minute in adults and weight-scaled rates in children.

Mechanisms

High-flow therapy washes out carbon dioxide from the anatomical dead space of the upper airway, provides a small amount of positive end-expiratory pressure, delivers a stable and accurately set fraction of inspired oxygen, and reduces the work of breathing. Because the gas is heated and fully humidified, it is generally well tolerated and preserves mucociliary function better than cold, dry oxygen.

Uses

The therapy is used for acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, after extubation, in bronchiolitis in infants, during pre-oxygenation for intubation, and as an alternative when conventional oxygen therapy is insufficient but non-invasive ventilation is not tolerated. Large trials such as FLORALI reported improved outcomes, including lower 90-day mortality in some analyses, for patients with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure compared with standard oxygen or non-invasive ventilation.

Limitations

High-flow therapy does not provide true ventilatory support for severe hypercapnia, and clinicians must take care that its comfort does not mask deterioration and delay needed intubation.
