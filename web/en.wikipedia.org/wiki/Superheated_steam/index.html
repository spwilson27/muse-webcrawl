Superheated steam

Superheated steam is steam at a temperature higher than its vaporisation point at the absolute pressure at which the temperature is measured. Superheated steam behaves much more like an ideal gas than saturated steam, and can have its temperature reduced somewhat before it condenses.

The superheated region lies beyond the saturated vapour curve on a T–s diagram

Production

Superheated steam is produced by passing saturated steam, generated in a boiler, through a separate heat exchanger called a superheater, where additional heat is applied at constant pressure. The amount by which the steam's temperature exceeds the saturation temperature is called the degree of superheat.

Uses

Steam engines and turbines

In steam turbines and reciprocating steam engines, superheated steam is used because it remains dry while expanding and doing work, reducing condensation inside the machine, blade erosion and corrosion, and improving thermal efficiency. Modern power station boilers commonly deliver steam superheated to around 540–600 °C. At pressures above the critical point of water (22.1 MPa, 374 °C), the distinction between liquid and vapour disappears entirely and the fluid is supercritical.

Other uses

Superheated steam is also used in drying processes, steam cleaning, soil sterilisation, and in the food industry, where its dry, high-temperature character can cook and dry products simultaneously.
