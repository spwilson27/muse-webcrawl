Solar heat gain coefficient

The solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) is the fraction of solar radiation admitted through a window, door or skylight — either directly transmitted or absorbed and subsequently released inward. Solar heat gain is important during summer cooling seasons in climates where solar radiation through windows causes overheating, and advantageous during winter heating seasons in cold climates.

Components of solar heat gain through a glazing system

Definition

SHGC is expressed as a number between 0 and 1. The lower a window's SHGC, the less solar heat it transmits. It comprises two parts: the directly transmitted solar energy, and the fraction of absorbed radiation that flows inward (the inward-flowing fraction of the shading coefficient's predecessor concept). SHGC replaced the older "shading coefficient" in North American standards; in Europe the equivalent metric is the g-value (total solar energy transmittance).

Typical values

  • Clear single glazing: ≈ 0.86
  • Clear double glazing: ≈ 0.76
  • Low-emissivity double glazing: ≈ 0.35–0.65 depending on coating placement
  • Spectrally selective glazing: ≈ 0.25–0.40 with high visible transmittance

Design use

Energy codes (e.g. ASHRAE 90.1, IECC) set maximum SHGC by climate zone. In hot climates low SHGC reduces air-conditioning loads; in cold climates higher SHGC on sun-facing façades can offset heating demand through passive solar gain, tied to the incident energy described by the solar constant after atmospheric losses.
