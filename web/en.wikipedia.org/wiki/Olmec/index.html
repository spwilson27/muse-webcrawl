Olmec

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A colossal Olmec stone head at La Venta, carved from a single basalt boulder]

The Olmec were an early Mesoamerican civilisation that flourished in the tropical lowlands of south-central Mexico (modern Veracruz and Tabasco) from roughly 1500 to 400 BC — the first major civilisation of Mesoamerica and often called its 'mother culture'. From centres such as San Lorenzo and La Venta they produced colossal stone heads, the earliest Mesoamerican ballcourts and pyramids, fine jade carving, and probably early forms of writing and the Long Count calendar tradition later perfected by the Maya. Their influence radiated across Mesoamerica through trade in jade, obsidian and cacao.

Centres and art

San Lorenzo (fl. c. 1200–900 BC) and La Venta (c. 900–400 BC) were planned ceremonial centres with earthen pyramids, plazas and drainage systems. Seventeen colossal basalt heads are known — each apparently a portrait of a ruler, weighing up to tens of tonnes, quarried in the Tuxtla Mountains and transported scores of kilometres. Olmec art's distinctive style — downturned 'baby-face' mouths, were-jaguar motifs — appears across highland and Pacific Mexico, evidence of prestige networks.

Decline and legacy

La Venta was abandoned by about 400 BC for reasons unknown (environmental change, invasion and internal revolt have all been proposed). The Olmec left no deciphered texts — the Cascajal block (c. 900 BC) may be the Americas' earliest writing — but later peoples inherited their calendar concepts, ballgame, cosmology and royal iconography. Rediscovered only in the 19th and 20th centuries, the Olmec reframed understanding of Mesoamerican origins.
