Gross domestic product

[Image: aerial view of a large container port, representing the production and trade measured by GDP]

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period, usually a quarter or a year. It is the most widely used single measure of economic activity and the size of an economy, and it can be calculated three equivalent ways: by production (value added by industry), by income (wages, profits, rents and taxes), or by expenditure.

Measurement

The expenditure identity writes GDP as Y = C + I + G + (X − M): consumption, investment, government spending and net exports. Nominal GDP is measured at current prices; real GDP adjusts for Inflation using a price index, so that its growth measures volume change. GDP per capita divides by population as a rough proxy for average living standards, and figures are often converted at purchasing power parity (PPP) to compare across countries. GDP was developed in the 1930s–40s, notably by Simon Kuznets for the United States and in wartime Britain, and standardised in the UN System of National Accounts after 1945.

Uses and limitations

By nominal GDP the largest economies are the United States and China, followed by Germany, Japan and India (approximately, in recent rankings); per-capita leaders include small financial centres and resource states. Kuznets himself warned that welfare cannot be inferred from GDP: it omits unpaid household and voluntary work, leisure, environmental damage, the distribution of income and much of the informal economy, and says nothing about distribution — hence complementary measures such as the Human Development Index and "genuine progress" indicators, and its role alongside Unemployment and growth in assessing recessions.
