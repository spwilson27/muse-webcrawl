Intracellular parasite

An intracellular parasite is a microparasite that can grow and reproduce inside the cells of a host. Intracellular parasites are divided into obligate types, which depend entirely on host cellular machinery and cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and facultative types, which can also live and multiply outside cells.

Examples

All viruses are obligate intracellular parasites. Obligate bacterial parasites include Chlamydia and Rickettsia species. Among protozoa, the apicomplexans — Plasmodium (malaria), Toxoplasma, and Cryptosporidium — invade host cells, as do kinetoplastids such as Leishmania and Trypanosoma cruzi. The fungus Histoplasma capsulatum survives inside macrophages. Facultative examples include Listeria monocytogenes, Salmonella, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which can persist within phagocytes.

Survival strategies

Living inside cells shelters parasites from antibodies and complement, so control depends mainly on cell-mediated immunity. Intracellular parasites manipulate host processes by preventing phagosome–lysosome fusion, escaping into the cytosol, or remodelling the vacuole in which they reside, and they obtain nutrients from the host cell.

Treatment

Because the pathogen is sequestered, drugs must penetrate host cell membranes, and some intracellular stages are dormant and hard to eradicate, contributing to relapse and latency.
