Henry Dunant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Henry Dunant
Born: 8 May 1828, Geneva
Died: 30 October 1910
Known for: Founder of the Red Cross; first Nobel Peace Prize (1901, shared)

Henry Dunant (1828–1910) was a Swiss humanitarian, social activist and businessman who founded the Red Cross and inspired the Geneva Conventions. In 1901 he became, jointly with Frédéric Passy, the first recipient of the Nobel Peace Prize.

In 1859, travelling in Italy on business, Dunant witnessed the aftermath of the Battle of Solferino, in which tens of thousands of wounded soldiers lay largely untended. He organised local civilians to care for the injured of both sides — "tutti fratelli" (all brothers) — and in 1862 published A Memory of Solferino, proposing permanent volunteer relief societies and an international agreement to protect the wounded and those aiding them.

His proposals led to the founding committee of 1863 — the future International Committee of the Red Cross — and the first Geneva Convention of 1864. Dunant's later life was marked by bankruptcy and obscurity; he spent decades in poverty in the village of Heiden before journalists rediscovered him in 1895, and the Nobel Prize restored his finances and reputation in his final years.
