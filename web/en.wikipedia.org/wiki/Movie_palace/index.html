A movie palace is a large, elaborately decorated cinema, built mainly between the 1910s and the 1930s. Movie palaces were designed to make filmgoing a luxurious experience: patrons passed through grand lobbies and staircases into vast auditoriums decorated in exotic or historical styles, with live orchestral accompaniment, stage shows, uniformed ushers, and amenities rivaling opera houses.

History

As films grew in popularity in the 1910s, exhibitors such as Samuel "Roxy" Rothafel raised presentation standards, culminating in huge New York venues like the Roxy Theatre (1927, over 5,900 seats). Chains including Loew's, Paramount-Publix, Fox, Warner Bros., and RKO built palaces across North America, designed by architects such as Thomas W. Lamb, John Eberson — inventor of the "atmospheric" theatre with starry-sky ceilings — and Rapp and Rapp. Famous survivors include Radio City Music Hall and Grauman's Chinese Theatre in Hollywood.

The Great Depression, then post-war television and suburbanisation, ended the era. Many palaces were demolished or subdivided into multiplexes, though from the 1960s onward preservation movements saved and restored numerous examples, often as performing arts centres.
