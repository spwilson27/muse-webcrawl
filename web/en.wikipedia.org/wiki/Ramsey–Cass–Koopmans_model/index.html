The Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model (also known as the Ramsey growth model or the neoclassical growth model) is a foundational model of economic growth in which saving and consumption choices are determined endogenously by forward-looking households that maximize utility over an infinite horizon. It builds on the Ramsey problem posed by Frank Ramsey in 1928 and was developed independently by David Cass and Tjalling Koopmans in 1965.

Setup

A representative household supplies labour, rents capital to firms, and chooses consumption to maximize discounted lifetime utility, typically of the constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) form. Firms produce output with a neoclassical production function of capital and labour exhibiting constant returns to scale. Unlike the earlier Solow–Swan model, the saving rate is not fixed: it emerges from the household's intertemporal optimization, summarized by the Keynes–Ramsey rule linking consumption growth to the real interest rate, the rate of time preference, and the elasticity of intertemporal substitution.

Dynamics and steady state

The model's equilibrium is characterized by a system of differential equations in consumption and capital per effective worker. In phase-diagram terms the steady state is a saddle point: for each initial capital stock there is a unique initial consumption level on the stable arm (the saddle path) along which the economy converges. In the long run, per-capita growth is driven solely by exogenous technological progress, and the equilibrium is Pareto optimal. The model underlies modern dynamic general equilibrium analysis, business-cycle theory, and optimal fiscal policy design.
