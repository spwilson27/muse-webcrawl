Nostalgia

EtymologyGreek nostos (homecoming) + algos (pain)
Coined1688, by Johannes Hofer
OriginallyA medical diagnosis (homesickness as disease)
Modern senseSentimental longing for the past

Nostalgia is a sentimental longing or wistful affection for the past, typically for a period or place with happy personal associations. The word was coined in 1688 by the Swiss medical student Johannes Hofer from the Greek nostos ("homecoming") and algos ("pain"), to describe a medical condition observed in Swiss mercenaries serving abroad — a pathological homesickness thought capable of causing severe illness or death. Today nostalgia is generally understood not as a disease but as a common, predominantly positive emotion with psychological functions.

History of the concept

Through the 18th and 19th centuries nostalgia remained a medical diagnosis, discussed in armies and navies; soldiers were sometimes treated (or discharged) for it, and it was listed among causes of death. Over time the condition was reframed as melancholy and then demedicalised. By the 20th century "nostalgia" had shifted from longing for a place (home) to longing for a time (one's past), and became a central concept in cultural criticism — for example in discussions of heritage, retro fashion, film and music revivals.

Psychology

Research since the early 2000s (notably by Constantine Sedikides, Tim Wildschut and colleagues) finds that nostalgia is usually triggered by loneliness, negative mood or existential threat, and that it tends to increase feelings of social connectedness, self-continuity and meaning, acting as a psychological resource. Nostalgic memories typically feature the self in a social setting, with a redemptive narrative arc. Excessive or ruminative nostalgia can nonetheless accompany depression and difficulty adapting to the present.

In culture and politics

Nostalgia is a powerful commercial force — in rebooted films, retro design, vinyl revivals and "heritage" branding — and a political one, where idealised versions of a national past can motivate movements. Theorists such as Svetlana Boym distinguished "restorative" nostalgia, which seeks to rebuild a lost past, from "reflective" nostalgia, which savours the longing itself.
