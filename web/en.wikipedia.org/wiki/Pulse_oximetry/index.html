Pulse oximetry

Pulse oximetry is a noninvasive method for monitoring a person's blood oxygen saturation (SpO2). Peripheral oxygen saturation readings are typically only slightly less accurate than arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2) from arterial blood gas analysis. A pulse oximeter probe — usually clipped to a fingertip or earlobe — also measures the pulse rate. Because of their simplicity, low cost and continuous readings, pulse oximeters became standard in operating theatres, recovery, intensive care and emergency medicine, and are common in home monitoring.

Principle

Oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin absorb red and infrared light differently. The oximeter shines two wavelengths — red (about 660 nm) and infrared (about 940 nm) — through a translucent body part to a photodetector. By analysing the pulsatile (arterial) component of absorption at each wavelength and comparing their ratio with calibration curves obtained from volunteers, the device estimates arterial saturation. The pulse-synchronous signal also yields heart rate and a plethysmographic waveform.

History

Oximetry began with devices such as Glenn Millikan's ear oximeter in the 1940s. The pulse oximetry principle — using the pulse waveform itself to isolate arterial blood — was developed by Takuo Aoyagi at Nihon Kohden in the early 1970s; commercial finger pulse oximeters spread after the Biox and Nellcor instruments of the early 1980s. Anaesthesia safety standards soon made continuous pulse oximetry essentially mandatory in surgery.

Limitations

Readings are degraded by poor peripheral perfusion, motion, ambient light, nail polish and intravenous dyes. Standard two-wavelength oximeters cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin or methaemoglobin, and may overestimate true saturation in carbon monoxide poisoning. Studies and regulatory reviews in the 2020s highlighted that accuracy can be lower in patients with darker skin pigmentation, prompting revised testing requirements. Pulse oximetry measures saturation, not ventilation: supplemental oxygen can mask hypoventilation that blood gas analysis (PaCO2) would reveal.
