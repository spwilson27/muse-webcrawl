Conductive polymer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conductive polymers or, more precisely, intrinsically conducting polymers (ICPs) are organic polymers that conduct electricity. While most polymers are electrical insulators, conjugated polymers — those with alternating single and double bonds along the backbone — can carry charge, particularly when oxidised or reduced ("doped") by the introduction of charge carriers. Conductivities can range from semiconducting values up to those approaching copper.

History

The field was transformed in 1977 when Hideki Shirakawa, Alan MacDiarmid, and Alan Heeger reported that polyacetylene, when doped with iodine vapour, increased in conductivity by many orders of magnitude. The three shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2000 "for the discovery and development of conductive polymers." Since then, widely studied systems have included polyaniline, polypyrrole, polythiophene and its derivative PEDOT (often used as PEDOT:PSS dispersions), poly(p-phenylene vinylene), and polyfluorene.

Properties and applications

Charge transport in these materials involves polarons and bipolarons formed on doping, and conductivity depends strongly on doping level, processing, and morphology. Conductive polymers combine electrical behaviour with the processability, light weight, and corrosion resistance of plastics. Applications include organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), organic solar cells, field-effect transistors, antistatic coatings, electromagnetic shielding, sensors, supercapacitors and batteries, artificial muscles (electroactive polymers), and bioelectronics, where PEDOT:PSS is valued for mixed ionic-electronic conduction. Challenges include environmental stability, reproducibility, and lower charge-carrier mobility than inorganic semiconductors.
