Phase (matter)

In the physical sciences, a phase is a region of material that is chemically uniform, physically distinct and mechanically separable. Within a system, each phase has uniform physical properties throughout, and different phases are separated by definite boundaries (phase boundaries) across which properties change discontinuously.

States of matter

The most familiar phases are the three states of matter: solid, liquid and gas. A glass of ice water contains two phases — solid ice and liquid water — of the same chemical substance, while a mixture of oil and water contains two liquid phases that do not mix. Ice itself exists in many crystalline phases (ordinary hexagonal ice is ice Ih), and many solids show polymorphism, forming different crystal structures under different pressures and temperatures.

Beyond the classical three, physics recognises further phases including plasma, Bose–Einstein condensates, liquid crystals, superfluids, superconducting and magnetic phases (ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic), and phases of individual components in alloys and solutions.

Phase diagrams and transitions

A phase diagram plots which phase is stable at each combination of temperature, pressure and composition. Boundaries on the diagram mark where two phases coexist in equilibrium; the triple point is where solid, liquid and gas coexist, and the critical point is where the liquid–gas boundary ends. Changes between phases — melting, freezing, vaporisation, condensation, sublimation — are phase transitions, classified as first-order (involving latent heat and a discontinuous change in density) or continuous (such as the ferromagnetic transition at the Curie temperature).

The Gibbs phase rule relates the number of phases present to the degrees of freedom of the system: F = C − P + 2, where C is the number of components and P the number of phases.
