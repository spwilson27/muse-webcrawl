Cohomology

In mathematics, specifically in homology theory and algebraic topology, cohomology is a general term for a sequence of abelian groups, or more generally objects in an abelian category, associated to a topological space or other mathematical object, often defined from a cochain complex. Cohomology can be viewed as a method of assigning richer algebraic invariants to a space than homology: its cup product gives it the structure of a graded ring, and it represents a contravariant functor, reversing the direction of maps.

Motivation

Cohomology groups Hn(X) measure, in each degree n, the failure of local data on a space X to assemble into global data. The first cohomology group, for example, classifies covering spaces and (in de Rham theory) closed differential forms that are not exact. Singular cohomology is defined by applying the Hom functor to a chain complex, so it is dual to singular homology; the universal coefficient theorem relates the two, involving the Ext functor.

Major theories

De Rham cohomology computes the topology of a smooth manifold from its differential forms, and de Rham's theorem identifies it with singular cohomology with real coefficients. Sheaf cohomology generalises the idea to arbitrary ringed spaces and underlies modern algebraic geometry, where it yields the cohomology of coherent sheaves. Group cohomology studies group actions and extensions, while étale cohomology, developed by Grothendieck and others, provided the machinery to prove the Weil conjectures. Extraordinary cohomology theories such as K-theory and cobordism arise from spectra in stable homotopy theory.

Operations

Beyond groups, cohomology carries extra structure: the associative, graded-commutative cup product; characteristic classes such as Chern and Stiefel–Whitney classes; and cohomology operations such as the Steenrod squares and Adams operations. These make cohomology one of the most computationally powerful tools in topology and geometry.
