Common knowledge (logic)

Common knowledge requires infinitely nested mutual knowledge between agents.

Common knowledge is a special kind of knowledge for a group of agents. There is common knowledge of p in a group of agents G when all the agents in G know p, they all know that they know p, they all know that they all know that they know p, and so on ad infinitum. It can be understood as information known by all participants, where that fact is itself known by all.

Mutual knowledge

Common knowledge is stronger than mutual knowledge, in which everyone merely knows p. The distinction matters in coordination problems: the classic "muddy children puzzle" and the coordinated attack problem show that behaviour can hinge on whether information is common knowledge or only mutual knowledge at some finite depth.

History and applications

The concept was introduced by philosopher David Lewis in his 1969 work Convention, and formalised by Robert Aumann in 1976. It is used in game theory, epistemic logic, economics, linguistics and distributed computing. Aumann's agreement theorem — that rational agents with common priors cannot "agree to disagree" — relies on common knowledge of posteriors. In computer science, the impossibility of achieving common knowledge over unreliable channels underlies results such as the Two Generals' Problem.

Public announcements

Public events, such as announcements made openly in the presence of all agents, can create common knowledge. Public announcement logic studies how such announcements change the knowledge states of groups.
