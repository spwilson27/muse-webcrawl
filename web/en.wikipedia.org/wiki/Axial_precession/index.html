Axial precession

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Axial precession
PeriodAbout 25,772 years for a full cycle
Discovered byHipparchus (c. 2nd century BC), by comparing star positions over ~150 years
CauseGravitational torques of the Sun and Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge
EffectThe celestial poles trace circles; the equinoxes drift westward through the zodiac ("precession of the equinoxes"); the pole star changes

Axial precession is the slow, conical wobble of Earth's rotation axis — like a spinning top winding down — in which the axis sweeps out a circle in the sky over about 25,772 years, driven by the gravitational pull of the Sun and Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge. The poles consequently wander among the stars: today's north pole star, Polaris, is a temporary appointment. Around 3000 BC the pole star was Thuban in Draco (the pyramid age); in about 12,000 years it will be near Vega, the brightest star of the northern summer sky; Polaris itself will make its closest approach to the pole in 2102 and then drift away.

Effects and significance

Because the axis moves, the equinoxes occur slightly earlier each year relative to the stars — a drift of about 50 arcseconds annually (a full degree in 72 years), the "precession of the equinoxes" that Hipparchus detected by comparing his star catalogue with Timocharis's from 150 years earlier, one of antiquity's great observational feats. Precession is why the tropical year (seasons) is about 20 minutes shorter than the sidereal year (stars), why astrological "star signs" have slipped a full sign against the constellations since Babylon, and why star charts carry an epoch (J2000). Combined with changes in axial tilt and orbital eccentricity, precession is one of the Milankovitch cycles that pace the ice ages — in about 11,000 years the northern hemisphere will have summer near perihelion, reversing today's arrangement. Nutation (a small nodding superimposed by the Moon's orbital tilt) and the far slower drift of the crust itself (true polar wander) add finer print to the planet's stately gyration.
