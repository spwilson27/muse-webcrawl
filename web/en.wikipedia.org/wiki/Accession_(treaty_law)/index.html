Accession (treaty law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Accession
Type: Method of becoming party to a treaty
Field: Law of treaties
Codified: Articles 11 and 15, Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties
Act: Deposit of an instrument of accession
Instruments of accession are deposited with the depositary of a treaty, for many multilateral treaties the Secretary-General of the United Nations in New York.

In treaty law, accession is the act by which a state that did not sign a treaty accepts the offer or opportunity to become a party to it, in circumstances where the treaty so provides. Accession has the same legal effect as ratification: the acceding state becomes bound by the treaty's terms, and the treaty enters into force for that state in accordance with its provisions.

Procedure

Accession is normally effected by depositing an instrument of accession with the depositary of the treaty — for many multilateral treaties, the Secretary-General of the United Nations — or by notifying the other parties. Article 15 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969) provides that consent to be bound by accession is possible where the treaty provides for it, where the negotiating states agreed to it, or where all parties later agree. States acceding may, where the treaty permits, attach reservations modifying the legal effect of certain provisions in their application to that state.

Distinction from signature and ratification

Signature generally expresses an intention to consider ratification and an obligation not to defeat the treaty's object and purpose, but does not by itself bind the state. Ratification is the act by which a signatory state confirms its consent to be bound, usually after domestic approval procedures. Accession allows a state to become bound in a single step, without having signed the treaty during the period it was open for signature — a practical necessity for multilateral treaties concluded before a state existed or before it wished to join.

Examples

Accession is the normal route by which new parties join treaties such as the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and the conventions of the World Trade Organization framework. In a different but related usage, states "accede" to the European Union under Article 49 of the Treaty on European Union, after negotiating terms of accession set out in an accession treaty.
