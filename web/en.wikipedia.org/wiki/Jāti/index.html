Jāti (Sanskrit: जाति) is the term for the thousands of hereditary groups — usually translated as "castes" — that make up the lived reality of the caste system in South Asia. While the four varṇas (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) provide a pan-Indian theoretical scheme, a person's actual social identity, marriage circle and traditional occupation are determined by their jāti, which is local and specific.

Characteristics

Jātis are typically endogamous groups associated with a traditional craft or occupation — potters (Kumhar), goldsmiths (Sonar), weavers, barbers, washermen and many others — and ranked locally relative to one another according to notions of ritual purity, diet and occupation. Caste panchayats have historically enforced group norms. Individuals usually know their jāti's rank in local hierarchies even where its position in the abstract varṇa scheme is ambiguous or contested; many groups (such as Jats, Marathas or Yadavs) have advanced collective claims to higher varṇa status, a process M. N. Srinivas called Sanskritisation.

History and modern status

The proliferation of jātis is traced to the early medieval period, through the absorption of tribes, guilds and sects into caste society. Colonial censuses (especially from 1871 to 1931) attempted to enumerate and rank jātis, arguably rigidifying the system; scholars debate how far British classification reshaped indigenous practice. Independent India's Constitution (1950) abolished "untouchability" and prohibits caste discrimination, while its reservation system allocates educational and government posts using caste categories built largely from jāti lists — Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes — keeping jāti politically salient even as urbanisation and inter-caste marriage weaken traditional rules.
