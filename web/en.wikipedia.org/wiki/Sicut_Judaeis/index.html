Sicut Judaeis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sicut Judaeis ("As the Jews") was a papal bull, first issued in about 1120 by Pope Callixtus II, that set out the official position of the Roman Catholic Church regarding the treatment of Jews. It takes its name from its opening words and is also known as the Constitutio pro Judaeis.

The bull forbade Christians, under pain of excommunication, from forcing Jews to convert, harming them or seizing their property, disturbing their festivals and cemeteries, or extorting money from them. At the same time, it reflected the medieval doctrine that Jews were to be tolerated only in a subordinate position, and it did not prevent discriminatory legislation elsewhere in canon law.

History

Callixtus II issued the bull in the aftermath of the violence against Jewish communities during the First Crusade. It was reissued by at least a dozen later popes over the following centuries — including Eugenius III, Alexander III, Clement III, Celestine III, Innocent III, Honorius III, Gregory IX, Innocent IV, and Martin V — making it one of the most frequently reconfirmed papal pronouncements of the Middle Ages.

The principle behind the bull traces back to Pope Gregory I (r. 590–604), who had taught that Jews should be won to Christianity by persuasion rather than force and that their legal rights should be respected. Despite repeated reissue, the protections of Sicut Judaeis were often violated in practice, particularly during crises such as the Black Death persecutions.
