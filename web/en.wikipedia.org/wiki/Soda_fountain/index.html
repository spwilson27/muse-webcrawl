Soda fountain

A soda fountain is a device that dispenses carbonated soft drinks, called fountain drinks. They can be found in restaurants, convenience stores, and other locations, as well as in private homes as small countertop versions. The device combines carbonated water with flavored syrup. The term also historically referred to the establishment — typically a counter within a drugstore or ice cream parlor — where such drinks were served, often by a "soda jerk".

History

Carbonated water was prized in the 18th and early 19th centuries for its supposed medicinal qualities. In 1806, Benjamin Silliman, a Yale chemistry professor, began selling soda water in New Haven, Connecticut, and similar ventures followed in New York and Philadelphia. The first soda fountain patent in the United States is often credited to Samuel Fahnestock in 1819, though his "fountain" was essentially a small barrel with a pump and spigot.

John Matthews, who began manufacturing fountains in New York City in 1832, is often called the "father of the American soda fountain." His company popularized the back-bar fountain apparatus. In 1863, Gustavus Dows opened an elaborate marble soda fountain in Lowell, Massachusetts, helping transform the soda fountain into an ornate social fixture. Pharmacy counters adopted fountains widely during the temperance movement and especially during Prohibition (1920–1933), when soda fountains partially replaced bars as social gathering places.

Walgreens popularized the malted milkshake in the 1920s, driving enormous growth in its soda fountain business. After World War II, self-service fountains in fast-food restaurants largely displaced the staffed pharmacy soda fountain, though nostalgic ice-cream parlors and some drugstores keep the tradition alive.

How it works

A modern fountain system typically consists of a carbonator that mixes chilled water with carbon dioxide to make soda water, syrup containers (often "bag-in-box" packages), pumps, and a dispensing head where syrup and carbonated water mix in a fixed ratio, commonly about five parts water to one part syrup. Post-mix systems mix at the point of dispensing; pre-mix systems dispense a finished beverage from a pressurized tank.

Cultural significance

The soda fountain became an iconic feature of 20th-century American small-town and urban life, a gathering place for teenagers and a stock setting in films and television. The soda jerk, who operated the fountain with theatrical flourishes, developed an elaborate slang for orders, such as "burn one" for a chocolate malted.
