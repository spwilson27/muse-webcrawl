Bone density

Bone density, or bone mineral density (BMD), is the amount of bone mineral in bone tissue, conventionally expressed as grams of mineral per square centimetre measured by scanning. The concept of bone density is used clinically as an indirect indicator of the likelihood of osteoporosis and fracture risk, since bones with higher mineral content are generally stronger and less likely to break.

Measurement

The reference method is dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA or DEXA), which uses two X-ray energies to separate bone from soft tissue, most commonly at the lumbar spine and proximal femur. Results are reported as a T-score, comparing the patient's density with that of a healthy young adult reference population, and a Z-score, comparing with an age-matched population. Quantitative computed tomography can measure true volumetric density, and ultrasound devices are sometimes used for screening.

Classification

Under World Health Organization criteria, a T-score at or above −1.0 is normal; between −1.0 and −2.5 indicates low bone mass (osteopenia); and −2.5 or below defines osteoporosis. Bone density is only one component of fracture risk, which also depends on age, prior fractures, medication use, and fall risk; tools such as FRAX combine these factors.

Determinants

Peak bone mass is reached in early adulthood and is influenced by genetics, nutrition (notably calcium and vitamin D), sex hormones, and weight-bearing exercise. Density declines with age, especially rapidly in women after the menopause. Smoking, excessive alcohol, glucocorticoid therapy, and various diseases accelerate loss. Weight-bearing exercise, adequate nutrition, and medications such as bisphosphonates can slow or partly reverse bone loss.
