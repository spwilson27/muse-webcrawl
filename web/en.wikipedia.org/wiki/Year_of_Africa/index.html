Year of Africa

The Year of Africa was a milestone in the history of decolonisation: 1960, when seventeen African states gained independence, the largest number to do so in any single year. The wave began with the Republic of the Congo (Congo-Léopoldville) on 30 June and included French West and Equatorial African territories, Nigeria, Somalia, and others, transforming the map of Africa and the composition of the United Nations. In a famous speech at the Cape Town parliament on 3 February 1960, British Prime Minister Harold Macmillan spoke of the "wind of change" blowing through the continent, signalling Britain's acceptance of decolonisation.

The independence wave

The states that became independent in 1960 were: Cameroon (1 January), Togo (27 April), Madagascar (26 June), Republic of the Congo (30 June), Somalia (1 July, union of British and Italian Somalilands), Dahomey — now Benin (1 August), Niger (3 August), Upper Volta — now Burkina Faso (5 August), Ivory Coast (7 August), Chad (11 August), Central African Republic (13 August), Republic of the Congo — now Congo-Brazzaville (15 August), Gabon (16/17 August), Senegal (20 August, leaving the Mali Federation), Mali (22 September), and Nigeria (1 October). Cyprus, outside Africa, also became independent in 1960.

The French territories were freed under Charles de Gaulle's Community reforms, which gave them the choice of independence; British territories followed scheduled timetables, while the Belgian Congo's abrupt decolonisation plunged into the Congo Crisis within days of independence.

Legacy

The sudden enlargement of the UN — Africa's membership bloc became decisive — fuelled the 1960 UN Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (Resolution 1514) and the formation of the Organization of African Unity in 1963. 1960 remains emblematic of decolonisation, though its aftermath was turbulent: within a decade, many of the new states fell under military or one-party rule.
