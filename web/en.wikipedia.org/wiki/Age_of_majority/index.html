Age of majority

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The age of majority is the threshold of legal adulthood recognised in law: the age at which a person ceases to be a minor and assumes full legal capacity — the right to make binding contracts, to vote (where the voting age coincides), to marry without parental consent, and to be held fully responsible as an adult, along with the end of parental guardianship and child-support obligations. In the great majority of countries the age of majority is 18, following the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), which defines a child as a person under 18.

History

In Roman law full majority came at 25, though puberty marked stages of capacity; in medieval England the age of majority for most purposes was 21, connected to the age at which a tenant could be knighted and bear the arms his land owed. Twenty-one remained the common-law standard for centuries — voting, contracting, marrying — until reform swept most Western countries in the early 1970s: the United Kingdom lowered it to 18 in 1969, and the United States, driven by the anomaly of conscripting 18-year-olds for Vietnam who could not vote, passed the 26th Amendment (1971) fixing the voting age at 18, with most states lowering the age of majority to match. Japan, long an outlier at 20, lowered its age of majority to 18 in 2022.

Variation

Most jurisdictions set majority at 18, but exceptions remain: 19 in several Canadian provinces and some US states (Alabama, Nebraska) and in South Korea's traditional reckoning (now aligned to 18/19 by "international age" reforms), 20 in Taiwan and Thailand, and 21 in a few countries including Singapore (for most purposes), Egypt, and Mississippi in the United States. Some systems tie majority partly to marriage or to religious criteria. The age of majority is distinct from other legal ages that cluster around it — ages of criminal responsibility, consent, school leaving, driving and drinking — which are set separately and often differ, as with the US drinking age of 21.

Related concepts

A minor may attain adult status early through emancipation, by court order, marriage, or military service in some jurisdictions. Ceremonies marking the transition — such as the quinceañera, the Jewish bar and bat mitzvah (religious, at 13/12), and Japan's Coming of Age Day — predate or parallel the legal threshold.
