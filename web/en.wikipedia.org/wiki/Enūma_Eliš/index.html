The Enūma Eliš (Akkadian Cuneiform: 𒂖𒈠𒂖𒂇), named for its opening words "when on high", is the Babylonian creation myth. Composed probably in the second millennium BC, it recounts how the god Marduk defeated the primordial sea goddess Tiamat, created the cosmos from her body, fashioned humankind from the blood of the slain god Kingu, and established Babylon and its temple Esagila.

Summary

From the mingling of fresh water (Apsu) and salt water (Tiamat) arise generations of gods. After conflict, in which Apsu and later Ea's son Marduk feature, Marduk agrees to battle Tiamat on condition of supremacy. Victorious, he splits her body to form heaven and earth, orders the stars, creates humans to serve the gods, and is proclaimed king with fifty names.

Discovery and significance

Tablets were excavated by Austen Henry Layard in 1849 from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh and published by George Smith in 1876. The text parallels aspects of Genesis, informing debates on shared Mesopotamian traditions. It was recited during Babylon's New Year (Akitu) festival.
