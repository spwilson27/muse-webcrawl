Botulinum toxin is among the most potent toxins known; its heavy chain targets nerve terminals while its light chain is a zinc protease that disables neurotransmitter release.

Botulinum toxin (commonly called BoNT, and widely known by the brand name Botox) is a neurotoxic protein produced by the bacterium Clostridium botulinum and related species. It prevents the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine from axon endings at the neuromuscular junction, causing flaccid paralysis. Infection with the bacterium, or ingestion of the toxin in improperly preserved food, causes the disease botulism. The toxin is the most acutely lethal toxin known, with an estimated lethal dose in humans of roughly 1.3–2.1 nanograms per kilogram intravenously or intramuscularly — yet in minute, controlled doses it is a valuable medicine.

Eight serotypes (A–H) are recognised. Types A and B are used in medical and cosmetic preparations. Commercial products include onabotulinumtoxinA (Botox), abobotulinumtoxinA (Dysport), incobotulinumtoxinA (Xeomin), prabotulinumtoxinA (Jeuveau), and rimabotulinumtoxinB (Myobloc).

Mechanism of action

The toxin is a two-chain polypeptide: a heavy chain that binds receptors on cholinergic nerve terminals and mediates entry into the cell, and a light chain that is a zinc-dependent protease. Inside the terminal, the light chain cleaves one of the SNARE proteins (SNAP-25, synaptobrevin/VAMP, or syntaxin, depending on serotype) that are required for synaptic vesicles to fuse with the membrane. With vesicle fusion disabled, acetylcholine cannot be released and the muscle cannot contract. The effect is temporary: over weeks to months the nerve sprouts new endings and function returns, which is why clinical treatments are repeated at intervals of roughly three months.

Botulism

Botulism occurs in several forms: foodborne (ingestion of preformed toxin, classically from inadequately sterilised home-canned or fermented foods), infant botulism (intestinal colonisation by spores, famously associated with honey, which should not be fed to infants under 12 months), wound botulism, and iatrogenic cases. Symptoms begin with cranial nerve palsies — blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty speaking and swallowing — and may progress to descending flaccid paralysis and respiratory failure. Treatment relies on antitoxin and supportive care, including ventilation.

Medical uses

Ophthalmologist Alan Scott pioneered the therapeutic use of the toxin in the 1970s for strabismus (misaligned eyes) and blepharospasm. Regulatory approvals and widespread clinical use now cover cervical dystonia, hemifacial spasm, upper and lower limb spasticity (including in cerebral palsy), chronic migraine prophylaxis, overactive bladder and detrusor overactivity, severe axillary hyperhidrosis (excessive sweating), sialorrhea, and bruxism, among other conditions. Effects at a treated site typically last about three months.

Cosmetic uses

In cosmetic practice, small injections temporarily relax the facial muscles that produce dynamic wrinkles — glabellar ("frown") lines, forehead lines, and crow's feet — giving a smoother appearance for three to four months. The cosmetic potential was noticed in the late 1980s by Vancouver ophthalmologist Jean Carruthers and her dermatologist husband Alastair Carruthers, whose patients treated for blepharospasm reported reduced frown lines. The US Food and Drug Administration approved Botox Cosmetic for glabellar lines in 2002, and aesthetic use has since become a multibillion-dollar global industry.

History

The German physician Justinus Kerner described botulism ("sausage poisoning," from Latin botulus, sausage) in the 1820s and speculated that the poison might one day be used therapeutically. In 1895 Émile van Ermengem isolated the causative bacterium from contaminated ham. The toxin was purified in the 1940s, its crystalline form prepared by Edward Schantz, and in the 1980s Scott's clinical work led to FDA approval of the first therapeutic product (Oculinum, later Botox) in 1989. Historically, botulinum toxin attracted interest as a potential biological weapon and is classified as a select agent; it is listed under the Biological Weapons Convention.

Warning: This article is for general information about the substance. Botulinum toxin products are prescription medicines; administration by unlicensed practitioners or use of counterfeit products has caused serious illness.
