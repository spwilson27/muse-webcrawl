Right to Internet access

The right to Internet access, also known as the right to broadband or freedom to connect, is the view that all people should be able to access the Internet in order to exercise and enjoy their rights to freedom of expression and opinion and other fundamental human rights. Proponents argue that states have a responsibility to ensure that Internet access is broadly available and cannot unreasonably restrict an individual's access.

Background

The idea gained prominence in the 2000s as the Internet became central to commerce, education, political participation and social life. In 2011 the United Nations Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression, Frank La Rue, issued a report emphasising that restricting Internet access can violate the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. In 2016 the UN Human Rights Council adopted a resolution condemning measures that prevent or disrupt access to information online.

National measures

Several countries have taken steps toward guaranteeing access. Estonia declared Internet access a human right in legislation as early as 2000. Finland made a functioning broadband connection a legal right in 2010, the first country to do so. France's Constitutional Council in 2009 linked Internet access to the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. Finland, Spain, Greece and Costa Rica have each adopted constitutional or statutory provisions of various strengths.

Debate

Supporters argue that connectivity is a prerequisite for economic opportunity and democratic participation, and that a digital divide deepens inequality. Critics, including Internet pioneer Vint Cerf, have argued that access is better understood as an enabler of rights rather than a right in itself, and have cautioned against elevating a particular technology to the status of a fundamental right.
