Schwarzschild radius

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram comparing Schwarzschild radii: Earth's would be a marble-sized sphere, the Sun's about 3 km]
Compress an object within its Schwarzschild radius and it becomes a black hole.

The Schwarzschild radius is the radius of the event horizon of a non-rotating black hole: the distance from the centre at which the escape velocity equals the speed of light. Any object compressed within its own Schwarzschild radius collapses into a black hole. The radius is proportional to mass: r_s = 2GM/c² — about 2.95 km for the Sun, 8.9 mm for the Earth, and 42 microarcseconds across for the Milky Way's Sagittarius A* as seen from Earth.

Karl Schwarzschild derived the first exact solution of Einstein's field equations in 1916, months after general relativity was published and shortly before his death at the front in the First World War. The 'radius' is not a material surface but a causal boundary: within it, all paths lead to the singularity. For rotating (Kerr) and charged black holes the horizon geometry differs, but the Schwarzschild case remains the reference — and its simple Newtonian coincidence (the same formula follows from setting escape velocity to c, noted by Michell in 1783 for his 'dark stars') is a famous accident of factors.

Scale examples

A human's Schwarzschild radius is ~10⁻²⁵ m; a mountain's, a nanometre; the observable universe's mass corresponds to a radius of the same order as the universe itself — a numerological curiosity occasionally inflated into claims that the universe is a black hole, which the differing definitions do not support.
