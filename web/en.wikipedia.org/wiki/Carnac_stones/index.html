The Carnac stones (Breton: Steudadoù Karnag) are an exceptionally dense collection of megalithic sites around the French commune of Carnac in Brittany, consisting of stone alignments (rows), dolmens (stone tombs), tumuli (burial mounds) and single standing stones (menhirs). More than 10,000 Neolithic stones were erected by pre-Celtic peoples during the Neolithic period, probably around 3300 BC but some may date to as early as 4500 BC. The sites comprise the largest such collection in the world.

The alignments

The three main groups of alignments are the Ménec, Kermario and Kerlescan alignments, together comprising over 1,000 surviving stones arranged in parallel rows running roughly east–west over distances of up to a kilometre. The Ménec alignments consist of 1,099 stones in 11 rows; the largest stones, at the western ends, reach about 4 metres in height, diminishing eastwards. Local legend, echoed in folklore recorded by folklorists, holds that the stones are a Roman legion turned to stone by the wizard Merlin, or by the Cornish saint Cornély.

Other monuments

The area includes major tumuli such as Saint-Michel (the largest burial mound in continental Europe) and the Kercado tumulus, among the oldest stone structures in Europe, as well as dolmens such as those at Kermario and Mané-Kerioned. The giant broken menhir of Locmariaquer (the Grand Menhir Brisé), now fallen in four pieces nearby, would originally have stood some 20 metres tall and weighed around 330 tonnes.

Interpretation

The purpose of the alignments remains debated. Alexander Thom and others proposed astronomical functions — sightlines to solstitial sunrises and an elaborate "megalithic calendar" — but such interpretations are controversial. Most archaeologists treat the complexes as ceremonial and funerary landscapes built by early farming communities. In July 2025, the Carnac megaliths were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the "Carnac and the Megaliths of the Morbihan Coast" listing.

Conservation

The stones were protected as historic monuments from the 19th century. Since the 1990s, visitor access to the alignments has been restricted in summer to guided tours, to combat erosion and vegetation damage caused by up to 600,000 visitors a year.
