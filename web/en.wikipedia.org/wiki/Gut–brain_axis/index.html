Gut–brain axis

The gut–brain axis is the two-way biochemical signalling network that takes place between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system. The term is often extended to include the role of the gut microbiota in these interactions — the microbiota–gut–brain axis. Communication occurs through neural, endocrine, immune, and metabolic pathways.

Pathways

  • Neural: the vagus nerve and the enteric nervous system — sometimes called the "second brain" — carry signals between gut and brainstem.
  • Endocrine: gut hormones such as ghrelin, leptin, GLP-1, and peptide YY modulate appetite and mood; the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis links stress to gut function.
  • Immune and metabolic: cytokines, short-chain fatty acids produced by microbial fermentation, and microbial metabolites of tryptophan influence neuroinflammation and neurotransmitter systems. Remarkably, most of the body's serotonin is produced in the gut by enterochromaffin cells.

Clinical significance

The gut–brain axis is implicated in functional gastrointestinal disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome, which commonly co-occurs with anxiety and depression. Animal studies show that germ-free mice display abnormal stress responses and social behaviour that can be normalised by colonisation with specific microbes — the basis of the proposed class of "psychobiotics". Research also explores links between gut microbiota and Parkinson's disease, autism spectrum disorders, and mood disorders, although causal evidence in humans remains limited and often preliminary.

History

Physicians recognised connections between digestion and emotion in antiquity; the scientific field grew from 19th-century work on the vagus nerve and the enteric nervous system, and expanded rapidly with microbiome sequencing in the 2000s and 2010s.
