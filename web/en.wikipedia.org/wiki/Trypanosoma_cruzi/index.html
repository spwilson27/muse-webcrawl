Trypanosoma cruzi is a species of parasitic euglenoid protozoan, a kinetoplastid transmitted by blood-sucking triatomine bugs ("kissing bugs") of the subfamily Triatominae. It causes Chagas disease (American trypanosomiasis) in humans and other mammals, a major public health problem in Latin America. The parasite was discovered in 1909 by the Brazilian physician Carlos Chagas, who described both the organism and the disease it causes — a unique double discovery.

Life cycle

The parasite alternates between insect vectors and mammalian hosts in several morphological forms. Metacyclic trypomastigotes, deposited in the faeces of the feeding bug near the bite wound, enter host cells and transform into amastigotes, which multiply by binary fission. Amastigotes convert back to trypomastigotes, rupture the cell, and circulate in the blood, where they can infect new cells or be taken up by another bug; in the insect gut they multiply as epimastigotes before reaching the infective stage. Transmission can also occur congenitally, by transfusion or organ transplant, and orally via contaminated food.

Disease

Chagas disease begins with an acute phase, often mild and sometimes marked by Romaña's sign (swelling around the eye), followed by a chronic indeterminate phase lasting decades. In about 20–30 per cent of infections, chronic disease develops, most importantly a dilated cardiomyopathy, or digestive complications such as megaesophagus and megacolon. Treatment relies on the antiparasitic drugs benznidazole and nifurtimox, most effective in the acute phase. An estimated 6–7 million people are infected worldwide, mostly in Latin America, with increasing cases reported in the United States and Europe due to migration.
