The Battle of the Chesapeake, also known as the Battle of the Virginia Capes, was a crucial naval battle in the American Revolutionary War, fought on 5 September 1781 between a French fleet under the Comte de Grasse and a British fleet under Rear Admiral Thomas Graves. Tactically indecisive in terms of ships lost, it was a decisive strategic victory for France: it prevented the Royal Navy from relieving the besieged British army at Yorktown, and thereby ensured the Franco-American victory that effectively ended the war.

Background

In the summer of 1781 General George Washington and the French general Rochambeau marched from New York to Virginia to trap Lord Cornwallis's army at Yorktown, counting on French naval superiority in the Chesapeake. De Grasse sailed from the West Indies with 28 ships of the line and 3,000 troops, entering the bay on 30 August and landing the soldiers to join the allied army. Graves, with 19 ships of the line from New York, arrived at the Capes on 5 September to find the French fleet anchored inside.

The battle

De Grasse ordered his fleet to sea in haste, cutting cables and leaving some crews ashore. The two fleets formed lines of battle and converged on an angle; because of conflicting signals from Graves (ordering both "line ahead" and "bear down" simultaneously), the British centre and rear failed to close with the enemy, and only the van divisions were heavily engaged. After about two hours of cannonade in the late afternoon, the fleets separated. For several days de Grasse manoeuvred to seaward, drawing Graves away from the bay, and then slipped back into the Chesapeake — where he found the French squadron from Newport, under the Comte de Barras, had arrived with the allied siege artillery. Graves, his fleet damaged and now heavily outnumbered, burned the crippled HMS Terrible and returned to New York.

Consequences

With the bay sealed, Cornwallis was cut off from supply or evacuation by sea. The siege of Yorktown proceeded with the French siege train, and on 19 October 1781 Cornwallis surrendered his army — the last major engagement of the war. News of the surrender broke the will of the British government to continue the war, leading to the peace negotiations that produced the Treaty of Paris (1783) recognising American independence. The battle is a classic demonstration that command of the sea, even briefly, can decide a land campaign; it is commemorated by the Cape Henry Memorial in Virginia.
