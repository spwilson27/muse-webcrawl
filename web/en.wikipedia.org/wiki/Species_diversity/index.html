Species diversity

Species diversity is the number of different species that are represented in a given community (a dataset). The effective number of species refers to the number of equally abundant species needed for the average proportional abundance of the species to equal that observed in the dataset of interest. Species diversity consists of two components: species richness and species evenness.

Components

Species richness is a simple count of species present. Species evenness quantifies how equal the abundances of the species are: a community in which one species dominates has lower diversity than one in which individuals are spread evenly among the same number of species, even though richness is identical.

Measures

Common indices include the Shannon index (Shannon–Wiener), which incorporates richness and evenness and is based on information entropy, and Simpson's index, which emphasizes dominance. Diversity is often discussed at three spatial scales following Whittaker: alpha diversity (within a habitat), beta diversity (turnover between habitats), and gamma diversity (total diversity of a landscape or region).

Patterns and importance

Species diversity generally increases toward the equator (latitudinal diversity gradient), with tropical rainforests and coral reefs among the most diverse ecosystems on Earth. High diversity is associated with ecosystem stability, productivity, and services such as pollination and nutrient cycling. Human activities, habitat loss, invasive species, and climate change are driving widespread declines in species diversity, a central concern of conservation biology.
