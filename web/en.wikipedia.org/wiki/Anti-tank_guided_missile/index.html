Anti-tank guided missile

An anti-tank guided missile (ATGM) is a guided missile primarily designed to hit and destroy heavily armoured military vehicles, especially tanks. ATGMs range from shoulder-launched infantry weapons to large helicopter- and vehicle-mounted systems with ranges of several kilometres, and most use a shaped-charge (HEAT) warhead, often in tandem configuration to defeat explosive reactive armour.

Generations of guidance

First-generation missiles (1950s–60s) used manual command to line of sight (MCLOS): the operator steered the missile with a joystick all the way to the target, as with the French SS.10 and early Soviet 3M6 Shmel. Second-generation systems use semi-automatic command (SACLOS): the operator merely keeps crosshairs on the target while the system tracks a flare on the missile and sends corrections, usually over a trailing wire — examples include the MILAN, TOW and HOT. Third-generation "fire-and-forget" missiles such as the FGM-148 Javelin home on the target's infrared signature, freeing the operator to relocate immediately, and can attack in a top-attack profile against the thin roof armour of tanks.

Warheads and countermeasures

The shaped charge produces a high-velocity metal jet capable of penetrating many times its own diameter in armour plate. Tandem warheads detonate reactive armour first, then penetrate with the main charge. Tanks counter ATGMs with composite and reactive armour, smoke, and active protection systems that intercept incoming missiles. Soft-kill systems attempt to jam or decoy the guidance.

Notable systems

Widely used ATGMs include the American TOW and Javelin, the Russian 9M133 Kornet, the Israeli Spike family, and the Franco-German MILAN. In recent conflicts large numbers of modern ATGMs have proven highly effective against armoured formations, reinforcing the missile's reputation — gained in the 1973 Yom Kippur War with Egyptian Sagger teams — as a great equaliser between infantry and tanks.
