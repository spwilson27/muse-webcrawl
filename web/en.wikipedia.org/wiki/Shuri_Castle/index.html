Shuri Castle

Location: Shuri, Naha, Okinawa, Japan
Type: Gusuku (Ryukyuan castle)
Status: Under reconstruction (after 2019 fire)
UNESCO: World Heritage Site (2000)

Shuri Castle (Japanese: 首里城, romanized: Shuri-jō; Okinawan: Sui Ugusuku) is a Ryukyuan gusuku castle in Shuri, Naha, Okinawa Prefecture, Japan. It served as the palace of the Ryukyu Kingdom from the reign of King Shō Shin in the late 15th century until the last king, Shō Tai, was deposed following the kingdom's annexation by Japan in 1879. Perched on a hill above Naha, the castle combined Japanese and Chinese architectural influences in a characteristically Ryukyuan style, with its main hall painted a brilliant vermilion.

History

The castle's origins are unclear, but fortifications existed at Shuri from at least the Sanzan period. It was repeatedly destroyed by fire over the centuries — in 1453, 1660, 1709, and during the Battle of Okinawa in 1945, when it served as an Imperial Japanese Army headquarters and was bombarded by the battleship USS Mississippi. The University of the Ryukyus occupied the site after the war until the 1970s; restoration using historical records and traditional materials culminated in the reopening of the main courtyard in 1992 for the 20th anniversary of Okinawa's reversion to Japan.

In 2000, Shuri Castle and related sites were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of "Gusuku Sites and Related Properties of the Kingdom of Ryukyu."

2019 fire and reconstruction

In the early hours of 31 October 2019, a fire destroyed the Seiden (main hall), the Hokuden and Nanden (north and south halls), and several other structures — six buildings and roughly 480 square metres in total. The blaze, believed to have started in the main hall, was a profound shock in Okinawa, and donations for reconstruction poured in from across Japan and abroad. The Japanese government and Okinawa Prefecture committed to restoring the castle, with the main hall's reconstruction targeted for completion in the mid-2020s using the timber-frame methods and red-tile roofing of the earlier restoration.
