Luciferase is a generic term for the class of oxidative enzymes that produce bioluminescence. Luciferases catalyse the oxidation of a light-emitting substrate called luciferin, in the presence of oxygen and (in many cases) ATP, generating an excited-state product that releases a photon as it decays to its ground state. Different luciferases occur in fireflies and other beetles, marine crustaceans and fish, dinoflagellates, fungi, and bacteria.

Types

Firefly luciferase, from the North American firefly Photinus pyralis, is an approximately 61 kDa enzyme that converts D-luciferin to oxyluciferin using ATP and magnesium ions, emitting yellow-green light near 560 nm. Related enzymes occur in click beetles, some emitting green and others orange light. Bacterial luciferase is a heterodimer (LuxA/LuxB) encoded by the lux operon that oxidises a long-chain aldehyde and reduced flavin mononucleotide. Marine organisms such as dinoflagellates and the sea pansy Renilla use distinct luciferins, including coelenterazine-based systems related to the photoproteins and to Renilla luciferase (RLuc) and Gaussia luciferase (GLuc).

Applications

Because light production is easy to detect with high sensitivity, luciferase genes are among the most widely used reporter genes in molecular biology: expression of a gene of interest can be quantified by the light emitted when luciferin is supplied. Firefly luciferase is used in ATP assays, cell viability tests, and pyrosequencing. Engineered variants such as NanoLuc, and bioluminescence imaging in live animals, extend these uses to drug screening and cancer research.
