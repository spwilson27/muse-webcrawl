Pan-Celticism

The six Celtic nations recognised by the Celtic League

Pan-Celticism (Irish: Pan-Cheilteachas) is a political and cultural movement promoting cooperation and solidarity among the Celtic nations — Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany, Cornwall and the Isle of Man — whose Celtic languages survive into the modern era. Analogous to pan-national movements elsewhere, it encourages shared cultural revival, language preservation and, for some adherents, political autonomy or independence for the Celtic regions.

History

The idea grew from 19th-century Celtic revival scholarship and romantic nationalism. The Pan-Celtic Congress movement held its first meeting in Dublin in 1901, and inter-Celtic festivals such as the Breton Goursez and the Welsh Eisteddfod fostered contacts. In 1961 the Celtic League was founded to campaign for the Celtic languages and political self-determination, with branches in each nation and a journal, Carn. Cultural institutions such as the Festival Interceltique de Lorient in Brittany, founded in 1971, became major showcases of pan-Celtic culture.

Aims and character

Pan-Celticism is primarily cultural and linguistic rather than a programme for a single Celtic state. Its practical projects include language teaching exchanges, broadcasting cooperation, and solidarity campaigns — for example, Scottish and Welsh support for Breton language rights. Critics note the diversity of the nations' situations: Ireland is an independent republic, while other Celtic nations remain parts of larger states with varying degrees of devolution.
