Superhydrophobic coating

A superhydrophobic coating is a nanoscopic surface layer that repels water. Droplets hitting such a coating can fully rebound in an impact regime, coming off the surface in shapes resembling pancakes. Surfaces with a water contact angle greater than 150° and a low roll-off angle (often under 10°) are generally classed as superhydrophobic.

Water droplets beading on a superhydrophobic leaf surface

Mechanism

Superhydrophobicity arises from a combination of low-surface-energy chemistry and micro- or nanoscale roughness. The best-known natural example is the lotus effect: the leaves of Nelumbo nucifera carry microscopic bumps coated in wax crystals, so water sits largely on pockets of trapped air. This state is described by the Cassie–Baxter model, in contrast to the fully wetted Wenzel state. Because droplets contact only a small fraction of the solid, they pick up dirt particles as they roll off, giving superhydrophobic surfaces self-cleaning properties.

Materials and fabrication

Coatings are produced by methods including chemical vapour deposition, sol–gel processing, etching, electrospinning of fluorinated polymers, and spraying of nanoparticle suspensions (commonly silica or titanium dioxide) in a binder. Fluoropolymers such as PTFE are frequently used for their low surface energy, although environmental concerns about per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances have driven research into silicone- and wax-based alternatives.

Applications

Superhydrophobic coatings are used or proposed for self-cleaning windows and solar panels, anti-icing surfaces for aircraft and power lines, drag reduction on ship hulls, anti-corrosion protection, stain-resistant textiles, and lab-on-a-chip devices. A persistent challenge is durability: the delicate surface structures that produce the effect are easily abraded, and many commercial coatings lose performance after modest wear.
