Abu'l Hasan Yamīn ud-Dīn Khusrau (1253 – October 1325), better known as Amīr Khusrau, was an Indo-Persian Sufi singer, musician, poet and scholar who lived under the Delhi Sultanate. A prolific writer in Persian and Hindavi, he served a succession of rulers in Delhi and is an iconic figure in the cultural history of South Asia, sometimes called the "Parrot of India" (Tuti-e-Hind).

Life and works

Khusrau was born in Patiyali to a Turkic father and an Indian mother, and was raised in Delhi. He became a devoted disciple of the Sufi saint Nizamuddin Auliya, by whose side he is buried. As court poet he chronicled the reigns of sultans from Balban through Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq. His Persian works include five divans of poetry, a Khamsa (quintet) modelled on Nizami's, and historical masnavis such as the Nuh Sipihr ("Nine Skies"), which praises India. He is also credited with riddles, songs, and verses in Hindavi, an early form of Hindustani.

Legacy

Khusrau is traditionally credited with enriching Hindustani classical music — legend attributes to him roles in the development of qawwali, khyal, and tarana, and even the invention of the tabla and sitar, though these attributions are debated by historians. His synthesis of Persian, Turkic, Arabic, and Indian cultural elements made him a symbol of the composite culture of medieval India, and his tomb in Delhi's Nizamuddin complex remains a place of veneration.
