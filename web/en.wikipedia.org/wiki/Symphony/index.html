Symphony

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: An open orchestral score with four movements marked, dense notation across paired pages]

A symphony is an extended musical composition for orchestra, typically in four movements: a fast opening movement (often in sonata form), a slow movement, a dance-like minuet or scherzo, and a fast finale. The form was consolidated in the Classical era by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart and, above all, Joseph Haydn, the 'father of the symphony' with over 100 examples.

Ludwig van Beethoven transformed the genre: his nine symphonies expanded its scale and expressive ambition, culminating in the Ninth (1824), which introduced voices in the finale — the 'Ode to Joy'. Romantic symphonists including Schubert, Schumann, Brahms, Bruckner, Mahler and Sibelius made the symphony the prestige form of orchestral music.
