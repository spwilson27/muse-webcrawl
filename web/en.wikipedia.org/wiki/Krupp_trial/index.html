Krupp trial

The Krupp trial was the tenth of twelve trials for war crimes held by United States authorities at Nuremberg after the end of World War II. Formally known as The United States of America vs. Alfried Krupp, et al., the case was heard from 8 December 1947 to 31 July 1948 and concerned the owners and managers of the Krupp industrial conglomerate, one of Germany's largest armaments manufacturers.

Trial details Case: United States v. Alfried Krupp et al.
Court: Military Tribunal III, Nuremberg
Dates: 8 December 1947 – 31 July 1948
Defendants: 12
Outcome: 11 convicted, 1 acquitted

Background

The Krupp works, centred in Essen, had been a principal supplier of artillery, armour plate, U-boats and munitions to the German war effort. The prosecution charged the directors with crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity and participation in a common plan or conspiracy, focusing on the plunder of occupied countries and the use of forced labour, including prisoners of war and concentration camp inmates.

Defendants and verdicts

Alfried Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach, the firm's proprietor, received the harshest sentence: twelve years' imprisonment and the forfeiture of all his property. Most other defendants received prison terms ranging from a few years to twelve years; one was acquitted. The sentences were later reviewed, and in 1951 U.S. High Commissioner John J. McCloy commuted most of the terms, while the confiscation of Alfried Krupp's property was reversed, restoring much of the family fortune.

Significance

The trial is considered a landmark in establishing the criminal responsibility of industrialists for the exploitation of slave labour and for waging aggressive war through economic means.
