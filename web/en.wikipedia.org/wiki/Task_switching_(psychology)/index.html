Task switching (psychology)

[Image: The alternating-runs paradigm used in task-switching experiments]

Task switching, or set-shifting, is an executive function that involves the ability to unconsciously shift attention between one task and another. In experimental psychology, the term also refers to a paradigm used to study this ability, in which participants alternate between two or more simple tasks, such as classifying digits as odd or even and classifying letters as vowels or consonants.

Switch costs

The central finding of the paradigm is the switch cost: participants are slower and more error-prone on trials where the task changes ("switch trials") than on trials where it repeats ("repeat trials"), even when they have ample time to prepare. A portion of the cost, the residual switch cost, persists despite preparation and is attributed to the time needed to reconfigure the task set and to interference from the previously relevant rules.

Paradigms

Common designs include the alternating-runs paradigm, in which tasks switch predictably every few trials, and the cued task-switching paradigm, in which an explicit cue on each trial indicates which task to perform. Arthur Jersild's early work in 1927 is often credited as the first systematic study of the phenomenon.

Interpretations

Competing accounts explain switch costs through task-set reconfiguration, task-set inertia (perseveration of the previous set), and associative interference between cues and tasks. Task-switching ability declines with age and is impaired in several clinical conditions, making the paradigm a common tool in cognitive ageing and neuropsychology research.
