Chorale cantata

A chorale cantata (German: Choralkantate) is a church cantata based on a chorale — a Lutheran hymn — in which both the words and the melody of the hymn form the basis of the work, in whole or in part. The genre flourished in Baroque Germany, above all in the output of Johann Sebastian Bach.

Structure

In the classic form, the opening movement is a large chorale fantasia in which the hymn tune is sung in long notes by one voice part against an independent orchestral texture, and the closing movement is a simple four-part harmonization of the chorale for the whole choir. Inner movements — arias, recitatives, and duets — paraphrase or comment on the hymn's stanzas.

Bach's chorale cantata cycle

In Leipzig in 1724–25, Bach composed a complete annual cycle of chorale cantatas, one for each Sunday and feast day, beginning with O Ewigkeit, du Donnerwort, BWV 20. Roughly forty of these works survive intact, and the cycle is considered one of the largest artistic undertakings of the Baroque era.

Earlier and later examples

Composers such as Dieterich Buxtehude and Johann Pachelbel wrote chorale-based cantatas before Bach, and the form was revived in the 19th century by Felix Mendelssohn, whose Lobgesang and several smaller cantatas draw on chorale material.
