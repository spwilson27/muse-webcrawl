Coulomb collision

[Image placeholder: Rutherford scattering trajectory]

A Coulomb collision is a binary elastic collision between two charged particles interacting through their own electric field. Because the Coulomb force is long-ranged, a charged particle in a plasma is simultaneously influenced by many distant particles; nevertheless, the cumulative effect of many small-angle deflections dominates over rare large-angle collisions, and the binary-collision picture with a cutoff remains the standard starting point for plasma transport theory.

For a single encounter, the deflection is described by the Rutherford scattering formula, in which the impact parameter determines the scattering angle. In a plasma, interactions are shielded beyond the Debye length, which provides a maximum effective impact parameter, while quantum diffraction or close-encounter distances provide a minimum. The ratio of these two cutoffs appears in the Coulomb logarithm, usually written ln Λ, which for many plasmas of interest has a value between about 10 and 20 and sets the effective size of collision frequencies for momentum and energy exchange.

Coulomb collisions govern resistivity, thermal conduction, temperature equilibration between species, and the slowing down of fast ions in plasmas, including fusion plasmas in tokamaks and stellar interiors.
