Commentariolum Petitionis

The Commentariolum Petitionis ("Little Handbook on Electioneering"), also known in English as the Handbook for Elections, is a Latin epistle traditionally attributed to Quintus Tullius Cicero, written around 65–64 BC as advice to his elder brother Marcus Tullius Cicero on running a successful campaign for the consulship of the Roman Republic. Marcus won the election for 63 BC. The work is frequently described as one of the earliest surviving manuals of practical politics and campaign strategy.

The handbook purports to advise Cicero's campaign for the consulship of 63 BC.

Content

The handbook divides the electorate and the candidate's coalition into friends, dependents, clients, freedmen, and the various orders and tribes, and advises Cicero to secure each group methodically. Among its famous recommendations: make promises freely (a promise is remembered longer than a refusal disappoints); remind voters of the corruption and scandals of his opponents, Catiline and Antonius; court the equestrian order and the "best men" of the municipalities; ensure a large and conspicuous retinue accompanies the candidate in the Forum each morning (deductores); cultivate popularity among the urban plebs through games, generosity, and accessibility; and be all things to all men — agreeable, attentive, and seen everywhere.

Authorship debate

The authenticity of the Commentariolum has been debated since the Renaissance and remains unsettled. Some scholars regard it as a genuine work of Quintus Cicero; others consider it a later rhetorical exercise from the early Roman Empire, composed as if advising Cicero. Arguments turn on style, anachronisms, and the suspicious neatness with which the "prophecies" in the text match Cicero's actual campaign and later career. Modern opinion is divided, though many scholars accept it as substantially authentic or at least as an ancient and valuable source for Roman electoral practice.

Significance

Whatever its authorship, the text is a uniquely candid window on Roman elections — on clientela, bribery (ambitus), public image, and the mechanics of canvassing — and has enjoyed a modern afterlife as a primer on political campaigning, frequently quoted by political strategists as evidence that the essentials of electioneering have changed little in two thousand years.
