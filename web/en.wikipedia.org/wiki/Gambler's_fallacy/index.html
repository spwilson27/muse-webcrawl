Gambler's fallacy

The gambler's fallacy, also known as the Monte Carlo fallacy or the fallacy of the maturity of chances, is the incorrect belief that, if a particular event occurs more frequently than normal during the past, it is less likely to happen in the future (or vice versa), when it has otherwise been established that the probability of such events does not depend on what has happened in the past. These arguments are often followed by a specific numerical fact about the probability of the event in question, which, although correct, is incorrectly ascribed to the prior history of the event. Such events, having the quality of being historically and causally independent, are known as statistically independent.

The fallacy is commonly associated with gambling, where it may be believed, for example, that the next dice roll is more likely to be a six because there have recently been fewer sixes than usual.

Monte Carlo Casino

Perhaps the most famous example of the gambler's fallacy occurred in a game of roulette at the Monte Carlo Casino on August 18, 1913, when the ball fell in black 26 times in a row. This was an extremely uncommon occurrence: the probability of a sequence of either red or black occurring 26 times in a row is (1/2)26 (here incorrectly assuming independent events with 50% chance for simplicity — in fact the odds were much more minute), roughly 1 in 66.7 million. Gamblers lost millions of francs betting against black, reasoning incorrectly that the streak was causing an imbalance in the randomness of the wheel, and that it would be followed by a long streak of red.

Coin flipping

The gambler's fallacy can be illustrated by considering the repeated toss of a fair coin. The outcomes in different tosses are statistically independent and the probability of getting heads on a single toss is 1/2 (one in two). The probability of getting two heads in two tosses is 1/4 (one in four) and the probability of getting three heads in three tosses is 1/8 (one in eight). In general, if Ai is the event that toss i of a fair coin comes up heads, then the probability that all of a sequence of n tosses come up heads is (1/2)n.

Reverse fallacy

The reverse gambler's fallacy, also known as the inverse gambler's fallacy, is the incorrect belief that a particular event has occurred less frequently than normal in the past, so it is more likely to happen in the future.
