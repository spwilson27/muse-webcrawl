Cathodic protection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cathodic protection (CP) is a technique used to control the corrosion of a metal surface by making it the cathode of an electrochemical cell. It is widely applied to protect buried or submerged pipelines, ship hulls, offshore platforms, storage tanks, reinforcing steel in concrete and other structures.

History

The principle was first described by Sir Humphry Davy in papers presented to the Royal Society in 1824, describing the use of zinc and iron blocks to protect the copper sheathing of Royal Navy ships. The method fell out of favour because the protected copper no longer poisoned marine fouling, but it was revived in the 20th century for pipelines, becoming standard practice in the oil and gas industry from the 1930s onwards.

Methods

In galvanic (sacrificial anode) protection, a more electrochemically active metal — typically zinc, magnesium or aluminium alloys — is electrically connected to the structure and corrodes preferentially, supplying protective current. In impressed current cathodic protection (ICCP), an external DC power source (rectifier) drives current through relatively inert anodes (such as mixed-metal-oxide coated titanium) to the structure. ICCP suits large structures and allows output adjustment, but requires power and monitoring.

Monitoring and criteria

Protection is verified using reference electrodes (e.g. copper–copper sulfate for buried structures, silver–silver chloride in seawater); a common criterion for steel in soil is a potential of −0.85 V or more negative relative to a Cu/CuSO4 electrode. Standards are maintained by bodies such as AMPP (formerly NACE International) and ISO.
