Japanese archipelago

The Japanese archipelago (Japanese: 日本列島, Nihon Rettō) is a long, curved chain of islands in the western Pacific Ocean, off the eastern coast of Asia, which constitutes the territory of Japan. It stretches roughly 3,000 kilometres from the Sea of Okhotsk in the north to the East China Sea in the south-west, lying at the junction of several tectonic plates — a position that gives Japan its mountainous terrain, frequent earthquakes, volcanoes and hot springs.

Geography

The four main islands are, from north to south, Hokkaido, Honshu (the largest, containing Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka), Shikoku and Kyushu. South-west of Kyushu runs the Ryukyu island chain toward Taiwan, including Okinawa; north-east of Honshu the Kuril Islands extend toward Kamchatka (the northernmost Kurils are administered by Russia but claimed in part by Japan). The mountainous spine of the archipelago — including the Japanese Alps of central Honshu — leaves only about a fifth of the land suitable for settlement and farming, concentrating population in coastal plains such as the Kantō Plain. Short, fast rivers, dense forests (about two-thirds of the land is wooded) and a long, indented coastline complete the picture.

Geology

The archipelago sits on the "Ring of Fire" where the Pacific and Philippine Sea plates subduct beneath the Eurasian and Okhotsk plates. The result is intense seismic and volcanic activity: Japan experiences a large share of the world's major earthquakes and counts over 100 active volcanoes. The same tectonics create thousands of onsen hot springs and rich geothermal resources. In 2023, a digital recount by the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan revised the official number of islands from 6,852 to 14,125.

Climate and nature

Running from subarctic Hokkaido to subtropical Okinawa, the archipelago spans several climate zones, with a pronounced monsoon and typhoon season. Its isolation and varied terrain have produced high biodiversity and many endemic species, from the Japanese macaque and red-crowned crane to the giant salamander, as well as celebrated cherry-blossom and autumn-colour seasons that structure the national calendar.
