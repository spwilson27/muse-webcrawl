Political spectrum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A political spectrum is a system to characterise and classify different political positions in relation to one another. These positions are mapped along one or more geometric axes representing independent political dimensions. The most common axis is the left–right political spectrum, which originated during the French Revolution from the seating arrangements in the National Assembly — supporters of the king and old order sat to the president's right, supporters of the revolution to the left.

The terms have since acquired loose and shifting meanings: in the twentieth century “left” generally denoted egalitarianism, labour rights and social change, and “right” hierarchy, tradition, property and national identity, with rival economic schemes — socialism versus capitalism — at the centre of the division. Because a single axis compresses many distinct issues, scholars have proposed multi-dimensional models, such as the Nolan Chart and the Political Compass model, separating economic from social or authoritarian–libertarian dimensions.

Positions

Standard placements run far left, left, centre-left, centre, centre-right, right and far right. Parties' positions vary by country and era, and some movements — populist, green and regionalist parties among them — fit awkwardly: environmental politics is often described as “neither left nor right but forward”. Analysts caution that voter attitudes are not consistently ordered on any single dimension, though the spectrum remains the default vocabulary of journalism and political competition.
