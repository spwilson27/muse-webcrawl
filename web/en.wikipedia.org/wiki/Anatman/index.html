Anatman

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

Anātman (Sanskrit: अनात्मन्; Pali: anattā) is a central doctrine of Buddhism teaching that there is no permanent, unchanging self, soul or essence in any phenomenon. Literally "no-self" or "no-soul", it is one of the three marks of existence (tilakkhaṇa), along with impermanence (anicca) and suffering (dukkha).

Doctrine

According to the teaching, what is ordinarily called a "person" is a temporary combination of five aggregates (skandhas): form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness. Each aggregate is impermanent and dependent on conditions, and none of them, separately or together, constitutes a self. The Buddha's discourse on the subject, the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, argues that since one cannot control the aggregates, they cannot be "mine" or "myself".

Contrast with Hindu thought

The doctrine directly opposes the Upanishadic concept of ātman, an eternal self identical with ultimate reality (Brahman). Buddhism holds that clinging to the notion of self is a root cause of suffering, and that insight into anātman is necessary for liberation (nirvāṇa). The teaching does not deny the conventional existence of persons, but analyses them as dependently arisen processes.

Interpretations

Different Buddhist schools elaborate the doctrine differently. The Pudgalavāda schools posited a "person" neither identical to nor different from the aggregates, a view rejected by other schools. In Mahāyāna philosophy, Nāgārjuna extended the emptiness of self to all phenomena (śūnyatā). The related Hindu concept is expressed differently — in Advaita Vedānta the self is ultimately real — making anātman one of the clearest doctrinal divides between Buddhism and Hinduism.
