Consensus decision-making

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Consensus decision-making or consensus process (often abbreviated to consensus) is a group decision-making process in which participants develop and decide on proposals with the aim, or requirement, of acceptance by all. It is not simply unanimous voting: rather than merely counting votes, the group works to find solutions acceptable to everyone by addressing concerns and amending proposals.

The process

A typical consensus process involves: identifying the issue; clarifying needs and concerns; developing proposals; amending them in light of objections; and testing for consensus. Participants who cannot support a proposal may "stand aside" (register disagreement without blocking) or, in a principled last resort, "block" — a veto generally reserved for cases where a proposal violates the group's core purpose. Many groups use a trained facilitator, hand signals, and "stack" (speaking queues) to keep discussion orderly and inclusive.

Some versions, such as the "consensus minus one" used by some bodies, or the graded scale used in Occupy-era assemblies and by the IETF's "rough consensus", soften the unanimity requirement while retaining the obligation to hear and address dissent.

Use and history

Consensus has ancient roots, including the practices of many indigenous societies and the deliberations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy. The Religious Society of Friends (Quakers) have made decisions by seeking "the sense of the meeting" since the 17th century, an influential model. In the 20th century consensus was adopted by feminist organisations, the anti-nuclear movement, and activist networks — the 1999 Seattle WTO protests and the 2011 Occupy movement popularised its hand signals and general assemblies. It is also used in Japanese business practice (nemawashi and the ringi system), co-operative enterprises, and some software and standards communities.

Advantages and criticisms

Supporters argue that consensus produces decisions that are more durable, creative, and legitimate, and that it protects minorities and builds community. Critics counter that it is slow, exhausting, vulnerable to blocking minorities and informal hierarchies (Jo Freeman's "tyranny of structurelessness"), and ill-suited to large or urgent decisions. Hybrid systems often reserve consensus for fundamental questions and use voting for routine business.
