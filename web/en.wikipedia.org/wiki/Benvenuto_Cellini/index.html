Benvenuto Cellini (3 November 1500 – 13 February 1571) was an Italian goldsmith, sculptor, draftsman, soldier, musician and author, and one of the most colourful figures of the Italian Renaissance. His bronze Perseus with the Head of Medusa in Florence and the gold Salt Cellar made for Francis I of France are masterpieces of Mannerist art, while his posthumously published Autobiography is a classic of Renaissance literature — a vivid, boastful record of artistic ambition, violence, imprisonment and escape.

Life

Cellini was born in Florence, son of a musician and maker of musical instruments who wanted him to become a flautist; instead he was apprenticed to a goldsmith. Exiled from Florence for brawling, he worked in Siena, Bologna and Pisa before settling in Rome, where he made jewellery and plate for Pope Clement VII. During the Sack of Rome in 1527 he fought in the defence of the Castel Sant'Angelo and later claimed to have shot the Constable de Bourbon and wounded the Prince of Orange.

Imprisoned in the Castel Sant'Angelo on charges of theft and murder, Cellini escaped, was recaptured, and was eventually pardoned. He later worked at the court of Francis I at Fontainebleau (1540–1545), where he produced the famous gold and enamel salt cellar — the Saliera — now in the Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna, stolen in 2003 and recovered in 2006. Returning to Florence under Cosimo I de' Medici, he cast the Perseus (1545–1554), a technical triumph of bronze casting that still stands in the Loggia dei Lanzi.

The Autobiography

Cellini dictated his life story, the Vita, between 1558 and 1566. It lay unpublished until 1728 and became famous in translations, notably Goethe's German version. Its frank account of workshop practice, duels, murders, love affairs and negotiations with popes and kings provides an unmatched picture of Renaissance artistic life, though historians treat its self-serving claims with caution.

Works

Surviving works include the Perseus, the Salt Cellar, the bronze bust of Cosimo I, the marble crucifix now in El Escorial, and the Nymph of Fontainebleau relief in the Louvre. Cellini also wrote treatises on goldsmithing and sculpture, published in 1568.
