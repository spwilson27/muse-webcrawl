Stereophonic sound

Stereophonic sound, commonly called stereo, is a method of sound reproduction that recreates a multi-directional, three-dimensional audible perspective. This is usually achieved by using two independent audio channels through a configuration of two loudspeakers (or stereo headphones) in such a way as to create the impression of sound heard from various directions, as in natural hearing.

History

The principles of stereophony were explored in the 1880s, when Clément Ader demonstrated two-channel audio transmission of opera performances in Paris using pairs of telephone lines. Modern stereo theory was developed in the early 1930s by Alan Blumlein at EMI in Britain, whose 1931 patent covered coincident microphone techniques, stereo disc cutting, and loudspeaker arrangements. In the United States, Harvey Fletcher and engineers at Bell Laboratories conducted parallel experiments, including a famous 1933 transmission of the Philadelphia Orchestra in three channels.

Commercial stereo arrived in the 1950s. Magnetic tape allowed multi-track recording, and in 1957 Westrex introduced the 45/45 stereo disc system, which became the industry standard for vinyl records. Stereo FM broadcasting began in 1961, and by the late 1960s stereo had largely displaced monophonic recording in popular music.

Principles

Stereo reproduction relies chiefly on two psychoacoustic cues: interaural level differences (differences in loudness between the ears) and interaural time differences (differences in arrival time). By varying the relative amplitude of a signal in the left and right channels ("panning"), a producer can position a phantom image anywhere between the two speakers. Recording techniques include coincident pairs (such as the Blumlein pair and X/Y configurations), spaced pairs (A/B), and near-coincident pairs (ORTF), each trading off imaging precision against spaciousness.

Balance and compatibility

A stereo mix must remain compatible with mono playback: when the two channels are summed, signals that are out of phase can partially cancel, producing a hollow or weakened sound. Engineers therefore monitor mono fold-down during production. The balance control on consumer equipment adjusts the relative level of the channels, while the "centre image" depends on the listener sitting near the apex of an equilateral triangle formed with the speakers — the so-called sweet spot.

Later developments

Stereo formed the basis for later multichannel formats, including quadraphonic sound in the 1970s and the 5.1 and 7.1 surround-sound systems used in cinema and home theatre. Binaural recording, intended specifically for headphone playback, uses a dummy head to capture interaural cues directly. Despite these developments, two-channel stereo remains the dominant format for music distribution.
