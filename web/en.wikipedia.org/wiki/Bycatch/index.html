Bycatch

Bycatch (or by-catch), in the fishing industry, is a fish or other marine species that is caught unintentionally while catching certain target species and target sizes of fish, crabs etc. Bycatch is either the wrong species, the wrong sex, undersized, or juveniles of the target species, or unwanted species for legal or market reasons. The term is also used for untargeted catch in other forms of animal harvesting or collecting. Species caught as bycatch are often of lower value and are frequently discarded at sea, dead or dying.

Examples

Shrimp trawling

Shrimp trawl fisheries historically produced some of the highest bycatch ratios of any fishery, with several kilograms of bycatch discarded for each kilogram of shrimp landed. Bycatch reduction devices (BRDs) and turtle excluder devices (TEDs) are now mandatory in many jurisdictions.

Dolphins and tuna

In the eastern tropical Pacific, dolphins are frequently found above schools of yellowfin tuna and were killed in large numbers in purse-seine nets set on them; hundreds of thousands died annually before the adoption of the "backdown" procedure and dolphin-safe labelling schemes reduced mortality dramatically.

Seabirds and cetaceans

Longline fisheries kill large numbers of albatrosses and petrels that swallow baited hooks during setting; mitigation measures include bird-scaring (tori) lines, weighted lines, and night setting. Gillnets entangle porpoises, dolphins, and sea turtles worldwide.

Mitigation

Bycatch mitigation includes gear modification (escape panels, excluder devices, pingers that warn cetaceans), time-area closures, changes in fishing practice, improved selectivity, and management measures such as bycatch quotas and observer programmes. Consumers' guides and certification schemes (such as "dolphin-safe" tuna and the Marine Stewardship Council) attempt to use market pressure to reduce bycatch.

Utilisation

In some fisheries, retained bycatch contributes to food security and income, and efforts have been made to land and use bycatch rather than discarding it, though conservation concerns favour reducing capture in the first place.
