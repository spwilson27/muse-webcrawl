Sturmian word

In mathematics, a Sturmian word (or Sturmian sequence), named after Jacques Charles François Sturm, is an infinite sequence of symbols drawn from a binary alphabet which has the lowest possible complexity among all aperiodic sequences: its complexity function satisfies p(n) = n + 1 for all n. Equivalently, a Sturmian word contains exactly n + 1 distinct subwords (factors) of each length n.

Definition and examples

Sturmian words can be constructed geometrically by intersecting a line of irrational slope with a square lattice: recording which grid lines the line crosses produces a Sturmian sequence known as a characteristic or cutting sequence. The most famous example is the Fibonacci word, generated by the substitution rules S0 = 0, S1 = 01, Sn = Sn−1Sn−2, whose limiting slope is related to the golden ratio.

Properties

Every Sturmian word is uniformly recurrent and aperiodic, and is balanced: any two factors of the same length differ in the number of occurrences of each letter by at most one. Sturmian words are closely connected with irrational rotations of the circle, Beatty sequences, and continued fractions, and they arise in combinatorics on words, symbolic dynamics, and the theory of quasicrystals.
