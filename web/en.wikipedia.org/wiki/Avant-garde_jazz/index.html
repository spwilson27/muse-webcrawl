Avant-garde jazz

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Avant-garde jazz (also known as avant-jazz or experimental jazz) is a style of music and improvisation that combines avant-garde art music and composition with jazz. It originated in the late 1950s and developed through the 1960s. Originally a synonym for "free jazz", the term came to imply an approach that differs from free jazz in that it has more structure and organisation, although it remains highly improvisational.

Avant-garde jazz emerged from New York and Chicago scenes of the 1960s.

History

Pioneers included Ornette Coleman, whose 1960 album Free Jazz named the related movement; Cecil Taylor; John Coltrane, whose later work (notably Ascension, 1965) pushed toward collective free improvisation; Sun Ra and his Arkestra; Albert Ayler; and Eric Dolphy. In Chicago the AACM (Association for the Advancement of Creative Musicians), founded in 1965, nurtured the Art Ensemble of Chicago and Anthony Braxton, who blended jazz with contemporary classical composition.

Characteristics

Avant-garde jazz may dispense with regular meter, fixed chord changes and swing rhythm, use extended instrumental techniques and unusual timbres, and incorporate elements of European modernism, world music and collective improvisation. It influenced later creative music, including free improvisation in Europe and elements of rock and contemporary composition.
