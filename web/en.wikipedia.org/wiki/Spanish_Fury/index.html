Spanish Fury

The Spanish Fury (in Dutch: Spaanse Furie) was a mutinous sack and massacre by unpaid Spanish troops of the Army of Flanders in the city of Antwerp in November 1576, during the Eighty Years' War. Over three days beginning on 4 November 1576, the mutineers looted and burned much of the richest city in northern Europe and killed thousands of its inhabitants — estimates range from about 7,000 to 18,000 deaths. The atrocity became a central episode of the "Black Legend" of Spanish cruelty and a turning point in the Dutch Revolt.

Background

Spain's finances collapsed in 1575, when Philip II suspended payments, and the tercios stationed in the Low Countries went unpaid for months and years. After the death of Governor Luis de Requesens in March 1576 left Spanish authority in confusion, mutinous soldiers seized the town of Aalst and marched on Antwerp, joined by other mutineers and German troops. The States-General had already declared the Spanish troops enemies of the country.

The sack

On 4 November the mutineers, perhaps 6,000 strong, stormed Antwerp despite resistance by a larger force of citizens, Walloon and German defenders. The city hall was burned, hundreds of houses were destroyed by fire, and citizens were murdered, tortured and held to ransom as soldiers looted gold, silver, jewellery and merchandise valued in the millions of florins. The killing continued for about three days, and the economic blow began Antwerp's long decline as Amsterdam rose in its place.

Consequences

News of the fury horrified Europe and united the seventeen provinces of the Low Countries — Catholic south and Calvinist north alike — against Spain. Within days the States-General concluded the Pacification of Ghent (8 November 1576), demanding the withdrawal of Spanish troops. The event entered Protestant propaganda as emblematic of Spanish tyranny and remains one of the most infamous atrocities of the Dutch Revolt.
