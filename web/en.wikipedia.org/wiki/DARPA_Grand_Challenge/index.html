DARPA Grand Challenge

The DARPA Grand Challenge was a prize competition for American autonomous vehicles, funded by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), the research arm of the United States Department of Defense. Congress had mandated that by 2015 one-third of US military ground combat vehicles should be uncrewed, and the competition was created to spur development of the underlying technology.

2004 competition

The first competition, held on 13 March 2004 in the Mojave Desert along a roughly 150-mile (240 km) route from Barstow, California, to Primm, Nevada, offered a $1 million prize. None of the 15 vehicles completed the course; the best performer, Carnegie Mellon University's Sandstorm, travelled only about 7.5 miles (12 km) before becoming stuck. No prize was awarded.

2005 competition

The second Grand Challenge, on 8 October 2005, doubled the prize to $2 million and required vehicles to traverse a 131.6-mile (212 km) desert course in under ten hours. Five vehicles finished, four within the time limit. The winner was "Stanley", built by the Stanford Racing Team led by Sebastian Thrun, which completed the course in 6 hours, 53 minutes, and 58 seconds. The runners-up were two vehicles from Carnegie Mellon's Red Team. The event is widely credited with kick-starting the modern self-driving car industry; many participants later joined autonomous-vehicle programmes at Google and elsewhere.

2007 Urban Challenge

The third competition, the Urban Challenge, was held on 3 November 2007 at the former George Air Force Base in Victorville, California. Vehicles had to complete a 60-mile (96 km) urban course in under six hours, obeying traffic rules and interacting safely with both robot and human-driven traffic. The $2 million first prize was won by "Boss", developed by Carnegie Mellon's Tartan Racing team; Stanford's "Junior" took second place and Virginia Tech's "Odin" third.

Legacy

The challenges demonstrated that autonomous ground navigation over long distances was feasible and created a community of researchers and engineers who went on to found or staff many of today's autonomous vehicle companies.
