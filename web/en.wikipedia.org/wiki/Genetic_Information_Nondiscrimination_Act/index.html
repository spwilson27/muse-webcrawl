The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) is an Act of Congress of the United States designed to prohibit some types of genetic discrimination. The act prohibits the use of genetic information in health insurance and employment: it prohibits group health plans and health insurers from denying coverage to a healthy individual or charging that person higher premiums based solely on a genetic predisposition to developing a disease in the future. The legislation also bars employers from using individuals' genetic information when making hiring, firing, job placement, or promotion decisions.

Background

The legislation had been the subject of debate in Congress for fourteen years before its passage. Its supporters argued that since the dawn of the genomic era, fear of misuse of genetic information had discouraged people from undergoing genetic testing that could benefit their health. The bill was passed in the Senate by a vote of 95–0 and in the House of Representatives by 414–1, and was signed into law by President George W. Bush on May 21, 2008.

Provisions

Title I of GINA addresses the use of genetic information in health insurance, amending the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA), the Public Health Service Act, and the Internal Revenue Code. Title II addresses employment, prohibiting employers with fifteen or more employees, employment agencies, labor organizations, and joint labor-management training programs from discriminating on the basis of genetic information.

Limitations

GINA does not apply to life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance, and it does not cover members of the United States military, veterans obtaining health care through the Department of Veterans Affairs, or the Indian Health Service. It also does not mandate coverage for any particular treatment or benefit. Critics have argued these gaps leave important areas of potential discrimination unaddressed.
