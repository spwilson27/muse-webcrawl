Sukuk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sukuk (Arabic: صكوك, plural of صك, sakk, "legal instrument, deed, cheque") are financial certificates, similar in function to bonds, that comply with Islamic law (Sharia). Because conventional bonds pay interest (riba), which Islamic law prohibits, sukuk are structured so that investors instead hold an undivided ownership interest in a tangible asset, project or business, and receive returns generated by that asset rather than interest payments.

Structure

In a typical issue, an originator sells assets to a special-purpose vehicle, which issues sukuk certificates to investors and uses the income generated by the assets to make periodic payments. At maturity, the assets are usually repurchased by the originator, allowing investors to recover their principal. Common structures are named after the underlying Islamic contracts, including ijara (lease), murabaha (cost-plus sale), musharaka (partnership) and wakala (agency).

Market

The modern sukuk market developed from the 1990s, with Malaysia and the Gulf states — including Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar and Indonesia — becoming the largest issuers, both sovereign and corporate. Sukuk have also been issued by non-Muslim-majority governments, including the United Kingdom, which issued a £200 million sovereign sukuk in 2014. The market is overseen by standard-setting bodies such as the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI).

Criticism and debate

Some scholars have questioned whether certain sukuk structures replicate the economics of conventional interest-bearing debt so closely that their Sharia compliance is merely formal. A 2007 critique by Sheikh Muhammad Taqi Usmani, who argued that many sukuk guaranteed repurchase at face value in ways inconsistent with genuine risk-sharing, caused a noticeable contraction and restructuring of issuance.
