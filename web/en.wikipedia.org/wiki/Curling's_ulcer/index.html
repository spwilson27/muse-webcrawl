Curling's ulcer

Acute stress ulceration of the duodenum following major burns.

SpecialtyGastroenterology; burn care
SiteDuodenum (proximal)
CauseSevere thermal injury (burns)
Named afterThomas Blizard Curling

A Curling's ulcer is an acute peptic ulcer of the duodenum that develops as a complication of severe burns. It is named for the English surgeon Thomas Blizard Curling, who described duodenal ulceration in burn patients in 1842.

Pathophysiology

Major burns cause hypovolemia and intense stress responses that reduce blood flow to the gastrointestinal mucosa, weakening its protective barrier. In the duodenum, the resulting ischemia, combined with acid and bile exposure, produces single or multiple acute ulcers, typically in the first portion of the duodenum. Unlike Cushing's ulcers (associated with raised intracranial pressure and hypersecretion of acid), Curling's ulcers are primarily a mucosal ischemia phenomenon.

Clinical features and management

Patients may present with painless upper gastrointestinal bleeding, melena, hematemesis or, in severe cases, perforation. Historically the complication carried substantial mortality. Modern burn care — early fluid resuscitation, enteral feeding and prophylactic acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers — has greatly reduced its incidence. Established ulcers are managed with acid suppression, endoscopic therapy and, rarely, surgery.
