The Stones of Venice (book)

[Hero image placeholder: the Doge's Palace in Venice, the centrepiece of Ruskin's study.]

The Stones of Venice is a three-volume treatise on Venetian art and architecture by the English art historian John Ruskin, published from 1851 to 1853. It is one of the most influential works of architectural criticism of the nineteenth century.

Content

Ruskin examines Venetian architecture from its Byzantine origins through its Gothic flowering to its Renaissance decline, treating buildings as moral documents of the societies that produced them. The second volume contains the celebrated chapter "The Nature of Gothic", in which Ruskin argues that Gothic architecture expresses the freedom and dignity of the medieval craftsman, in contrast to the soulless perfection of industrial manufacture. The chapter was later reprinted by William Morris and became a foundational text of the Arts and Crafts movement.

Influence

The work helped spur the Gothic Revival in Britain and shaped attitudes toward architectural conservation. It also influenced Marcel Proust, who translated Ruskin into French.
