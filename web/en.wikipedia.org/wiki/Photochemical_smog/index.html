Photochemical smog


Photochemical smog (also called Los Angeles-type smog) is air pollution formed when sunlight acts on nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds to produce ozone and other oxidants.

Photochemical smog is a type of air pollution derived from the photochemical reaction of sunlight with nitrogen oxides (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) emitted mainly by motor vehicles and industry. The reactions produce a characteristic brownish haze containing ground-level ozone, peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN), aldehydes and fine particles.

Formation

In sunlight, nitrogen dioxide is photolysed to nitric oxide and an oxygen atom, which combines with O2 to form ozone. Hydrocarbon radicals interfere with the normal cycle that would otherwise destroy the ozone, allowing ozone and other oxidants to accumulate, especially under warm, stagnant conditions and temperature inversions. The chemistry was elucidated in the early 1950s by Arie Haagen-Smit, who showed that Los Angeles smog — first recognised in 1943 — came from automobile exhaust rather than industrial smoke.

Effects and control

Ozone in photochemical smog irritates eyes and airways, aggravates asthma, damages crops and forests, and degrades rubber and materials. Control measures include catalytic converters, vapour recovery, reformulated fuels and emissions standards under laws such as the U.S. Clean Air Act, which greatly reduced smog severity in Los Angeles and other cities, though ozone exceedances persist worldwide.
