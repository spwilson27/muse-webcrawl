Colombian conflict

The Colombian conflict (Spanish: Conflicto armado interno de Colombia) is an ongoing low-intensity asymmetric war between the government of Colombia, far-right paramilitary groups, crime syndicates, and far-left guerrilla groups such as the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC) and the National Liberation Army (ELN). It is generally dated from the mid-1960s, with roots in the partisan violence of La Violencia (1948–1958) and longstanding disputes over land and political exclusion.

Background and course

The FARC emerged in 1964–1966 from peasant self-defence groups after the army assault on the Marquetalia enclave; the ELN was founded in 1964, and other guerrilla movements such as the M-19 followed. From the 1980s the conflict became entangled with the cocaine trade, which financed guerrillas, paramilitaries, and cartels alike. Right-wing paramilitary groups, consolidated from 1997 in the United Self-Defense Forces of Colombia (AUC), committed extensive massacres and were formally demobilised in the mid-2000s, though successor armed groups persisted. The conflict displaced millions of Colombians and caused, by official estimates, more than 260,000 deaths, the great majority of them civilians.

Peace processes

After earlier failed negotiations, the government of President Juan Manuel Santos reached a peace agreement with the FARC in 2016, following talks in Havana; the accord led to the FARC's demobilisation and transformation into a political party, and Santos received the Nobel Peace Prize. A narrow plebiscite initially rejected the deal, and a revised agreement was ratified by Congress. Implementation has been uneven; FARC dissident factions, the ELN, and drug-trafficking organisations such as the Clan del Golfo continue armed violence in parts of the country, and later governments have pursued further negotiations under a policy of "Total Peace".
