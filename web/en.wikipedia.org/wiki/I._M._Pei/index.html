I. M. Pei

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Louvre Pyramid at night, glass and light geometry rising from the Cour Napoléon fountains]

Ieoh Ming Pei (26 April 1917 – 16 May 2019), commonly known as I. M. Pei, was a Chinese-American architect, regarded as a master of modern architecture and one of the most successful architects of the 20th century. His work is characterised by geometric clarity, monumental forms and the marriage of modernism to site and history: the Louvre Pyramid in Paris (1989), the Bank of China Tower in Hong Kong (1989), the East Building of the National Gallery of Art in Washington (1978), the John F. Kennedy Library in Boston (1979), the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in Cleveland (1995) and the Museum of Islamic Art in Doha (2008).

Born in Guangzhou to a Suzhou banking family, Pei emigrated to the United States in 1935, studied at MIT and Harvard (under Gropius and Breuer), worked for developer William Zeckendorf, and founded his own firm in 1955. He won the Pritzker Prize in 1983 — using the money to endow a scholarship for Chinese architects to study in America.

The Pyramid

Mitterrand's direct commission of Pei for the Louvre's modernisation (1983, without competition) and the glass pyramid proposed for the Cour Napoléon provoked years of Parisian outrage — the 'dead pharaoh's greenhouse' — before the 1989 opening converted the critics: the pyramid became the museum's beloved symbol and the era's proof that audacity and reverence can be the same gesture.

Longevity

Pei designed into his nineties (the Suzhou Museum near his ancestral home, 2006; Doha at 91), advised his sons' firm Pei Cobb Freed & Partners' successors, and died at 102 in 2019 — having spanned architecture from the Bauhaus diaspora to the supertall age, his triangles and circles unchanged in discipline. His buildings' reputation for dignity has aged better than almost any modernism.
