Central Alaskan Yup'ik (Yup'ik: Yugtun) is a Yupik language of the Eskimo–Aleut family, spoken by the Yup'ik people of southwestern Alaska, principally in the Yukon–Kuskokwim delta and along the Kuskokwim River and Bristol Bay. With more speakers than any other Alaska Native language, it remains one of the most widely spoken Indigenous languages in the United States, though transmission to children has declined sharply outside the delta heartland.

Dialects

Major dialect groupings include General Central Yup'ik — encompassing Lower Kuskokwim, Upper Kuskokwim, and coastal varieties — together with the more divergent dialects of Nunivak Island (Cup'ig), Chevak (Cup'ik), Nelson Island, and the Egegik area. Speakers of different dialects can generally understand one another, though Nunivak Cup'ig is notably distinct.

Grammar

Yup'ik is a polysynthetic, ergative language written in a Latin-based orthography developed by linguists at the Alaska Native Language Center with Yup'ik collaborators. Words are built from a base followed by derivational suffixes ("postbases") and an inflectional ending marking case, number, and agreement; a single word can express what English requires a whole sentence to say. Noun cases include absolutive, ergative/relative, locative, ablative, and allative. The language has a three-way number distinction (singular, dual, plural) and four vowel qualities with contrastive length.

Status and revitalisation

Moravian and other missionaries, and later territorial schools, suppressed Yup'ik in favour of English through much of the twentieth century. Bilingual education began in the 1970s, and the language now has a standard orthography, published dictionaries and grammars (notably the work of Steven Jacobson), immersion preschools, and a bachelor's degree programme at the University of Alaska Fairbanks. In 2014 Alaska recognised Yup'ik and other Alaska Native languages as official languages of the state. Despite these efforts, fluent first-language speakers are concentrated among older generations in many villages, and the language is considered threatened.
