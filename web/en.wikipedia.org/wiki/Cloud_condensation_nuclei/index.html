Cloud condensation nuclei

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cloud droplets condensing on tiny aerosol particles (CCN)

Cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) are small particles (typically 0.2 µm, or one hundredth the size of a cloud droplet) about which cloud droplets coalesce as water vapour condenses. Without CCN, pure water vapour would need supersaturations of several hundred percent to form droplets; in the real atmosphere, ubiquitous aerosol particles allow droplets to form at supersaturations near 1%.

Sources

Common CCN include:

  • Sea salt from ocean spray
  • Sulfate particles from volcanoes, fossil-fuel burning, and marine dimethyl sulfide
  • Dust, soot (black carbon), pollen, and bacteria
  • Smoke from wildfires and biomass burning

Climate effects

Human activity has increased CCN concentrations, especially over land and downwind of industrial regions. More CCN produce clouds with more, smaller droplets, which are brighter (the Twomey effect) and may rain less readily (the Albrecht effect), potentially prolonging cloud cover — an overall cooling influence that partly masks greenhouse warming. The balance of these aerosol indirect effects is a major uncertainty in climate science.

Cloud seeding

Cloud seeding deliberately adds particles such as silver iodide to clouds to encourage precipitation, exploiting the same physics of droplet nucleation.
