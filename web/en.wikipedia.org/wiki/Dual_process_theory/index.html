Dual process theory

In psychology, a dual process theory provides an account of how thought can arise in two different ways, or as a result of two different processes. Often, the two processes consist of an implicit (automatic), unconscious process and an explicit (controlled), conscious process. Verbalised explicitly, the older system (Type 1) is often called "intuitive" and the newer one (Type 2) "reflective" or "deliberative".

System 1 and System 2

The terminology of System 1 and System 2 was introduced by Keith Stanovich and popularised by Daniel Kahneman in Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011). Type 1 processing is fast, automatic, frequent, emotional, stereotypic, and largely unconscious; it requires little working memory and operates by association and heuristic rules. Type 2 processing is slow, effortful, infrequent, logical, and calculating; it requires working memory and is associated with the subjective experience of agency, choice, and concentration. Later writers (including Kahneman and Stanovich) prefer "Type 1/Type 2" to emphasise that these are types of processing, not distinct brain systems.

History

Dual-process ideas have roots in William James, and modern versions were developed in reasoning research by Peter Wason and Jonathan Evans, in persuasion research (the elaboration likelihood model of Petty and Cacioppo, and Chaiken's heuristic-systematic model), in social cognition (Fiske, Neuberg), and in memory and learning research distinguishing implicit from explicit systems.

Applications and criticism

Dual process theory has been applied to moral judgment (Joshua Greene's work on trolley problems), decision-making, addiction, clinical reasoning, and marketing. Critics note that the defining features of the two "systems" do not always cluster together, that evidence for strict dichotomies is mixed, and that single-process and continuum accounts can explain many of the same findings; proponents respond that the framework is a useful organising model even if the boundaries are fuzzy.
