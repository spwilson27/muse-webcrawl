Nuclear fuel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nuclear fuel is material used in nuclear power stations to produce heat to power turbines, or in nuclear weapons. Heat is created when nuclear fuel undergoes nuclear fission. Most nuclear fuels contain heavy fissile actinide elements that are capable of undergoing and sustaining nuclear fission: the three most relevant fissile isotopes are uranium-233, uranium-235, and plutonium-239.

Forms of fuel

The most common fuel in commercial power reactors is uranium dioxide (UO2), pressed into ceramic pellets and stacked inside metal cladding tubes (usually a zirconium alloy) to form fuel rods, which are grouped into fuel assemblies. Low-enriched uranium (3–5% U-235) fuels most light-water reactors; natural uranium (0.7% U-235) is used in heavy-water CANDU reactors. Mixed oxide (MOX) fuel blends plutonium dioxide with uranium dioxide. Other forms include metallic fuels for research and fast reactors, TRISO particles embedded in graphite for high-temperature reactors, and molten salts in experimental designs.

The fuel cycle

The nuclear fuel cycle runs from uranium mining and milling, through conversion, enrichment, and fuel fabrication (the "front end"), to use in the reactor, interim storage of spent fuel, and either reprocessing or geological disposal (the "back end"). A typical light-water reactor assembly remains in the core for three to six years, reaching burnups around 50 GW·days per tonne. Spent fuel remains intensely radioactive and thermally hot, requiring cooling in pools for years before dry cask storage or disposal.

Weapons material

Nuclear weapons use highly enriched uranium (typically >90% U-235) or weapons-grade plutonium (mostly Pu-239). The same enrichment technology that produces reactor fuel can, if extended, produce weapons material, which is why enrichment is a central concern of non-proliferation policy and of the International Atomic Energy Agency's safeguards system.
