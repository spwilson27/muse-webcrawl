Overview

Mount Waiʻaleʻale is a shield volcano and the second-highest point on the island of Kauai in the Hawaiian Islands, rising to about 1,569 metres (5,148 ft). Its name literally means "rippling water" or "overflowing water" in Hawaiian. The summit plateau collects an extraordinary amount of rain: long-term averages of roughly 375 inches (9,500 mm) per year, with higher estimates for some decades, make Waiʻaleʻale one of the wettest spots on Earth.

Climate

The summit's extreme rainfall results from several factors: the island lies directly in the path of the moist north-east trade winds; the mountain's steep cliffs force the air upward, cooling it and condensing its moisture; and the broad, saucer-like plateau catches the rain rather than shedding it. The summit is almost perpetually shrouded in cloud. In the record year of 1982 an estimated 683 inches (17,300 mm) of rain fell. Nearby Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in India rival Waiʻaleʻale for the title of the world's wettest place, depending on the period measured.

Geography and ecology

Streams radiating from the plateau have carved deep canyons into the old shield, including Waimea Canyon. The perpetually waterlogged summit bogs, such as the Alakaʻi Swamp, are home to rare plants and to native forest birds including the ʻakikiki and ʻakekeʻe. A famous marker at the summit, repeatedly replaced, records the rainfall statistics for the benefit of the small number of visitors who reach it, usually by helicopter tours that view the "Wall of Tears" waterfalls pouring down its flanks after rain.
