Acyl

In chemistry, an acyl group is a moiety derived by the removal of one or more hydroxyl groups from an oxoacid, including inorganic acids. It contains a double-bonded oxygen atom and an organyl group (R−C=O) or hydrogen in the case of formyl group. In organic chemistry, the acyl group (IUPAC name: alkanoyl group) is usually derived from a carboxylic acid, in which case the formula is written RCO−, where R indicates the organyl group or hydrogen in the case of the formyl group connected to the CO group by a single bond.

Acyl species

Acyl groups are derived from specific acids: acetyl (CH3CO–) from acetic acid, propionyl from propionic acid, benzoyl (C6H5CO–) from benzoic acid, and formyl (HCO–) from formic acid. The corresponding compounds containing acyl groups include acyl halides, carboxamides, esters, thioesters, ketones, aldehydes, and anhydrides.

Reactivity

Acyl compounds undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution, in which the carbonyl carbon is attacked by a nucleophile and the leaving group is displaced. Acyl chlorides are among the most reactive derivatives and are widely used in Friedel–Crafts acylation to introduce acyl groups onto aromatic rings.

Biochemistry

Acyl groups are central to metabolism: acyl carrier proteins carry growing fatty acid chains in fatty acid synthesis, and acyl-CoA thioesters such as acetyl-CoA transfer two-carbon units through the citric acid cycle.
