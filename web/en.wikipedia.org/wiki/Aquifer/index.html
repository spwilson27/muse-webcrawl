An aquifer is an underground layer of water-bearing permeable rock, rock fractures or unconsolidated sediment (gravel, sand or silt) from which groundwater can be extracted using wells. Aquifers are crucial sources of fresh water for agriculture, industry and drinking.

Types

An unconfined aquifer lies beneath a permeable surface with its upper boundary at the water table, while a confined aquifer is sandwiched between impermeable layers, often under pressure so that wells drilled into it flow as artesian wells. Impermeable strata that block flow are called aquitards or aquicludes.

Recharge and depletion

Aquifers recharge where precipitation infiltrates, sometimes over centuries. Excessive pumping causes falling water tables, land subsidence and saltwater intrusion in coastal regions; the Ogallala Aquifer under the US Great Plains is a widely studied case of depletion.
