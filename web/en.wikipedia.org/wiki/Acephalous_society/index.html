Acephalous society

An acephalous society (from Greek a- "without" and kephalē "head") is a society without permanent political leaders or a centralised hierarchy. In anthropology the term describes social systems — often organised by kinship, lineage segments, age grades or councils — in which authority is diffuse and decisions are reached collectively rather than imposed by rulers.

Characteristics

Acephalous societies are typically small-scale and egalitarian. Social order is maintained through custom, reciprocity, mediation and the threat of feud or fission rather than by police or courts. The classic ethnographic examples come from mid-twentieth-century studies of African political systems, notably Evans-Pritchard's account of the Nuer of South Sudan, whose segmentary lineage system balanced groups against one another without chiefs, and the Igbo of Nigeria, governed substantially by village assemblies, title societies and age grades.

In political theory

The concept was popularised by the 1940 volume African Political Systems, edited by Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, which contrasted centralised states with "stateless" or acephalous orders. Later writers, including Pierre Clastres in Society Against the State, argued that some acephalous societies actively resist the emergence of coercive authority rather than merely lacking it.
