The Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing (BOINC) is an open-source middleware system for volunteer computing, a type of distributed computing. Developed originally to support SETI@home, it became generalized as a platform for other distributed applications in areas as diverse as mathematics, linguistics, medicine, molecular biology, climatology, environmental science, and astrophysics, among others. BOINC aims to enable researchers to tap into the enormous processing resources of many personal computers around the world.

The BOINC Manager client application.

History

BOINC development originated with a team based at the Space Sciences Laboratory (SSL) at the University of California, Berkeley, and was led by David P. Anderson, who also led SETI@home. Anderson's team received funding from the National Science Foundation in 2002. The first BOINC-based projects included Predictor@home, Einstein@Home, and LHC@home. The platform was publicly released in 2004.

Design and structure

BOINC is free software released under the terms of the GNU Lesser General Public License (LGPL). A BOINC project consists of a set of server programs that distribute units of work ("tasks") to volunteer computers ("hosts"). Each host runs the BOINC client, which downloads tasks, performs the computation, and uploads results. The system includes mechanisms for redundant computing — issuing the same task to several hosts and comparing results — to combat cheating and hardware errors.

Projects

Well-known BOINC projects include Rosetta@home (protein structure prediction), World Community Grid (humanitarian research), MilkyWay@home (galactic modelling), PrimeGrid (prime number search), and Climateprediction.net (climate modelling). SETI@home, the project that inspired BOINC, entered hibernation in March 2020 after 21 years of operation.

Credit system

BOINC awards "credit" — informally measured in Cobblestones, named after Jeff Cobb of SETI@home — to volunteers in proportion to the computation they perform. The credit system is designed so that a computer performing one teraFLOP of computation over one day earns roughly 1,000 credits. Credit cannot be exchanged for currency, but it fuels competition among volunteers and teams.
