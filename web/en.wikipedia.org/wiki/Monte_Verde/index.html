Monte Verde

Monte Verde is an archaeological site in southern Chile, near Puerto Montt in the Los Lagos Region, which preserves evidence of human occupation dating to approximately 14,500 years ago — and possibly much earlier. Excavated from the 1970s onward by a team led by archaeologist Tom Dillehay, the site fundamentally altered scholarly understanding of the peopling of the Americas.

The site

Because the settlement was rapidly buried and preserved under a peat bog, organic materials that rarely survive were recovered in remarkable condition: wooden tent-like structures, stakes, hearths, stone tools, animal remains (including mastodon meat), wild potatoes, seaweed, and even a human footprint. The main occupation layer, Monte Verde II, has been radiocarbon-dated to about 12,500 radiocarbon years BP (roughly 14,500 calendar years ago).

Significance

Monte Verde predates the Clovis culture of North America, long held to represent the first Americans, by at least a thousand years. Its acceptance by the archaeological community in 1997 helped establish that peoples had reached the far south of South America before the ice-free corridor through Canada opened, supporting theories of an early coastal migration route. A deeper, more controversial layer, Monte Verde I, has been dated to around 33,000 years ago, though that claim remains debated.
