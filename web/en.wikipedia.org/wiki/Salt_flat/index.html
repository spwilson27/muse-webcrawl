Salt flat

A salt flat (also called a salt pan or salar) is a flat expanse of ground covered with salt and other minerals, formed where a lake or pond evaporates faster than precipitation replenishes it, leaving dissolved minerals behind. Salt flats are found in deserts worldwide.

Formation

In endorheic basins with no outlet, runoff carries dissolved salts inward; intense evaporation concentrates them until halite and other evaporites precipitate as a bright, polygonal crust. Flooding briefly in the wet season dissolves and re-levels the surface, making great salt pans exceptionally flat — the Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia varies by less than a metre over more than 10,000 km² and is used to calibrate satellite altimeters.

Notable salt flats

Famous examples include Salar de Uyuni (Bolivia, the world's largest), the Bonneville Salt Flats in Utah (used for land speed records), Badwater Basin in Death Valley (the lowest point in North America), Etosha Pan (Namibia), the Rann of Kutch (India), and Salar de Atacama (Chile), a major source of lithium-rich brine.

Uses and hazards

Salt flats are mined for table salt, potash and lithium. Their apparent solidity can deceive: thin crusts may hide soft mud, stranding vehicles, and mirages and featureless horizons make navigation difficult.
