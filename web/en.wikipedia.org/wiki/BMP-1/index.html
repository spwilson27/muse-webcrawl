The BMP-1 is a Soviet amphibious tracked infantry fighting vehicle (IFV), in service from 1966. BMP stands for Boyevaya Mashina Pekhoty (Боевая Машина Пехоты), "infantry combat vehicle". It has been widely exported and used in warfare around the world, and is widely regarded as the first true infantry fighting vehicle to enter mass production and service.

Design

The BMP-1 was designed by Pavel Isakov's design bureau at Chelyabinsk to carry a squad of infantry into battle under armour, able to fight alongside tanks even on a battlefield contaminated by nuclear, biological, or chemical weapons. It combines a low silhouette, full amphibious capability, NBC protection, and unusually heavy armament for its class: a 73 mm 2A28 Grom smoothbore gun in a one-man turret, backed by a rail launcher for the 9M14 Malyutka wire-guided anti-tank missile. Eight infantrymen sit back-to-back in the rear compartment, able to fire their weapons through ports in the hull sides, and leave through two rear doors that double as fuel tanks — a much-criticised vulnerability. Steel armour reaches 33 mm on the turret front.

Service history

The BMP-1 was first displayed publicly in the 1967 November parade in Red Square, where it caused considerable concern in NATO and spurred Western IFV programmes such as the West German Marder and the American M2 Bradley. It saw combat in the 1973 Yom Kippur War with Egypt and Syria, in the Iran–Iraq War, in Angola, in Afghanistan, and in many conflicts since, remaining in service with dozens of armies, in numerous licensed, copied, and modernised variants.
