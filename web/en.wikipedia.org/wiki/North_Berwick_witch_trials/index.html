North Berwick witch trials

The North Berwick witch trials were a series of trials held in Scotland in 1590–1592 in which a number of people from East Lothian were accused of witchcraft in St Andrew's Kirk in North Berwick. The trials were notable for the involvement of King James VI of Scotland and for producing the pamphlet Newes from Scotland (1591), one of the earliest published accounts of a witch trial. Over one hundred people were implicated and several were executed.

Background

In 1589 James VI sailed to Copenhagen to marry Anne of Denmark. Storms disrupted both the princess's first attempted voyage and the royal couple's return, and Danish officials blamed witchcraft; several women were tried and burned in Copenhagen. On returning to Scotland, James was primed to suspect a similar conspiracy at home.

The accusations

Suspicion fell first on Geillis Duncan, a servant in Tranent whose healing abilities drew attention. Under torture she named accomplices, including the schoolmaster John Fian and Agnes Sampson, a midwife from Haddington. The accused were said to have met in the kirk at North Berwick on Halloween 1590, where, according to the confessions extracted under torture, the Devil preached to them from the pulpit and they plotted to raise storms against the king's ships.

Trials and outcome

Agnes Sampson was examined personally by the king at Holyrood; she reportedly convinced him by whispering words he had spoken to his queen on their wedding night. She and John Fian were executed in 1591. The trials deepened James's personal interest in demonology, which he later expressed in his treatise Daemonologie (1597), and they are often seen as a starting point for the great Scottish witch-hunts of the following century.
