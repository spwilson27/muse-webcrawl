Debit card

A debit card, also known as a check card or bank card, is a payment card that can be used in place of cash to make purchases. The card usually consists of the bank's name, a card number, the cardholder's name, and an expiration date, on either side. When a purchase is made, the funds are withdrawn directly from the cardholder's bank account, unlike credit cards where the money is borrowed and repaid later.

How it works

Debit card transactions may be authorized in two main ways: by entering a personal identification number (PIN), routed over PIN debit networks, or by signature, routed over the networks of the major card brands such as Visa and Mastercard. Modern cards commonly carry an EMV chip and support contactless payment via near-field communication; many are also added to mobile wallets. Because the money comes straight from the account, a debit card transaction can be declined if the balance is insufficient, or may draw the account into overdraft depending on the holder's agreement with the bank.

History

Debit cards developed out of ATM cards, which banks began issuing in the 1970s and 1980s purely for use at cash machines. In the United States, the First National Bank of Seattle issued an early combined ATM and debit card in 1978. Through the 1990s, regional PIN debit networks and later the card brands' debit products spread widely, and by the 2000s debit cards were rivalling credit cards and cash as a primary means of payment in many countries. In some markets, such as Canada with the Interac system, debit became the dominant card payment method.

Advantages and disadvantages

Debit cards are convenient, widely accepted, and help users avoid debt because purchases are limited by the account balance. However, they generally offer fewer rewards than credit cards and, in some jurisdictions, weaker consumer protection against fraud or disputed transactions, since the customer's own money is at stake during an investigation. Merchants pay interchange fees on debit transactions, which in the United States were capped for large banks by the Durbin Amendment to the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010.
