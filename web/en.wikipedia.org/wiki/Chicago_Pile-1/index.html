Chicago Pile-1 (CP-1) was the world's first artificial nuclear reactor. On 2 December 1942, beneath the west viewing stands of the University of Chicago's Stagg Field, a team led by physicist Enrico Fermi initiated the first human-made self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction. The experiment was a key part of the Manhattan Project, the Allied effort to develop atomic weapons during the Second World War.

The reactor was built of a "pile" of graphite blocks — some containing lumps of uranium metal and uranium oxide — stacked in an approximately ellipsoidal arrangement about 25 feet (7.6 m) wide and 20 feet (6.1 m) high. Cadmium-coated control rods absorbed neutrons and could be withdrawn to start the reaction or inserted to stop it.

Origins

The concept of a nuclear chain reaction was first proposed by Leó Szilárd in 1933, shortly after the neutron was discovered. Szilárd and Fermi jointly pursued the idea of a reactor using natural uranium moderated by graphite. Persuaded by the Einstein–Szilárd letter, the United States government began supporting the work, which was consolidated at the Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago under Arthur Compton in early 1942.

By November 1942 construction of the pile was under way in a squash court under Stagg Field. Fermi's group assembled the pile layer by layer, measuring neutron multiplication as they went to predict when criticality would be reached.

The experiment

On the morning of 2 December 1942, with Fermi directing operations from the balcony, the final control rod was withdrawn in stages. At about 3:25 p.m. the pile achieved a self-sustaining chain reaction, which ran for 28 minutes at roughly half a watt before Fermi ordered it shut down. Compton informed James Bryant Conant of the success with the coded message: "The Italian navigator has landed in the New World."

Later history

CP-1 was dismantled and rebuilt at the Argonne Forest site as Chicago Pile-2 in 1943. The Stagg Field site is now marked by Henry Moore's sculpture Nuclear Energy and is designated a National Historic Landmark. The experiment is widely regarded as the beginning of the Atomic Age.
