Tonnage

Tonnage is a measure of the cargo-carrying capacity of a ship, and was originally the taxation paid on tuns or casks of wine. In modern maritime usage, "tonnage" specifically refers to a calculation of the volume or cargo volume of a ship. Tonnage figures are used for registration, safety rules, manning regulations and the assessment of port dues and canal tolls. The term is a near-homophone of tun, the old English unit for a large cask of wine.


Tonnage measures a ship's internal volume

Types of tonnage

Gross tonnage (GT) is a dimensionless index calculated from the total enclosed volume of a ship, standardised internationally by the International Convention on Tonnage Measurement of Ships, 1969, which came into force in 1982 and applied to all ships by 1994. Net tonnage (NT) is derived from gross tonnage by deducting non-revenue-earning spaces such as crew accommodation and machinery spaces, and represents the volume available for cargo and passengers.

Displacement tonnage, by contrast, is an actual weight: the mass of water displaced by the ship, equal to the ship's own weight when floating. Deadweight tonnage (dwt) is the maximum weight a ship can safely carry, including cargo, fuel, stores and crew. Earlier measures included gross register tonnage and net register tonnage, in which one register ton equalled 100 cubic feet of enclosed space; these were superseded by the 1969 convention's dimensionless values.
