Isma'il Pasha (Arabic: إسماعيل باشا; 31 December 1830 – 2 March 1895), known as Ismail the Magnificent, was the Khedive of Egypt and ruler of Sudan from 1863 to 1879, when he was removed at the behest of Great Britain and France. Sharing the ambitious outlook of his grandfather, Muhammad Ali Pasha, he greatly modernized Egypt and Sudan during his reign, investing heavily in industrial and economic development, urbanization, and the expansion of the country's boundaries in Africa.

His philosophy can be glimpsed in a statement that he made in 1879: "My country is no longer in Africa; we are now part of Europe. It is therefore natural for us to abandon our former ways and to adopt a new system adapted to our social conditions."

Family and early life

Isma'il, the second son of Ibrahim Pasha, was born in Cairo into the Albanian family of Muhammad Ali. After receiving a European education in Paris, where he attended the École d'état-major, he returned home. On the death of his elder brother, Isma'il became heir to his uncle, Said I, the Wāli of Egypt and Sudan. Said, who apparently conceived his nephew to be a threat to his safety, made use of excuses to the Ottoman Sultan to have Isma'il sent on missions away from Egypt, including to the Pope, Napoleon III, and the Sultan himself.

Reign

Isma'il succeeded Said in January 1863. He procured from the Ottoman Sultan a firman changing the title from wāli to khedive in 1867, and another limiting succession to direct male heirs. He spent heavily on the Suez Canal, which was ceremonially opened in 1869, on railways, telegraphs, irrigation works, sugar refineries, and the rebuilding of Cairo on a Parisian model. His ambitious borrowing from European lenders, however, brought Egypt to the verge of bankruptcy; in 1875 he sold Egypt's shares in the Suez Canal to the British government. Mounting debt led to the Anglo-French Dual Control over Egyptian finances and, eventually, to his deposition in 1879 in favour of his son Tewfik.

Death and legacy

Isma'il lived the rest of his life in exile, principally at Constantinople, where he died in 1895. Though his financial extravagance hastened foreign intervention in Egypt, he is remembered as a great modernizer whose reforms in education, infrastructure, and administration reshaped the country.
