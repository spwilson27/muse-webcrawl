Supercalifragilisticexpialidocious

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

"Supercalifragilisticexpialidocious" (pronounced /ˌsuːpərˌkælɪˌfrædʒɪˌlɪstɪkˌɛkspiˌælɪˈdoʊʃəs/) is a song from the 1964 Disney musical film Mary Poppins, written by the Sherman Brothers, Richard M. and Robert B. Sherman, and sung by Julie Andrews as Mary Poppins and Dick Van Dyke as Bert. The word — 34 letters long — is presented in the film as something to say when one has nothing to say, and is used during the animated chalk-picture sequence in which Mary Poppins wins a horse race and is interviewed by reporters.

Origins

The Sherman Brothers said they assembled the word from fragments evoking grandiose nonsense: super- ("above"), cali- (beauty), fragilistic- (delicate), expiali- (to atone) and -docious (educable), yielding a joking gloss such as "atoning for educability through delicate beauty". Similar long nonsense words had circulated earlier in the twentieth century; a version of the word was claimed in a 1949 song by Parker and Young, which led to a lawsuit against Disney and the Shermans that Disney ultimately won, the court finding the word had earlier antecedents in print. The word has since entered dictionaries as a humorous term for something extraordinarily good or impressive, and is a staple example of a very long English word, surpassed by coinages such as pneumonoultramicroscopicsilicovolcanoconiosis.

In popular culture

The song remains one of the most famous in the Disney catalogue; in the film's stage-musical adaptation and the 2018 sequel Mary Poppins Returns, the word is referenced and spelled out at length. Saying it backwards — "dociousaliexpilisticfragicalirupus" — is a running gag originating in the film itself.
