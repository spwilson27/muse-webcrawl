Phrasal verb

A phrasal verb is a multi-word verb in English (and some other languages) consisting of a verb together with one or more particles — typically a preposition or adverb — whose meaning is often idiomatic and cannot be deduced from the parts. Examples include give up, look into (investigate), put off (postpone), run into (meet unexpectedly) and take off (depart; remove).

Types

Phrasal verbs may be transitive or intransitive. Transitive phrasal verbs are further divided into separable ones, where the object can come between verb and particle ("turn the lights off" / "turn off the lights", and necessarily "turn them off"), and inseparable ones, where it cannot ("look into the matter", not *"look the matter into"). Three-word phrasal verbs combine a verb with two particles, as in put up with (tolerate) or look forward to, and are always inseparable.

Characteristics

Phrasal verbs are a distinctive and productive feature of English, inherited from its Germanic roots; the particles often correspond to separable prefixes in German and Dutch. For most phrasal verbs there is a more formal single-word Latinate synonym — put off / postpone, find out / discover, make up / invent — and phrasal verbs dominate in everyday speech, while the synonyms are preferred in formal writing. Because their meanings are frequently figurative, phrasal verbs are a notorious difficulty for learners of English, and several thousand are in common use.
