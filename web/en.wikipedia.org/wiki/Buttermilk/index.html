Buttermilk is a fermented dairy drink. Traditionally, it was the liquid left behind after churning butter out of cultured cream. As most modern butter is produced from non-cultured sweet cream, most modern buttermilk sold commercially is instead cultured: milk is inoculated with lactic acid bacteria (Lactococcus lactis and Leuconostoc species), which ferment lactose into lactic acid, giving the drink its characteristic thickness, tang, and long shelf life.

Types

  • Traditional buttermilk — the thin, slightly sour liquid remaining after butter is churned from fermented cream; still made at home and by small producers.
  • Cultured buttermilk — the standard commercial product, made by fermenting pasteurised skim or low-fat milk with bacterial cultures.
  • Powdered buttermilk — spray-dried cultured buttermilk, shelf-stable and used in baking mixes.

Culinary use

Buttermilk is drunk in many cultures — it is a staple drink in India (where spiced chaas is common), parts of Europe, and the American South. In cooking, its acidity reacts with baking soda to leaven batters for pancakes, soda bread, scones, and cakes; it is also used in marinades (notably for fried chicken), dressings such as ranch, soups, and mashed potatoes. Acidified milk (milk with lemon juice or vinegar) is a common household substitute.

Nutrition

Buttermilk is low in fat (when made from skim milk) and provides protein, calcium, riboflavin, and vitamin B12. The live cultures in traditionally fermented versions may have probiotic qualities.
