The Custom-House

Author: Nathaniel Hawthorne
Published: 1850, as the introductory essay to The Scarlet Letter
Genre: Autobiographical essay, sketch

"The Custom-House" is an essay by American author Nathaniel Hawthorne, published in 1850 as the lengthy introduction to his novel The Scarlet Letter. Framed as a memoir of his years working as a surveyor in the Salem Custom House, the sketch ostensibly explains how he discovered the manuscript and scarlet letter that inspired the novel, while in reality serving as a meditation on his Puritan ancestors, his native Salem, and the act of writing itself.

Summary

Hawthorne describes, with gentle self-mockery, his appointment as Surveyor of the Port of Salem through political patronage and the drowsy routine of the Custom House among aging inspectors and political appointees. In a deserted upper room he claims to have found a package containing a rag of scarlet cloth — a gold-embroidered letter "A" — together with a manuscript by a predecessor, Jonathan Pue, recounting the story of Hester Prynne. Holding the letter to his breast, he fancies it burning him "as if the letter were not of red cloth, but red-hot iron." He then describes losing the post with a change of administration, remarking ironically that a "political guillotine" beheaded him, and how the dismissal freed him to write the romance.

Themes and significance

The essay blurs autobiography and fiction — the Pue manuscript is Hawthorne's invention — and establishes the novel's themes of guilt, inheritance, and the lingering power of the past. Its portrait of Salem and of his ancestors William Hathorne and John Hathorne, the witch-trial judge, famously has the narrator take shame upon himself for their sakes. The sketch is regarded as one of the finest short prose pieces in American literature and a key statement of Hawthorne's theory of the "romance."
