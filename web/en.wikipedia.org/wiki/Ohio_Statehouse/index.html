Ohio Statehouse

Location: 1 Capitol Square, Columbus, Ohio

Construction: 1839–1861

Architectural style: Greek Revival

Materials: Columbus limestone

Designation: National Historic Landmark (1977)

The Ohio Statehouse is the state capitol building and seat of government for the U.S. state of Ohio, located in downtown Columbus on Capitol Square. The Greek Revival building houses the Ohio General Assembly — the Ohio House of Representatives and the Ohio Senate — as well as the ceremonial offices of the governor, lieutenant governor, state treasurer, and state auditor.

History

Columbus was founded in 1812 expressly to serve as the state capital. A design competition for a permanent capitol was held in 1838, and the cornerstone of the present building was laid on July 4, 1839. Construction proceeded fitfully over more than two decades, with numerous architects contributing to the design — including Henry Walter, Alexander Jackson Davis, William Russell West, and Nathan B. Kelley — and much of the labor provided by prisoners from the Ohio Penitentiary, who quarried and dressed the Columbus limestone. The legislature first met in the unfinished building in 1857, and the Statehouse was completed in 1861, topped not by a dome but by a low circular cupola (a drum-shaped rotunda cap that gives the building its distinctive silhouette, sometimes described as resembling a cake).

Abraham Lincoln spoke at the Statehouse in 1859 and addressed the legislature there in 1861 on his way to his inauguration; after his assassination in 1865 his body lay in state in the rotunda. In the 1990s the building underwent a comprehensive restoration that returned its chambers and historic spaces to their nineteenth-century appearance while adding modern systems; the project also completed the underground Capitol Square parking and the Ohio Statehouse Museum on the ground floor. The building was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1977.

Architecture

The Statehouse is one of the finest American examples of Greek Revival civic architecture, with simple Doric porticoes recessed into its facades on all four sides and a studied plainness that contemporaries associated with republican virtue. Its grounds display numerous monuments, including memorials to Ohio's Civil War soldiers and governors.
