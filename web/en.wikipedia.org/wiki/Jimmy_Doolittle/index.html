James Harold Doolittle (December 14, 1896 – September 27, 1993) was an American military general and aviation pioneer. He is best known for leading the Doolittle Raid of April 1942, the first American air raid on the Japanese home islands during World War II. A decorated pilot before the war, Doolittle made early contributions to instrument flying and won several major air races.

Aviation pioneer

Doolittle learned to fly during World War I and stayed in the Army as a test and demonstration pilot. In 1922 he made the first cross-country flight across the United States in under a day. He earned a doctorate in aeronautical engineering from MIT and in 1929 performed the first complete blind takeoff and landing, relying solely on instruments. As a racing pilot he won the Schneider Cup (1925), Bendix Trophy (1931) and Thompson Trophy (1932).

The Doolittle Raid

After the attack on Pearl Harbor, Doolittle planned and led a daring mission: sixteen B-25 Mitchell medium bombers launched from the aircraft carrier USS Hornet on April 18, 1942, striking Tokyo and other cities. The bombers could not return to the carrier and flew on toward China and the Soviet Union. Most crew members survived. The raid caused modest physical damage but greatly boosted American morale and compelled Japan to retain fighter forces at home. Doolittle received the Medal of Honor.

Later career

Doolittle went on to command the Twelfth Air Force in North Africa, the Fifteenth Air Force in the Mediterranean, and from 1944 the Eighth Air Force in Europe, which he later led against Japan. He retired from active duty after the war, worked in the aviation and petroleum industries, and advised the government on air and space policy. In 1985 he was promoted to full general on the retired list, and in 1989 he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom. He died in 1993 at the age of 96.
