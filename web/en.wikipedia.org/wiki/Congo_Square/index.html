Congo Square

Congo Square (French: Place Congo) is an open space in the Tremé neighbourhood of New Orleans, Louisiana, within what is now Louis Armstrong Park. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it was the customary gathering place on Sunday afternoons for enslaved Africans and free people of colour, who were permitted to assemble there to trade, socialise and perform their music and dances — a rare sanctioned space in the slaveholding United States.

Sunday gatherings at Congo Square preserved African musical traditions in New Orleans.

History

Under French colonial rule, slaves were customarily granted Sundays free; after the Louisiana Purchase, a 1817 municipal ordinance confined slave gatherings to the one location, then on the edge of the city near the ramparts. Witness accounts describe ring dances such as the bamboula and the calinda, drumming on instruments recalling African prototypes, singing, and markets. The gatherings were suppressed before the Civil War, though informal meetings continued afterward.

Musical significance

Congo Square is regarded as a crucible of African American music: the rhythms, call-and-response singing and dance practices maintained there fed into the development of New Orleans music and ultimately jazz. The site is commemorated within Louis Armstrong Park, named for the city's most famous musician, and hosts concerts and festivals celebrating the city's heritage.
