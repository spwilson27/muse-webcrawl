City symphony

City symphony

Genre: Documentary / avant-garde film
Era: 1920s–1930s (with later revivals)
Key works: Manhatta (1921), Berlin: Symphony of a Metropolis (1927), Man with a Movie Camera (1929)

A city symphony is a genre of documentary and avant-garde film, particularly popular in the 1920s and 1930s, which portrays the life of a city through impressionistic montage rather than narrative. Typically structured around the course of a single day — from dawn to night — city symphonies celebrate urban modernity: machines, crowds, traffic, work, leisure, and the rhythms of metropolitan life. They emphasise rhythm and visual pattern, often organising sequences like movements of a musical symphony.

Key films

The genre is usually traced to Manhatta (1921) by Charles Sheeler and Paul Strand, a portrait of New York City. Alberto Cavalcanti's Rien que les heures (1926) depicted Paris, and Walter Ruttmann's Berlin: Symphony of a Metropolis (1927), with a score by Edmund Meisel, became the genre's archetype. Dziga Vertov's Man with a Movie Camera (1929), filmed in Kyiv, Kharkiv, Moscow, and Odesa, pushed the form furthest, adding self-reflexive commentary on filmmaking itself. Other examples include Joris Ivens' Rain (1929), Robert Florey's Skyscraper Symphony (1929), and Jean Vigo's À propos de Nice (1930), which turned a satirical eye on its resort city.

Characteristics

Common features include rapid montage, superimpositions, split screens, and rhythmic editing matched to musical accompaniment; an absence of individual protagonists, with the city itself as the “character”; and a fascination with modern technology — trains, trams, elevators, neon signs, and factory machinery. Critics have debated whether the films celebrate or critique industrial capitalism; many combine both enthusiasm for modernity and attention to poverty and labour.

Legacy

The city symphony influenced later documentary and experimental cinema, from Koyaanisqatsi (1982) to contemporary time-lapse portrait films, and remains a touchstone in film studies for its exploration of cinema's relationship to urban life.
