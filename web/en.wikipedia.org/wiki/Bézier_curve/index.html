Bézier curve

A Bézier curve is a parametric curve used in computer graphics and related fields. A set of discrete "control points" defines a smooth, continuous curve by means of a formula. Usually the curve is intended to approximate a real-world shape that otherwise has no mathematical representation or whose representation is unknown or too complicated. The Bézier curve is named after French engineer Pierre Bézier, who used it in the 1960s for designing curves for automobile bodies at Renault; the underlying mathematics rests on Bernstein polynomials, studied by Sergei Bernstein in 1912, and an efficient evaluation algorithm was developed by Paul de Casteljau at Citroën in 1959.

A cubic Bézier curve defined by four control points P0–P3.

Definition

A Bézier curve of degree n is defined by n + 1 control points P0 … Pn and the Bernstein basis polynomials:

B(t) = Σi=0..n C(n,i) (1−t)n−i ti Pi,   0 ≤ t ≤ 1

The curve starts at P0 and ends at Pn, and is tangent at its ends to the first and last segments of the control polygon. It lies within the convex hull of its control points and exhibits the variation-diminishing property. Quadratic (n = 2) and cubic (n = 3) curves are the most common; cubic Béziers are the standard curve primitive in PostScript, PDF, SVG, and font outlines (with quadratics used in TrueType).

Evaluation

De Casteljau's algorithm evaluates the curve by repeated linear interpolation between control points; it is numerically stable and also subdivides the curve into two Bézier segments, which is used for rendering and intersection testing. Curves can be joined with positional or tangent continuity, and derivative control permits shapes such as circles to be approximated closely (though not exactly — rational Bézier curves, used in NURBS, can represent circles exactly).

Applications

Bézier curves are fundamental to vector graphics editors, computer-aided design, animation keyframing (easing curves), typography, and robotics path planning.
