Alfred Graf von Schlieffen (28 February 1833 – 4 January 1913) was a German field marshal and strategist who served as Chief of the Imperial German General Staff from 1891 to 1906. He is best known for the Schlieffen Plan, the deployment scheme intended to win a two-front war against France and Russia.

Career

Born in Berlin, the son of a Prussian general, Schlieffen entered the army in 1853 and served in the Austro-Prussian War (1866) and the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71). He spent much of his career on the General Staff, becoming its chief in 1891 in succession to Alfred von Waldersee. A quiet, workaholic planner, he made the annual staff rides and war games the test-bed of German strategy and rose to field marshal in 1903.

The Schlieffen Plan

Confronted by the Franco-Russian alliance, Schlieffen concluded that Germany must defeat one opponent quickly before turning on the other. His memoranda, above all the famous memorandum of December 1905, proposed holding defensively against Russia while the bulk of the army swept through Belgium and Luxembourg in a vast right-wing wheel, enveloping Paris and destroying the French army in a battle of annihilation (a modern Cannae) within about six weeks. His successor Helmuth von Moltke the Younger modified the plan, weakening the right wing and strengthening the left; the modified version was executed in August 1914 and failed at the Marne. Historians continue to debate how far the 1914 campaign reflected Schlieffen's own design.
