Cardinal Thomas Wolsey, chief minister of Henry VIII from 1515 to 1529.

Thomas Wolsey (c. March 1473 – 29 November 1530) was an English statesman and Catholic cardinal. As a royal chaplain to Henry VII and then the dominant minister of Henry VIII from 1515 to 1529, he became the most powerful figure in England after the king, styling himself an alter rex ("other king"). He was Archbishop of York — never able to obtain Canterbury — and, as cardinal and papal legate, the highest-ranking churchman in the realm.

Early life and rise

Wolsey was born in Ipswich, the son of Robert Wolsey, a prosperous butcher and grazier. He studied at Magdalen College, Oxford, graduating remarkably young, became a fellow and then bursar of the college, and as chaplain successively to the Archbishop of Canterbury and to Henry VII — for whom he undertook diplomatic missions — attracted the new king's notice. Under Henry VIII his organisational brilliance in supplying the 1513 French campaign won him rapid promotion: Bishop of Lincoln and then Archbishop of York in 1514, cardinal in 1515, and Lord Chancellor the same year.

Chief minister

For fourteen years Wolsey directed England's domestic and foreign policy. At home he centralised power in the royal council and the prerogative courts — above all Star Chamber — pursued enclosures and tax reform (the failed "Amicable Grant" of 1525 provoked revolt), and accumulated fabulous personal wealth, building Hampton Court Palace and York Place in London and founding Cardinal College, Oxford (refounded after his fall as Christ Church). Abroad he aimed to make England the arbiter of Europe: he organised the Treaty of London (1518) and the Field of the Cloth of Gold (1520), tilting between Francis I of France and the Emperor Charles V; twice, in 1521 and 1523, he harboured hopes of the papacy itself.

Fall and death

Wolsey's ruin came through the "King's Great Matter" — Henry's determination to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. As legate he presided with Cardinal Campeggio over the Blackfriars court of 1529, but Rome, under imperial pressure after the Sack of Rome, revoked the case, and Wolsey could not deliver the annulment. In October 1529 he was indicted for praemunire, stripped of the chancellorship and most of his wealth, and retired to York in 1530. That November, accused of high treason, he was arrested at Cawood and summoned south; he died on the journey, at Leicester Abbey, on 29 November 1530 — saying, according to his gentleman usher George Cavendish, "If I had served God as diligently as I have done the King, he would not have given me over in my grey hairs."

Legacy

Wolsey's fall cleared the way for the rise of Thomas Cromwell and the English Reformation. Historians have variously portrayed him as a greedy pluralist, a brilliant administrator frustrated by an impossible master, and the last great minister of the medieval church-state. Cavendish's Life of Wolsey, Shakespeare's Henry VIII, and Hilary Mantel's Wolf Hall (in which he is Cromwell's revered patron) have kept his image vivid.
