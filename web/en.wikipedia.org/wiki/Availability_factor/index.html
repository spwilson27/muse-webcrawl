Availability factor

The availability factor of a power plant is the amount of time that it is able to produce electricity over a certain period, divided by the amount of time in the period. Occasions where only partial capacity is available may or may not be deducted. Where they are deducted, the metric is titled the equivalent availability factor (EAF). The availability factor should not be confused with the capacity factor, which measures actual output relative to maximum possible output and is normally much lower for intermittent sources.

Definition and calculation

Availability factor = (available hours / period hours) × 100%. A unit is counted as available if it could generate at full or declared capacity, whether or not it was actually called upon to run. Planned outages (maintenance, refuelling) and unplanned (forced) outages both reduce availability, though some reporting conventions track them separately. The equivalent availability factor additionally derates periods of partial capability.

Typical values

Nuclear power plants achieve high availability — commonly around 90% or more — because refuelling outages are infrequent and carefully planned, though each refuelling takes the plant fully offline. Coal plants historically average in the low-to-mid 80% range, limited by boiler and turbine maintenance. Wind turbines are mechanically available roughly 95–98% of the time, but their capacity factor is far lower because the wind does not always blow; solar plants are similarly available whenever panels are serviceable, regardless of sunlight.

Importance

Grid planners use availability data to estimate dependable capacity, schedule maintenance outside peak seasons, and value generating assets. Because availability measures readiness rather than production, a peaking plant may have excellent availability yet a very low capacity factor, running only a few hundred hours per year.
