K-pop (Korean: 케이팝; short for Korean popular music) is a form of popular music originating in South Korea. It encompasses many genres — pop, hip hop, R&B, EDM and rock — performed by idol groups and soloists produced through a rigorous trainee system, and is characterised by polished production, elaborate choreography, striking visuals and dedicated global fandoms.

History

Modern K-pop is usually dated to the television debut of Seo Taiji and Boys in 1992, which fused American hip hop and new jack swing with Korean lyrics. The late 1990s saw the founding of the major agencies SM, YG and JYP Entertainment and the rise of first-generation idol groups such as H.O.T., S.E.S. and Fin.K.L. The Korean Wave (Hallyu) spread K-pop across Asia in the 2000s — TVXQ, Girls' Generation, Big Bang, Super Junior and Wonder Girls — and in the 2010s it broke globally: Psy's "Gangnam Style" (2012) became a worldwide viral phenomenon, while BTS topped the US Billboard charts and addressed the United Nations, and Blackpink headlined major Western festivals.

Industry

Agencies recruit trainees, often in their early teens, and train them for years in singing, dance, languages and media skills under strict contracts. Groups are marketed with "comebacks", variety-show appearances, fan meetings and merchandise, and fandoms organise streaming and charity campaigns. The industry is a major South Korean cultural export, supported by the government as soft power, though it has faced criticism over trainee contracts, working conditions and performers' mental health.
