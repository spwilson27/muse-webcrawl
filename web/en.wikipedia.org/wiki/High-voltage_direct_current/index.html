High-voltage direct current


HVDC converter station

A high-voltage direct current (HVDC) electric power transmission system uses direct current (DC) for electric power transmission, in contrast with the more common alternating current (AC) transmission systems. Most HVDC links use voltages between 100 kV and 800 kV.

Advantages and applications

HVDC is preferred for very long-distance bulk power transmission because it suffers lower electrical losses than AC over comparable distances, especially in submarine cables, where AC charging currents would otherwise consume the cable's capacity. HVDC links can also interconnect asynchronous grids (or grids of different frequencies), precisely control power flow, and improve grid stability. Famous examples include the Pacific DC Intertie in the United States, the North Sea Link between Norway and the United Kingdom (the world's longest subsea power cable at 720 km), and China's ultra-high-voltage DC (UHVDC) lines operating at ±800 kV and ±1100 kV, which carry power thousands of kilometres from western generation bases to eastern cities.

Technology

Conversion between AC and DC is performed at converter stations. Classic line-commutated converters (LCC) use thyristors and require a strong AC system; modern voltage-source converters (VSC), based on IGBTs, can feed weak or passive networks, control active and reactive power independently, and are more compact. The first commercial HVDC link, between mainland Sweden and Gotland, entered service in 1954 using mercury-arc valves.
