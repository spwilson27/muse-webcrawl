The Trưng sisters (Vietnamese: Hai Bà Trưng, "the two Trưng ladies") were Vietnamese noblewomen who led a rebellion against the Chinese Han dynasty in 40 AD, expelling the Han administration from northern Vietnam and ruling as queens for about three years. They are celebrated as national heroines of Vietnam and symbols of resistance to foreign domination.

The rebellion

The sisters were daughters of a Lạc lord of Mê Linh district in the Red River Delta, then part of the Han commandery of Jiaozhi. According to tradition, the harsh rule of the Han governor Su Ding (Tô Định)—and, in some accounts, the execution of Trưng Trắc's husband Thi Sách—sparked the revolt. In 40 AD the sisters raised an army said to number tens of thousands, famously including many women generals, recaptured dozens of strongholds, and drove out the Han. Trưng Trắc was proclaimed queen, abolished or reduced taxes, and ruled from Mê Linh.

Defeat and death

The Han emperor Guangwu dispatched the veteran general Ma Yuan ("Wave-Subduing General") with a large expedition in 42 AD. After hard campaigning, including the battles of Lãng Bạc and Cấm Khê, the sisters' forces were defeated in 43 AD. Tradition holds that the sisters took their own lives—commonly said to have drowned themselves in the Hát Giang River—rather than be captured; Ma Yuan had their heads sent to the Han capital at Luoyang. Ma Yuan then consolidated Han control, erecting the bronze pillars that marked the empire's southern frontier.

Legacy

The Trưng sisters are venerated in temples throughout Vietnam—most prominently in Hanoi's Hai Bà Trưng District—and their anniversary is commemorated with processions featuring elephant-mounted re-enactors. Streets, schools, and a district of Hanoi bear their name, and they appear on lists of Vietnam's greatest national heroes, invoked by later independence movements and during the Vietnam War era on both sides of the struggle.
