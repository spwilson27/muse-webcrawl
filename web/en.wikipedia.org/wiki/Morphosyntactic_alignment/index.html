Morphosyntactic alignment

In linguistics, morphosyntactic alignment is the grammatical relationship between the arguments in a sentence — the way a language groups the single argument of an intransitive verb (S) with the agent (A) and patient (P) arguments of a transitive verb, in case marking and agreement.

Main types

  • Nominative–accusative: S and A are treated alike (nominative), P differently (accusative). This is the pattern of English and most European languages.
  • Ergative–absolutive: S and P are treated alike (absolutive), A differently (ergative). Found in Basque, many Caucasian, Australian Aboriginal, Mayan and Inuit languages.
  • Tripartite: S, A and P are all marked differently.
  • Active–stative (split-S/fluid-S): the intransitive argument is marked like A or like P depending on its semantic role or volition.
  • Austronesian (symmetrical voice): verbs carry voice morphology selecting which argument is the privileged "subject".

Split systems

Many languages show split ergativity, conditioned by tense/aspect (as in Hindi and Georgian, where ergativity appears in perfective/past clauses), by the animacy or pronominal status of the arguments (as in Dyirbal), or by clause type. Alignment may also differ between case marking and verb agreement within the same language ("morphological" vs "syntactic" ergativity).
