Benevolent dictator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A benevolent dictator is an authoritarian leader who is said to exercise absolute power for the benefit of the population, maintaining order, economic development or social reform while tolerating little or no opposition. The idea echoes the older concept of the enlightened despot — Frederick the Great or Joseph II — and the Roman figure of Cincinnatus who relinquishes power.

The label is usually applied by admirers rather than by political science, and examples are invariably contested: Lee Kuan Yew's Singapore (developmental success under constrained political competition), Tito's Yugoslavia, Atatürk's Turkey, and Park Chung Hee's South Korea are frequently cited; critics in each case point to repression, and note that benevolence depends on the ruler's character without institutional guarantee — the problem political theory calls the succession problem: nothing ensures the next dictator is benevolent. Philosophers add that even genuinely beneficial rule treats citizens as subjects rather than agents, and that information under censorship is too corrupted for a ruler reliably to know the public good.

Related ideas

The benevolent dictator should be distinguished from the “developmental authoritarian” thesis, which claims authoritarian rule can precede and enable later democratisation, and from the software-community joke of the “benevolent dictator for life” (BDFL), the project founder whose decisions are final — a usage that borrows the term's benign surface.
