Lamentation of Christ

The Lamentation of Christ (also known simply as the Lamentation, or Threnos in Greek) is a subject in Christian art depicting the mourning over the dead body of Jesus Christ after his crucifixion and removal from the cross, usually by the Virgin Mary, Mary Magdalene, Nicodemus, Joseph of Arimathea, the apostle John and other followers. The Gospels do not describe the scene directly; it developed from the accounts of the Deposition and Entombment and became, from the Middle Ages onward, one of the central devotional images of the Passion.

Development

The theme emerged in Byzantine art in the 11th and 12th centuries as the Threnos, an emotionally heightened image for Holy Week devotion, and spread to Western Europe. Giotto's fresco in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua (c. 1305), with its grieving angels and the stark diagonal of the rocky slope, set the pattern for Italian Renaissance treatments. Northern artists such as Rogier van der Weyden and Albrecht Dürer also made celebrated versions.

Related scenes

The Lamentation is closely related to, and often combined with, other episodes between the Crucifixion and the burial: the Descent from the Cross (Deposition), the Pietà — in which the Virgin alone cradles the body — the anointing of the body on the Stone of Unction, and the Entombment. In the Lamentation proper, a group of mourners surrounds the body, which is typically stretched out on the ground or on a shroud, emphasising collective grief rather than the intimate two-figure composition of the Pietà.

Notable examples

  • Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel, Padua (c. 1305)
  • Andrea Mantegna, Lamentation over the Dead Christ, Brera, Milan (c. 1480s) — famous for its foreshortened perspective
  • Pietro Perugino, Pitti Palace, Florence (1495)
  • Albrecht Dürer, versions in Munich and Nuremberg (c. 1498–1503)
  • Giovanni Bellini, Brera Pietà/Lamentation treatments
