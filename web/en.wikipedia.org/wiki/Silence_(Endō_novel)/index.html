Silence (Japanese: 沈黙, Chinmoku) is a 1966 novel by the Japanese Catholic writer Shūsaku Endō, widely considered his masterpiece. It tells the story of Sebastião Rodrigues, a Portuguese Jesuit missionary sent to Japan in the 1630s, when Christianity was banned and its converts savagely persecuted. The novel explores faith, suffering, apostasy and the apparent silence of God in the face of evil. It won the Tanizaki Prize and has been translated into many languages.

Plot

Rodrigues and his companion Garrpe travel to Japan to minister to the hidden Christians (kakure kirishitan) and to investigate rumours that their former teacher, Cristóvão Ferreira, has apostatised. Guided by the treacherous Kichijirō, Rodrigues witnesses the torture and martyrdom of Japanese converts, who are forced to trample on images of Christ (the fumie). Captured and brought before the magistrate Inoue, he learns that Ferreira did indeed apostatise. When Rodrigues himself is finally forced to step on the fumie to end the torture of others, he hears — or imagines — Christ speaking: "Trample! Trample!" The novel closes with Rodrigues living out his days in Japan under a Japanese name, his faith uncertain, still serving in obscurity.

Themes and reception

Endō, a Japanese Catholic in a country where Christians are a tiny minority, used the novel to examine the clash between Western Christianity and Japanese culture — the "mudswamp" of Japan in which, he suggested, imported faiths are transformed. The ambiguous ending has generated extensive theological debate over whether Rodrigues's apostasy represents failure or a deeper, kenotic faith.

Adaptations

The novel was filmed by Masahiro Shinoda in 1971 (Chinmoku) and by Martin Scorsese in 2016 as Silence, starring Andrew Garfield and Adam Driver, a project Scorsese had pursued for over 25 years. It has also been adapted as an opera and for radio.
