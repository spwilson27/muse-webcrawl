Controlled Substances Act

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) is the statute establishing federal U.S. drug policy under which the manufacture, importation, possession, use, and distribution of certain substances is regulated. It was passed by the 91st United States Congress as Title II of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970 and signed into law by President Richard Nixon.

Schedules

The CSA places substances into five schedules according to accepted medical use, potential for abuse, and safety or dependence liability. Schedule I covers substances deemed to have high abuse potential and no accepted medical use (such as heroin and, federally, cannabis); Schedule II includes morphine, oxycodone and cocaine; Schedules III–V cover substances of progressively lower abuse potential, many available by prescription.

Enforcement

The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), created in 1973, administers the CSA together with the Food and Drug Administration, which provides scientific evaluations. The Attorney General may add, remove, or transfer substances between schedules by rulemaking.

Criticism and reform

The Act has been criticised for racial disparities in enforcement and for classifying cannabis alongside heroin. Numerous states have legalised cannabis despite its federal Schedule I status, creating an ongoing conflict between state and federal law.
