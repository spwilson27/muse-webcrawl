Immigration and Nationality Act

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) refers to United States federal statutes governing immigration and nationality law. The term most commonly denotes two major enactments: the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 (the McCarran–Walter Act) and the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (the Hart–Celler Act), the latter of which fundamentally reshaped American immigration policy by abolishing the national-origins quota system.

Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952

The 1952 Act, formally the McCarran–Walter Act, consolidated the many earlier statutes governing immigration and nationality into a single body of law. It retained a quota system based on national origins but eliminated racial restrictions on naturalization, allowing immigrants from Asia to become citizens for the first time. It also introduced a system of preferences favoring skilled workers and relatives of U.S. citizens. President Harry S. Truman vetoed the bill, criticizing its discriminatory quota provisions, but Congress overrode the veto. The Act also codified grounds of inadmissibility, including provisions aimed at excluding individuals deemed security risks or affiliated with subversive organizations.

Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965

The Hart–Celler Act of 1965 abolished the national-origins quota system established in the 1920s, replacing it with a preference system centered on family reunification and employment-based immigration. It set annual numerical limits for the Eastern and Western Hemispheres and imposed the first cap on immigration from the Western Hemisphere. Signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson at the foot of the Statue of Liberty, the Act is widely regarded as a landmark of the civil rights era and dramatically changed the demographic composition of subsequent immigration to the United States.

Later amendments

The INA has been amended repeatedly, notably by the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, which granted legalization to many undocumented immigrants while imposing employer sanctions, and by the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, which expanded grounds for removal and expedited deportation procedures. The Act is codified principally in Title 8 of the United States Code.
