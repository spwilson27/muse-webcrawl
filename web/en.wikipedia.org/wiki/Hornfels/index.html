Hornfels

Hornfels
TypeMetamorphic rock (contact metamorphic)
ProtolithsShale, mudstone, slate, volcanic rocks
TextureFine-grained, non-foliated, granoblastic
FormationBaking of country rock in a contact aureole around an igneous intrusion
Key mineralsAndalusite, cordierite, biotite, pyroxene (by grade)

Hornfels is the group name for a set of contact metamorphic rocks that have been baked and hardened by the heat of intrusive igneous masses and have been rendered massive, hard, splintery, and in some cases exceedingly tough and durable. The name derives from the German Hornfels, "horn rock", referring to its resemblance in toughness and texture to animal horn.

Formation

When magma intrudes into cooler country rock, the surrounding rocks are heated in a contact aureole without being subjected to strong directed pressure. Because the stress is hydrostatic, hornfels typically lacks foliation: new minerals grow as a fine-grained, interlocking (granoblastic) mosaic. Most hornfels develop from fine-grained sedimentary protoliths such as shale, mudstone, or slate, though volcanic and other rocks may also be hornfelsed.

Metamorphic facies and grades

With increasing temperature towards the intrusion, hornfels passes through recognised grades (facies): the albite–epidote hornfels facies at low temperature, the hornblende hornfels facies at moderate temperature, and the pyroxene hornfels facies at high temperature. Pelitic (mudstone-derived) hornfels at intermediate grades commonly develops porphyroblasts of andalusite (including the cross-patterned variety chiastolite) and cordierite, producing "spotted" rocks. At the highest grades close to large intrusions, partial melting may begin (sanidinite facies).

Properties and uses

Hornfels is typically dark grey to black or brown, very fine grained, and breaks with a subconchoidal to splintery fracture. Its hardness and toughness have led to local uses as road metal and aggregate; historically, fine Welsh hornfels-like rock (the "coticule" whetstone tradition is related to metamorphosed sediments) and certain hornfels were used for sharpening stones. Because hornfels responds to heating in predictable mineral zones, aureoles around intrusions are classic field laboratories for metamorphic petrology.
