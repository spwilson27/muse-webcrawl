Generation-skipping transfer tax

The generation-skipping transfer tax (GST tax) is a United States federal tax imposed on transfers of property to a person two or more generations below the transferor — a "skip person" such as a grandchild or great-grandchild — or to a trust for such persons. It supplements the federal estate and gift taxes, which historically could be avoided by passing wealth directly to grandchildren so that the property escaped tax at the children's generation.

A direct skip: property passes from grandparent to grandchild

History

Congress first enacted a generation-skipping tax in the Tax Reform Act of 1976, but repealed and replaced it with the modern GST tax in the Tax Reform Act of 1986, retroactive to transfers after 1985. The modern tax is codified in Chapter 13 of the Internal Revenue Code (sections 2601–2663) and applies at a flat rate equal to the maximum estate tax rate (40% in recent years).

Taxable events

Three events trigger the tax: a direct skip (an outright transfer to a skip person subject to gift or estate tax), a taxable distribution from a trust to a skip person, and a taxable termination of an interest in trust property after which only skip persons hold interests. Transfers to persons more than 37½ years younger than the transferor who are not descendants may also count as skips.

Exemption and planning

Each transferor has a GST exemption that has risen over time — to $13.61 million per person in 2024, indexed to the basic exclusion amount for estate tax — which can be allocated to transfers or trusts to shelter them. Dynasty trusts in states that have abolished or lengthened the rule against perpetuities use allocated exemption to transfer wealth across many generations free of repeated transfer taxes. Transfers qualifying for the gift tax annual exclusion, and direct payments of tuition and medical expenses, are generally also exempt from GST tax.
