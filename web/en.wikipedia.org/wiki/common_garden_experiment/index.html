Common garden experiment

A common garden experiment is a research method in ecology and evolutionary biology in which organisms from different populations or environments are grown (or reared) together under the same conditions. Because the environment is held constant, any remaining differences between the groups can be attributed to genetic differences rather than to phenotypic plasticity.

Rationale

Organisms in nature often differ both genetically and environmentally, so field observations alone cannot separate the two causes of variation. By transplanting individuals from several source populations into one shared "common garden", researchers create a controlled comparison. If populations still differ when raised together, the differences are inferred to have a genetic basis — for example, local adaptation to different climates.

History and applications

Classic common garden studies were carried out by Göte Turesson in the 1920s, who coined the term "ecotype" after showing that habitat-correlated plant forms persisted in a shared garden, and by Jens Clausen, David Keck, and William Hiesey, whose transplant experiments across Californian elevation gradients are landmarks of experimental ecology. Common gardens are now standard in forestry provenance trials, studies of local adaptation, invasion biology, and research into how species may respond to climate change. The reciprocal transplant experiment is a related design in which each population is planted in every site.
