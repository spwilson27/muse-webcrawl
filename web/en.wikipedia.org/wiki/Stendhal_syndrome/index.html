Stendhal syndrome

Stendhal syndrome, or Florence syndrome, is a psychosomatic condition involving rapid heartbeat, dizziness, fainting, confusion and even hallucinations, allegedly occurring when individuals become exposed to objects, artworks or phenomena of great beauty. It is named after the nineteenth-century French author Stendhal (pen name of Marie-Henri Beyle), who described his experience of being overwhelmed during an 1817 visit to Florence in his book Naples and Florence: A Journey from Milan to Reggio.

History

Stendhal wrote of visiting the Basilica of Santa Croce, where Giotto's frescoes and the tombs of Michelangelo, Machiavelli and Galileo are found: he reached "that point of emotion where the celestial sensations given by the fine arts and the sentiments of passion meet", his heart beat fast, and he walked in fear of falling. The condition was named in 1979 by the Italian psychiatrist Graziella Magherini, chief of psychiatry at Florence's Santa Maria Nuova Hospital, who described more than one hundred cases of tourists hospitalised after viewing art in Florence, typically with symptoms lasting two to eight days.

Clinical status

Stendhal syndrome is not listed as a recognised condition in the DSM-5, and its scientific validity is debated; sceptics attribute cases to the general stresses of travel, fatigue and expectation, and some psychiatrists class the presentations as forms of panic attack or acute stress reaction in vulnerable individuals. Related culture-bound experiences include Jerusalem syndrome and Paris syndrome.

In popular culture

The phenomenon gave its title to Dario Argento's 1996 horror film The Stendhal Syndrome, in which a detective is incapacitated by paintings. It is frequently cited in discussions of the psychological power of art and "aesthetic chills".
