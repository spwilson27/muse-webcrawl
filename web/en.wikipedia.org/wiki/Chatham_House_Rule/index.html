Chatham House Rule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Chatham House, St James's Square, London, home of the Royal Institute of International Affairs]

The Chatham House Rule is a system for holding debates and discussion panels on controversial topics under which participants are free to use the information received, but neither the identity nor the affiliation of the speakers, nor that of any other participant, may be revealed. The rule originated at Chatham House, the London home of the Royal Institute of International Affairs, in June 1927.

The rule

The current formulation of the rule, as revised in 2002, reads:

When a meeting, or part thereof, is held under the Chatham House Rule, participants are free to use the information received, but neither the identity nor the affiliation of the speaker(s), nor that of any other participant, may be revealed.

The rule is designed to encourage openness and the sharing of information by removing the professional or public-relations constraints that participants might feel if their remarks were directly attributed. It is important to note that the rule protects the identity of speakers, not the content of what is said: the ideas discussed may be reported, but not who expressed them.

Usage

The rule is widely used by governments, corporations, academic institutions, and conferences around the world, particularly in discussions of diplomacy, national security, finance, and other sensitive subjects. Meetings held under the rule are often described in invitations and agendas so that participants know they may speak candidly.

Criticism

Critics, including some journalists and transparency advocates, argue that the rule can be abused to allow powerful figures to make controversial statements without accountability. Supporters respond that attribution is less important than the free exchange of ideas, and that many discussions would simply not take place, or would be far less candid, without the protection the rule provides.
