Attributable risk

In epidemiology, attributable risk (AR) or risk difference is the difference between the incidence of a disease or outcome in an exposed group and the incidence in an unexposed group. It measures the excess risk associated with the exposure and is interpreted as the amount of disease that could be attributed to the exposure itself (assuming causality).

Definition

If Ie is the incidence in the exposed group and Iu the incidence in the unexposed group, then:

AR = Ie − Iu

Related measures include the attributable risk percent (or attributable fraction among the exposed), (Ie − Iu) / Ie × 100%, which expresses the proportion of disease among the exposed that is attributable to the exposure, and the population attributable risk, which accounts for how common the exposure is in the whole population and estimates the proportion of all cases that could be prevented if the exposure were removed.

Interpretation

Attributable risk is an absolute measure, expressed in the same units as incidence, in contrast to the relative risk, which is a ratio. It is particularly useful for public health, because it indicates the potential impact of eliminating an exposure in a population. The concept assumes that the association between exposure and disease is causal and that removing the exposure would return the exposed group's risk to that of the unexposed group.

Example

If smokers develop lung cancer at 200 cases per 100,000 person-years and non-smokers at 10 per 100,000 person-years, the attributable risk is 190 per 100,000 person-years, and about 95% of lung cancer among smokers is attributable to smoking.
