Aamu

Aamu (Ancient Egyptian: ꜥꜣmw) was an Egyptian term used to designate peoples from Western Asia — broadly the Levant and Syria — in ancient Egyptian texts and art. The word is conventionally vocalised "Aamu", though its exact pronunciation is uncertain. Related terms include Retjenu for the Levantine region and Setjiu for Asiatic desert-dwellers or Bedouin.

Depictions

The Aamu are depicted in Egyptian tomb paintings and temple reliefs as bearded men and women in colourful, patterned garments, distinct from the Egyptians' own appearance. The most famous depiction is the procession scene in the 12th-dynasty tomb of Khnumhotep II at Beni Hasan (c. 1890 BC), which shows a caravan of 37 Aamu led by a chief named Abishai (Abi-shai) bringing eye-paint (mesdemet) to Egypt, accompanied by donkeys, musicians and families. Their leader bears the title heqa khasut, "ruler of foreign lands", the source of the word Hyksos.

Historical significance

The Aamu appear in sources from the Old Kingdom through the New Kingdom, as traders, migrants, mercenaries, captives and raiders. During the Second Intermediate Period, Aamu populations settled in the eastern Nile Delta, centred at Avaris, gave rise to the Hyksos rulers of the 15th Dynasty. The Beni Hasan painting is frequently discussed in connection with the biblical patriarchal narratives because of its depiction of Semitic-speaking pastoralists entering Egypt, though the scene itself predates any plausible dating of those traditions and does not depict a specific recorded event from them.
