Selective breeding

Placeholder image: the diversity of domestic dog breeds, all descended from the wolf.

Selective breeding (also called artificial selection) is the process by which humans breed plants and animals for particular traits, choosing which individuals reproduce so that desirable characteristics become more common over generations. It underlies the domestication of crops and livestock: modern maize from teosinte, wheat and rice varieties, dairy cattle, and the enormous diversity of dog breeds all result from millennia of selective breeding.

History

Farmers practised selection long before its scientific basis was understood. In the 18th century, breeders such as Robert Bakewell systematised livestock improvement in Britain. Charles Darwin used artificial selection as an analogy for natural selection in On the Origin of Species (1859), arguing that if humans could reshape species in a few generations, nature acting over geological time could do far more. In the 20th century, quantitative genetics made selection more efficient through progeny testing, estimated breeding values, and later genomic selection.

Methods and consequences

Breeders select on traits such as yield, growth rate, disease resistance, and appearance, using techniques including mass selection, pedigree selection, crossbreeding, and inbreeding to fix traits. Intensive selection can reduce genetic diversity and produce harmful correlated effects — for example, health problems in heavily selected dog breeds and broiler chickens — leading to interest in conserving traditional breeds and wild relatives as genetic resources.
