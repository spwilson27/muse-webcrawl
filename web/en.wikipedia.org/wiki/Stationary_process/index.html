Stationary process

A stationary (top) and a non-stationary (bottom) time series.

In mathematics and statistics, a stationary process (or a strictly stationary process) is a stochastic process whose statistical properties do not change over time. Formally, a process {Xt} is strictly stationary if the joint probability distribution of any collection of its values is the same as the distribution of the same values shifted in time by any amount.

Weak stationarity

A weaker and more widely used notion is weak, covariance, or second-order stationarity, which requires only that (1) the mean is constant over time, (2) the variance is finite and constant, and (3) the covariance between values at two times depends only on the time lag between them, not on the absolute times. Most time-series analysis methods, such as the ARMA family of models, assume weak stationarity.

Examples and transformations

White noise is the simplest stationary process. A random walk, by contrast, is non-stationary because its variance grows with time. Many real-world series — prices, temperatures with seasonal cycles, economic output — are non-stationary but can be rendered stationary by differencing, detrending, or removing seasonality. Unit-root tests, such as the Dickey–Fuller test, are used to detect non-stationarity.

For stationary processes, important results include the Wold decomposition (any covariance-stationary process is the sum of a linearly deterministic and a purely non-deterministic component) and the ergodic theorem, which guarantees that time averages converge to ensemble averages for ergodic stationary processes.
