Nucleosynthesis

Nucleosynthesis is the process that creates new atomic nuclei from pre-existing nucleons (protons and neutrons) and nuclei. Primarily, it explains the chemical abundances of the universe. After the initial synthesis of the lightest elements in the first minutes after the Big Bang, nucleosynthesis has continued in stars and in explosive stellar events, producing nearly all the heavier elements found in nature.

Big Bang nucleosynthesis

In the hot, dense early universe, roughly between one second and twenty minutes after the Big Bang, protons and neutrons fused to form deuterium, helium-3, helium-4, and trace amounts of lithium-7. Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts that ordinary matter is about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium-4 by mass, in good agreement with observations of pristine gas clouds. The instability of nuclei with mass numbers 5 and 8 prevented the formation of heavier elements in significant amounts.

Stellar nucleosynthesis

Stars generate energy by fusing light nuclei. Main-sequence stars convert hydrogen to helium by the proton–proton chain or the CNO cycle. In later stages, red giants fuse helium by the triple-alpha process into carbon and oxygen, and massive stars proceed through carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon burning, building an iron core. Elements up to iron and nickel can be produced by exothermic fusion; this theory was set out in the famous 1957 B²FH paper by Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler, and Hoyle.

Explosive nucleosynthesis

Elements heavier than iron are made mainly by neutron capture: the slow s-process in asymptotic giant branch stars, and the rapid r-process, which requires the enormous neutron fluxes of core-collapse supernovae and neutron star mergers — the 2017 kilonova GW170817 confirmed mergers as a major r-process site, producing gold, platinum, and other heavy elements. Proton-rich nuclei are attributed to the rarer p-process. Cosmic ray spallation produces light elements such as lithium, beryllium, and boron by breaking apart heavier nuclei in interstellar space.
