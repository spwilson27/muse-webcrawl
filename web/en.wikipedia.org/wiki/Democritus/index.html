Democritus (c. 460 – c. 370 BC) was an Ancient Greek pre-Socratic philosopher from Abdera in Thrace, remembered principally for formulating, together with his teacher Leucippus, the theory of atomism: the doctrine that the universe consists of indivisible particles — atoms — moving in empty space, the void. None of his extensive writings survive complete; his ideas are known through quotations and reports by later authors such as Aristotle and Diogenes Laërtius.

Life

According to ancient tradition, Democritus travelled widely in search of knowledge, visiting Egypt, Persia and possibly India, and spent his inherited wealth on his journeys. He was known in antiquity as the "Laughing Philosopher", said to laugh at human folly, in contrast to the "Weeping Philosopher" Heraclitus. He reportedly lived to a very advanced age.

Atomism

Democritus taught that reality consists of atoms and the void. Atoms are eternal, indivisible, and differ only in shape, arrangement and position; all qualitative differences in the world — colour, taste, heat — arise from these geometric differences and from convention: "By convention sweet and by convention bitter, by convention hot, by convention cold, by convention colour; in truth, atoms and the void." The soul, too, was composed of fine, spherical atoms. The theory answered the Eleatic challenge of Parmenides by allowing plurality and motion while denying that being could arise from non-being.

Ethics and other work

Democritus wrote on ethics, physics, mathematics, music and language; the catalogue of his works lists some seventy titles. His ethical thought centred on euthymia, cheerfulness or well-being of the soul, achieved through moderation and rational self-control. His atomism influenced Epicurus and, through the Roman poet Lucretius, the revival of corpuscular philosophy in early modern science.
