Stefan Banach (30 March 1892 – 31 August 1945) was a Polish mathematician who is generally considered one of the world's most important and influential 20th-century mathematicians. He was the founder of modern functional analysis, and an original member of the Lwów School of Mathematics.

Life

Banach was born in Kraków and largely educated himself in mathematics, receiving little formal training. His talent was recognised by Hugo Steinhaus, who became his mentor and collaborator. Banach received his doctorate at Lwów University in 1920 and became a professor there in 1922. His 1932 book Théorie des opérations linéaires (Theory of Linear Operations) codified the field of functional analysis.

Banach was famous for working in cafés, notably the Scottish Café in Lwów, where mathematicians gathered to discuss problems, recording them in what became known as the Scottish Book. He survived the Nazi occupation working as a lice-feeder at Rudolf Weigl's institute, but died of lung cancer shortly after the war ended, in August 1945.

Work

Banach introduced the concepts now known as Banach spaces — complete normed vector spaces — which are central to functional analysis. Among the results bearing his name are the Banach fixed-point (contraction mapping) theorem, the Hahn–Banach theorem, the Banach–Steinhaus (uniform boundedness) theorem, the open mapping and closed graph theorems, and, with Alfred Tarski, the Banach–Tarski paradox on the decomposition of a sphere.

Stanisław Ulam recalled Banach as one of the dominant figures of the Lwów school; the asteroid 16856 Banach and a lunar crater are named in his honour.
