Word spacing

Word spacing in typography is the adjustment of the horizontal space between words in a line of text. Alongside letter-spacing and line spacing (leading), it is one of the main controls over the texture and readability of a text block. In justified text the word spaces are stretched or compressed so that every line reaches the same width; in left-aligned ("ragged right") text they are usually kept constant.

History

Classical Latin was written in scriptio continua without word spaces; interpuncts were sometimes used to separate words. Systematic word separation was developed by Insular (Irish and Anglo-Saxon) scribes in the 7th–8th centuries, whose unfamiliarity with Latin made word boundaries essential, and it spread through Carolingian scriptoria to become standard in medieval Latin and the vernaculars. Early printers adopted spaces of metal type body width; the standard interword space in metal type was commonly a third to a quarter of an em ("3-to-the-em" spaces), with wider en and em spaces available.

Practice

Typographers generally recommend modest, even word spacing: excess space creates "rivers" of white running down justified columns, while too little space merges words. In CSS, the word-spacing property accepts a length added to the font's default space width; justification on the web and in page-layout programs distributes extra space between words (and, in some systems such as TeX, adjusts inter-letter glue as well). Knuth's line-breaking algorithm in TeX penalises lines whose word spaces must stretch or shrink far from their ideal width, which is a principal reason TeX documents have even colour.

Sentence spacing

A related debate concerns spacing after a full stop: the convention of typing two spaces after a sentence, inherited from typewriter monospaced fonts, versus the single space standard in professional typesetting. A 2020 Microsoft Word update began flagging double spaces as errors, illustrating the modern consensus for single sentence spacing in proportional fonts.
