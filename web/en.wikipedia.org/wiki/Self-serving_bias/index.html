Self-serving bias

A self-serving bias is any cognitive or perceptual process that is distorted by the need to maintain and enhance self-esteem, or the tendency to perceive oneself in an overly favourable manner. It is the common habit of a person taking credit for positive events or outcomes, but blaming outside factors for negative events.

Attributional pattern

The bias is a type of attributional bias: successes are attributed to internal, stable, and controllable causes ("I passed because I am smart"), while failures are attributed to external, unstable, or uncontrollable causes ("the exam was unfair"). The pattern has been documented across many domains, including education, the workplace, sports, driving, and close relationships.

Explanations

Two classes of explanation have been proposed. Motivational accounts hold that the bias serves to protect and enhance self-esteem. Cognitive accounts emphasise information-processing factors: people expect to succeed, recall their past successes more easily, and notice situational constraints on their own failures more than on successes.

Cultural and clinical variation

Meta-analyses find the bias is stronger in Western, individualistic cultures than in East Asian, collectivistic cultures, where self-effacing attributions are more common. The bias is reduced or reversed in depression, and exaggerated in some conditions involving grandiosity.
