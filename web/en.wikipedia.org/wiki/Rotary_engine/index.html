Rotary engine

Type: Internal combustion engine

Inventor: Felix Wankel

First run: 1957

The term rotary engine covers several designs in which the usual reciprocating pistons are replaced by rotating components. The most successful is the Wankel engine, developed by German engineer Felix Wankel and first run in 1957, in which a triangular rotor with curved sides rotates eccentrically inside an oval (epitrochoid) housing. The term also refers, in early aviation, to engines such as the Gnome in which the entire crankcase and cylinders rotated around a fixed crankshaft — a different design discussed separately.

Wankel design

As the Wankel rotor turns, the three chambers formed between its flanks and the housing successively expand and contract, carrying out intake, compression, combustion, and exhaust in a continuous four-stroke cycle in each chamber. A single rotor therefore produces one power pulse per revolution of the output shaft (the rotor itself turns at one-third shaft speed). Because there are few moving parts and no valvetrain, Wankel engines are compact, light, smooth, and capable of high revolutions.

Advantages and disadvantages

The design's weaknesses are the long, thin combustion chamber, which harms fuel efficiency and hydrocarbon emissions, and the apex seals at the rotor tips, whose wear historically caused durability problems. Poor fuel economy and emissions ultimately ended the Wankel's career as a mainstream car engine.

Applications

NSU offered the first production Wankel cars, the Spider (1964) and Ro 80 (1967). Mazda became the technology's champion, selling rotary-powered sports cars from the Cosmo (1967) through the RX-7 and RX-8, and winning the 24 Hours of Le Mans outright in 1991 with the four-rotor 787B. Mazda revived the rotary in 2023 as a generator (range extender) in the MX-30 e-Skyactiv R-EV. Wankel-type engines also power some UAVs, go-karts, and experimental aircraft.
