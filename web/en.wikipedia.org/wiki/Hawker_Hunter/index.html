The Hawker Hunter is a transonic British jet-powered fighter aircraft that was developed by Hawker Aircraft for the Royal Air Force (RAF) during the late 1940s and early 1950s. It was designed to take advantage of the newly developed Rolls-Royce Avon turbojet engine and the swept wing, and was the first jet-powered aircraft produced by Hawker to be procured by the RAF.

On 7 September 1953, the modified first prototype broke the world air speed record for aircraft, achieving a speed of 727.63 mph (1,171.01 km/h; 632.29 kn). The single-seat Hunter was introduced to service in 1954 as a manoeuvrable day interceptor aircraft, quickly succeeding first-generation jet fighters in RAF service such as the Gloster Meteor and the de Havilland Venom. The all-weather radar-equipped Hunter variants were introduced shortly afterward, and the type was progressively re-purposed for ground-attack and reconnaissance missions.

Development

The Hunter originated from Air Ministry Specification F.3/48, which sought a day interceptor to replace the Meteor. Chief designer Sydney Camm produced a swept-wing design powered by the Avon engine, and the prototype P.1067 (WB188) first flew from RAF Boscombe Down on 20 July 1951 with test pilot Neville Duke at the controls. Production Hunters entered squadron service from July 1954.

Design

The Hunter is a conventional swept-wing monoplane of all-metal construction. The wing has a sweep of 35 degrees and modest anhedral; the tailplane is mounted partway up the fin. Power came from a single Rolls-Royce Avon turbojet mounted in the centre fuselage, fed by triangular intakes in the wing roots. Armament comprised four 30 mm ADEN cannon in a removable pack beneath the cockpit, plus provision for bombs, rockets and drop tanks on later ground-attack variants such as the FGA.9. A two-seat trainer version, the T.7, had a side-by-side cockpit.

Operational history

Hunters served with the RAF in Germany, Cyprus, Aden and the Far East, and saw action during the Suez Crisis (1956), the Aden Emergency and the Indonesia–Malaysia confrontation. The type was exported widely: India was the largest overseas operator and flew Hunters in the Indo-Pakistani wars of 1965 and 1971; Switzerland, Sweden, the Netherlands, Belgium, Chile, Peru and several Middle Eastern air forces also operated the type. The RAF retired its last Hunters from front-line use in the 1970s, though trainer and trials aircraft lasted longer. Many Hunters survive in museums and a number remain airworthy in civilian hands.

Variants

  • F.1–F.6 – successive day-fighter versions with Avon or Sapphire engines.
  • FR.10 – fighter-reconnaissance variant with nose cameras.
  • FGA.9 – dedicated ground-attack conversion of the F.6.
  • T.7 / T.8 – two-seat trainers for the RAF and Royal Navy.

Specifications (Hunter F.6)

  • Crew: 1
  • Length: 45 ft 10.5 in (13.98 m)
  • Wingspan: 33 ft 8 in (10.26 m)
  • Powerplant: 1 × Rolls-Royce Avon 207 turbojet, 10,150 lbf thrust
  • Maximum speed: Mach 0.94, 715 mph (1,151 km/h) at sea level
  • Armament: 4 × 30 mm ADEN cannon; up to 7,400 lb of stores on four underwing pylons
