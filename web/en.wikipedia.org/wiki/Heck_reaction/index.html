Heck reaction

The Heck reaction (also called the Mizoroki–Heck reaction) is the chemical reaction of an unsaturated halide (or triflate) with an alkene in the presence of a base and a palladium catalyst to form a substituted alkene. It is named after the American chemist Richard F. Heck, who shared the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for palladium-catalyzed cross couplings in organic synthesis; Tsutomu Mizoroki reported a closely related reaction independently in 1971.

Mechanism

The catalytic cycle for a Pd(0) catalyst proceeds through oxidative addition of the organic halide to palladium, coordination and migratory insertion of the alkene (carbopalladation), β-hydride elimination to release the substituted alkene product, and base-assisted reductive elimination that regenerates the Pd(0) catalyst. The reaction generally favours formation of the trans (E) alkene and attaches the organic group to the less hindered carbon of the alkene.

Conditions and scope

Typical conditions use palladium acetate or Pd(PPh3)4 with a phosphine ligand and a base such as triethylamine, potassium carbonate or sodium acetate, in solvents like acetonitrile, DMF or toluene. Aryl and vinyl iodides, bromides and triflates react readily; chlorides require more electron-rich ligands. Intramolecular versions build rings, and asymmetric variants use chiral phosphine ligands to set new stereocentres.

Applications

The Heck reaction is a cornerstone of modern cross-coupling chemistry and is used industrially and in the laboratory for the synthesis of pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, dyes and advanced materials such as conjugated polymers for organic electronics.
