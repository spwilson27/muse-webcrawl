Stalagmite

A stalagmite (UK: /ˈstæləɡˌmaɪt/, US: /stəˈlæɡmaɪt/) is a rock formation that rises from the floor of a cave due to the accumulation of material deposited on the floor from ceiling drippings. Stalagmites are a type of speleothem, typically composed of calcium carbonate precipitated from mineralised water dripping through the cave ceiling.

Formation

Rainwater percolating through soil absorbs carbon dioxide, becoming a weak carbonic acid solution that dissolves limestone as it passes through cracks in the rock. When this water reaches the air of a cave, it degasses carbon dioxide and can no longer hold all its dissolved calcium carbonate, which precipitates out. Water dripping from the ceiling deposits a small amount of calcite where it lands on the floor, and over thousands of years these deposits build upward into a stalagmite. Growth rates are slow — commonly a few centimetres per century, though they vary greatly with water supply and cave conditions.

The corresponding formation hanging from the ceiling is a stalactite. When a stalactite and a stalagmite meet, they form a column or pillar. A common mnemonic is that a stalagmite has the letter "g" for ground, while a stalactite has "c" for ceiling; alternatively, stalactites "hold tight" to the ceiling while stalagmites "push up with might" from the floor.

Composition and types

Most stalagmites are formed of calcite or aragonite in limestone and dolomite caves. Other, rarer varieties occur in lava tubes (formed from dripping lava), and in ice caves, where seasonal melting and refreezing produce ice stalagmites. Stalagmite sections preserve layered growth bands that record past climate: scientists date the layers by uranium–thorium methods and analyse their oxygen isotopes and trace elements to reconstruct rainfall and temperature over hundreds of thousands of years, making stalagmites valuable archives of palaeoclimate.

Conservation

Because skin oils can coat the surface and impede further deposition, visitors to show caves are commonly told not to touch stalagmites; a formation damaged in this way may stop growing where it was touched.
