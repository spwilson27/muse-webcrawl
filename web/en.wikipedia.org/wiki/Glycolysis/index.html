Glycolysis

Glycolysis is the metabolic pathway that converts glucose (C6H12O6) into pyruvate. The free energy released in this process is used to form the high-energy molecules adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH). Glycolysis is a sequence of ten reactions catalyzed by enzymes, occurring in the cytosol of nearly all living cells, and is thought to be one of the most ancient metabolic pathways.

Overview

Glycolysis is the first step in the breakdown of energy sources such as glucose for cellular metabolism. It does not require oxygen, and so can occur under anaerobic conditions; in the absence of oxygen, the pyruvate produced may be converted to lactate (lactic acid fermentation) or to ethanol and carbon dioxide (alcoholic fermentation), regenerating the NAD+ needed to keep the pathway running. In aerobic organisms, pyruvate instead enters the citric acid cycle after oxidative decarboxylation to acetyl-CoA.

The pathway is divided into an energy-investment phase, in which two ATP molecules are consumed, and an energy-payoff phase, in which four ATP are produced by substrate-level phosphorylation, giving a net gain of two ATP per glucose.

History

The elucidation of glycolysis was one of the great achievements of early biochemistry. Eduard Buchner demonstrated cell-free fermentation in 1897, and the pathway's intermediates were worked out chiefly by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof, and Jakub Karol Parnas in the first decades of the twentieth century; it is therefore also called the Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas pathway. Hans von Euler-Chelpin, Arthur Harden, and Otto Warburg also made major contributions.

Regulation

The principal rate-limiting step is the reaction catalyzed by phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1), which is allosterically inhibited by ATP and citrate and activated by AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate. Hexokinase and pyruvate kinase are also regulated, ensuring that glycolytic flux matches the cell's energetic needs and the availability of biosynthetic precursors.

Fates of pyruvate

Under aerobic conditions pyruvate is transported into mitochondria and oxidized. Under anaerobic conditions in muscle, it is reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase; in yeast it is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde and then reduced to ethanol. These fermentations regenerate NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue.
