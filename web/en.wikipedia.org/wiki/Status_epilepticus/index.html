Status epilepticus (SE) is a single epileptic seizure lasting more than five minutes, or two or more seizures within a five-minute period without the person returning to normal between them. It is a life-threatening medical emergency, particularly if treatment is delayed. Previous definitions used a 30-minute time limit; modern operational definitions emphasise starting treatment at five minutes for convulsive seizures.

Classification

Status epilepticus may be convulsive (generalised tonic–clonic) or non-convulsive, the latter including absence status and focal status with impaired awareness, which requires EEG for diagnosis. Cases that continue despite first- and second-line therapy are termed refractory; those persisting despite anaesthetic infusion are super-refractory.

Causes

Common precipitants include stopping or missing antiepileptic medication, acute stroke, central nervous system infection, head injury, alcohol or drug withdrawal, and metabolic abnormalities such as hyponatraemia. In children, febrile status epilepticus associated with fever is the most common form.

Treatment

Management follows a time-based protocol: immediate stabilisation of airway, breathing and circulation and correction of hypoglycaemia; first-line intravenous benzodiazepines (lorazepam, diazepam or midazolam); second-line agents such as levetiracetam, fosphenytoin or valproate (shown to be roughly equivalent in the ESETT trial); and, for refractory cases, continuous infusions of midazolam, propofol or pentobarbital in an intensive care setting with continuous EEG monitoring.

Prognosis

Short-term mortality is around 20 percent and rises with age, duration of seizure activity and underlying cause; survivors may have lasting neurological deficits.
