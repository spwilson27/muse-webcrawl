Smyrna

Smyrna (Greek: Σμύρνη, Smýrnē; Turkish: İzmir) was an ancient Greek city on the Aegean coast of Anatolia, at the site of modern İzmir, Turkey. One of the principal cities of Roman Asia and a famed centre of early Christianity, Smyrna was continuously inhabited for millennia and remained a cosmopolitan port until the catastrophe of 1922.

Ancient history

The earliest settlement, Old Smyrna at Bayraklı, was founded around the 11th century BC and became one of the oldest Greek cities of the Aegean; tradition associates it with Homer, said to have been born there. Destroyed by the Lydian king Alyattes around 600 BC, the city was refounded in the late 4th century BC under Alexander the Great's successors on the slopes of Mount Pagos (Kadifekale), following a plan attributed to Alexander himself. Under Rome, Smyrna prospered as a wealthy commercial city, competing with Ephesus and Pergamum for the title of "first city of Asia," and it boasted famous temples, a stadium, and one of the largest agoras of the ancient world.

Early Christianity

Smyrna was one of the seven churches of Asia addressed in the Book of Revelation. Its bishop Polycarp, a disciple of the apostle John, was martyred there around AD 155, and his martyrdom is among the earliest recorded accounts of Christian persecution. The city retained a large Christian population throughout the Byzantine and Ottoman periods.

Ottoman era and 1922

After periods of Byzantine, Seljuk, and Genoese control, Smyrna passed permanently to the Ottoman Empire in the 15th century and grew into the empire's great Levantine trading port, famous for figs, raisins, carpets, and its Greek, Armenian, Jewish, and Levantine communities. Following the First World War, Greek forces occupied the city from 1919 under the Treaty of Sèvres. In September 1922 Turkish forces retook Smyrna at the end of the Greco-Turkish War, and the Great Fire of Smyrna destroyed the Greek and Armenian quarters, killing many thousands and driving the Christian population into exile. The subsequent population exchange between Greece and Turkey ended the city's multi-ethnic character; rebuilt, it continues as modern İzmir.
