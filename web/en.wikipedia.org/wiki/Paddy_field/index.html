A paddy field is a flooded field of arable land used for growing semiaquatic crops, most notably rice. The word paddy derives from the Malay padi, meaning rice plant. Paddy cultivation — wet-rice farming — feeds more people than any other single cropping system and shapes the landscapes and cultures of much of Asia.

Construction and water management

Paddy fields are level plots enclosed by low earthen bunds (levees) that impound water to a controlled depth. Before planting, the soil is saturated and puddled — ploughed while wet to form an impermeable layer that reduces water loss. Water may come from rivers, reservoirs, rainfall, or irrigation canals, and fields are drained before harvest so the crop can be cut from firm ground. On hillsides, paddies are built as terraces, some of them many centuries old, such as the rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras and of Yuanyang in Yunnan, China.

Cultivation cycle

Rice is commonly raised first in a nursery bed and transplanted by hand or machine into the flooded field, though direct seeding is also practised. Flooding suppresses most weeds and fixes the field's nitrogen partly through cyanobacteria and the aquatic fern Azolla. Traditionally, fish were raised in the paddies alongside the rice, providing protein and pest control; ducks are used similarly in some regions. Flooding is not strictly required by the rice plant but greatly increases yields by controlling weeds and pests.

History and distribution

Wet-rice farming developed in China by at least the 5th millennium BC and spread across monsoon Asia, reaching Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia, and South Asia. In the 20th century paddy cultivation also became established in areas such as the Po Valley in Italy, the Camargue in France, California's Sacramento Valley, and parts of West Africa.

Environment

Flooded paddies are artificial wetlands of considerable ecological value, supporting frogs, fish, waterbirds, and insects. They are also a significant anthropogenic source of methane, produced by methanogenic bacteria in the anaerobic soil; alternate wetting and drying can reduce emissions while saving water.
