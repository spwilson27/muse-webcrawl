S&P 500

[Image: a financial news graphic showing the S&P 500 index chart rising]

The S&P 500 (Standard & Poor's 500) is a stock market index tracking the performance of 500 large companies listed on US exchanges, selected by a committee of S&P Dow Jones Indices by market capitalisation, liquidity and sector representation. Covering roughly 80% of the US market's value, it is the most widely used benchmark of American equities and the basis of index funds and derivatives worth trillions of dollars.

History and role

Standard & Poor's introduced a 500-stock index in 1957 (its predecessors date to 1923). The S&P 500's market-cap weighting contrasts with the price-weighted Dow Jones Industrial Average: its biggest constituents — in recent years the largest technology companies — carry correspondingly large weight, a concentration debated by investors. Most actively managed US equity funds underperform it over long periods after fees, a fact central to the rise of passive Investment through mutual and exchange-traded funds. Futures on the index (from 1982) and the SPDR ETF (1993, ticker SPY) made 'buying the S&P' a single trade, and its level is a staple of economic news alongside the Dow and the Nasdaq Composite.
