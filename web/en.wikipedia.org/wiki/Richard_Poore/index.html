Richard Poore (or Poor; died 15 April 1237) was an English churchman who served successively as Bishop of Chichester, Bishop of Salisbury, and Bishop of Durham in the early 13th century. He is remembered chiefly as the founder of the present Salisbury Cathedral, one of the finest examples of Early English Gothic architecture, and for laying out the planned cathedral city of New Sarum (modern Salisbury).

Early life

Poore was the son of Ilbert de Poore and the younger brother of Herbert Poore, Archbishop of Canterbury from 1194 to 1205. He was educated at Paris, where he studied under Stephen Langton. He became Dean of Salisbury around 1197 and was involved in the administration of the diocese during his brother's archiepiscopate. He supported the reforming programme of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215).

Bishop of Chichester and Salisbury

Poore was elected Bishop of Chichester in 1215 and served briefly before being translated to Salisbury in 1217, following the death of his brother Herbert, who had held that see. At Salisbury he inherited the long-standing problem of the cathedral's cramped hilltop site at Old Sarum, exposed to wind and short of water, with strained relations between the chapter and the castle garrison.

With papal approval, Poore moved the see to a new site in the Avon valley below. The foundation stones of the new cathedral were laid on 28 April 1220, and the building rose with remarkable speed and stylistic unity over the following decades. Alongside it he planned the city of New Sarum with a grid of streets and market square. He also issued statutes for the cathedral chapter and promoted the cult of Saint Osmund, his predecessor as bishop, whose canonisation he helped secure in 1226.

Bishop of Durham

In 1228 Poore was elected Bishop of Durham, one of the wealthiest and most powerful sees in England, and was enthroned the following year. He governed the palatinate until his death on 15 April 1237 at the manor of Tarrant Keyneston in Dorset. His body was buried at Salisbury Cathedral, in the church he had founded; an effigy traditionally identified as his survives there. He is also associated with the Statute of Mortmain's ecclesiastical background and with early diocesan synodal statutes that influenced English church government.
