Wolf

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The wolf (Canis lupus), or grey wolf, is a large canine native to Eurasia and North America — the largest wild member of the dog family and the ancestor of the domestic dog. Wolves are social predators living in family packs, cooperatively hunting large ungulates (deer, elk, moose, bison), communicating by scent, posture and howling, and capable of travelling dozens of kilometres in a day.

Once the world's most widely distributed land mammal, wolves were exterminated from most of their range by persecution — bounties, poisoning and habitat loss — surviving mainly in wilderness. Conservation and legal protection since the 1970s have allowed recovery in parts of Europe and North America, including the famous 1995 reintroduction to Yellowstone.

Ecology

As apex predators, wolves influence prey behaviour and numbers, with debated knock-on ('trophic cascade') effects on vegetation and rivers in Yellowstone. Their return creates real conflict with livestock farming, managed through compensation, guard animals and, controversially, culls — the central tension of wolf conservation.

And humans

Wolves loom large in mythology (Fenrir, Romulus and Remus) and folklore (the 'big bad wolf'), though attacks on humans are rare. Interbreeding with dogs and coyotes (producing hybrids and the red-wolf question) complicates wolf genetics and taxonomy.
