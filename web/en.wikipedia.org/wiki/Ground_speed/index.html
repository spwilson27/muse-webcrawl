Ground speed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ground speed is the horizontal speed of an aircraft relative to the Earth's surface. It is vital for accurate navigation because it determines the rate at which the aircraft covers ground distance. Ground speed is the vector sum of the aircraft's airspeed and the wind speed: the aircraft's true airspeed combined with the prevailing wind gives the speed actually made good over the ground.

An aircraft flying with a headwind will have a ground speed lower than its airspeed, while a tailwind increases ground speed. For example, an airliner cruising at a true airspeed of 480 knots in a 60-knot tailwind has a ground speed of 540 knots, shortening the flight; the same wind as a headwind on the return leg reduces ground speed to 420 knots.

Pilots historically computed ground speed using the wind triangle with a flight computer such as the E6B, or by timing passage between landmarks. Modern aircraft obtain it directly from GPS and inertial navigation systems, and it is displayed on the flight deck and to passengers on in-flight entertainment maps. Ground speed differs from indicated airspeed, which is what the pilot reads from the airspeed indicator and which governs the aerodynamic behaviour of the aircraft.

The concept applies equally to ships and vehicles moving in a current or flow, where speed over ground (SOG) is distinguished from speed through the water.
