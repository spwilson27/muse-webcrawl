Saltatory conduction

In neuroscience, saltatory conduction (from Latin saltus 'leap, jump') is the propagation of action potentials along myelinated axons from one node of Ranvier to the next, increasing the conduction velocity of action potentials. The uninsulated nodes of Ranvier are the only places along the axon where ions are exchanged across the axon membrane, regenerating the action potential between regions of the axon that are insulated by myelin.

Saltatory conduction: the action potential is regenerated at each successive node of Ranvier, appearing to jump along the axon.

Mechanism

Myelinated axons differ from unmyelinated axons in that voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier, the short gaps between adjacent Schwann cells (in the peripheral nervous system) or oligodendrocyte processes (in the central nervous system). Under the myelin sheath the signal travels passively and rapidly by electrotonic spread; at each node it is boosted back to full amplitude. Because only the nodes need to be depolarized, conduction is much faster — up to about 150 m/s in large myelinated fibers, compared with roughly 0.5–10 m/s in small unmyelinated fibers — and more energy-efficient, since far less ion pumping is needed to restore resting potentials.

History

The mechanism was proposed on theoretical grounds by Ralph Lillie in 1925, demonstrated experimentally in the late 1930s and 1940s by Ichiji Tasaki, and independently established by Andrew Huxley and Robert Stämpfli in 1949. The concept is often contrasted with the continuous conduction found in unmyelinated axons.

Clinical significance

Demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis and Guillain–Barré syndrome destroy or damage the myelin sheath, slowing or blocking saltatory conduction and producing neurological deficits. Temperature sensitivity of symptoms in multiple sclerosis (Uhthoff's phenomenon) reflects the reduced safety factor of conduction in demyelinated fibers.
