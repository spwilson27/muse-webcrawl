Mawangdui Silk Texts

The Mawangdui Silk Texts (Chinese: 馬王堆帛書) are a collection of ancient Chinese manuscripts written on silk, discovered in 1973–74 in Tomb 3 at Mawangdui in Changsha, Hunan, China. The tomb was sealed in 168 BCE during the Western Han dynasty. The cache is one of the most important archaeological finds for the study of early Chinese philosophy, medicine, astronomy and divination.

Discovery

The manuscripts were found in a lacquered chest in the eastern compartment of Tomb 3, which belonged to a member of the family of Li Cang, chancellor of the feudal kingdom of Changsha. Rolled and folded silk sheets had survived in remarkably legible condition, though fragile, and required painstaking conservation.

Contents

The texts include two early versions (known as A and B) of the Tao Te Ching attributed to Laozi, accompanied by four works sometimes identified with the Huang-Lao school; versions of the I Ching with commentaries; historical annals; a text on astronomy and calendrical prognostics (the Wuxing zhan); medical manuscripts including the Wushi'er Bingfang ("Prescriptions for Fifty-two Ailments"); charts of physical exercises (Daoyin tu); and three maps — of topography, a city, and military dispositions — among the oldest surviving maps from China.

Significance

The Mawangdui versions of the Tao Te Ching predate all other surviving copies and differ in arrangement and detail from the received text, reshaping scholarship on early Daoism. The medical and exercise texts document early Chinese therapeutic gymnastics and materia medica, while the silk star charts preserve records of comets and planetary motions.
