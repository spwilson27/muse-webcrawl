Duchy of Berg

Schloss Burg in Solingen, seat of the counts of Berg

The Duchy of Berg (German: Herzogtum Berg) was a state of the Holy Roman Empire situated on the east bank of the Rhine in present-day North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. It originated as a county in the eleventh century, was raised to a duchy in 1380, and survived — latterly in union with Jülich and Cleves, and for seven years as the Napoleonic Grand Duchy of Berg — until the settlement of 1815, when its territory passed to Prussia. Its principal towns included Düsseldorf (the ducal capital from the late fourteenth century), Solingen, Remscheid and Wuppertal; the region survives today as the Bergisches Land.

History

The counts of Berg emerged around Altenberg and Schloss Burg in the eleventh and twelfth centuries and steadily enlarged their lordship between the Rhine, the Ruhr and the Sieg. In 1380 Count Wilhelm II was raised to duke by King Wenceslaus. Berg entered a lasting personal union with the Duchy of Jülich in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and in 1521 the inheritance of Cleves and Mark created the powerful composite territory of Jülich-Cleves-Berg on the lower Rhine.

The childless death of Duke Johann Wilhelm in 1609 triggered the War of the Jülich Succession; by the Treaty of Xanten (1614), Berg and Jülich passed to the dukes of Palatinate-Neuburg, while Cleves and Mark went to Brandenburg. Berg subsequently passed with the Palatinate inheritance to Bavaria (1777). Occupied by France during the Revolutionary Wars, it was reconstituted in 1806 as the Grand Duchy of Berg under Joachim Murat and then Napoleon himself, serving as a Napoleonic model state in which feudal dues were abolished and the Code civil introduced. After Napoleon's fall, the Congress of Vienna awarded the territory to Prussia, which incorporated it into the Rhine Province.

Economy

The Bergisches Land was one of Germany's earliest industrial regions: the valleys of the Wupper and its tributaries supported metalworking, blade-making (Solingen), textile finishing and ribbon-weaving, driven by water power and by the region's merchants putting out work to cottage producers.
