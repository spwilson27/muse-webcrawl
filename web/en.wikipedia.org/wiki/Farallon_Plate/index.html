Farallon Plate

The Farallon Plate was an ancient oceanic plate that began subducting under the west coast of the North American Plate — then located in modern Utah — as Pangaea broke up during the Jurassic period. It is named for the Farallon Islands off the coast of San Francisco.

History

The Farallon Plate once covered much of the eastern Pacific Ocean. During the late Mesozoic and Cenozoic it subducted beneath North America at a relatively shallow angle during the Laramide orogeny, producing mountain building far inland, including the Rocky Mountains. Its subduction also fed the volcanic arcs of western North America.

Remnants

Most of the Farallon Plate has been subducted beneath North America, with portions subducted beneath the Caribbean and elsewhere. Its surviving remnants include the Juan de Fuca Plate and Explorer Plate off the Pacific Northwest, the Rivera Plate, and the Cocos Plate off Mexico and Central America. Much of the plate also survives as slabs within the Earth's mantle, imaged by seismic tomography, and as accreted terranes along the west coast of North America.

As the Farallon Plate's spreading ridge reached the North American trench about 30 million years ago, subduction along California ceased and the San Andreas transform fault system began to develop.
