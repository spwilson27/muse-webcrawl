Skill

Skill
DefinitionThe learned ability to perform an action with determined results and good execution, often within a given amount of time or energy
Acquired byTraining, practice, experience
Related conceptsExpertise, competence, ability, know-how, talent

A skill is the learned ability to act with determined results and good execution, often within a given amount of time, energy, or both. Skills can commonly be divided into domain-general and domain-specific skills: a person might develop the general skill of time management alongside highly specific skills such as welding or playing the violin. In contrast with innate ability or talent, skills are capabilities developed through deliberate, systematic, and sustained effort — training, practice, and experience — to smoothly and adaptively execute complex activities or job functions involving cognitive, technical, and interpersonal elements.

Types of skills

Hard and soft skills

Employers and educators commonly distinguish hard skills — teachable, measurable abilities such as typing, operating machinery, programming, or speaking a foreign language — from soft skills, interpersonal and character attributes such as communication, teamwork, adaptability, and leadership. Labour economists note that both categories command wage premiums, and that social (soft) skills have grown in relative value as routine manual and cognitive tasks are automated.

Life skills

Life skills are abilities for adaptive and positive behaviour that enable individuals to deal effectively with the demands of everyday life — decision-making, problem-solving, creative and critical thinking, empathy, and coping with stress. The World Health Organization has promoted life-skills education in schools since the 1990s.

Motor and cognitive skills

Motor skills involve precisely timed movement of muscles to perform tasks such as catching, driving, or surgery, progressing with practice from conscious, effortful control to fluid automaticity. Cognitive skills are mental abilities used in thinking, learning, and problem-solving. Most real-world skills blend both: a surgeon's manual dexterity is inseparable from diagnostic judgement.

Skill acquisition

The classic model of skill acquisition (Fitts and Posner) describes three stages: a cognitive stage of understanding the task, an associative stage of refining performance through feedback, and an autonomous stage in which performance is fluent and requires little attention. Research on deliberate practice, popularised by K. Anders Ericsson, shows that expert performance depends on extended, effortful practice targeted at weaknesses with immediate feedback, rather than mere accumulated experience — though the popularised "10,000-hour rule" oversimplifies Ericsson's findings. Skills decay without use ("skill fade"), which is why safety-critical professions mandate refresher training.

Skills and work

Skill is a central concept in economics and workforce policy. Human capital theory treats skills as investments raising productivity and earnings. Economists debate skill-biased technological change — technology's tendency to raise demand for highly skilled workers — and the resulting wage polarisation. Governments run vocational education and apprenticeship systems to build workforce skills, and taxonomies such as ESCO and O*NET classify occupations by required skills. The terms upskilling and reskilling describe training incumbent workers for changing or new roles.
