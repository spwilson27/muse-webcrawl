Acupuncture is a form of alternative medicine and a component of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) in which thin needles are inserted into the body at specific points, known as acupuncture points. It is most commonly used to attempt pain relief, though acupuncturists say that it can also be used for a wide range of other conditions. Acupuncture is generally used only in combination with other forms of treatment.

History

The practice of acupuncture is thought to have originated in China more than 2,000 years ago. Stone and bone needles found in ancient tombs suggest that needling practices may predate written records. The foundational text of Chinese medicine, the Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon), compiled between the second century BCE and the second century CE, systematized theories of qi, meridians, and needling.

Acupuncture spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, and reached Europe in the 17th century through reports by Dutch and Jesuit travellers. Interest in the West grew considerably in the 1970s following US President Richard Nixon's visit to China in 1972.

Theory and practice

Traditional theory holds that health depends on the balanced flow of qi (vital energy) through channels called meridians. Illness is attributed to blockages or imbalances in this flow. The acupuncturist inserts fine needles at points along the meridians to restore balance. Modern variants include electroacupuncture, in which a small electric current is passed through the needles, and dry needling of myofascial trigger points, practised by some physiotherapists.

Effectiveness and scientific assessment

The conclusions of trials and systematic reviews concerning acupuncture are inconsistent, which has been attributed to poor study design and publication bias. There is evidence that acupuncture may provide short-term relief for some types of chronic pain, including chronic lower back pain, neck pain, osteoarthritis of the knee, and tension-type headache, although much of the effect may be attributable to placebo and contextual effects. Scientific research has not found a histological or physiological correlate for qi, meridians, or acupuncture points, and some practitioners no longer use them in explanation.

Safety

Acupuncture is generally safe when performed by trained practitioners using sterile, single-use needles. Reported adverse effects are usually minor, such as bruising, bleeding, and soreness. Serious complications, including infection and pneumothorax, are rare but have been reported, particularly with inadequate training or non-sterile technique.

Regulation

Regulation varies widely by jurisdiction. In China, acupuncture is an integral part of the state healthcare system. In the United States, acupuncturists are licensed in most states; in the United Kingdom, the practice is largely self-regulated. The World Health Organization has published benchmarks for acupuncture practice and training.
