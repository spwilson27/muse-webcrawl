Guided missile

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A guided missile being launched from a naval vessel.

A guided missile is a rocket-propelled weapon system that can be steered to a target after launch, typically by an onboard guidance system, by external commands, or by homing on signals emitted by or reflected from the target. Guided missiles are distinguished from unguided rockets by their guidance and control systems and carry conventional or nuclear warheads against targets on land, at sea, and in the air.

History

The first operational guided missiles were developed during World War II, including Germany's V-1 flying bomb and V-2 rocket and anti-ship weapons such as the Fritz X. After the war, the United States and Soviet Union expanded missile development during the Cold War, producing the first intercontinental ballistic missiles in the 1950s.

Guidance systems

Guidance methods include inertial navigation, satellite navigation (such as GPS), terrain-contour matching, radar homing (active, semi-active, and passive), infrared homing, laser guidance, wire guidance, and command guidance radio links. Modern missiles often combine several methods to improve accuracy and resist countermeasures.

Categories

Guided missiles are classified by launch platform and target: surface-to-surface, surface-to-air, air-to-air, air-to-surface, anti-ship, and anti-tank missiles. Ballistic missiles follow a high sub-orbital trajectory for most of their flight, while cruise missiles fly aerodynamically at relatively low altitude, powered throughout by jet engines.

Components

A typical guided missile consists of a propulsion system (rocket or jet motor), a guidance and control section with movable fins or thrust vectoring, a warhead (high explosive, shaped charge, fragmentation, or nuclear), and a fuze to detonate the warhead at the desired point.
