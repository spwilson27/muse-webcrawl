Clifton Suspension Bridge

Location: Bristol, England

Crosses: River Avon (Avon Gorge)

Design: Suspension bridge

Longest span: 214 m (702 ft)

Total length: 412 m (1,352 ft)

Designer: Isambard Kingdom Brunel

Construction: 1831–1864

The Clifton Suspension Bridge is a suspension bridge spanning the Avon Gorge and the River Avon, linking Clifton in Bristol to Leigh Woods in North Somerset. Since opening in 1864, it has been a toll bridge, the income from which provides funds for its maintenance. The bridge is built to a design by William Henry Barlow and John Hawkshaw, based on an earlier design by Isambard Kingdom Brunel. It is a Grade I listed building and forms part of the B3129 road.

History

The idea of building a bridge across the Avon Gorge originated in 1753, when Bristol wine merchant William Vick left £1,000 in his will towards a stone bridge. A competition in 1829 selected the 24-year-old Brunel's design as engineer, but construction, begun in 1831, was repeatedly halted by financial difficulties and the Bristol riots; work was abandoned in 1843 with the towers built but the deck and chains unfinished.

After Brunel's death in 1859, his colleagues at the Institution of Civil Engineers decided to complete the bridge as a memorial to him. Work restarted in 1862 using chains salvaged from Brunel's Hungerford Bridge in London, and the bridge finally opened on 8 December 1864.

Structure

The bridge's two stone towers rise 26 m (85 ft) above the deck, and the main span is 214 m (702 ft) long, suspended 75 m (245 ft) above the high water level of the River Avon. Though similar in size, the towers differ in construction details. The bridge carries roughly four million vehicles a year and is a defining symbol of Bristol.
