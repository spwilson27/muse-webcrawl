Bacteriophage T4 (officially Escherichia virus T4) is a bacteriophage that infects Escherichia coli bacteria. It is one of the seven T-even/T-odd phages identified by Max Delbrück and is among the largest and most complex viruses known, as well as one of the best characterised model organisms in molecular biology. T4 is a virulent phage: it follows only the lytic cycle, destroying its host cell.

Structure

The T4 virion is about 200 nm long and 90 nm wide. It consists of:

  • a prolate icosahedral head (capsid) containing the DNA genome;
  • a contractile tail with an inner tube surrounded by a sheath;
  • a hexagonal baseplate with tail spikes and six long tail fibres that recognise receptors (lipopolysaccharide and OmpC) on the host surface.

On infection, the tail sheath contracts like a syringe, driving the tail tube through the cell envelope and injecting the DNA into the host.

Genome

The T4 genome is about 168,903 base pairs of double-stranded DNA encoding roughly 289 proteins. It contains the modified base hydroxymethylcytosine instead of cytosine, protecting the DNA from host restriction enzymes. The genome is circularly permuted and terminally redundant, and its replication involves recombination-dependent initiation from multiple origins.

Life cycle

Adsorption and genome injection take minutes; early genes shut down host transcription and degrade the host chromosome, middle genes drive DNA replication, and late genes encode structural proteins and lysozyme. About 100–150 progeny phages are assembled per cell and released by lysis after roughly 25–30 minutes at 37 °C.

Role in research

T4 was central to foundational molecular genetics: the Hershey–Chase experiment used T2 (a close relative) to show DNA is the genetic material; Seymour Benzer's fine-structure mapping of the T4 rII locus defined the cistron and proved the gene is divisible by recombination; studies of T4 DNA polymerase, ligase and topoisomerase yielded widely used laboratory enzymes, and T4 genes were used to demonstrate that codons are triplets (via the rII frameshift studies building on Crick and Brenner's work).
