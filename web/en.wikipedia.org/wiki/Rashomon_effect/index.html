The Rashomon effect is a term related to the notorious unreliability of eyewitnesses: it describes a situation in which different people give contradictory yet individually plausible accounts of the same event, making it difficult or impossible to reconstruct what truly happened. It is named after Akira Kurosawa's 1950 film Rashomon, in which a murder is described in four mutually contradictory ways by its witnesses.

Origin of the term

In Rashomon, a bandit, a samurai's wife, the murdered samurai (speaking through a medium), and a woodcutter recount incompatible versions of a crime, each shaped by self-interest and shame. The anthropologist Karl G. Heider coined "Rashomon effect" in 1988 for the effect of subjectivity in perception and reporting.

Interpretations

The effect has been read as a commentary on the impossibility of objective truth (an interpretation Kurosawa resisted), as a study of ego and self-deception, and — in law and journalism — as a practical warning about witness credibility and conflicting testimony. It is distinct from but related to the unreliable narrator device.

Use in other fields

The term is used in social science for contradictory interpretations of research findings, in law for conflicting eyewitness accounts, and in software engineering there's even an unrelated use in ensemble learning research inspired by the same multiplicity of models.
