Nine Tripod Cauldrons

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bronze ding tripod vessels of the kind cast by Yu the Great

The Nine Tripod Cauldrons (Chinese: 九鼎; pinyin: Jiǔ Dǐng) were, in Chinese tradition, a collection of nine bronze ritual tripod vessels (ding) said to have been cast by Yu the Great, legendary founder of the Xia dynasty, using bronze contributed by the nine provinces of ancient China. They became the foremost symbol of legitimate imperial authority, and possession of the cauldrons was held to signify possession of tianxia — "all under heaven".

Legend and history

According to tradition, Yu cast the nine cauldrons after taming the great flood, decorating each with images of the creatures and products of one of the nine provinces so that travellers could recognize the spirits and dangers of each region. The cauldrons passed from the Xia to the Shang and then to the Zhou dynasty, the transfer of the vessels marking the transfer of the Mandate of Heaven. They were kept in the Zhou capital and displayed on ceremonial occasions.

As Zhou power declined, rival rulers coveted the cauldrons: in 606 BCE King Zhuang of Chu famously "asked about the weight of the cauldrons" (wen ding) while campaigning near the Zhou capital — an act understood ever since as a byword for coveting the throne. In 307 BCE King Wu of Qin was said to have injured himself fatally attempting to lift one of the cauldrons in a contest of strength. After the Qin conquest, the cauldrons disappeared; one popular tradition held that they were lost in the Si River near Pengcheng during their transport to the Qin capital, and later emperors, including Qin Shi Huang and Han Wudi, were said to have mounted dredging expeditions that failed to recover them.

Symbolism

The nine cauldrons stood for the unity of the realm: the phrase "fixing the cauldrons" (ding ding) came to mean establishing a capital or consolidating a dynasty, and the character ding itself connotes stability and authority. Later dynasties occasionally cast new sets of nine cauldrons to advertise their legitimacy, and in the modern era the imagery of the nine tripods has been revived in monuments celebrating Chinese unity, such as the China Millennium Monument cauldron in Beijing.
