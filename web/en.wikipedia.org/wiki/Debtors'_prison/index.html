Debtors' prison

A debtors' prison is a prison for people who are unable to pay a debt. Imprisonment for debt was widespread in Europe and North America from the medieval period until the nineteenth century: unlike most prisoners, debtors were typically held not as punishment for a crime but to coerce payment — by the debtor, or by family and friends — and were often kept indefinitely until the debt was settled.

England and Wales

Famous London debtors' prisons included the Fleet, the King's Bench Prison and the Marshalsea, the last immortalised in Dickens's Little Dorrit after the novelist's own father was imprisoned there in 1824. Prisoners with money could buy comfortable rooms, food and even permission to live outside the walls; the poor lived in squalor and depended on charity. Reformers attacked the system's obvious irrationality — an imprisoned debtor could not earn money to pay — and the Debtors Act 1869 largely abolished imprisonment for debt in England and Wales, the prisons closing over the following decades.

United States and modern practice

The United States abolished federal imprisonment for debt in 1833, and most state constitutions forbid it. Nevertheless, the jailing of people for unpaid fines, fees and child support — where inability to pay is not properly established — has been criticised by the ACLU and others as a modern analogue, and the Supreme Court held in Bearden v. Georgia (1983) that a person cannot be imprisoned solely for genuine inability to pay a fine. In a few countries, imprisonment for certain debts, such as bounced cheques, persists in some form.
