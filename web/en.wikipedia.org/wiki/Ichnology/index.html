Ichnology

Ichnology
FieldPalaeontology, geology, biology
SubjectTrace fossils and organism traces
Key termsIchnofossil, ichnogenus, ichnofacies

Ichnology is the study of traces made by organisms — such as tracks, trails, burrows, borings, and nests — whether preserved as trace fossils in rock or observed in modern environments. The name derives from the Greek ikhnos, meaning "track" or "footprint."

Scope

Ichnology divides into palaeoichnology, the study of fossil traces, and neoichnology, the study of traces made by living organisms, which provides analogues for interpreting the fossil record. Because a single animal can make many different traces and different animals can make similar ones, trace fossils are classified in their own parallel taxonomy of ichnogenera and ichnospecies, independent of the organisms that made them.

Uses

Trace fossils record behaviour rather than anatomy: they reveal how extinct animals walked, fed, burrowed, and interacted, and characteristic assemblages (ichnofacies) are used by geologists to reconstruct ancient environments such as water depth and energy. Famous examples include the Laetoli footprints of early hominins and dinosaur trackways that show herd behaviour and running speeds.
