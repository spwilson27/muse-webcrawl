The liberation of Paris (French: libération de Paris) was a battle that took place during World War II from 19 August 1944 until the surrender of the German garrison on 25 August 1944. Paris had been occupied by Nazi Germany since the signing of the Second Armistice at Compiègne on 22 June 1940; the capital remained relatively intact, and the uprising of the French Forces of the Interior (FFI), followed by the entry of General Leclerc's French 2nd Armoured Division and the US 4th Infantry Division, freed the city with far less destruction than many feared.

Background

After the Normandy breakout, Allied commanders initially intended to bypass Paris to avoid a costly urban battle and the burden of feeding two million civilians. The Resistance inside Paris, organised by the National Council of the Resistance and communist-led FFI commander Henri Rol-Tanguy, pressed for an uprising; a police strike began on 15 August, followed by a general strike. On 19 August the FFI rose, seizing the Préfecture de Police and erecting barricades across the city. Fearing that delay would bring massacres — and that Warsaw's uprising was being crushed as a warning — Charles de Gaulle persuaded Eisenhower to let Leclerc's division race for Paris.

The battle

Fighting between the FFI and the roughly 20,000-strong German garrison, commanded by General Dietrich von Choltitz, intensified on 22–23 August. A truce negotiated through Swedish consul Raoul Nordling partly held but broke down repeatedly. On the evening of 24 August, advance elements of Leclerc's division — the 9th Company "La Nueve", largely Spanish Republican exiles — reached the Hôtel de Ville. The main force entered on 25 August. Choltitz, who had disobeyed Hitler's orders to destroy the city's bridges and monuments, surrendered that afternoon at the Hôtel Meurice and signed the capitulation at the Gare Montparnasse.

Aftermath

On 26 August, de Gaulle led a triumphal parade from the Arc de Triomphe down the Champs-Élysées to Notre Dame, amid vast crowds and sporadic sniper fire, declaring that Paris had been "liberated by itself, liberated by its people". The Provisional Government of the French Republic established itself in the capital, and Paris resumed its role as France's capital, its monuments largely undamaged. The liberation became a foundational episode of post-war French memory, though the roles of the Resistance, the communists, the regular army and the Allies remain debated.
