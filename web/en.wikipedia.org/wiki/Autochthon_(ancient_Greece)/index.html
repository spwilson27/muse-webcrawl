In ancient Greece, an autochthon (Greek: αὐτόχθων, from autos, "self", and chthōn, "earth/land") was an inhabitant believed to have sprung directly from the soil of the land he inhabited, rather than having migrated from elsewhere. The concept was most famously claimed by the Athenians, who held that they had always occupied Attica continuously since their earth-born ancestors, and it became a central element of Athenian civic identity and democratic ideology.

Athenian autochthony

Athenian myth traced the city's kingship to Cecrops, who was represented as half man and half serpent because he was born from the earth. His successors included Erechtheus and Erichthonius, the latter born from the seed of Hephaestus that fell upon the earth (Gaia) after Athena wiped it away with wool; Erichthonius was raised by Athena and placed in a chest guarded by serpents. Funeral orations, such as those attributed to Lysias and echoed in Plato's Menexenus and Euripides' Ion, praised Athens as the land whose citizens were literally children of their own soil — a claim used to assert Athenian superiority over cities founded by immigrants, such as Thebes (founded by the Phoenician Cadmus) or the Dorian cities (descended from the invading Heracleidae).

Ideological functions

Scholars such as Nicole Loraux have analysed autochthony as a civic myth that imagined the citizen body as one great family sharing common descent, legitimating democratic equality among citizens while excluding women from political identity (the earth, not citizen mothers, was the true "mother") and underpinning doctrines of Athenian exceptionalism. The myth also contrasted Athenians with barbarians and with rival Greeks alike. Other peoples, including the Arcadians — who claimed to be older than the moon — and the Thebans (through the sown men, the Spartoi) had parallel earth-born traditions.

Later usage

The word survives in modern languages as "autochthonous", denoting peoples, languages, species or geological formations originating where they are found, as opposed to allochthonous (imported) ones; it is used in anthropology, ecology, geology and linguistics.
