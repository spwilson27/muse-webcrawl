Charge of the Light Brigade

The Charge of the Light Brigade was a cavalry charge of British light cavalry against Russian forces during the Battle of Balaclava in the Crimean War, on 25 October 1854. Some 600 troopers of the Light Brigade under the Earl of Cardigan rode, owing to a misunderstood order, down a valley swept by Russian artillery on three sides against a battery at its far end — "into the valley of Death", in Alfred, Lord Tennyson's poem, published weeks later, which made the blunder the most famous in British military history.

The blunder

Raglan, watching from the heights, ordered the cavalry to retake guns the Russians were removing from captured redoubts on the Causeway Heights — a position invisible from the valley floor. The written order, carried by Captain Nolan, was read by Lucan and Cardigan as directing an attack on the Russian battery at the valley's end. Cardigan led the brigade down the mile-and-a-quarter valley under fire from both flanks and the front, reached the guns, sabred the gunners, and — unsupported — rode back. Nolan was killed early, perhaps trying to redirect the charge. The French Chasseurs d'Afrique cleared one flank's guns, limiting the slaughter.

Legacy

Recriminations among Raglan, Lucan and Cardigan (Nolan, dead, could not testify) poisoned reputations. Tennyson's poem — "Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die" — fixed the charge as the archetype of military obedience and staff failure, and the phrase "the six hundred" entered the language. Survivors of the charge were celebrated into old age; the last, Edwin Hughes, died in 1927.
