Subgroup

In group theory, a branch of mathematics, a subgroup of a group G is a subset H of G that is itself a group under the operation of G. Given a group G under a binary operation ∗, a subset H of G is called a subgroup of G if H also forms a group under the operation ∗. A subgroup H is written H ≤ G; it is a proper subgroup if H ≠ G, and the subgroup consisting only of the identity element is called the trivial subgroup.

Subgroup criterion

A non-empty subset H of G is a subgroup if and only if for all a, b ∈ H, the element a−1b also lies in H (the one-step subgroup test); for finite groups it suffices that H be non-empty and closed under the operation. Equivalent elementary conditions are that H is closed under the operation and under taking inverses, and contains the identity element.

Cosets and Lagrange's theorem

For a subgroup H, the left cosets gH partition G into classes of equal size |H|. It follows that the order of a finite subgroup divides the order of the group — Lagrange's theorem. The number of cosets is the index [G : H]. Important special classes include normal subgroups (whose cosets form a quotient group), cyclic subgroups generated by a single element, and the centre of a group.

Examples

The even integers form a subgroup of the integers under addition. The alternating group An is a subgroup of index 2 in the symmetric group Sn. Every group has the trivial subgroup {e} and the improper subgroup G itself. The set of subgroups of a group, ordered by inclusion, forms the subgroup lattice, a standard tool for visualising group structure.
