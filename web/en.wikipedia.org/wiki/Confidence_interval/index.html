Confidence interval

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A series of horizontal lines (intervals) around sample means, most crossing the true population mean]

In statistics, a confidence interval (CI) is a type of interval estimate computed from sample data, giving a range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter. A 95% confidence interval is constructed by a procedure that, in repeated sampling, would capture the true parameter in 95% of cases.

The concept was introduced by Jerzy Neyman in 1937 as the frequentist answer to inverse-probability reasoning: the parameter is fixed, the interval random. A 95% CI for a mean typically takes the form estimate ± 1.96 × (standard error), relying on the central limit theorem.

Interpretation

Strictly, one cannot say 'there is a 95% probability the parameter is in this interval' (in frequentist terms) — a subtlety widely misreported; Bayesian credible intervals do carry that direct interpretation, at the cost of a prior. Reporting CIs rather than mere p-values is recommended practice (APA style, medical journals) because they show effect size and precision together.

Width shrinks with sample size (as 1/√n) and grows with confidence level: 99% intervals are wider than 95% ones.
