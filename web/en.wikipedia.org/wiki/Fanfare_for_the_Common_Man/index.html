Fanfare for the Common Man is a musical work by the American composer Aaron Copland. It was written in 1942 for the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra under conductor Eugene Goossens, and premiered on 12 March 1943. Scored for brass and percussion, it was inspired in part by a speech by Vice President Henry A. Wallace proclaiming the dawning of the "Century of the Common Man". It is arguably Copland's most famous work and forms the basis of the final movement of his Third Symphony.

History

During the Second World War, Goossens — following a practice he had begun in the First World War with British composers — commissioned fanfares from eighteen American composers to be played at the start of Cincinnati Symphony concerts during the 1942–43 season as stirring, patriotic curtain-raisers. Others who contributed included Walter Piston, William Grant Still, Roy Harris, and Paul Creston, but Copland's entry was the only one to enter the permanent repertoire.

Copland considered titles such as "Fanfare for a Solemn Ceremony" and "Fanfare for Four Freedoms" before settling on the present title, drawn from Wallace's speech of May 1942 envisioning a century belonging to ordinary people rather than empires. Copland later wrote that he chose the title because he felt the common man, who was doing the fighting and enduring the home-front burdens, deserved a fanfare as much as any soldier or statesman.

Music

The piece opens with a solo timpani and bass-drum strokes, answered by a solemn, hymn-like trumpet theme built from open fourths and fifths. The fanfare is passed around the brass section — horns, trumpets, then trombones and tuba — over slow, processional percussion rhythms, building from restraint to a massive, blazing conclusion. Its spacious sonorities, diatonic melody, and deliberate pacing exemplify the populist, "American" sound Copland developed in the 1930s and 1940s in works such as El Salón México, Billy the Kid, and Appalachian Spring. In 1946 Copland incorporated the fanfare, nearly complete, as the principal theme of the finale of his Third Symphony.

Legacy and adaptations

The fanfare has been used ubiquitously in American public life — at ceremonies, sporting events, memorials, and launches — and has been sampled and adapted across popular culture. The progressive rock group Emerson, Lake & Palmer recorded a famous rock adaptation on their 1977 album Works Volume 1, which reached number two on the UK singles chart and became one of the band's signature pieces. Copland is said to have given his blessing to the arrangement after hearing it. The opening has also served as a theme for broadcasts and space missions, including music played for astronauts.
