The Empire of Nicaea was the largest of the Byzantine Greek successor states created after Crusaders of the Fourth Crusade captured Constantinople in 1204 and founded the Latin Empire. Centred on Nicaea (modern İznik) in northwestern Anatolia, it preserved Byzantine government, the Orthodox patriarchate in exile, and the imperial claim until its forces recovered Constantinople in 1261, restoring the Byzantine Empire.

History

The state was founded by Theodore I Laskaris, son-in-law of the deposed emperor Alexios III, who rallied Byzantine resistance in Anatolia and was crowned emperor by the exiled patriarch in 1208. Nicaea successfully defended itself against the Latins — decisively at Poimanenon in 1224 — and against the Seljuk Turks, whom Theodore defeated at Antioch on the Meander (1211), where the sultan Kaykhusraw I was killed.

Under John III Doukas Vatatzes (r. 1222–1254), widely considered Nicaea's greatest ruler, the empire doubled its territory, absorbed most of the Latin Empire's remaining lands, recovered Thessalonica (1246), and built a prosperous, carefully managed economy with thriving agriculture and state workshops. His son Theodore II Laskaris (r. 1254–1258), a scholar-emperor, maintained the state's position against Bulgarians and Mongols.

In 1259 the regent Michael Palaiologos seized the throne as Michael VIII. On 25 July 1261 his general Alexios Strategopoulos slipped into Constantinople with a small force while the Latin garrison was absent, ending the Latin Empire and restoring Byzantine rule — the culmination of the Nicaean state's purpose.

Government and society

Nicaea reproduced the institutions of the old empire in compressed form: a court, senate, patriarchate, and thematic-style military organisation in Anatolia. It became a centre of Greek learning, where scholars such as Nikephoros Blemmydes kept Byzantine intellectual life alive, and it consciously cultivated a Hellenic identity in opposition to the "Latin" occupiers.
