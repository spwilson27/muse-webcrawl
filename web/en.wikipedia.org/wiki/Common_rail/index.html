Common rail

[Image: Common rail diesel injection system]

Common rail direct fuel injection is a fuel delivery system employed in modern diesel engines. The distinguishing feature of common rail is that a single (common) high-pressure fuel rail feeds solenoid valves, as opposed to a low-pressure fuel pump feeding unit injectors or individual injector pumps.

In a common rail system, a high-pressure pump stores a reservoir of fuel at very high pressure — up to and above 2,000 bar (29,000 psi) in modern systems — in the rail. The engine control unit (ECU) controls the pressure independently of engine speed and opens each injector electronically, allowing the timing, quantity and rate of injection to be precisely metered. Multiple injections per combustion cycle (pilot, main and post injections) can be used to reduce noise, vibration and emissions.

History

The common rail principle was developed over the twentieth century, with prototypes by Vickers in the early 1900s and work by the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Zurich and the Japanese company Denso. The first commercially successful application in a production vehicle was by Denso for Hino trucks in 1995. For passenger cars, research by Magneti Marelli, Centro Ricerche Fiat and Elasis led to systems sold to Robert Bosch GmbH; the technology debuted in 1997 in the Alfa Romeo 156 JTD and the Mercedes-Benz 220 CDI.

Advantages

Because injection pressure is independent of engine speed and load, common rail systems deliver better fuel atomisation, lower particulate and NOx emissions after treatment, improved fuel economy and quieter operation than older distributor-pump or cam-driven systems. They are now standard on virtually all road-vehicle diesel engines and are widely used in marine, locomotive and industrial engines.
