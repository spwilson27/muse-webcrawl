Political decoy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A political decoy is a candidate, party or campaign used to draw attention, votes or resources away from a competitor, or to mislead voters, journalists or opponents about the real source or target of political strength. Decoy strategies appear in many electoral systems and range from openly satirical "joke" candidacies promoted by rivals to systematic efforts in authoritarian states to simulate opposition.

Types

Vote-splitting decoys

In plurality (first-past-the-post) systems, a decoy candidate with a similar name, party label or demographic appeal to a genuine contender may be entered — sometimes with covert funding from a rival campaign — in order to split that contender's vote. "Namesake" candidates, who share a surname with a famous politician, have repeatedly been fielded in India, the Philippines and Russia to confuse voters. Similar tactics include registering parties with names nearly identical to established parties, a practice that has led several countries to tighten party-registration rules.

Spoiler and stalking-horse candidates

Related concepts include the spoiler candidate, who cannot win but changes the outcome between the leading candidates, and the stalking horse, who challenges an incumbent party leader primarily to test support and clear the way for a stronger challenger.

Managed opposition

In electoral authoritarian systems, regimes may sponsor decoy opposition parties and candidates to fragment the genuine opposition, occupy the ballot and media space, and lend elections a facade of competition. Analysts of Russian politics, for example, have described Kremlin-backed "systemic opposition" parties and spoiler candidates fielded against independent contenders; similar claims are made about "crocodile" or clone parties in several post-Soviet and African states.

Security decoys

The term also covers physical decoys for leaders' security — lookalikes and duplicate motorcades used to mislead would-be attackers. Political doubles have been attributed (with varying evidence) to figures including Joseph Stalin, Saddam Hussein and Adolf Hitler.

Legality and countermeasures

Many jurisdictions prohibit materially deceptive candidacies, fraudulent party names and undisclosed sponsorship of candidates. Election commissions may reject confusingly similar party names or symbols, and courts have annulled results where decoy tactics demonstrably misled voters. Nonetheless, enforcement is difficult because entering an election is itself a democratic right, and distinguishing a decoy from a sincere but hopeless candidate is often impossible.
