Haarlemmermeer (Dutch pronunciation: [ˌɦaːrlɛmərˈmeːr]) is a municipality in the province of North Holland in the Netherlands, consisting almost entirely of a single large polder reclaimed from a former lake of the same name. Its principal town is Hoofddorp, and it also contains Amsterdam Airport Schiphol, one of Europe's busiest airports.

History

The Haarlemmermeer — "Haarlem's lake" — grew over the centuries as smaller lakes merged through peat erosion and storm flooding, eventually threatening the nearby cities of Haarlem, Leiden and Amsterdam; in storms it was nicknamed the "Water Wolf". After repeated floods, including a devastating one in 1836, King William I ordered the lake drained. A ring canal (the Ringvaart) was dug around it, and three great steam pumping stations — the Leeghwater, the Cruquius and the Lijnden — emptied the lake between 1849 and 1852, an emblematic triumph of Dutch hydraulic engineering. The Cruquius station, with one of the largest steam engines ever built, is now a museum.

The polder today

The drained lake bed, lying about four metres below sea level, is exceptionally flat and laid out in a regular grid of roads, canals and plots. Originally agricultural, the polder has urbanised rapidly: Hoofddorp, Nieuw-Vennep and Badhoevedorp grew as commuter towns for Amsterdam, while Schiphol — which began in 1916 as a military airfield on the polder's edge — expanded into a major international hub occupying a large share of the municipality. In 2019 the neighbouring municipality of Haarlemmerliede en Spaarnwoude merged into Haarlemmermeer.

Several historic fortifications of the Stelling van Amsterdam (Defence Line of Amsterdam), a UNESCO World Heritage Site, stand around the polder's edges, built to defend the reclamation works and the approach to the capital.
