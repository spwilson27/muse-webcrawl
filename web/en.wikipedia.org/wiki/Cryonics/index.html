Cryonics

Cryonics (from Greek: κρύος kryos, meaning "cold") is the low-temperature freezing (usually at −196 °C / −320.8 °F / 77.1 K) and storage of human remains, with the speculative hope that resuscitation may be possible in the future. Cryonics is generally regarded as a pseudoscience by the mainstream scientific community, although its proponents argue that it is a rational, if unproven, life-extension strategy.

History

The modern era of cryonics began in 1962 with the publication of Robert Ettinger's The Prospect of Immortality, which proposed that freezing people at the point of legal death might allow future medical technology to repair the damage caused by disease, ageing, and the freezing process itself. Ettinger founded the Cryonics Institute in Michigan in 1976. The first man to be cryopreserved was Dr. James Bedford, a psychology professor, frozen in 1967; his body remains preserved at the Alcor Life Extension Foundation in Scottsdale, Arizona.

Procedure

Cryonicists hold that a patient must be declared legally dead before procedures begin, because current law permits preservation only of the deceased. Ideally, a standby team begins cooling and cardiopulmonary support immediately after cardiac arrest to minimize ischemic injury. Blood is washed out and replaced with a cryoprotectant solution — a medical-grade antifreeze mixture — which is perfused through the body to reduce ice formation, a process called vitrification. The patient is then cooled gradually to liquid-nitrogen temperature and stored in a vacuum-insulated dewar, often suspended head-down so that any leak leaves the brain in the coldest region.

Criticism and scientific view

Mainstream cryobiologists are skeptical. Critics argue that the damage caused by cryoprotectant toxicity, thermal stress fractures, and the original disease or cause of death is so extensive that revival with present or foreseeable technology is extremely unlikely, and that cryonics offers false hope. Some scientists also distinguish between "death" as a legal status and "information-theoretic death" — the point at which the brain's structure encoding memory and personality is lost — which cryonics advocates use to argue that a cryopreserved patient may not be truly dead by the standards of future medicine. No mammal has ever been revived from full-body cryopreservation at liquid nitrogen temperatures.

Culture and notable patients

Cryonics has been a recurring theme in science fiction and popular culture. Notable cryopreserved individuals include baseball player Ted Williams, whose preservation at Alcor in 2002 generated controversy, and futurist FM-2030. A persistent urban legend claims Walt Disney was cryopreserved; he was in fact cremated.
