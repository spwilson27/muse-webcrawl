Hull–White model

In financial mathematics, the Hull–White model is a model of future interest rates. In its primary formulation it is a single-factor short-rate model in which the instantaneous short rate follows a mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process under the risk-neutral measure. It was proposed by John Hull and Alan White in 1990 as an extension of the Vasicek model that can be fitted exactly to the initial term structure of interest rates.

Formulation

The one-factor model is dr(t) = [θ(t) − α r(t)] dt + σ dW(t), where α > 0 is the speed of mean reversion, σ the volatility, and the time-dependent function θ(t) is chosen so that model bond prices reproduce the observed initial yield curve exactly. Because the model is affine, zero-coupon bond prices have a closed form P(t,T) = A(t,T) e−B(t,T) r(t), and European bond options and caps can be priced analytically; the model is widely used for swaptions and other interest-rate derivatives, valuing them on lattices or by Monte Carlo simulation.

Two-factor version and usage

Hull and White also proposed a two-factor version, adding a second stochastic factor (interpreted as a stochastic central tendency), which produces a richer, less perfectly correlated term-structure dynamics and can match the term structure of volatilities more closely. The model's strengths are its exact fit to the initial curve, analytic tractability in the one-factor case, and the absence of negative-rate arbitrage inconsistencies that troubled earlier models (though, as a normal model, it does permit negative rates — a feature that became realistic after 2008). Its limitations include a single volatility parameter in the basic version and imperfect fits to the volatility smile for some products.
