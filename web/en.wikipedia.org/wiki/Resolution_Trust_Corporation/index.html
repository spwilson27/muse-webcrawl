Resolution Trust Corporation

The Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC) was a United States government-owned asset management company created by the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989 (FIRREA) to resolve the savings and loan crisis. Between 1989 and its closure in 1995 it took over and liquidated 747 failed thrift institutions, disposing of their assets and paying insured depositors.

Mandate and operations

When a thrift was declared insolvent, the RTC was appointed its conservator or receiver: it would sell the institution to a healthy buyer, merge it, pay out deposits, or run it temporarily while selling its loan and real-estate portfolio. The RTC held assets with a book value of roughly $450 billion at its peak and became one of the largest owners of commercial real estate in the country, particularly in the southwestern "see-through" office markets of Texas and Arizona.

Asset disposition

To move assets quickly, the RTC pioneered large-scale bulk sales, auctions and securitisation of commercial mortgages, and created equity partnerships in which private investors managed and sold portfolios while sharing gains with the government. These methods influenced later crisis programmes, including the 2008 Troubled Asset Relief Program.

Cost and dissolution

The total cost of the thrift clean-up, including earlier FSLIC outlays, is generally estimated at about $150–160 billion, of which taxpayers bore roughly $124 billion. The RTC completed its work ahead of schedule and transferred its remaining functions to the FDIC on 31 December 1995. It is usually cited as a comparatively successful example of a temporary "bad bank" resolution agency.
