Tyndall effect

The Tyndall effect is the scattering of light as it passes through a colloid or a very fine suspension, making the beam itself visible from the side. It is named after the nineteenth-century Irish physicist John Tyndall, who studied the phenomenon extensively in the 1860s. The effect provides a simple test distinguishing colloids from true solutions, whose particles are too small to scatter light noticeably.

Explanation

Particles in a colloid (roughly 1 nm to 1 μm across) are large enough to scatter light in all directions, so a beam — a torch in fog, headlights in mist, a shaft of sunlight in a dusty room — becomes visible along its path. Shorter (blue) wavelengths are scattered more strongly than longer ones, which is why the scattered light from very fine particles appears bluish; this borders on Rayleigh scattering, the mechanism behind the blue of the sky, while larger particles scatter all wavelengths more evenly (Mie scattering) and appear white.

Everyday examples

The Tyndall effect explains the visible beam of a projector in a smoky cinema, the blue tint of certain eyes and of skimmed milk's opalescence, and the way a laser pointer's path shows up in a colloidal solution but not in pure water. It is used in nephelometry to measure particle concentrations, and medically the "Tyndall phenomenon" is observed in dermatology and ophthalmology.
