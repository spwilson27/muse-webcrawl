Catenoid

The catenoid is a type of surface in geometry: a surface of revolution generated by rotating a catenary curve about an axis. It was discovered by Leonhard Euler in 1744, who proved that it is a minimal surface — a surface whose mean curvature is zero everywhere — the first minimal surface known after the trivial plane. Euler showed that among all surfaces spanning a given boundary curve, the catenoid (where it exists) has the least area.

Geometry

Rotating the catenary y = c cosh(x/c) about the x-axis yields the catenoid. In parametric form it may be written with parameters u and v as x = c cosh(v/c) cos u, y = c cosh(v/c) sin u, z = v. A physical model is produced by dipping two circular wire rings into soapy water and drawing them apart: the soap film between them takes the catenoid's shape, until, beyond a critical separation (roughly 1.33 times the ring diameter in total), the solution ceases to exist and the film collapses into two separate discs — an illustration of the instability known from the Goldschmidt discontinuous solution.

Properties and relations

The catenoid and the helicoid are associate minimal surfaces: they can be bent into one another locally without stretching, through a family of minimal surfaces, although the catenoid is topologically an annulus while the helicoid is simply connected. The catenoid also appears in architecture, where its doubly curved shape gives strong, material-efficient roofs and cooling-tower-like structures.
