Diplomatics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diplomatics (in American English also diplomatic) is a critical, historical discipline concerned with the analysis and interpretation of historical documents, particularly documents issued by official authorities such as royal, papal, and notarial chanceries. Originally developed to test the authenticity of medieval charters and diplomas, it studies the forms, formulas, materials, scripts, seals, and conventions by which documents were created, in order to establish their genuineness and to understand the administrative processes that produced them.

History

The discipline was founded by the Benedictine monk Jean Mabillon, whose De re diplomatica (1681) systematically set out criteria for distinguishing genuine documents from forgeries. The work arose from a controversy over Merovingian charters held by the Abbey of Saint-Denis, whose authenticity had been questioned by the Bollandist Daniel Papebroch; Mabillon's methodical response established principles still in use. In the following centuries, diplomatics was developed further by scholars such as René-Prosper Tassin and Charles-François Toustain in France, and by Theodor von Sickel and the Monumenta Germaniae Historica in the German-speaking world.

Method

Diplomatic analysis distinguishes a document's external features (support material, ink, handwriting, seals, and layout) from its internal features (language, style, and the structure of its text). A typical medieval charter is analysed in parts: the protocol (invocation, intitulation, inscription, salutation), the text (arenga, promulgation, narration, disposition, sanction), and the eschatocol (dating clause, subscriptions, and validation). Deviations from the known practice of the issuing authority can reveal forgery, interpolation, or later copying.

Modern diplomatics has extended its methods beyond medieval charters to contemporary records, including electronic records, where it informs archival science and digital preservation.
