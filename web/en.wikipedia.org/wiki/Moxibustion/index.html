Moxibustion

Moxibustion is a traditional Chinese medicine therapy that consists of burning dried mugwort (moxa, from Artemisia argyi or Artemisia vulgaris) on or near particular points on the body, usually the same points used in acupuncture. Practitioners believe that warming these points stimulates the flow of qi and dispels cold and dampness from the body. In 2010, UNESCO inscribed acupuncture and moxibustion of traditional Chinese medicine on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Practice

In direct moxibustion, a small cone of moxa is placed on the skin at an acupuncture point and burned; in the older "scarring" form the cone is allowed to burn down to the skin, producing a blister and scar, while the non-scarring form removes it before the skin burns. In indirect moxibustion — the more common modern practice — the practitioner holds a cigar-shaped moxa stick close to the skin until the area reddens, or burns moxa on top of an acupuncture needle, or on an insulating layer of ginger, garlic or salt placed on the point.

Uses and evidence

Traditionally moxibustion is used for conditions attributed to "cold" or stagnation, including digestive complaints, menstrual pain, arthritis and certain chronic pains. Its best-known modern use is the attempt to turn a breech baby before delivery by heating the point BL67 (Zhiyin) beside the little toenail; studies of this application have produced mixed results. Systematic reviews of the wider literature have generally found the evidence for specific clinical benefits to be weak or inconclusive, owing to small trials and high risk of bias. Risks of the therapy include burns, blistering, infection and smoke exposure.

History

Heat therapy with mugwort is among the oldest Chinese medical practices, described in texts such as the Huangdi Neijing. The practice spread across East and Southeast Asia, where it remains in use in traditional Korean, Japanese (where it is called okyū), Vietnamese and Mongolian medicine.
