Clarity Act

The Clarity Act (Loi sur la clarté) is an Act of the Parliament of Canada providing conditions under which the Government of Canada would enter into negotiations with a province that had voted by referendum to secede. It was enacted in 2000 in response to the close result of the 1995 Quebec referendum and to the Supreme Court of Canada's 1998 opinion in the Quebec Secession Reference.

Background

In the 1995 referendum, the Quebec sovereignty option was defeated by just 50.58 per cent to 49.42 per cent, on a question that critics called ambiguous because it contemplated sovereignty after an offer of partnership with Canada. Prime Minister Jean Chrétien's government sought clarification of the rules for any future referendum. In the Secession Reference, the Supreme Court held that Quebec had no right to unilateral secession under the Canadian constitution or international law, but that a clear majority vote in favour of a clear question would give rise to a constitutional obligation on the rest of Canada to negotiate in good faith.

Provisions

The Act gives the House of Commons the power to decide, before or after a provincial referendum, whether the referendum question is "clear"; a question that merely asks for a mandate to negotiate, or that avoids asking directly about secession, is not clear. After a vote, the House also determines whether a "clear majority" has expressed itself, considering the size of the majority, voter turnout, and any irregularities — deliberately not fixing a specific numerical threshold. If both conditions are met, the government may negotiate secession, but any such negotiation must address the division of assets and liabilities, borders, the rights of Aboriginal peoples and minorities, and other matters, and secession would still require a constitutional amendment. The Act provides that a unilateral declaration of independence would not be recognised.

Reactions

The Act was strongly opposed by Quebec sovereigntists and by the Quebec National Assembly, which in 2000 passed Bill 99 asserting Quebec's right to determine its own future and that 50 per cent plus one is a sufficient majority. The Clarity Act nevertheless applied its rules after the fact to debates over the 2014 Scottish independence referendum and other secession movements abroad, where it was frequently cited as a model — and sometimes as a cautionary example — for regulating secession referendums. Minister Stéphane Dion, who had sparred publicly with Quebec premier Lucien Bouchard in an exchange of open letters, was its principal architect.
