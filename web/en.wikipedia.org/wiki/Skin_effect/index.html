Skin effect

The skin effect is the tendency of an alternating electric current (AC) to become distributed within a conductor such that the current density is largest near the surface of the conductor and decreases exponentially with depth. The effect causes the effective resistance of a conductor to increase with frequency, because less of the cross-section carries current.

The depth at which the current density falls to 1/e (about 37%) of its surface value is called the skin depth, δ = √(2ρ/ωμ), where ρ is resistivity, ω the angular frequency, and μ the permeability. For copper at 60 Hz the skin depth is about 8.5 mm; at 1 MHz it is only about 66 µm. The phenomenon was described by Lord Kelvin and analyzed by Oliver Heaviside and others in the 1880s.

Cause

A changing current induces eddy currents in the conductor that oppose the current flow in the centre and reinforce it at the surface, following from Maxwell's equations and Lenz's law.

Mitigation

Engineers counter the skin effect with hollow tubular conductors for busbars, silver plating of RF components, and litz wire — bundles of individually insulated strands woven so each strand samples all positions in the cross-section — widely used in switch-mode power supplies and radio coils at frequencies up to a few megahertz.
