Diamond cutting

Diamond cutting is the practice of shaping a rough diamond into a faceted gem. The process requires specialised knowledge, tools, equipment and techniques because of the extreme hardness and value of the material. A diamond cutter (sometimes called a lapidarist for gem work generally) must plan each stone carefully: the final cut determines the gem's brilliance, fire and scintillation, and mistakes can destroy much of a rough stone's value.

Stages

  • Planning — the rough stone is studied, today often with computer scanning and modelling, to decide the shapes and sizes of gems it can yield, balancing weight retention against clarity and cut quality.
  • Cleaving or sawing — the rough is divided along its crystal grains by cleaving with a blade, or sawn with a phosphor-bronze blade impregnated with diamond dust, or by laser.
  • Bruting (girdling) — two diamonds are set against each other on a lathe and ground against one another to form the round outline of the girdle.
  • Blocking and polishing (faceting) — the main facets are ground and then polished on a rotating cast-iron lap (scaif) charged with diamond powder and oil, held at precise angles using a tang and dop.

History

Diamond cutting developed in India, the earliest source of diamonds, and in European centres such as Venice, Bruges and Amsterdam; Antwerp became the dominant cutting centre from the 15th century. Lodewyk van Bercken is traditionally credited with the scaif polishing wheel in the 15th century. Marcel Tolkowsky's 1919 mathematical analysis of the round brilliant cut set the proportions still used as a benchmark for maximising light return.

Cutting centres

Today most of the world's diamonds by volume are cut in Surat, India, while Antwerp, Tel Aviv, New York and Botswana handle significant shares, particularly of larger and higher-value stones. The term "cut" in diamond grading (as in the "four Cs") refers to the quality of the proportions and finish, the only characteristic entirely determined by human workmanship.
