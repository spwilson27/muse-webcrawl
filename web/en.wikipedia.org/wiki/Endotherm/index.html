Endotherm

An endotherm (from Greek endon "within" and thermē "heat") is an organism that maintains its body at a metabolically favourable temperature, largely by heat released from internal bodily functions. Endothermy is the defining physiological strategy of birds and mammals, colloquially called "warm-blooded" animals, in contrast to ectotherms, which rely mainly on external heat sources.

Mechanisms

Endotherms generate heat as a by-product of metabolism, especially in organs such as the liver, brain, and heart, and increase heat production through shivering and non-shivering thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue. Insulation — fur, feathers, or blubber — reduces heat loss. The hypothalamus acts as a thermostat, triggering sweating, panting, and changes in blood flow to shed excess heat, or vasoconstriction and piloerection to conserve it.

Advantages and costs

A stable internal temperature lets endotherms remain active across a wide range of ambient temperatures and sustain high levels of aerobic activity, supporting endurance hunting, flight, and large brains. The cost is enormous energy expenditure: a resting endotherm may use roughly ten times the energy of an ectotherm of similar size, requiring frequent feeding.

Variations

Some endotherms allow body temperature to vary: hibernators and daily torpor users (such as hummingbirds) drop their temperature to save energy (heterothermy). Regional endothermy occurs in some fish — tunas and lamnid sharks keep swimming muscles and viscera warm — and leatherback turtles retain heat through size and insulation.
