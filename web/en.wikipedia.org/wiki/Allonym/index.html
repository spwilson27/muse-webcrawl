Allonym

An allonym is a book or other work published under the name of a person other than its actual author — in other words, a real person's name used as the credit for someone else's work. The term derives from the Greek allos, "other", and onyma, "name". An allonym differs from a pseudonym, which is an invented fictitious name, and from an anonym, a work published with no name at all.

Usage and examples

Allonyms have been used for pious, commercial and fraudulent purposes. Works of Christian antiquity were frequently attributed to apostles or church fathers to lend them authority — a practice scholars call pseudepigraphy. In the commercial book trade, the name of a celebrated author might be borrowed for works they did not write: after his death, novels continued to appear under the name of Karl May from other hands, and in modern publishing the names of deceased thriller writers such as Robert Ludlum and Tom Clancy have been used as brands on sequels written by other authors, sometimes with disclosure on the cover ("Robert Ludlum's™"). Ghostwriting under contract, where a celebrity "authors" a book written by an unnamed collaborator, is an adjacent practice usually classed separately from the historical allonym.

Related terms

Bibliographers distinguish several kinds of false or altered attribution: the pseudonym (invented name), the anonym (no name), the allonym (another real person's name), the apocryphon (a work of doubtful attribution to an ancient author), and the ghostwriter arrangement in which the credited author's role is nominal. Cataloguing rules in libraries provide notes to record the true authorship where it is known.
