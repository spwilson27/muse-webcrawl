Marshal of France

Marshal of France (French: Maréchal de France) is a French military distinction, not a rank, awarded to generals for exceptional achievements. Since 1848 the title has been awarded in peacetime only for outstanding service in command of an army in war, and holders are presented with a baton adorned with stars, the bâton de maréchal, which symbolises the dignity.

History

The office originated in the twelfth century, when the Marshal of France was one of the Great Officers of the Crown, responsible for the army's horses and, later, for command. Under the Ancien Régime the dignity was conferred sparingly, often on commanders in the wars of Louis XIV, including Turenne and Villars. Napoleon I revived the title on a grand scale, creating 26 marshals of the Empire (including Bernadotte, Davout, Lannes, Masséna, Ney and Soult) between 1804 and 1815.

Later holders

After the Restoration and under the July Monarchy and the Second Empire, the distinction continued to be granted to successful commanders. In the First World War it was conferred on Joffre, Foch — who also became Marshal of Poland and Field Marshal of Britain — Pétain, Lyautey, Franchet d'Espèrey, Fayolle and Maunoury. In the Second World War, only Philippe Leclerc de Hauteclocque, Jean de Lattre de Tassigny and Alphonse Juin received the dignity. Marshal Pétain was stripped of the title after his trial in 1945. The last appointment, posthumously for Marie-Pierre Kœnig in 1984, was a rare peacetime honour.

Status

Marshals of France are considered above all other French military ranks and are accorded the precedence of a five-star general. The title is distinct from that of marshal in other armies, and from the military office once held by marine commanders embarked aboard French warships.
