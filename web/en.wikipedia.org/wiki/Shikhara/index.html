Shikhara

A shikhara (Sanskrit: शिखर, "mountain peak") is the rising tower over the sanctum (garbhagriha) of a Hindu temple in the North Indian Nagara style of architecture. It is the most prominent and characteristic feature of the Nagara temple, symbolising Mount Meru, the sacred axis of the world. The corresponding tower in South Indian Dravidian architecture is the vimana.

Form

The classic shikhara is curvilinear: its sides curve inward as they rise, and its surface is articulated with horizontal bands and carved vertical ribs. It is crowned by a large ribbed, cushion-like stone called the amalaka, on which sits the finial pot, the kalasha. Three principal varieties are distinguished in the architectural texts: the Latina (simple curvilinear tower, common from about the 6th–7th centuries), the Phamsana (a stepped, pyramidal roof of slabs), and the Valabhi (a barrel-roofed, wagon-vault form).

Development

Early free-standing stone temples such as the Dashavatara Temple at Deogarh (c. 500 CE) show early forms of the tower. The shikhara grew taller and more elaborate under the Pratiharas, Chandelas, Solankis, and the rulers of Odisha — whose temples at Bhubaneswar, Puri, and Konark call the tower the deul — reaching celebrated heights at the Kandariya Mahadeva Temple at Khajuraho and the Brihadishvara Temple in the south. The silhouette of the shikhara also influenced the spires of Jain temples and, beyond India, the towers of Khmer and Javanese temples.
