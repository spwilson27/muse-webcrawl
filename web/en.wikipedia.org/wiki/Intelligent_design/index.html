Intelligent design

Intelligent design (ID) is a pseudoscientific argument for the existence of God, presented by its proponents as an evidence-based scientific theory about life's origins. Proponents claim that "certain features of the universe and of living things are best explained by an intelligent cause, not an undirected process such as natural selection." The movement arose in the United States in the late 1980s, centred on the Discovery Institute's Center for Science and Culture, following court rulings that barred the teaching of creation science in public schools.

Key concepts

Central arguments include irreducible complexity, popularised by biochemist Michael Behe, which holds that certain biological systems composed of several interacting parts — such as the bacterial flagellum and the blood-clotting cascade — could not have evolved by gradual modification because removing any part stops the system functioning; and specified complexity, developed by mathematician William Dembski, which proposes a statistical criterion for detecting design. The mainstream scientific community rejects these arguments, pointing to documented evolutionary pathways for purportedly irreducible systems, often through co-option of parts with other functions, and notes that ID generates no testable hypotheses or peer-reviewed research programme.

Legal and scientific status

In the 2005 US federal case Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District, Judge John E. Jones III ruled that intelligent design is not science, that it is a religious view and a relabelling of creationism, and that teaching it in public school science classes violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment. Scientific organisations including the National Academy of Sciences and the American Association for the Advancement of Science classify ID as pseudoscience, both because it invokes supernatural or unspecified intelligent causes and because its central claims have been empirically refuted or shown to be logically flawed.

History

The phrase gained currency after the 1987 Supreme Court decision in Edwards v. Aguillard struck down a Louisiana law mandating "creation science". Drafts of the 1989 textbook Of Pandas and People were shown in litigation to have substituted "intelligent design" for "creation" after the ruling. The Discovery Institute's internal "Wedge Document" described a strategy to influence public culture and policy, which critics cite as evidence of religious motivation and supporters describe as a mission statement for challenging materialism.
