Hydraulic mining

Hydraulic mining is a form of mining that uses high-pressure jets of water to dislodge rock material or move sediment. Sluice boxes are then used to separate the desired minerals — classically gold from placer deposits — from the sediment. The method was used in a precursor form by the Romans (called hushing), and reached its greatest scale during the California Gold Rush.

California

From the 1850s, miners in California developed the technique of directing water through large nozzles known as "monitors" against gravel banks containing gold. Whole hillsides were washed away; the debris was run through long sluices where gold settled behind riffles, often with mercury used to amalgamate fine gold. The best-known site is preserved at Malakoff Diggins State Historic Park.

Environmental impact and legal end

Hydraulic mining discharged enormous quantities of sediment ("slickens") into rivers, raising riverbeds, worsening floods and burying farmland in the Sacramento Valley. Farmers' lawsuits led to the landmark 1884 federal decision in Woodruff v. North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company, in which Judge Lorenzo Sawyer enjoined the discharge of mining debris into rivers, effectively ending large-scale hydraulic mining in California. The federal Caminetti Act of 1893 later allowed a limited resumption under permit with debris dams.

Modern use

High-pressure water jets are still used in some placer mining, in kaolin mining in Cornwall, and historically in tin mining in Malaysia; the basic technique also survives in firefighting monitors and in water-jet cutting in a different form.
