Diophantine equation

In mathematics, a Diophantine equation is an equation, typically a polynomial equation in two or more unknowns with integer coefficients, for which only integer solutions are of interest. The word Diophantine refers to the Hellenistic mathematician Diophantus of Alexandria, who studied such equations in his Arithmetica. A linear Diophantine equation equates the sum of two or more monomials to a constant; an exponential Diophantine equation involves exponents.

Examples

Famous examples include the Pythagorean equation x² + y² = z², whose positive integer solutions are the Pythagorean triples; Pell's equation x² − Dy² = 1; and the equation xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ, for which Fermat's Last Theorem states there are no positive integer solutions when n > 2. The coin problem and problems of representing numbers as sums of squares also lead to Diophantine equations.

Linear Diophantine equations

The linear equation ax + by = c has integer solutions if and only if c is a multiple of the greatest common divisor of a and b. When solvable, all solutions can be described parametrically, a result closely connected with Bézout's identity and the extended Euclidean algorithm.

Hilbert's tenth problem

Hilbert's tenth problem asked for an algorithm to decide whether any given Diophantine equation has an integer solution. In 1970 Yuri Matiyasevich, building on work by Martin Davis, Hilary Putnam and Julia Robinson, proved that no such algorithm exists: the problem is undecidable.
