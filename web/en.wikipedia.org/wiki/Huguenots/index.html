Huguenots

The Huguenots were French Protestants of the Reformed (Calvinist) tradition in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.


The Huguenot cross, symbol of French Protestantism.

The Huguenots were a religious group of French Protestants who held to the Reformed (Calvinist) tradition of Protestantism. The term, of uncertain origin (possibly from German Eidgenossen, "confederates"), was used by opponents before being adopted by the French Reformed themselves. From modest beginnings under persecution, the movement grew to perhaps two million adherents at its mid-sixteenth-century peak, including much of the nobility and urban middle class.

Persecution and the Wars of Religion

Reformed ideas spread in France from the 1520s despite royal persecution (the Affair of the Placards, 1534). John Calvin, himself a French exile in Geneva, shaped the movement. The attempted assassination of Admiral Gaspard de Coligny and the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572 — in which thousands of Huguenots were killed in Paris and the provinces — ignited the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598), in which Huguenot strongholds such as La Rochelle resisted the crown under leaders including Henry of Navarre.

Edict of Nantes and its revocation

Henry IV, the former Huguenot leader who converted to Catholicism, issued the Edict of Nantes (1598), granting Huguenots freedom of conscience and limited public worship, fortified places of security and civil rights. Cardinal Richelieu stripped their military and political privileges after the siege of La Rochelle (1627–28) while leaving worship intact. Louis XIV progressively persecuted the Reformed (the dragonnades) and revoked the Edict with the Edict of Fontainebleau (1685), banning Protestantism outright.

Diaspora and legacy

Around 200,000–400,000 Huguenots fled France for England, the Dutch Republic, Brandenburg-Prussia (invited by the Edict of Potsdam), Switzerland, South Africa and North America, carrying skills in textiles, banking, watchmaking and wine-making that enriched their host countries. Those who remained practised in secret as the "Church of the Desert" until toleration was restored in 1787; full civil equality came with the Revolution.
