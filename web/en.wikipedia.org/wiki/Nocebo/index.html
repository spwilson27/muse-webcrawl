Nocebo effect

The nocebo effect is the phenomenon in which negative expectations about a treatment cause a person to experience negative effects, even from an inert substance or sham procedure. It is the counterpart of the placebo effect; the word comes from Latin nocebo, "I shall harm".

Mechanisms

Expectations of pain or side effects can increase anxiety and activate neurobiological pathways, including cholecystokinin-mediated amplification of pain (hyperalgesia). Nocebo responses are influenced by prior conditioning, verbal suggestions, and observation of others.

Clinical significance

In clinical trials, patients given placebos frequently report the side effects listed in consent forms, and a substantial share of reported adverse effects of real drugs may be nocebo-driven. Studies of statin intolerance, for example, found that most muscle symptoms reported on statins also occurred on placebo (the SAMSON trial).

Ethics and practice

The effect raises an ethical tension between fully informing patients about side effects and avoiding harm caused by that information. Approaches include contextualised consent and framing risks positively, alongside emphasising benefits.
