Islamic military jurisprudence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Islamic military jurisprudence refers to the body of rules in Islamic law (fiqh) governing the conduct of war, the treatment of combatants and non-combatants, prisoners, treaties and the distribution of spoils. Its sources are the Quran, the Sunnah (practice) of Muhammad, and the works of classical jurists, notably the siyar literature associated with Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani (d. 805), sometimes described as an early system of international law.

Principles

Classical jurists discussed the conditions under which war could be undertaken, generally requiring legitimate authority and, in many formulations, a prior summons to Islam or to a treaty arrangement. Fighting was directed at those who fight (Q 2:190: "Fight in the way of God those who fight you, but do not transgress; God loves not the transgressors").

Protection of non-combatants

Instructions attributed to Muhammad and to the first caliph Abu Bakr prohibit the killing of women, children, the elderly and religious recluses, and forbid mutilation, treachery and the destruction of crops, trees and livestock beyond need. Scholars debated the scope of these protections and their application to siege warfare and weapons that could not discriminate.

Prisoners, treaties and spoils

The Quran (47:4) envisages release of prisoners by grace or ransom after battle; jurists elaborated rules on captives, safe-conduct (amān), the binding force of treaties (Q 9:4), jizya arrangements with protected peoples (dhimmī), and the division of ghanīma (spoils), of which one-fifth (khums) was assigned to public purposes (Q 8:41).

Modern interpretations

Contemporary scholars disagree over how classical doctrines, developed in an imperial context, map onto the modern state system, the UN Charter and international humanitarian law. Some argue that the classical rules contain close analogues to modern jus ad bellum and jus in bello; others emphasise the historical contingency of doctrines such as the division of the world into dār al-Islām and dār al-ḥarb.
