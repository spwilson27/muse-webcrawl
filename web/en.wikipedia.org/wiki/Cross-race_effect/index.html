The cross-race effect (CRE), also called the other-race effect or own-race bias, is the tendency for people to more easily recognize faces that belong to their own racial group. In social psychology, the cross-race effect is described as the "ingroup advantage," whereby people can more easily recognize faces of those in their ingroup. Other research has extended the phenomenon to suggest ease in recognizing groups to which one has greater levels of experience.

The effect is one of the best-documented findings in face-recognition research, having been replicated across many populations. It has significant practical implications for eyewitness identification, since cross-race misidentification is a contributing factor in many wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence.

Research history

The first study of the cross-race effect was published in 1914, and a landmark recognition experiment by Malpass and Kravitz (1969) showed that both Black and white participants recognized faces of their own race more accurately. A large meta-analysis by Meissner and Brigham (2001), covering 39 studies and nearly 5,000 participants, confirmed that people are about 1.4 times more likely to correctly identify a face of their own race and about 1.56 times more likely to falsely identify a face of another race.

Explanations

Two families of explanation dominate. The perceptual expertise account holds that people develop finely tuned face-processing for the kinds of faces they encounter most; infants initially can discriminate faces of many groups, but perceptual narrowing in the first year of life prunes this ability toward familiar categories. The social-cognitive account holds that people categorize outgroup faces at the group level ("a face of race X") rather than individuating them, which impairs later recognition; instructions and motivations to individuate can reduce the effect. Most researchers consider both mechanisms to operate together.

Development

Three-month-old infants show recognition advantages for own-race faces if raised in homogeneous environments, and nine-month-olds raised without exposure to other-race faces lose the ability to discriminate them. Studies of Korean children adopted into European families show the effect reversed toward the adoptive population's faces — strong evidence that the effect is driven by experience rather than anything intrinsic to a race.

Legal implications

Cross-race identification is a known risk factor in eyewitness testimony. Of the first several hundred US exonerations obtained through DNA evidence, a large majority involved eyewitness misidentification, and a substantial share of those were cross-race identifications. Some jurisdictions now permit expert testimony about the effect or include it in jury instructions, and police reform guidelines recommend double-blind, sequential lineups.

Reducing the effect

Interracial contact and experience reduce the bias, as does training that encourages individuating encoding. Experiments also show that warning witnesses that cross-race identification is error-prone can improve caution, though the effect is difficult to eliminate entirely.
