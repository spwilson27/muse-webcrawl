Non-performing loan

A non-performing loan (NPL) is a bank loan that is subject to late repayment or is unlikely to be repaid by the borrower in full. Definitions vary by jurisdiction, but the most common criterion is that principal or interest payments are more than 90 days past due, or that the borrower is assessed as unlikely to pay even if payments are not yet that late. Non-performing loans are also called bad debt, distressed loans, or impaired loans.

Measurement and significance

The ratio of non-performing loans to total loans (the NPL ratio) is a standard indicator of the health of a bank and of a banking system. High NPL levels tie up bank capital, require provisions against losses, and reduce the capacity to make new loans, which can prolong recessions. Regulators therefore require banks to recognise, provision for, and report problem loans, and accounting standards such as IFRS 9 classify loans into stages of credit deterioration.

Resolution

Banks resolve NPLs through restructuring, collateral enforcement, write-offs, or sale to specialised investors and "bad bank" asset-management companies at a discount. Large NPL overhangs followed crises in Japan in the 1990s, the global financial crisis of 2008, and the euro-area sovereign debt crisis, where countries such as Greece, Cyprus, and Italy carried very high stocks of bad loans. In China, asset management companies were created in 1999 to absorb the bad loans of the large state banks.
