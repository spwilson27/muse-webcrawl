Paradox of hedonism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The paradox of hedonism, also called the pleasure paradox, refers to the observation that pleasure and happiness tend to elude those who seek them directly for their own sake, and are more reliably obtained as a by-product of pursuing other goals and activities. The idea poses a problem for hedonism, the view that pleasure is the highest good and that it should be pursued.

Formulations

The philosopher Henry Sidgwick discussed the phenomenon in The Methods of Ethics (1874), calling it the "fundamental paradox of hedonism": the impulse toward pleasure, if too predominant, defeats its own aim. John Stuart Mill described a similar experience in his Autobiography (1873):

"Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so." Those only are happy who have their minds fixed on some object other than their own happiness; on the happiness of others, on the improvement of mankind, even on some art or pursuit, followed not as a means, but as itself an ideal end. Aiming thus at something else, they find happiness by the way. — John Stuart Mill

The psychiatrist and Holocaust survivor Viktor Frankl argued likewise that happiness "cannot be pursued; it must ensue" as the unintended side-effect of dedication to a cause or another person.

Explanations

Proposed explanations include the idea that pleasure accompanies the satisfaction of desires and engagement in absorbing activities (such as "flow"), which direct attention away from oneself; constant self-monitoring disrupts such absorption. Evolutionary psychologists suggest that pleasure functions as a signal guiding behaviour toward fitness-relevant goals, rather than as a goal to be maximised in isolation. Hedonistic adaptation — becoming accustomed to pleasures — also undermines sustained direct pursuit.

Relation to ethical theory

Some hedonists respond by distinguishing the truth of hedonism as a theory of value from the best strategy for achieving pleasure: an indirect or "sophisticated" hedonism may recommend cultivating aims and affections whose pursuit yields pleasure as a by-product. Critics argue instead that the paradox shows pleasure is not the sole intrinsic good, pointing toward pluralism about value or to the importance of meaning and accomplishment.
