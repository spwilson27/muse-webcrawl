Okra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Fresh green okra pods, one cut to show the star-shaped cross-section]

Okra (Abelmoschus esculentus) is a warm-climate vegetable of the mallow family — kin to hibiscus and cotton — eaten for its green, ridged pods, whose cut faces show a star of pale seeds in a mucilaginous matrix. That mucilage is okra's signature and its service: it thickens the stews of West Africa, the gumbo of Louisiana (whose name may descend from a Bantu word for okra itself, ki ngombo), and the curries of India, where as bhindi it is stir-fried dry until the slime cooks away. Okra reached the Americas through the transatlantic slave trade, carried as sustenance and seed by enslaved Africans, and became foundational to African-American cooking.

Names and travels

The plant's homeland is debated between Ethiopia/Eritrea and South Asia, but its American career is an African story: okra in gumbo, okra soup in the Lowcountry, fried okra in the South. In India it is a monsoon staple; in Japan it joins the neba-neba family of sticky foods thought to give stamina; in Brazil it anchors Afro-Brazilian caruru. Its seeds were ground as a coffee substitute in times of blockade, and its fibre has served for paper and rope.

Cooking it

Two schools rule: embrace the mucilage (gumbo, stews, where it body-builds the pot) or defeat it (high dry heat, acid from tomato or vinegar, whole pods uncut — as in a crisp bhindi masala or cornmeal-fried okra). Pods must be young; old okra turns to rope and wood. Few vegetables divide a table faster or reward their defenders so well.
