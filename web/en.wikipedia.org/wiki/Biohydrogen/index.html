Biohydrogen

Biohydrogen is hydrogen gas produced biologically, most commonly by microorganisms such as algae and bacteria. Interest in biohydrogen arises because hydrogen is a clean fuel whose use produces only water, and biological production could in principle generate it from renewable feedstocks such as sunlight, water and organic waste, avoiding the fossil fuels used in conventional steam reforming.

Production processes

Several biological routes to hydrogen are studied. In biophotolysis, photosynthetic microorganisms such as green algae and cyanobacteria split water using sunlight, releasing hydrogen through enzymes called hydrogenases; the process is inhibited by the oxygen produced, a major research challenge. In photofermentation, purple non-sulfur bacteria convert organic acids to hydrogen using light. In dark fermentation, anaerobic bacteria break down carbohydrates in the absence of light, producing hydrogen alongside organic acids and carbon dioxide. Microbial electrolysis cells use electrochemically active bacteria with a small applied voltage to generate hydrogen from organic matter.

Challenges

Hydrogen yields from biological systems remain well below theoretical maxima, and the sensitivity of hydrogenase enzymes to oxygen limits direct photolysis. Large-scale production would require cheap, large photobioreactors and efficient gas separation. Biohydrogen is therefore not yet competitive with conventional hydrogen production, but it remains an active research area within the broader hydrogen economy.

History

The production of hydrogen by algae was first observed by Hans Gaffron in 1939–1942, when he found that the green alga Scenedesmus could switch between producing oxygen and hydrogen. Research expanded during the energy crises of the 1970s and again with twenty-first-century interest in sustainable fuels.
