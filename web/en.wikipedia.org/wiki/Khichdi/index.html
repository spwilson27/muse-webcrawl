Khichdi

Khichdi (pronounced [kʰɪtʃɽiː]), also transliterated khichri, khichdee, khichadi and khichuri, is a dish in South Asian cuisine made of rice and lentils (dal) cooked together in one pot. It is one of the oldest dishes of the Indian subcontinent and is widely regarded as a comfort food. In 2017 it was unofficially promoted as a representative "queen of all foods" of India during the World Food India festival.

History

Preparations of rice boiled with pulses are mentioned in ancient Indian texts, and the dish appears in accounts of the Mughal court: it was said to be a favourite of the emperor Jahangir. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta mentioned a dish of rice cooked with mung beans during his stay in India in the 14th century. Khichdi is also considered a precursor of the Anglo-Indian dish kedgeree and the Egyptian dish kushari, both developed through culinary exchange during the colonial period.

Preparation

The basic form combines rice with split lentils — most commonly moong dal — seasoned with turmeric, cumin and salt, and finished with a tempering (tadka) of ghee, cumin seeds, asafoetida and sometimes garlic or ginger. Regional variants add vegetables, use different pulses such as masoor or toor dal, or make the dish drier or porridge-like ("geeli" versus "sookhi" khichdi). In Bengal, khichuri is a richer festival version offered during Durga Puja and monsoon meals, often served with fried vegetables, omelettes or hilsa fish.

Cultural significance

Because it is easy to digest, khichdi is commonly served to children, the elderly and the sick, and features in Ayurvedic diets. It is eaten during fasting periods in some communities and is a staple of ashrams and temple kitchens. On Makar Sankranti, khichdi is traditionally cooked and shared in parts of northern India.
