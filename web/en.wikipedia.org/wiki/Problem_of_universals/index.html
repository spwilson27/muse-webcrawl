Problem of universals

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The problem of universals is a central problem in metaphysics concerning the nature of properties and kinds. When several individual things share a feature — several objects are all green, or all circular — the question arises: what is this shared feature, the universal, and does it exist independently of the particular things that have it?

Positions

Realism, associated with Plato, holds that universals exist as abstract entities in their own right; a moderate form, Aristotelian realism, holds that universals exist only in the things that instantiate them (in re). Nominalism denies that universals exist at all: only particulars exist, and general terms are names (nomina) or concepts we apply to similar particulars. Conceptualism is an intermediate view on which universals exist as mental concepts. Trope theory replaces universals with particularised properties (tropes).

History

The problem was posed by Plato and Aristotle, transmitted to medieval philosophy through Porphyry's Isagoge and Boethius, and became the great controversy of scholasticism, with figures such as Peter Abelard, Thomas Aquinas and William of Ockham defending competing accounts. It remains live in contemporary philosophy in debates about properties, natural kinds, abstract objects and the semantics of general terms.

Related issues

The problem is connected with the problem of induction, the metaphysics of laws of nature, resemblance classes, and the question of whether mathematics describes mind-independent abstract structures.
