Satori (Japanese: satori, 悟り) is a term in Japanese Zen Buddhism for awakening — a sudden experience of seeing into the true nature of existence, comparable to the Sanskrit bodhi. It denotes a moment of insight in which the duality of subject and object falls away and reality is perceived directly, beyond conceptual thought.

Kenshō and satori

Satori is frequently paired with kenshō ("seeing one's nature"), an initial glimpse of awakening. In the Rinzai school, kenshō is often the first breakthrough, typically attained through intensive kōan practice, while satori may describe a deeper or more settled realisation. Usage of the two terms varies among teachers, and they are sometimes treated as synonyms.

Practice

Zen training aims at satori through zazen (seated meditation), kōan study under a master, and periods of intensive retreat (sesshin). A sudden shout, blow, or an apparently mundane event — the sound of a pebble striking bamboo, in the famous account of Xiangyan — may trigger the breakthrough. D. T. Suzuki popularised satori in the West, describing it as the central experience of Zen and an irrational, intuitive apprehension of truth.

Zen also cautions that awakening is not an end: the saying "before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water; after enlightenment, chop wood, carry water" stresses that satori must be integrated into everyday life, and practice continues to deepen it.
