Cerealia

Ceres, goddess of agriculture, in whose honour the Cerealia was held.

In ancient Roman religion, the Cerealia was the major festival celebrated in honour of the grain goddess Ceres. It was held over seven days from mid- to late April, with the main observances traditionally dated from 12 to 19 April.

The festival

The Cerealia was one of the oldest and most popular festivals of the Roman calendar. Its celebration included the Ludi Cereales ("Games of Ceres"), which featured theatrical performances and, most famously, chariot races in the Circus Maximus. The races of 19 April were preceded by a procession and by the ritual release of foxes with burning torches tied to their tails into the Circus — a rite the poet Ovid attempted to explain with an aetiological tale in his Fasti.

Ceres and the plebeians

Ceres was the central deity of the Aventine Triad (Ceres, Liber and Libera) and was closely associated with the plebeians of Rome. Her temple on the Aventine Hill, dedicated in 493 BC, stood near the Circus Maximus and served as a plebeian stronghold and grain-distribution centre. White was the festival dress prescribed for worshippers of Ceres, and mourners were excluded from her rites.

Offerings

Offerings to Ceres included wheat, salt, incense and torches, and the sacrifice of a sow. As goddess of the grain harvest, Ceres presided over the fertility of the fields, and her April festival fell at the season when the crops were growing and most vulnerable.
