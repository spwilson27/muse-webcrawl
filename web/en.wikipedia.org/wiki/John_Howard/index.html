John Howard (2 September 1726 – 20 January 1790) was an English philanthropist and the first major prison reformer in Britain. Appointed High Sheriff of Bedfordshire in 1773, he was appalled by the conditions he found in county gaols and spent the rest of his life travelling through Britain and Europe inspecting prisons and campaigning for reform. His book The State of the Prisons in England and Wales (1777) is regarded as a foundational text of penal reform.

Early life

Howard was born in Hackney, London, the son of a prosperous upholsterer. After inheriting a considerable fortune he settled at Cardington in Bedfordshire, where he became known for improving the cottages and education of his tenants. His appointment as High Sheriff in 1773 brought him into direct contact with the county gaol at Bedford.

Prison investigations

Howard discovered that gaolers were paid nothing by the county and instead lived on fees extracted from prisoners — including an "acquittal fee" demanded from prisoners who had been found innocent or against whom charges were dropped. He also found debtors and felons confined together in overcrowded, disease-ridden cells. His evidence to the House of Commons in 1774 helped secure legislation abolishing acquittal fees and requiring gaolers to be salaried and prisons to be kept in sanitary condition.

Over the following years Howard travelled tens of thousands of miles, visiting prisons, bridewells and lazarettos (quarantine stations) across Europe. He documented dimensions, diet, water supply, disease and mortality in meticulous tables, and repeatedly returned to verify whether reforms had been carried out.

Death and legacy

Howard died of typhus ("gaol fever") at Kherson in the Russian Empire in January 1790, having contracted the disease while visiting a military hospital. His work inspired the Howard Association, founded in 1866, whose successor body, the Howard League for Penal Reform, remains a leading penal-reform charity. A statue of Howard in St Paul's Cathedral was the first erected there to a non-military figure.
