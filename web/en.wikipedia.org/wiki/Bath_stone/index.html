Bath stone

Bath stone is an oolitic limestone from the Great Oolite Group of Jurassic age, quarried around Bath and Corsham in Somerset and Wiltshire, England. Its warm honey colour gives the city of Bath its distinctive appearance and led to its UNESCO World Heritage status. The stone is soft and easily worked when freshly quarried, hardening on exposure to air.

Quarrying

Because the stone beds lie close to hillsides, much Bath stone was extracted from underground mines rather than open quarries — notably at Combe Down, Box, and Corsham, where vast pillar-and-stall workings survive. One Corsham quarry complex was later converted into a Cold War government bunker. Stone was moved by canal and later by rail, including through Brunel's Box Tunnel.

Use in building

Ralph Allen, who promoted the quarries in the 18th century, supplied the stone for Georgian Bath's crescents and terraces designed by John Wood the Elder and Younger. Bath stone was exported widely for churches, country houses, and public buildings; the façade of Buckingham Palace was refaced in it in the early 20th century. Its softness makes it vulnerable to weathering and pollution, and conservation of Bath's buildings is a continuing concern.
