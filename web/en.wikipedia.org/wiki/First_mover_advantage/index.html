First-mover advantage

First-mover advantage Field: Strategic management, marketing
Mechanisms: technology leadership, preemption of assets, switching costs, network effects
Counterpart concept: second-mover (fast follower) advantage
Key literature: Lieberman and Montgomery (1988)

In marketing strategy, first-mover advantage (FMA) is the benefit a company gains by being the first significant entrant into a new market segment. Being first can allow a firm to build brand recognition and customer loyalty, secure scarce resources and distribution channels, and shape industry standards before rivals appear.

Mechanisms

Literature following Lieberman and Montgomery (1988) identifies three sources of advantage: technology leadership, through learning curves and patents; preemption of assets, such as prime locations, suppliers, and shelf space; and buyer switching costs, which lock in early customers, a benefit amplified where network effects make a product more valuable as its user base grows.

Disadvantages

First movers bear the cost of educating the market and developing immature technology, while later entrants can free-ride on those investments, imitate or improve on the pioneer's product, and benefit from shifts in technology or consumer tastes—a "second-mover advantage." Many pioneers (such as early social networks before Facebook) failed to remain market leaders, whereas fast followers sometimes came to dominate.

Empirical findings

Research results are mixed: pioneering is associated with higher long-run market share in many categories, but also with higher failure rates, suggesting that first-mover advantage depends on the firm's resources, the pace of technological change, and the durability of the mechanisms that protect its early position.
