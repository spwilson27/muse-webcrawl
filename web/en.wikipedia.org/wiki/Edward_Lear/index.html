Edward Lear (12 May 1812 – 29 January 1888) was an English artist, illustrator, musician, author and poet, now known mostly for his literary nonsense in poetry and prose, especially his limericks, a form he popularised. His most famous poem is "The Owl and the Pussycat" (written 1867, published 1870). He was also a serious and accomplished landscape artist and ornithological illustrator whose parrot plates rival those of John James Audubon.

Life

Lear was born in Holloway, London, the twentieth child of a stockbroker whose fortunes collapsed; he was raised largely by his eldest sister Ann, from whom he was separated by her death in 1861. Fragile, epileptic and asthmatic, with poor eyesight, Lear was mostly self-educated and began earning at 15 drawing birds and animals. At 19 he published Illustrations of the Family of Psittacidae, or Parrots (1830–32), drawn from live birds at the London Zoo — among the first ornithological folios executed by a teenager — and was employed by the Zoological Society and by John Gould, for whom he worked alongside Elizabeth Gould. The Earl of Derby employed him to paint his menagerie at Knowsley Hall, where Lear began writing nonsense rhymes for the Earl's grandchildren; these became A Book of Nonsense (1846), published under the pseudonym "Derry down Derry".

Travels and art

From 1837 Lear lived chiefly abroad for his health, travelling and painting watercolour landscapes throughout Italy, Greece, Albania, Turkey, Egypt, Palestine, India and Ceylon, and publishing acclaimed travel journals. His thousands of topographical watercolours — often inscribed with colour notes and nonsense spellings (his "topographies") — are held by the Houghton Library at Harvard, the Yale Center for British Art and the V&A. He settled at San Remo, Italy (first at Villa Emily, then Villa Tennyson), with his devoted Albanian cook Giorgi and his beloved tabby cat Foss, whose death in 1887 preceded Lear's own by a few weeks; Lear died unmarried on 29 January 1888 and is buried in San Remo.

Nonsense literature

Lear's nonsense books include Nonsense Songs, Stories, Botany and Alphabets (1871) — containing "The Owl and the Pussycat", "The Jumblies", "The Dong with a Luminous Nose" and others — and Laughable Lyrics (1877). He coined words and place-names ("runcible spoon", "Chankly Bore", the Quangle Wangle), wrote nonsense botany and alphabets, and perfected the five-line limerick, though his limericks often repeat the first line's ending word in the last line rather than adding a surprise twist. He stands with Lewis Carroll — whom his work preceded and influenced — as a founder of English literary nonsense, admired by writers from W. H. Auden (who wrote a poem about him) to James Joyce.
