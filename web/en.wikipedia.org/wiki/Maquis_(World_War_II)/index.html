Maquis (World War II)

Active1940–1944
CountryFrance
AllegianceFrench Resistance, Free France
TypeGuerrilla bands

The Maquis (French pronunciation: [maˈki]) were rural guerrilla bands of French Resistance fighters, called maquisards, during the Nazi occupation of France in World War II. Initially, they were composed of desperate young men, particularly those who had fled from France into the mountains and forests to avoid conscription into the Service du travail obligatoire (STO), the forced labour service in Germany. As the war continued, the Maquis grew to include Spanish Civil War veterans, communist Francs-tireurs et partisans, escaped prisoners and Allied agents.

Origins and name

The term derives from the dense Mediterranean scrubland, the maquis, in which bandits and fugitives traditionally hid, especially in Corsica. Men who "took to the maquis" (prendre le maquis) formed camps in remote areas — the mountains of the Massif Central, the Alps, Brittany and the southwest — supplied by local villagers and by Allied air drops organised with the help of the Special Operations Executive and the Free French Bureau central de renseignements et d'action.

Operations

Maquis groups sabotaged railways, roads, telephone lines and power supplies, ambushed German convoys and gathered intelligence. Some groups formed larger concentrations, such as the maquis of the Vercors plateau and the Glières plateau, both crushed by German assaults in 1944 with heavy loss of life. Maquis units also faced the Vichy Milice, whose hunting of resisters added a civil-war dimension to the conflict.

Following the Normandy landings of June 1944, the Maquis emerged into open warfare, harassing German movements — notably contributing to the delay of the 2nd SS Panzer Division Das Reich — and liberating many towns ahead of the Allied advance. Most maquisards were subsequently incorporated into the French Forces of the Interior (FFI).
