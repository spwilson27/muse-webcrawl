Hedge laying

Hedge laying (or hedgelaying) is the traditional craft of creating a living stock-proof boundary by partially cutting and bending the stems of a hedgerow. Practised for centuries in the United Kingdom and Ireland, and recorded since Roman times — Julius Caesar described Gaulish hedges maintained in this way — the technique both rejuvenates the hedge and maintains it as a barrier to livestock.

Technique

The hedger cuts most of the way through the base of each stem (leaving a hinge of sapwood and bark, in a cut called a pleach), then bends, or "lays", the stem at an angle so that it remains alive and continues to grow. The laid stems, called pleachers, are woven between upright stakes driven along the hedge line, and the top is often bound with woven hazel rods called heathering or binding. New growth springs vertically from the laid stems and the cut stools, producing a dense, thick hedge. A laid hedge typically needs relaying every 10 to 30 years, depending on species and conditions.

Regional styles

More than thirty regional styles are recognised in Britain, each adapted to local farming and materials. The Midland (or "bullock") style has a smooth face to the field side and brush facing the stock; the South of England style uses double stakes and combing; the Welsh border and Derbyshire styles differ in stake placement and binding. Competitions run by local hedgelaying societies and the National Hedgelaying Society keep the skills alive.

Wildlife value

Laid hedges provide habitat corridors, food and shelter for birds, small mammals and invertebrates, and are increasingly valued in modern conservation and agri-environment schemes as an alternative to wire fencing.
