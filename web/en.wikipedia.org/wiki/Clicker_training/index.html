Clicker training

Clicker training is a method of animal training that uses positive reinforcement together with a small hand-held device — the clicker — that makes a short, distinct clicking sound to mark the exact moment an animal performs a desired behaviour. The click acts as a conditioned reinforcer, or "bridge", signalling to the animal that a reward will follow.

History

The method descends from the work of B. F. Skinner on operant conditioning and from the commercial animal training of Keller Breland and Marian Breland in the 1940s and 1950s. Behaviourist and former dolphin trainer Karen Pryor popularised the approach for pet owners with her 1984 book Don't Shoot the Dog! and subsequent works on clicker training for dogs.

Method

Training begins by "charging the clicker": the click is repeatedly paired with a high-value food reward until the animal associates the sound with good things. Thereafter the trainer clicks at the precise instant the desired behaviour occurs and delivers the reward. Behaviours can be obtained by capturing (waiting for the animal to offer them), luring, or shaping — reinforcing successive approximations toward a target behaviour. Once a behaviour is reliable, a verbal cue is added and the clicker may be faded out.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters argue that the click's brevity and consistency allow far more precise timing than voice markers, speeding learning, and that the method avoids the stress and fallout associated with punishment-based training. Studies of dogs have produced mixed results on whether the clicker itself outperforms a verbal marker, though positive reinforcement methods generally are favoured by veterinary behaviour organisations. Critics note that the trainer must manage clicker, treats, and lead simultaneously, and that poor timing can reinforce unwanted behaviour.
