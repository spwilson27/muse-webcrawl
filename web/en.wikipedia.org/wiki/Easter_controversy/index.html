The Easter controversy comprises a series of disputes within Christianity over the correct date on which to celebrate Easter, the feast of the Resurrection of Jesus, and the proper method (computus) of calculating it. Because Easter determines the dates of Lent, Holy Week and Pentecost, the disagreements had wide effects on church unity and the calendar.

The Quartodeciman controversy

In the second century the churches of the Roman province of Asia celebrated the Pasch on the 14th day of the Jewish month of Nisan — the day of Passover — whatever the day of the week, following the chronology of the Gospel of John; they were accordingly called Quartodecimans ("fourteeners"). Most other churches kept the feast on the following Sunday, the day of the Resurrection. Around AD 190 Pope Victor I attempted to excommunicate the Asian churches over the practice and was rebuked by Irenaeus of Lyons; the Quartodeciman practice gradually died out over the following centuries.

Nicaea and the computus

The First Council of Nicaea (325) decreed that Easter should be kept on a Sunday, by all churches, independently of the Jewish calendar. It did not, however, lay down a single detailed algorithm, and different centres used different cycles and equinox dates, so Easters still sometimes diverged. The tables of Dionysius Exiguus (AD 525), based on a 19-year cycle anchored to 21 March as the equinox, eventually prevailed in the West and (in modified form) in the East.

Whitby and the Celtic churches

The Insular (Celtic) churches of Ireland and northern Britain used an older 84-year cycle, which could place Easter a week apart from the Roman reckoning. The Synod of Whitby (664), convened by King Oswiu of Northumbria, decided for the Roman dating, a landmark in the integration of the English church with continental practice; Iona itself conformed in 716.

East–West divergence

When Pope Gregory XIII reformed the calendar in 1582, the Catholic (and eventually Protestant) West adopted corrected Easter tables, while the Orthodox East retained the Julian calendar and the older computus. As a result Western and Orthodox Easter coincide only in some years and can differ by as much as five weeks. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century ecumenical proposals — notably the 1997 Aleppo consultation — have recommended a common date computed by accurate astronomical methods from the meridian of Jerusalem, but no proposal has yet been adopted universally.
