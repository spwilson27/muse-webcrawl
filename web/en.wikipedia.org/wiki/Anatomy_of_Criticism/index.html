Anatomy of Criticism: Four Essays is a book of literary theory by the Canadian critic Northrop Frye, published in 1957. Considered one of the most influential works of literary criticism of the twentieth century, it attempts to place literature within a total, systematic framework of genres, symbols, myths, and modes, treating criticism as an autonomous discipline rather than an offshoot of social or historical commentary.

The four essays

The book consists of a "Polemical Introduction" and four essays. The introduction argues that criticism should proceed inductively from the internal structure of literature itself, rather than imposing external value judgements. The first essay classifies literature by modes, based on the hero's power of action relative to the reader and the environment: myth, romance, high mimetic, low mimetic, and irony. The second essay develops a theory of symbols in phases — literal, descriptive, formal, mythical, and anagogic. The third essay presents Frye's famous theory of mythoi, the four narrative patterns of comedy (spring), romance (summer), tragedy (autumn), and irony or satire (winter), which he linked to the seasonal cycle. The fourth essay addresses genre, distinguishing the forms of drama, epic, lyric, and prose fiction.

Influence

Frye's archetypal criticism profoundly shaped the study of literature in the decades after publication, influencing Harold Bloom and the broader structuralist movement. Although later schools — deconstruction, New Historicism, and cultural studies — challenged its universalising claims, the Anatomy remains a landmark attempt at a comprehensive poetics.
