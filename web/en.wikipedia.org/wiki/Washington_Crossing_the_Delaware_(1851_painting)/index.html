Washington Crossing the Delaware is the title of three full-sized paintings by the German-American artist Emanuel Leutze depicting George Washington's crossing of the Delaware River during the American Revolutionary War on the night of 25–26 December 1776, the opening move of the surprise attack that led to the Battle of Trenton. The surviving 1851 version, long on display at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, is among the most famous images in American art.

History

Leutze, raised in the United States and trained at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf, painted the work in Germany hoping, in the aftermath of the failed European revolutions of 1848, to inspire liberal reformers with an American example. The first version (1849–50) was damaged in a fire in his studio and, after restoration, was bought for the Kunsthalle Bremen, where it was destroyed in a Second World War air raid in 1942. Leutze painted a second, full-scale version in 1851; it was exhibited in the United States to great acclaim, purchased by Marshall O. Roberts, and eventually donated to the Metropolitan Museum of Art. A smaller third version hangs in the White House.

Composition and inaccuracies

The monumental canvas shows Washington standing heroically in a boat packed with a cross-section of American types — frontiersmen, farmers, a Scotsman, a man of African descent and others — among ice floes at dawn. The painting is knowingly theatrical rather than accurate: the crossing happened at night in a storm, the flag shown (the Stars and Stripes) had not yet been adopted, the boats are too small, the river — modelled on the Rhine — is far too wide with alpine ice, and Washington would hardly have stood upright in such conditions. Nevertheless, the image fixed itself in the American imagination and has been endlessly reproduced, parodied and referenced, from textbook covers to the New Jersey state quarter.
