Isoperimetric inequality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Of all shapes with a given perimeter, the circle encloses the greatest area.

In mathematics, the isoperimetric inequality states that, among all closed curves in the plane of a given perimeter, the circle encloses the maximum area. Equivalently, for a plane region with area A and boundary length L, the inequality L² ≥ 4πA holds, with equality only for the circle. The problem is ancient: it underlies the legend of Dido, who is said to have founded Carthage by enclosing the greatest plot a bull's hide, cut into strips, could surround.

History

Zenodorus (c. 200 BC – c. 140 BC) proved partial results, showing among polygons of given perimeter the regular polygon has the greatest area. Jakob Steiner gave influential symmetrisation arguments in the nineteenth century, though they assumed the existence of a maximiser — a gap pointed out by Weierstrass and closed by later analysts. Modern proofs use the calculus of variations, Fourier series (Hurwitz's proof), or geometric measure theory.

Higher dimensions

The inequality generalises: among all sets of a given volume in ℝn, the ball has the least surface area. In three dimensions, 36πV² ≤ S³ for volume V and surface area S, with equality for the sphere. This is why soap bubbles and droplets are spherical — surfaces minimise area for the volume enclosed. Rigorous results for general sets were established by De Giorgi and others within the theory of sets of finite perimeter.

Related inequalities

Isoperimetric principles appear throughout analysis and geometry: on spheres and in hyperbolic space (where the extremals are geodesic balls), in the isoperimetric profile of Riemannian manifolds, and in functional inequalities such as the Sobolev and Faber–Krahn inequalities, which bound eigenvalues and integrals by geometric quantities. Discrete versions govern expansion in graphs, connecting the idea to Cheeger constants and expander graphs.

Applications

Beyond geometry, isoperimetric reasoning appears in physics (surface tension, minimal surfaces, bubble clusters), probability (concentration of measure in high dimensions), and computer science (network design and error-correcting codes).
