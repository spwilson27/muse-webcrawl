Inner Hebrides

The Inner Hebrides (Scottish Gaelic: Na h-Eileanan a-staigh) are an archipelago off the west coast of mainland Scotland, to the south-east of the Outer Hebrides. Together the two groups form the Hebrides. The Inner Hebrides comprise 35 inhabited islands and many more uninhabited ones, with a total population of roughly 19,000. The largest and best known are Skye, Mull, Islay and Jura. The climate is mild and oceanic, and the islands' economies rest on tourism, whisky distilling, fishing, aquaculture and crofting.

Geography and geology

The islands divide into a northern group (Skye, the Small Isles — Rùm, Eigg, Muck and Canna — and Raasay) administered by the Highland Council, and a southern group (Mull, Islay, Jura, Iona, Tiree, Coll, Colonsay, Lismore and others) in Argyll and Bute. Much of the northern group is built of Palaeogene flood basalts of the North Atlantic Igneous Province, forming the dramatic landscapes of the Cuillin and the Quiraing on Skye and the columnar basalt of Staffa with Fingal's Cave. Islay and Jura include some of Scotland's oldest rocks, Dalradian metasediments and the raised beaches of Jura's west coast.

History

The islands were settled in the Mesolithic and hold important Neolithic and Iron Age remains, including brochs and crannogs. In the early medieval period they lay within the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata; the monastery founded by St Columba on Iona in 563 became a centre of Celtic Christianity. From the 9th century Norse settlers dominated, and the islands (the Suðreyjar, "Southern Isles") were ruled from Norway until ceded to Scotland by the Treaty of Perth (1266). The semi-independent Lordship of the Isles, centred on Islay, flourished in the 14th–15th centuries before its forfeiture in 1493. The 18th–19th centuries brought kelp booms, crofting townships and the trauma of the Highland Clearances.

Culture and economy

Scottish Gaelic remains spoken, most strongly on Skye and Tiree, and the islands host the Gaelic college Sabhal Mòr Ostaig. Islay is famous for its peated single malt Scotch whiskies (including Laphroaig, Lagavulin and Ardbeg) and hosts the Fèis Ìle festival. Tourism, salmon farming, shellfish, and renewable energy projects are key modern industries, while ferries operated by Caledonian MacBrayne link the islands to Oban, Mallaig, Ullapool and other mainland ports.
