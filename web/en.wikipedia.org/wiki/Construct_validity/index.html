Construct validity

[Hero image placeholder: diagram of a nomological network linking a construct to observable measures]

In psychometrics and research methodology, construct validity refers to how well an assessment or test measures the theoretical concept, or construct, it is intended to measure. For example, an intelligence test has construct validity insofar as its scores genuinely reflect intelligence and not reading speed or cultural familiarity.

History

The concept was introduced by Paul Meehl and Lee Cronbach in a landmark 1955 paper, "Construct validity in psychological tests". Cronbach and Meehl argued that many psychological attributes have no definitive criterion against which a test can be checked; instead, the test must be validated by embedding it in a nomological network — a set of theoretical relations linking the construct to other constructs and to observable outcomes — and testing the predictions of that network.

Later, Samuel Messick (1989) proposed a unified theory of validity in which construct validity subsumes all forms of validity, including content and criterion-related evidence, and incorporates the social consequences of test use.

Components

  • Convergent validity: the test correlates strongly with other measures of the same or related constructs.
  • Discriminant (divergent) validity: the test does not correlate strongly with measures of distinct constructs.
  • Nomological validity: the test behaves in theoretically predictable ways within its network of constructs.
  • Face validity (a weaker, surface property): the test appears, on its face, to measure the construct.

The multitrait-multimethod matrix of Campbell and Fiske (1959) is a classic tool for assessing convergent and discriminant validity.

Threats

Threats to construct validity include construct underrepresentation (the measure misses dimensions of the construct) and construct-irrelevant variance (the measure is contaminated by unrelated factors such as test-taking anxiety or method effects).
