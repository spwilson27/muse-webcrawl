Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act

The Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act is a United States federal law enacted on 21 July 2010 in response to the financial crisis of 2007–2008. Named after its sponsors, Senator Chris Dodd and Representative Barney Frank, it made sweeping changes to financial regulation, aiming to promote financial stability, end "too big to fail", and protect consumers from abusive financial practices.

Major provisions

The Act created the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) to monitor systemic risk, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) to regulate consumer financial products. It established an orderly liquidation authority for failing systemically important firms, imposed the Volcker Rule limiting proprietary trading by banks, required most standardised derivatives to be traded on exchanges and centrally cleared, raised capital and supervision requirements for large banks, and mandated annual stress tests. It also overhauled mortgage lending rules and credit-rating agency oversight.

Debate and later changes

Supporters credit Dodd–Frank with making the financial system safer, while critics argue it burdened community banks and did not end too-big-to-fail. In 2018 the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act raised the threshold for enhanced supervision from $50 billion to $250 billion in assets, rolling back parts of the law for mid-sized banks. Debate over its provisions — and over the independence of the CFPB — has continued ever since.
