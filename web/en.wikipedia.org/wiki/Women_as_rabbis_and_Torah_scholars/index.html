Women as rabbis and Torah scholars

Women as rabbis and Torah scholars are women who have undertaken advanced study of Jewish texts and, in some denominations, received rabbinic ordination (semikhah). The role of women as religious leaders has been a major subject of debate in modern Judaism, with Reform, Reconstructionist, Conservative and some Open Orthodox movements ordaining women, while traditional Orthodox Judaism generally does not ordain women as rabbis but has developed advanced Torah-study roles for women.

History

Although traditional Jewish sources record learned women — most famously Bruriah in the Talmud — formal ordination of women began in the twentieth century. Regina Jonas, ordained privately in Germany in 1935, was the first woman rabbi in modern times. Sally Priesand became the first woman ordained by a rabbinical seminary in 1972 (Reform), followed by Sandy Eisenberg Sasso (Reconstructionist, 1974) and Amy Eilberg (Conservative, 1985).

Orthodox developments

In Orthodox communities, institutions such as Drisha and Nishmat have trained women as advanced scholars and halakhic advisers (yoetzot halacha). Since 2009, Yeshivat Maharat has ordained women for clergy roles, and titles such as "maharat" and "rabba" have been used, generating continuing discussion.
