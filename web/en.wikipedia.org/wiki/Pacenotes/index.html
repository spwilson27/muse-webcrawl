Pacenotes

Pacenotes are detailed shorthand descriptions of a rally special stage, read aloud by a co-driver to the driver during the event so that the driver can anticipate the road ahead — corners, crests, jumps, hazards and distances — and drive at maximum speed over a route he or she cannot see around. The notes are prepared during reconnaissance ("recce") passes over the stages before the rally, corrected and refined by the crew, and constitute one of the defining skills of rally co-driving.

Content and notation

A pacenote describes each feature of the road in sequence. Corners are graded by severity — how tight they are — and direction. Several systems are in use:

  • Numeric (descriptive) systems grade corners by the gear or by a 1–6 (or 1–9) scale of tightness, e.g. "6" for a fast, slight bend and "1" for a hairpin.
  • "Jemba"-style and other standardised systems use consistent numerical codes generated or checked against measurements.
  • Modifiers add detail: long, tightens, opens, don't cut, caution, distances in metres between features, and warnings for crests, jumps, water splashes, slippery surfaces and obstacles inside or outside the corner.

A typical string of notes might read:

"100, crest into 4 right long, tightens 3, don't cut — 60 — 5 left over crest, opens — 200 — hairpin right, caution, rocks inside"

The co-driver must deliver each call early enough for the driver to react, maintaining a rhythm and emphasising hazards; timing the delivery is as important as the content.

Preparation

On most international rallies, crews make two low-speed recce passes in a standard or specially prepared road car. On the first pass the driver dictates the notes while the co-driver writes them down; on the second the co-driver reads them back and both refine them — increasingly, crews also review onboard video between passes. In some events, notably in North America, "stage notes" are instead supplied by the organisers (the Jemba system generates them from measured data), and some historic or club events are "blind", run without notes beyond the road book.

History

Early rally co-drivers (then usually called navigators) relied on maps and route instructions; the reconnaissance-based pacenote system developed from the 1950s and 1960s, particularly in continental European events and the RAC Rally, where Scandinavian drivers such as Erik Carlsson and Rauno Aaltonen pioneered detailed route knowledge. In Britain, the Forestry Commission's ban on practising on forest stages shaped the famous "blind" Molls Gap–style tradition, while works teams refined elaborate note systems in the 1970s and 1980s. Today pacenotes are near-universal at World Rally Championship level, with each driver–co-driver pairing developing a personal dialect: champions such as Sébastien Loeb (with Daniel Elena) and Sébastien Ogier (with Julien Ingrassia) are noted for distinctive systems.

In popular culture

The rapid-fire cadence of co-drivers calling notes ("Samir, you're breaking the car!") has become a widely recognised feature of rally broadcasts and video games such as the DiRT Rally series, where simulated pacenotes guide the player; accuracy of timing and phrasing is a noted part of their realism.
