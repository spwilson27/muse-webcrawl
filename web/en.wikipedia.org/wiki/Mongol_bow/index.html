Mongol bow

The Mongol bow is a type of recurved composite bow historically used in Mongolia. The Mongol bow refers both to a type of bow once used by the Mongols during the era of the Mongol Empire (13th–14th centuries), and to a type of traditional bow used in Mongolia today. The older type was larger and had string bridges (siyahs) that rested the string on the limbs when unstrung; the modern form is shorter and lacks these bridges.

Construction

The bow was built on a wooden core with horn on the belly (the side facing the archer) and sinew on the back, glued together with animal glue and bound with birch bark to protect it from moisture. Horn resists compression and sinew resists tension, so the laminated limbs store far more energy than a self bow of the same size. Unstrung, the limbs curve sharply forward (reflex), and when strung the bow forms a compact, deeply recurved shape suited to shooting from horseback.

Military use

Mongol mounted archers used the bow with draw weights commonly in the range of 100 to 160 pounds, giving it the power to penetrate armour at considerable distances and an effective range of several hundred metres. Archers carried multiple bows and quivers with arrows of different types — light arrows for long range, heavy short-range arrows, whistling signal arrows, and fire arrows. The ability to shoot accurately in any direction from a galloping horse, including the famous rearward "Parthian shot", made the bow the central weapon of Mongol warfare and a key factor in the conquest of much of Eurasia.

Modern use

Today the Mongol bow is used in the archery competitions of the Naadam festival, where men and women shoot at targets at set distances using traditional thumb-draw technique with a thumb ring. Bowyers in Mongolia continue to make bows by traditional methods, a process that can take more than a year including drying time.
