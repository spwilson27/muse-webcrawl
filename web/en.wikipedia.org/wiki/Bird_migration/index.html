Bird migration

Bird migration is the regular seasonal movement, often north and south along a flyway, between breeding and wintering grounds. Many species of bird migrate. Migration carries high costs in predation and mortality, including from hunting by humans, and is driven primarily by the availability of food. It occurs mainly in the northern hemisphere, where birds are channelled onto specific routes by natural barriers such as seas and mountain ranges.

Patterns

About 1,800 of the world's roughly 11,000 bird species are long-distance migrants. The Arctic tern makes the longest known migration, travelling between Arctic breeding grounds and Antarctic waters — a round trip of some 70,000 km or more per year. The bar-tailed godwit makes the longest known non-stop flight, over 13,000 km across the Pacific Ocean. Not all migration is latitudinal: many species make altitudinal movements, moving down mountainsides for winter, and in Australia and southern Africa many birds move nomadically in response to rain.

Physiology and navigation

Before migrating, birds undergo hyperphagia, eating intensively to build fat stores, and many shrink internal organs to reduce weight. Migrants navigate using a suite of cues: the sun and stars, landmarks, olfactory maps, and a magnetic compass based on magnetoreception in the eye and beak. Migration is controlled by circannual rhythms entrained by photoperiod; captive migrants show a characteristic nocturnal restlessness called Zugunruhe.

Flyways

Major global flyways include the East Atlantic, Mississippi, East Asian–Australasian, and West Pacific flyways. Key stopover sites, where birds refuel, are critical conservation priorities.

Threats

Migratory birds face habitat loss at breeding, wintering and stopover sites, collisions with buildings, wind turbines and power lines, light pollution, illegal hunting, and climate change, which can cause phenological mismatch between arrival times and peak food availability.

Study

Migration is studied using bird ringing (banding), satellite telemetry, geolocators, radar (including weather radar, which detects mass movements), stable isotope analysis, and citizen-science platforms such as eBird.
