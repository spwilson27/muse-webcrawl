Ymir

Ymir and the cow Auðumbla in an 18th-century Icelandic manuscript illustration.

In Norse mythology, Ymir (Old Norse: [ˈymez̠]), also called Aurgelmir, is the first being and a primordial jötunn (giant). Ymir is attested in the Poetic Edda, compiled in the 13th century from earlier traditional material, and in the Prose Edda, written by Snorri Sturluson in the 13th century. According to the creation myth, the gods Odin and his brothers Vili and Vé slew Ymir and fashioned the world from his body.

Mythology

Ymir was formed in the primordial void of Ginnungagap from the rime and frost that accumulated where the icy rivers of the north met the heat of Muspell in the south. As he slept, Ymir sweated, and from the sweat of his armpits grew a male and a female, while his legs produced a six-headed son — the ancestors of the race of frost giants. Ymir was nourished by the primeval cow Auðumbla, who licked salty rime-covered stones and in doing so freed Búri, the grandfather of Odin.

Creation of the world

Odin and his brothers killed Ymir, and his blood flooded the world, drowning nearly all of the frost giants; only Bergelmir and his wife escaped. The brothers carried Ymir's body to the centre of Ginnungagap and made the earth from his flesh, the sea and lakes from his blood, the mountains from his bones, and trees and stones from his hair and teeth. From his skull they formed the vault of the sky, held up by four dwarfs, and from his brains they made the clouds. His eyelashes (or brows) were used to build the defensive wall of Miðgarðr, the dwelling of humankind. Maggots that appeared in Ymir's flesh were given human form and became the dwarfs.

Interpretation

Scholars have compared the dismemberment of Ymir with other Indo-European creation traditions in which the world is formed from the body of a primordial being, such as the Vedic Purusha. The name Ymir has been connected with ideas of noise or murmuring, and with the notion of a twin or double being.
