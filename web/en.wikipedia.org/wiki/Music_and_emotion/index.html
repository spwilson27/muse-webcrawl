Music and emotion

Music and emotion is the study of the relationship between music and emotion, a field within music psychology and musicology. It examines how music can convey emotions, how listeners perceive emotion in music, and how music induces felt emotions in listeners. The capacity of music to evoke powerful emotions — including joy, sadness, nostalgia and the experience of "chills" or frisson — has been recognised since antiquity, and modern research has used brain imaging, physiology and behavioural experiments to investigate the mechanisms involved.

Expressed versus induced emotion

Researchers distinguish between the emotions a listener perceives music as expressing and the emotions the music actually induces in the listener. These are related but not identical: a listener may judge a piece to be sad without feeling sad, and may enjoy sad music in a way they would not enjoy real sadness — the so-called "paradox of sad music".

Mechanisms

Patrick Juslin's BRECVEMA framework proposes eight mechanisms by which music can induce emotion: brain stem reflex, rhythmic entrainment, evaluative conditioning, emotional contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory, musical expectancy, and aesthetic judgement. Expectancy — the fulfilment, delay or violation of expectations built up by a melody or harmony, as first theorised by Leonard B. Meyer in Emotion and Meaning in Music (1956) — is considered one of the most powerful sources of musical affect. Neuroimaging studies show that intensely pleasurable music engages the brain's reward circuitry, including dopamine release in the striatum.

Cue utilisation

Listeners use structural features of music as cues to emotion: fast tempo, major mode and bright timbre are typically associated with happiness, while slow tempo, minor mode and dissonance are associated with sadness or tension. Performers can manipulate expressive cues such as timing, dynamics and articulation to shape the perceived emotion.
