Polydactyly

Type: Congenital condition

Feature: Supernumerary fingers or toes

Polydactyly is a congenital condition in which a person or animal is born with more than the usual number of digits on the hands or feet. The extra digit may range from a small nub of soft tissue to a fully formed, functional finger or toe. It is one of the most common congenital limb differences, occurring in roughly 1 in 500 to 1,000 live births, with varying incidence among populations.

Classification

Polydactyly is classed by the position of the extra digit: preaxial (on the thumb or big-toe side), central (among the middle digits, rarer), and postaxial (on the little-finger or little-toe side, the most common form). Postaxial polydactyly is especially frequent in people of African ancestry, while preaxial forms are more common in European and Asian populations.

Causes

The condition often runs in families as an autosomal dominant trait and results from disruptions in the signalling that patterns the limb bud during development, involving genes such as GLI3 and the sonic hedgehog pathway. It may occur in isolation or as part of a syndrome, including Ellis–van Creveld syndrome, trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) and Bardet–Biedl syndrome.

Treatment

Small soft-tissue nubbins may be tied off or removed in infancy; more developed extra digits are usually removed surgically, typically in the first years of life, with reconstruction of tendons, ligaments and skin as needed for hand function and appearance.

In other animals

Polydactyl cats are well known — famously the many-toed cats of Ernest Hemingway's home in Key West. The trait is also a hallmark of certain mouse mutants used in developmental biology research.
