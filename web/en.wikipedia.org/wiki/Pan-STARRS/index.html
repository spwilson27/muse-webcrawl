Pan-STARRS

OrganizationUniversity of Hawaiʻi Institute for Astronomy and partners
LocationHaleakala Observatory, Maui, Hawaiʻi
TelescopesPS1 and PS2, 1.8 m Ritchey–Chrétien
First lightPS1: 2008 (full survey from 2010)

The Panoramic Survey Telescope and Rapid Response System (Pan-STARRS), located at Haleakala Observatory, Hawaii, US, consists of astronomical cameras, telescopes and a computing facility that is surveying the sky for moving or variable objects on a continual basis, and also producing accurate astrometry and photometry of already-detected objects. It aims to discover asteroids, comets, variable stars, supernovae and other transient phenomena, and is particularly important in the search for potentially hazardous near-Earth objects.

Instruments

The first telescope, PS1, carries a 1.4-gigapixel camera — for years the largest digital camera used in astronomy — covering a field of view about 3 degrees across. A second telescope, PS2, was commissioned alongside it in the late 2010s. Each 1.8-metre telescope images thousands of square degrees per night in multiple filters, and the survey revisits each field several times to detect motion and variability.

Surveys and discoveries

Pan-STARRS conducted a 3π steradian survey of the sky north of declination −30°, releasing a catalogue of billions of objects in its public data releases (DR1 in 2016 and DR2 in 2019) — at the time the largest volume of astronomical data ever released. Its discoveries include large numbers of near-Earth asteroids and comets, among them comet C/2011 L4 (PANSTARRS). Most famously, in October 2017 Pan-STARRS 1 detected ʻOumuamua, the first known interstellar object to pass through the Solar System.

The project was developed by the University of Hawaiʻi Institute for Astronomy with funding principally from the US Air Force Research Laboratory, and data processing is shared with partner institutions in the Pan-STARRS Science Consortium.
