Dehesa

A dehesa (Spanish: [de'esa]) is a multifunctional, agrosylvopastoral system and cultural landscape of southern and central Spain and southern Portugal, where it is known as montado. It consists of open woodland of evergreen oaks — mainly holm oak and cork oak — with a grassy understory, managed for grazing livestock, cork, firewood, hunting, and crops in rotation.

Extent and ecology

Dehesas cover roughly 20,000 to 30,000 square kilometres of the Iberian Peninsula, principally in Extremadura, Andalusia, Castile-La Mancha, Castile and León, and the Portuguese Alentejo. The system is a human-made landscape, created by clearing Mediterranean forest and maintaining scattered trees over pasture; it supports exceptional biodiversity, including the Iberian lynx, Spanish imperial eagle, and black stork, and serves as wintering ground for tens of thousands of common cranes.

Uses

The dehesa is famous as the home of the Iberian pig, which is fattened on acorns (bellotas) during the autumn montanera to produce jamón ibérico de bellota. Merino sheep, cattle (including fighting bulls), goats, cork production on a nine-year stripping cycle, charcoal, honey, and game hunting all contribute to the income of dehesa estates.

Challenges

The system faces pressures from oak decline (including root rot caused by Phytophthora cinnamomi), poor tree regeneration due to grazing, rural depopulation, and changing economics, and is increasingly valued and protected for its ecological and cultural importance, including under the EU Habitats Directive.
