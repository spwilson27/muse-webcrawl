Resonance (chemistry)

In chemistry, resonance is a way of describing bonding in certain molecules or polyatomic ions by the combination of several contributing structures (or forms, also variously known as resonance structures or canonical structures) into a resonance hybrid (or hybrid structure) in valence bond theory. It has particular value for analyzing delocalized electrons where the bonding cannot be expressed by one single Lewis structure.

Under the framework of valence bond theory, resonance is an extension of the idea that the bonding in a chemical species can be described by a Lewis structure. For many chemical species, a single Lewis structure, consisting of atoms obeying the octet rule, possibly bearing formal charges, and connected by bonds of positive integer order, is sufficient to describe the physical bonding. However, certain molecules require more than one such structure; resonance theory treats the true structure as a weighted average, or "hybrid", of all valid Lewis structures.

Overview

The classic example is benzene, whose six carbon–carbon bonds are all equivalent despite alternating single and double bonds in any one drawn structure. The two equivalent Kekulé structures contribute equally to the hybrid. Other common examples include the carbonate ion (CO32−), the nitrate ion, ozone, and the carboxylate group.

Resonance forms differ only in the positions of electrons, never in the positions of atoms. Structures with a low formal charge, more covalent bonds, and negative charge on electronegative atoms contribute most to the hybrid. The energy of the actual molecule is lower than that estimated for any single contributing structure; this stabilisation is called the resonance energy.

History

The concept was introduced by Linus Pauling in the late 1920s and early 1930s, building on the earlier work of Kekulé on benzene and on quantum mechanical treatments of the hydrogen molecule ion. Pauling's book The Nature of the Chemical Bond (1939) popularised the theory. The double-headed arrow (↔) is used to separate contributing structures and must not be confused with the equilibrium arrow of chemical equilibrium.
