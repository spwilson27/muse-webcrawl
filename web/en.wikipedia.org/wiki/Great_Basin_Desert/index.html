Great Basin Desert

The Great Basin Desert is part of the Great Basin between the Sierra Nevada and the Wasatch Range in the western United States. It is a temperate desert with hot, dry summers and snowy winters. The desert spans a large part of the state of Nevada, and extends into western Utah, southeastern Oregon, and eastern California. It is one of the four major deserts of the United States, alongside the Chihuahuan, Mojave, and Sonoran deserts.

Geography

The Great Basin Desert is defined by its basin and range topography: a series of parallel north–south mountain ranges separated by broad valleys, or basins. Because the region lies in the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada and Cascade Range, most Pacific moisture is blocked before it can reach the interior. The desert is the largest in the United States, covering roughly 409,000 square kilometres. Unlike the hot deserts to the south, it is a cold desert, with much of its precipitation falling as snow.

Climate

The climate is arid and continental. Summers are hot in the valleys, while winters are cold, with average January temperatures below freezing in much of the region. Annual precipitation generally ranges from about 150 to 300 millimetres, increasing with elevation. The high mountain ranges receive considerably more snow and support forests of juniper, piñon pine, and at the highest elevations, bristlecone pine.

Ecology

The dominant plant community is sagebrush steppe, characterised by big sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata), accompanied by grasses, rabbitbrush, and greasewood in saltier soils. Playas and salt flats, such as those of the Bonneville Salt Flats, occupy the lowest basin floors. Characteristic animals include the pronghorn, mule deer, jackrabbits, sage grouse, and the Great Basin rattlesnake. Sky island mountain ranges harbour isolated populations of species such as the pika and the Bonneville cutthroat trout in cold streams.

Human history

Indigenous peoples including the Western Shoshone, Paiute, and Ute have inhabited the region for thousands of years. Euro-American exploration intensified in the 1840s, and the desert became a major barrier for emigrants travelling the California Trail. Mining booms, ranching, and the construction of the transcontinental railroad shaped later settlement.
