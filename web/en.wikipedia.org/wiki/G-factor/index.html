g-factor

A g-factor (also called g value) is a dimensionless quantity that characterizes the magnetic moment and angular momentum of an atom, a particle or a nucleus. It is essentially the proportionality constant that relates the observed magnetic moment μ of a particle to that particle's angular momentum quantum number s and a unit of magnetic moment (more correctly, the Bohr magneton or nuclear magneton). The definition is μ = g (e/2m) S, where e and m are the charge and mass of the particle and S is its spin angular momentum.

Definition

The g-factor is defined by the relation between the magnetic dipole moment and the angular momentum. For an electron, μ = −ge (e/2me) S, where the minus sign reflects the electron's negative charge. For a free electron, ge ≈ −2.00231930436. The value differs slightly from exactly 2 because of quantum electrodynamic radiative corrections, first computed by Julian Schwinger in 1948.

Electron g-factor

The electron g-factor, denoted ge, is one of the most precisely measured quantities in physics. The Dirac equation predicts ge = −2 exactly; the measured deviation, related to the anomalous magnetic dipole moment ae = (|g|−2)/2, arises from loop corrections in quantum electrodynamics. Measurements of the electron g-factor provide stringent tests of the Standard Model.

Landé g-factor

In atomic physics, the Landé g-factor describes the splitting of energy levels in a weak magnetic field (the Zeeman effect) when both orbital and spin angular momentum contribute. It is given by gJ = 3/2 + [S(S+1) − L(L+1)] / [2J(J+1)], named after Alfred Landé, who introduced it in 1921.

Nucleon and nuclear g-factors

Composite particles such as the proton and neutron also have g-factors measured in nuclear magnetons: gp ≈ 5.586 and gn ≈ −3.826. Their non-integer values reflect their internal quark structure. Nuclear g-factors are specific to each isotope and nuclear spin state and are tabulated for use in nuclear magnetic resonance and related techniques.
