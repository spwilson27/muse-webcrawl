Isma'ilism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Isma'ilism (Arabic: al-Ismāʿīliyya) is a branch of Shia Islam deriving its name from its acceptance of Isma'il ibn Ja'far as the divinely appointed successor to his father, the Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765). Isma'ilis form the second-largest Shia community after the Twelvers, with the largest branch today being the Nizari Ismailis, who follow the Aga Khan as their living Imam.

History

After Ja'far al-Sadiq's death, his followers split over the succession: Twelvers followed Musa al-Kazim, while Isma'ilis held that Isma'il, or his son Muhammad, was the rightful Imam. In the ninth and tenth centuries Isma'ili missionaries (dāʿī) spread the movement across the Islamic world. The Qarmatians established a state in Bahrain, and in 909 the Isma'ilis founded the Fatimid Caliphate in North Africa, which conquered Egypt (969), built Cairo and al-Azhar mosque-university, and ruled much of the Mediterranean until 1171.

Splits

The death of the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir (1094) split the movement into Nizari and Musta'li branches. The Nizaris, led in Persia by Hasan-i Sabbah from the fortress of Alamut, became famous in Crusader-era accounts as the "Assassins"; the Mongol invasion destroyed Alamut in 1256. Musta'li Isma'ilism survives chiefly among the Dawoodi, Sulaymani and Alavi Bohras of India and Yemen, who acknowledge a dāʿī al-muṭlaq acting for a hidden Imam. The Druze faith originated in an offshoot of Fatimid Isma'ilism in the eleventh century.

Beliefs

Isma'ili thought characteristically distinguishes the exoteric (ẓāhir) from the esoteric (bāṭin) meaning of scripture, the latter accessible through authoritative interpretation (taʾwīl) by the Imam of the time. Classical Isma'ili philosophy integrated Neoplatonic cosmology with Quranic teaching. The Nizari community, under the Aga Khans, has built a global development network (the AKDN) active in education, health and culture.
