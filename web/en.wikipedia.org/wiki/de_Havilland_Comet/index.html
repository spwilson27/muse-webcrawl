de Havilland Comet

The de Havilland DH.106 Comet was the world's first commercial jet airliner. Developed and manufactured by de Havilland in the United Kingdom, the Comet 1 prototype first flew in 1949. It entered scheduled service with BOAC in May 1952, featuring a pressurised cabin, large square windows and four de Havilland Ghost turbojet engines buried in the wing roots.

Early service and accidents

The Comet initially halved journey times on routes to Africa and Asia. However, after a year of service, Comets began suffering catastrophic in-flight break-ups, including BOAC Flight 781 and South African Airways Flight 201 in 1954. The fleet was grounded, and an exhaustive investigation — including water-tank fatigue testing of a full airframe at Farnborough — found that metal fatigue from repeated pressurisation cycles initiated cracks at stress concentrations around the square window corners and other apertures.

Redesign and later service

The redesigned Comet 4, with oval windows, a strengthened structure and Rolls-Royce Avon engines, returned to service in 1958 and pioneered transatlantic jet services, but by then the Boeing 707 and Douglas DC-8 had surpassed it in size and economics. A total of 114 Comets were built. The design was adapted into the Hawker Siddeley Nimrod maritime patrol aircraft, which served with the Royal Air Force until 2011. The fatigue lessons from the Comet investigations permanently changed airliner design and certification practice.
