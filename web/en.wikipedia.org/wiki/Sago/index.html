Sago (//) is a starch extracted from the spongy centre, or pith, of various tropical palm stems, especially those of Metroxylon sagu (the true sago palm). It is a major staple food for the lowland peoples of New Guinea and the Maluku Islands, where it is called saksak, rabia and sagu. The largest supply of sago comes from Southeast Asia, particularly Indonesia and Malaysia. Large quantities of sago are sent to Europe and North America for cooking purposes.

It is traditionally cooked and eaten in various forms, such as rolled into balls, mixed with boiling water to form a glue-like paste (papeda), or as a pancake. Sago is often produced commercially in the form of "pearls" (small rounded starch aggregates, partly gelatinised by heating), which can be boiled with water or milk and sugar to make a sweet pudding. Sago pearls are similar in appearance to those of other origin, for the pearls of cassava starch (tapioca), making the two interchangeable in some dishes.

Production

The sago palm, Metroxylon sagu, is found in tropical lowland forest and freshwater swamps across Southeast Asia and New Guinea and is the primary source of sago. It tolerates a wide variety of soils and can be found from sea level up to about 700 m. Sago was a common food dating back to the Malay Archipelago for hundreds of years.

The starch is obtained by splitting the trunk lengthwise and removing the pith, which is then crushed and kneaded to release the starch before being washed and strained to extract the starch from the fibrous residue. A suspension of the raw starch in water is then collected in a settling container. A single palm yields roughly 150–300 kg of sago over its 15-year lifespan, and a palm is cut just before flowering, when the stems are richest in starch.

Nutrition and health

Sago starch comprises approximately 27% amylose and 73% amylopectin. Sago is nearly pure carbohydrate, with about 94 g of carbohydrate per 100 g of dry sago, 0.2 g of protein, 0.5 g of dietary fibre, low amounts of calcium, iron, fat, and carotene, and about 355 calories per 100 g. Sago is often combined with other foods as a carbohydrate source.

Because sago is almost pure starch, it is traditionally paired with fish, vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods to make a complete diet. In gluten-free baking, sago flour is sometimes combined with other flours.

Uses

Food

Sago is used across Southeast Asia and the Pacific in dishes such as papeda (a viscous sago porridge eaten in Maluku and Papua), ambuyat (the national dish of Brunei), sago pudding in Malaysia and Singapore, and sago gula melaka, in which the pearls are served with palm sugar syrup and coconut milk. In India, sago pearls (sabudana) are used in dishes such as sabudana khichdi, especially during religious fasts.

Textiles and other industries

Sago starch is used as a sizing agent in textile manufacture. It has also been investigated as a feedstock for biodegradable plastics and for the production of ethanol and other fermentation products.
