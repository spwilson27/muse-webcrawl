Singing school

A singing school was a school in which students were taught to sight-read vocal music, principally hymns and psalm tunes for congregational worship. Singing schools were a major institution of musical education and social life in colonial and early national America, and their descendants survive today in the shape-note singing conventions of the American South, most famously those devoted to The Sacred Harp.

Origins in New England

In early eighteenth-century New England, reforming ministers complained that congregational psalm singing — traditionally "lined out" by a leader and repeated by ear — had become ragged and unmusical. Advocates such as Thomas Symmes argued for singing "by rule" (from notation) rather than "by rote". From the 1720s, itinerant singing masters began offering short courses, typically meeting in a church, schoolhouse or tavern for a few weeks during the winter, teaching rudiments of notation from tunebooks such as John Tufts's Introduction to the Singing of Psalm-Tunes and the works of William Billings, America's first prominent composer.

Spread and shape notes

The singing-school movement spread south and west with settlement. To ease learning, American tunebooks adopted shape notes (also called patent notes or "buckwheat" notes), in which each degree of the scale has a distinctive notehead shape; systems of four shapes (from guidonian solmisation) and later seven shapes flourished. Singing masters travelled a circuit, and their schools were also important courtship and social venues for rural youth.

Decline and survival

In the North, singing schools were gradually displaced in the nineteenth century by public-school music education and reform movements that considered shape notes and the old repertory unrefined. In the upland South, however, the tradition persisted: all-day singings and annual conventions from tunebooks such as The Sacred Harp (1844) and numerous seven-shape gospel books continue to this day, and the movement has experienced revivals far beyond its original region.
