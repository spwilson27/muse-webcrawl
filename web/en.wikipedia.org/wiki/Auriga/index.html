Auriga is a constellation in the northern celestial hemisphere. It is one of the 88 modern constellations; it was among the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy. Its name is Latin for "(the) charioteer", associating it with various mythological beings, including Erichthonius and Myrtilus. Auriga is most prominent during winter evenings in the northern hemisphere, as are five other constellations that have stars in the Winter Hexagon asterism.

Because of its position near the winter Milky Way, Auriga has many bright open clusters in its borders, including M36, M37, and M38, which are popular targets for amateur astronomers. In addition, it has one prominent nebula, the Flaming Star Nebula, associated with the variable star AE Aurigae.

Bright stars

The brightest star in Auriga is Capella (Alpha Aurigae), the sixth-brightest star in the night sky: a system of two binary pairs of yellow giant stars about 43 light-years away. Other notable stars include Beta Aurigae (Menkalinan), the prototypical eclipsing binary pair Algol-like systems Epsilon Aurigae and Zeta Aurigae, and the red dwarf-bearing field of the Kids — three small stars once drawn as baby goats held by the charioteer.

Mythology

In Greek mythology Auriga is often identified with Erichthonius of Athens, the lame king credited with inventing the four-horse chariot, or with Myrtilus, charioteer of King Oenomaus. The charioteer is conventionally depicted holding a goat (Capella) and kids over his shoulder, with the reins of his chariot in hand.

Deep-sky objects

The open clusters M36, M37 and M38 lie in a line in the southern part of the constellation and are visible in binoculars. IC 405, the Flaming Star Nebula, is an emission/reflection nebula illuminated by the runaway star AE Aurigae.
