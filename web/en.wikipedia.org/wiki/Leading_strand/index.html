Leading strand

Leading strand
ProcessDNA replication
Synthesis direction5′ → 3′, continuous, toward the replication fork
Primers neededOne (at the origin)
Contrast withLagging strand (discontinuous Okazaki fragments)

The leading strand is the strand of DNA at a replication fork that is synthesised continuously in a single piece. Because DNA polymerase can only build new DNA in the 5′→3′ direction, one of the two template strands — the one running 3′→5′ toward the fork — allows the polymerase to move in the same direction as the unwinding fork, synthesising the new strand without interruption.

Mechanism

At an origin of replication, helicase unwinds the double helix and primase lays down a single short RNA primer on the leading-strand template. DNA polymerase III (in bacteria) extends this primer continuously, following the fork as it opens. When replication terminates, the primer is removed and the strand is complete; unlike the lagging strand, no Okazaki fragments or repeated priming are required.

Leading vs lagging strand

The two new strands differ because the template strands are antiparallel. The lagging strand must be built in short backward-stitched fragments, each requiring its own primer, making its synthesis discontinuous and slightly slower. In eukaryotes, leading-strand synthesis is carried out mainly by DNA polymerase ε, while polymerase δ handles much of the lagging strand.
