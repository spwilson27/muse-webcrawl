Cædmon

Cædmon
Flourished: c. 657–680
Occupation: cowherd, lay brother, poet
Monastery: Whitby Abbey under Abbess Hild
Principal source: Bede, Ecclesiastical History, Book IV
Work: Cædmon's Hymn (9 lines survive)

Cædmon (fl. c. 657–680) is the earliest English poet whose name is known. An Anglo-Saxon cowherd in the service of Whitby Abbey in Northumbria, he is said by Bede to have received the gift of song miraculously in a dream. His only surviving work is the nine-line Cædmon's Hymn, a praise of God the Creator, which is among the oldest recorded poems in Old English.

Bede's account

In his Ecclesiastical History of the English People (completed 731), Bede tells that Cædmon, who had never learned to sing, would leave feasts when the harp came to him. One night, asleep in the stable, he dreamed that a figure commanded him to sing of "the beginning of created things"; on waking he remembered the verses and sang them. Abbess Hild recognized the gift, admitted him as a lay brother, and had scripture taught to him, which he turned into verse on creation, Genesis, the Exodus, and other biblical subjects. Bede gives a Latin paraphrase of the Hymn; Old English versions survive in manuscripts of Bede's history.

Attribution and legacy

Older scholarship attributed the major Old English biblical poems of the Junius manuscript (Genesis, Exodus, Daniel, Christ and Satan) to Cædmon, but this is no longer accepted. Cædmon remains a founding figure of English poetry, and a memorial cross to him stands in St Mary's churchyard at Whitby.
