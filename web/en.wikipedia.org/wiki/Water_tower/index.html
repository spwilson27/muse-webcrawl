Water tower

Elevated tanks like this one are landmarks in many towns.

A water tower is an elevated structure supporting a water tank constructed at a height sufficient to pressurize a distribution network of potable water, providing storage for use during peak demand. Elevation of the tank — often 40 to 60 meters above the surrounding area — converts stored water into potential energy: each 10 meters of height yields roughly 1 bar (about 100 kPa) of pressure at ground level, enough to push water through the pipes of a community.

Function and design

Water towers let pumps run at steady, efficient rates while the tank absorbs hourly fluctuations in demand, and they provide reserves for firefighting and continued supply during power failures. Designs include standpipes (tall ground-level cylinders), elevated single-pedestal spheroids and ellipsoidal tanks, multi-column towers, and composite concrete-and-steel structures. Capacities range from a few tens of thousands of liters for small or industrial systems to tens of millions for large cities. Many towers are painted with the name of their community and serve as local landmarks; historic examples include the Louisville Water Tower in Kentucky (1860) and the ornate tower at the Chicago Water Works, which survived the Great Chicago Fire of 1871.

Decline and reuse

Where terrain allows, ground-level reservoirs on hilltops or pumped systems with variable-speed drives can substitute for towers, and some obsolete towers have been demolished; others are preserved, converted to apartments or observation decks, or retained as telecommunications masts.
