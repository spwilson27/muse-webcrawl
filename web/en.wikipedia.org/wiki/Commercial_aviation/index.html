Commercial aviation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A busy airport apron at golden hour with several airliners being serviced]
Commercial aviation at a major hub.

Commercial aviation is the business of transporting passengers and cargo by air for payment, forming a major part of the aviation industry and of global trade and tourism. It encompasses scheduled airlines, charter operations and air cargo, moving billions of passengers annually — about 4.5 billion in 2019 before the COVID-19 pandemic — and roughly a third of world trade by value in air freight.

Development

Airmail subsidies launched early airlines in the 1920s; the DC-3 made passenger operations profitable in the 1930s. Post-1945 surplus aircraft and the Chicago Convention\'s framework built an international system, jets cut journey times in the 1960s, and wide-bodies plus deregulation created mass tourism from the 1970s.

Industry structure

Manufacturing is a duopoly of Airbus and Boeing (with COMAC emerging in China). Airlines consolidate into three global alliances, while leasing companies own much of the fleet. Airports, ground handlers, caterers and air navigation providers complete the ecosystem overseen by ICAO and national regulators.

Challenges

The industry faces decarbonisation pressure (sustainable aviation fuel, new propulsion), pilot shortages, airspace congestion and geopolitical closures of routes. Despite thin margins — often a few percent — it underpins tourism economies and just-in-time supply chains.
