In cell biology, a vesicle is a small structure within or outside a cell, consisting of liquid or cytoplasm enclosed by a lipid bilayer membrane. Vesicles form naturally during secretion (exocytosis), uptake (endocytosis), and transport of materials within the cell, and can also be prepared artificially, in which case they are called liposomes. Because their membrane is similar in composition to the plasma membrane, vesicles can merge with it to release or take up contents.

Formation and transport

Most vesicles bud from donor membranes — the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, endosomes, or the plasma membrane — aided by protein coats such as COPII (ER-to-Golgi), COPI (within and back from the Golgi), and clathrin (endocytosis and post-Golgi traffic). Cargo is selected by sorting signals and adaptor proteins. Motor proteins move vesicles along cytoskeletal tracks, and SNARE proteins mediate specific docking and fusion with target membranes. The 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to James Rothman, Randy Schekman, and Thomas Südhof for elucidating this machinery.

Types of vesicles

  • Transport vesicles — shuttle proteins and lipids between organelles.
  • Secretory vesicles — store products (e.g., hormones) for regulated release.
  • Synaptic vesicles — store neurotransmitters at nerve terminals for release into the synaptic cleft.
  • Lysosomes — large vesicle-like organelles containing digestive enzymes.
  • Extracellular vesicles — shed by cells into body fluids; include exosomes and microvesicles, involved in intercellular communication and studied as biomarkers.
  • Gas vesicles — protein-shelled, gas-filled structures giving buoyancy to some bacteria and archaea.
  • Liposomes — artificial vesicles used as drug-delivery vehicles and model membranes.

In chemistry

In chemistry, vesicles form spontaneously when amphiphilic molecules such as phospholipids or block copolymers are dispersed in water: the molecules self-assemble into closed bilayers that minimize exposure of their hydrophobic tails. Such systems are studied as models for the origin of cellular compartments and as nanocarriers in medicine and cosmetics.
