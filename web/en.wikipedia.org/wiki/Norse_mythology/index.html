Norse mythology is the body of myths of the North Germanic peoples, drawn chiefly from Old Norse texts of medieval Iceland — the anonymous poems of the Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (c. 1220). It tells of Odin, one-eyed seeker of wisdom; Thor, defender of gods and men; the trickster Loki; and a cosmos held in the branches of the world tree Yggdrasil, doomed to end in fire and flood at Ragnarök — after which a renewed world rises from the sea.

Myths and legacy

Creation begins with ice and fire meeting in the void Ginnungagap; the first gods dismember the giant Ymir to build earth, sea and sky. Tales include Odin hanging on the tree to win the runes, Thor fishing for the World Serpent, and Baldr's death through Loki's malice — the first link in the chain to Ragnarök. Christianisation ended cult practice, but the myths survived in Icelandic manuscripts and now permeate modern fantasy, from Wagner's Ring to Tolkien and Marvel's Thor.
