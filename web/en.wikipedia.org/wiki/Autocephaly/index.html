Autocephaly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Autocephaly (from Greek αὐτοκεφαλία, meaning "quality of self-headedness") is the status of a hierarchical Christian church whose head bishop does not report to any higher-ranking bishop. The term is used primarily in Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches, and denotes a church that elects its own primate, consecrates its own bishops and prepares its own holy chrism, while remaining in communion with other churches.

Meaning and practice

An autocephalous church is fully self-governing in internal administration but is not a separate denomination: it shares the same faith, sacraments and canon law tradition with the other Orthodox churches, whose primates are commemorated according to the order of the diptychs. Autocephaly is normally proclaimed by a tomos issued by a mother church or recognised by an ecumenical council. Churches that govern themselves but whose primate must be confirmed by a mother church are termed autonomous.

History

The ancient patriarchates — Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem — formed the historic pentarchy. As Christianity spread beyond the Roman Empire, national churches such as those of Cyprus (recognised at the Council of Ephesus in 431), Georgia, Bulgaria, Serbia and Russia received self-government. The longest-standing modern dispute concerned the Orthodox Church of Ukraine, granted a tomos of autocephaly by the Ecumenical Patriarchate in January 2019, a decision rejected by the Moscow Patriarchate, which broke communion with Constantinople in 2018.

Autocephalous churches

The churches generally recognised as autocephalous include the four ancient patriarchates of the East, the patriarchates of Russia, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria and Georgia, the churches of Cyprus, Greece, Poland, Albania, the Czech Lands and Slovakia, and the Orthodox Church in America (whose autocephaly is recognised by some but not all churches).
