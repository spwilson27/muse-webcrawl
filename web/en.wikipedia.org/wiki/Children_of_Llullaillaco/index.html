Children of Llullaillaco

The Children of Llullaillaco (Spanish: Niños del Llullaillaco) are three Inca child mummies discovered in 1999 near the summit of Llullaillaco, a volcano on the border of Argentina and Chile. Sacrificed around the year 1500 as part of the Inca capacocha ritual, they are among the best-preserved mummies ever found, owing to the freezing, dry conditions at an altitude of about 6,739 metres (22,110 ft).

Discovery

An expedition led by archaeologist Johan Reinhard and Constanza Ceruti located a burial platform near the summit in March 1999. Excavating through frozen ground, the team recovered three children: a girl of about 15, known as "La Doncella" (the Maiden); a girl of about six, known as "La Niña del Rayo" (the Lightning Girl), whose face had been struck by lightning after burial; and a boy of about seven, known as "El Niño". Grave goods included textiles, pottery, figurines of gold, silver and shell, and food offerings.

Scientific study

CT scans and tissue analyses showed the children had been drugged with coca and alcohol in their final months, with doses increasing sharply before their deaths, apparently to keep them compliant and insensible. The Maiden's hair samples revealed a dramatic improvement in diet about a year before her death — from a peasant diet of potatoes to one including maize and animal protein — suggesting she was selected well in advance and elevated in status as an aclla, a chosen woman. The cause of death appears to have been exposure after being left sedated in the cold.

Display and controversy

Since 2007 the children have been displayed, one at a time in rotation, in a specially designed low-temperature chamber at the Museum of High Altitude Archaeology (MAAM) in Salta, Argentina. The display has drawn both scientific interest and criticism from some indigenous groups, who regard the excavation and exhibition of the bodies as desecration.
