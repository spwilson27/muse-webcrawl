Battle of Watling Street

The Battle of Watling Street took place in Roman Britain in AD 60 or 61 and ended the revolt led by Boudica, queen of the Iceni. A greatly outnumbered Roman army under the governor Gaius Suetonius Paulinus defeated the Britons in a pitched battle at a location that remains unidentified, somewhere along the Roman road of Watling Street, probably in the West Midlands.

Background

Boudica's revolt had destroyed the Roman towns of Camulodunum (Colchester), Londinium (London) and Verulamium (St Albans) with great loss of life. Suetonius, returning from campaigning in Wales with the Fourteenth Legion, detachments of the Twentieth, and auxiliaries — around 10,000 men — chose a battlefield in a narrow defile with woods protecting his rear.

The battle

Tacitus describes the Britons, confident in their numbers (his figure of 230,000 is certainly exaggerated), placing their families in wagons at the edge of the field to watch the expected victory. The Romans advanced in wedge formation after discharging their javelins, and the British host, unable to manoeuvre in the confined ground, was driven back onto its own wagon line, which trapped the fugitives. Tacitus claims 80,000 Britons died against some 400 Roman dead — figures modern historians treat as rhetorical, though the defeat was plainly catastrophic. Boudica died shortly afterwards, by suicide or illness according to different accounts.

Aftermath

The revolt was crushed, and punitive campaigns followed, though the procurator Julius Classicianus criticised Suetonius's severity and he was eventually replaced by a more conciliatory governor. The battle secured Roman rule in southern Britain for more than three centuries.
