Soil seed bank

The soil seed bank is the natural store of seeds, often dormant, in the soil of most ecosystems. It consists of seeds shed from vegetation above ground as well as seeds buried by animals, wind, and litter. The study of soil seed banks began with Charles Darwin, who observed seedlings emerging from mud taken from a pond, and the field became central to plant ecology, weed science, and restoration ecology.

Longevity and persistence

Seed banks are commonly classed as transient (seeds surviving less than a year), short-term persistent (one to five years), or long-term persistent (more than five years). Some seeds survive extraordinarily long: a Judean date palm seed roughly 2,000 years old germinated successfully, and viable seeds of the annual Verbascum blattaria germinated after 120 years in the long-running Beal seed viability experiment begun in 1879. Longevity depends on seed-coat properties, dormancy mechanisms, depth of burial, and the soil environment.

Ecological roles

  • Regeneration after disturbance — after fire, flood, or clearing, buried seeds germinate to re-establish vegetation, sometimes long after the parent plants have disappeared.
  • Population buffering — a reservoir of genetic diversity spreads germination risk across years ("bet-hedging"), aiding survival through bad seasons.
  • Weed persistence — agricultural weed seed banks frustrate control efforts, since only a fraction germinates each year.
  • Restoration — seed banks can assist ecosystem restoration, but may also be dominated by undesirable or invasive species.

Measurement

Researchers estimate seed banks by taking soil cores and either extracting and identifying seeds directly or germinating them in trays under controlled conditions and identifying the seedlings. Densities range from a few hundred seeds per square metre in some forests to tens of thousands in arable fields.
