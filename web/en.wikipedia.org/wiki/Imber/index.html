Imber

Imber is an uninhabited village and former civil parish within the British Army's Salisbury Plain Training Area in Wiltshire, England. The village lies in a deep, isolated valley roughly 4 km west of the A360 between Tilshead and West Lavington. In 1943 the village's inhabitants were evicted so the area could be used for military training, and the village has remained in military use ever since, with public access granted only on a few days each year.

History

Settlement in the area dates to Saxon times, and the village is recorded in the Domesday Book of 1086. The church of St Giles dates largely from the 13th and 14th centuries and is a Grade I listed building. By the early 20th century Imber was a small farming community whose population had been declining for decades, like many Wiltshire downland villages.

Evacuation

In November 1943, at the height of the Second World War, the villagers were given 47 days' notice to leave their homes so the area could be used to train American troops for the invasion of Europe. The War Office had been buying up land around the village for years. Residents were led to believe they would be able to return after the war, but despite campaigns led by former residents and their descendants—most prominently in the 1960s and 1970s—the Army retained the village permanently. The last resident is said to have been the blacksmith, Albert Nash, who died shortly after the eviction and is buried in St Giles' churchyard.

The village today

Many original buildings were destroyed by shellfire or demolished, and have been replaced by purpose-built training structures resembling a small urban warfare village. The church of St Giles survived, protected by its listed status, and was restored in the 2000s; it opens to the public on specific days, often including an annual service around St Giles' Day (1 September) and at Christmas. A famous annual tradition sees vintage London buses run a special bus service (route 23A) to the village on one open day each summer.

Access to Imber is controlled by the Ministry of Defence under the Salisbury Plain byelaws; the surrounding training area is a live firing range and remains dangerous outside the published open days.
