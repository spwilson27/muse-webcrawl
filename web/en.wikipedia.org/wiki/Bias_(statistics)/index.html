Bias (statistics)

Bias (systematic offset) versus precision (scatter) in repeated measurement.

In statistics, the bias of an estimator is the systematic difference between the estimator's expected value and the true value of the parameter being estimated. An estimator with zero bias is called unbiased; otherwise it is biased. More generally, the term refers to systematic error in any statistical procedure, as distinct from random error (variance).

Definition

For an estimator θ̂ of a parameter θ, the bias is defined as

Bias(θ̂) = E[θ̂] − θ.

For example, the sample variance computed with a divisor of n is biased downward; dividing instead by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) yields an unbiased estimator.

Types of bias

Many named biases arise in sampling and observational studies, including selection bias, sampling bias, response bias, recall bias, observer bias, publication bias, and survivorship bias. Randomization, blinding, and careful study design are the standard defences; unlike random error, bias does not diminish with larger sample sizes.

Trade-offs

Unbiasedness is not always the best criterion: the mean squared error of an estimator decomposes into variance plus squared bias, so accepting a small amount of bias can substantially reduce variance and total error — the essence of the bias–variance tradeoff in estimation and machine learning.
