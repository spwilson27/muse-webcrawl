Limousine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A limousine (or limo) is a large, chauffeur-driven luxury vehicle traditionally defined by a partition between the driver's compartment and the passenger compartment. The word derives from the French region of Limousin, whose shepherds wore a hooded cloak resembling the covered compartment of early vehicles of the type.

History

The first automobile limousines appeared around 1902. By the 1920s the term was firmly established for enclosed luxury cars with an outside or partitioned driving seat. In the United States, coachbuilders began cutting and lengthening production sedans to create "stretch limousines"; Armbruster of Fort Smith, Arkansas, is credited with the first stretch limousine around 1928, initially used to transport big-band leaders and their orchestras.

Types

Traditional limousines are built on luxury sedan platforms, while stretch limousines — extended by coachbuilders to carry eight to twenty or more passengers — are hired for weddings, proms, corporate travel, and nights out, and are often fitted with bars, entertainment systems, and mood lighting. Limousine conversions of SUVs such as the Hummer H2 became common in the 2000s. In German usage, Limousine simply denotes any sedan/saloon car, a source of occasional confusion in translation.

Use

Limousines are associated with wealth and ceremony: they serve as state cars for heads of government (such as the United States presidential state car), airport transfer vehicles, and funeral cars. Their operation is licensed as livery service in most jurisdictions, distinguishing them from taxicabs.
