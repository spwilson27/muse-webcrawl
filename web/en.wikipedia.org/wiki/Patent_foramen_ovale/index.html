Patent foramen ovale

A patent foramen ovale (PFO) is a congenital heart defect in which the foramen ovale in the interatrial septum of the heart does not close at birth as it normally does. The foramen ovale is a flap-like opening between the right and left atria that allows blood to bypass the fetal lungs in utero. A PFO is present in roughly one quarter of the adult population, making it one of the most common congenital cardiac findings, and in most people it causes no symptoms and requires no treatment.

The foramen ovale normally closes shortly after birth; when it remains open, a potential right-to-left shunt persists.

Anatomy and physiology

During fetal development, oxygenated blood from the placenta enters the right atrium and passes through the foramen ovale into the left atrium, bypassing the non-functioning lungs. At birth, increased pressure in the left atrium presses the septum primum flap against the septum secundum, functionally closing the opening. In most infants the tissues fuse permanently within the first year of life. When fusion does not occur, the result is a PFO — a potential channel that can open transiently whenever right atrial pressure exceeds left atrial pressure, such as during coughing, straining, or a Valsalva maneuver.

Clinical significance

Most people with a PFO never experience any related problem. However, a PFO can allow a blood clot from the venous circulation to cross into the arterial circulation — a paradoxical embolism — bypassing the filtering function of the lungs. This mechanism has been implicated in cryptogenic stroke (stroke of unknown cause), particularly in younger patients. PFOs have also been associated with decompression sickness in divers and, more controversially, with migraine with aura.

Diagnosis

A PFO is most reliably detected by transesophageal echocardiography with an agitated saline ("bubble") contrast study, in which microbubbles injected into a vein are observed crossing into the left atrium, especially during a Valsalva maneuver. Transthoracic echocardiography and transcranial Doppler are also used for screening.

Treatment

An incidental PFO requires no treatment. For patients with cryptogenic stroke attributed to paradoxical embolism, management options include antiplatelet or anticoagulant medication, or percutaneous catheter-based closure with a double-disc occluder device. Several randomized trials published in 2017 showed that closure reduced recurrent stroke in carefully selected patients under 60 compared with medical therapy alone.
