Marginal tax rate

In a tax system, the marginal tax rate is the rate of tax paid on the next dollar (or other unit of currency) of taxable income. It is contrasted with the average (or effective) tax rate, which is the total tax paid divided by total income. In progressive tax systems, the marginal rate increases as income rises, because income is divided into brackets taxed at successively higher rates; only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate.

Definition

The marginal tax rate is defined as the change in tax liability divided by the change in income: MTR = ΔTax / ΔIncome. Under a graduated income tax, a taxpayer's marginal rate is the rate of the highest bracket into which their income falls. A common misconception is that moving into a higher bracket subjects all of a taxpayer's income to the higher rate; in fact only income above the bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate.

Example

Consider a simplified system with a 10% rate on income up to $10,000 and 20% above that:

IncomeTaxMarginal rateEffective rate
$10,000$1,00010%10.0%
$20,000$1,000 + $2,000 = $3,00020%15.0%
$50,000$1,000 + $8,000 = $9,00020%18.0%

The effective rate approaches, but never reaches, the top marginal rate.

Marginal versus effective rate

The marginal rate is most relevant for decisions about earning additional income, taking on extra work, or realizing additional investment returns, since it determines how much of the next unit of income the taxpayer keeps. The effective rate better describes the overall tax burden. Politicians and commentators may cite either figure; confusion between them is common in public debate.

Implicit marginal rates

Marginal rates can also arise implicitly from the phase-out of deductions, credits, and means-tested benefits as income rises. When benefit withdrawal is combined with explicit taxes, effective marginal tax rates can be very high — a situation sometimes described as a "poverty trap" or "welfare cliff". Economists often study these combined rates when analysing work incentives.

Flat taxes

Under a flat tax with no exemption, the marginal rate equals the average rate. With an exemption or standard deduction, a nominally flat tax still has a marginal rate above the average rate for most taxpayers, because the exempted portion lowers the effective rate while each additional dollar is taxed at the single flat rate.
