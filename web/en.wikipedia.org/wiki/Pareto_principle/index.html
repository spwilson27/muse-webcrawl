The Pareto principle, also known as the 80/20 rule or the law of the vital few, states that for many outcomes, roughly 80% of consequences come from 20% of causes. It is a rule of thumb about uneven distributions, not a precise law: the figures need not sum to 100, and actual ratios vary from near-equal to far more skewed.

Origin

The principle is named for the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, who observed in 1896–97 that approximately 80% of Italy's land was owned by 20% of the population, and (in a story often retold) that 20% of the pea pods in his garden produced 80% of the peas. Pareto formalised such distributions in the Pareto distribution of probability theory. Management consultant Joseph M. Juran generalised the observation in the 1940s–50s, naming it after Pareto and applying it to quality control: most defects, he argued, stem from a "vital few" causes rather than the "trivial many" — later softened to the "useful many".

Applications

The pattern is invoked across many domains: in business, 80% of sales often come from 20% of customers or products; in computing, a small fraction of code paths accounts for most execution time and errors (related to Pareto charts used in quality management to rank defect causes); in economics, wealth and income distributions are typically heavy-tailed, consistent with Pareto's original finding; and in time management the rule is used to prioritise the minority of tasks producing most value. Zipf's law and the Pareto distribution describe the underlying mathematics of such skewed patterns.

Criticism

Critics note that the 80/20 split is approximate and often quoted without evidence; that correlation between inputs and outputs does not establish which causes matter; and that indiscriminate application can excuse neglect of the long tail, which in aggregate may be large. Nonetheless, as a heuristic for prioritisation — focus improvement effort where a distribution is most concentrated — the principle remains widely used in management, engineering and personal productivity.
