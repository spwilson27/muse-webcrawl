Universal joint

A universal joint (also called a U-joint, Cardan joint, or Hooke's joint) is a joint or coupling connecting rigid rods whose axes are inclined to each other, commonly used in shafts that transmit rotary motion. It consists of a pair of hinges located close together, oriented at 90° to each other, connected by a cross shaft.

History

The basic concept appeared in ancient gimbals. Gerolamo Cardano described the arrangement in the 16th century, and Robert Hooke analyzed its non-uniform motion in the 17th century, using it in helioscopes and clocks. The device was widely applied in mill machinery and later in the drive shafts of automobiles.

Mechanics

A single universal joint does not transmit motion at a constant velocity ratio: when the shafts are at an angle, the output speed fluctuates twice per revolution, causing vibration. Pairing two joints in a double Cardan arrangement, with the intermediate shaft's yokes aligned, cancels the fluctuation if the input and output shafts are parallel — the principle behind the propeller shaft layout of rear-wheel-drive vehicles.

Applications

Universal joints are used in vehicle drive shafts, steering columns, agricultural PTO shafts, and machinery where shafts must change direction. Modern alternatives include constant-velocity (CV) joints in front-wheel-drive axle shafts.
