Copyright Act of 1976

Copyright Act of 1976
Enacted: October 19, 1976
Signed by: President Gerald Ford
Effective: January 1, 1978
Codified: Title 17, United States Code

The Copyright Act of 1976 is a United States copyright law and remains the primary basis of copyright law in the United States today. It superseded the Copyright Act of 1909 and took effect on January 1, 1978. The Act was the product of more than two decades of study and revision, incorporating the work of the Copyright Office and the recommendations of the 1961 Report of the Register of Copyrights.

Significant changes

The Act extended federal copyright protection to works from the moment of creation, replacing the system in which unpublished works were protected by state common law and published works required registration and notice for federal protection. It defined the exclusive rights of copyright holders in section 106, set the general term of copyright as the life of the author plus 50 years (later extended to 70 by the Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998), and, for works made for hire, 75 years from publication or 100 years from creation, whichever is shorter (later extended to 95/120 years).

Fair use

Section 107 codified the doctrine of fair use for the first time in statutory law, listing four factors courts should consider. The Act also introduced the first-sale doctrine in section 109 and a compulsory license for cable television retransmission.
