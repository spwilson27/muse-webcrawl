Tigre people

The Tigre people are an ethnic group native to Eritrea and northeastern Sudan. They speak Tigre, a South Semitic language closely related to Tigrinya and more distantly to Ge'ez and the Ethiopian Semitic languages. The Tigre are concentrated in the western and northern lowlands of Eritrea, particularly around the town of Keren in the Anseba region, and extend across the border into eastern Sudan. Estimates of their population vary, but they number roughly one and a half to two million, making them the second-largest ethnic group in Eritrea after the Tigrinya.

History

The Tigre are generally considered descendants of the indigenous Beja-related peoples of the Red Sea hills and of Aksumite-era populations of the Eritrean highlands and lowlands. Historically they were organised into hierarchical societies in which a class of aristocratic overlords, known as the shumagle, ruled over serf-like cultivators and herders. Many Tigre groups paid tribute to neighbouring powers, including the Funj Sultanate of Sennar and later Egyptian and Ethiopian rulers. During the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the Tigre gradually settled into village agriculture alongside their older pastoralism.

Language

The Tigre language is written in the Ge'ez script. It preserves a number of archaic features lost in Tigrinya, and linguists regard the two languages as the sole surviving members of the North Ethiopian Semitic branch. Dialects include Mensa, Beni-Amir, and Habab varieties. Because of their centuries of contact with Cushitic-speaking Beja and Bilin neighbours, Tigre vocabulary contains a substantial Cushitic component.

Religion and culture

The great majority of Tigre are Sunni Muslims, Islam having spread among them from the Red Sea coast beginning in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; a minority, especially among the Mensa groups near Keren, are Christians, both Orthodox and Catholic. Traditional Tigre society is patrilineal and clan-based, with a strong pastoral tradition centred on cattle, camels, and goats. Oral poetry, sung to the accompaniment of a lyre-like instrument, remains an important art form.
