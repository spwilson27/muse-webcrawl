Classical period (music)

The Classical period was an era of classical music between roughly 1750 and 1820. It is characterized by a lighter, clearer texture than Baroque music, emphasis on balance, proportion, and clarity of form, and the development of sonata form and the modern symphony orchestra. The period is sometimes called the "Viennese Classical" era after its dominant center.

Style and forms

Classical composers favored homophonic melody-and-accompaniment textures over Baroque counterpoint, periodic phrasing, and dynamic contrast made possible by the new pianoforte replacing the harpsichord. The era codified the symphony, string quartet, piano sonata, and concerto in multi-movement plans organized around sonata form. Joseph Haydn — the "father of the symphony and string quartet" — and Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart brought the style to maturity in Vienna; Beethoven's middle-period works pushed against its bounds and opened the way to Romanticism.

Social context

The shift from aristocratic patronage to public concerts, publishing, and a growing middle-class audience reshaped composers' careers; C. P. E. Bach and the Mannheim school developed the early symphony, and opera flourished in both opera seria, opera buffa, and Mozart's Singspiele such as The Magic Flute (1791).
