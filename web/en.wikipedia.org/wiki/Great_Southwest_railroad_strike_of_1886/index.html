The Great Southwest railroad strike of 1886 was a labour union strike involving more than 200,000 workers. Beginning in March 1886, railroad workers in five states struck against the Union Pacific and Missouri Pacific railroads, part of financier Jay Gould's Southwest system. The strike was led by the Knights of Labor, then at the height of their power after a successful 1885 strike against Gould's lines had forced him to rescind a wage cut.

Course

The immediate cause was the firing of a Knights of Labor foreman, C. A. Hall, by the Texas and Pacific Railway at Marshall, Texas, in February 1886, in violation of the 1885 understanding. Under district leader Martin Irons, workers walked out on 1 March and the strike spread across Gould's lines in Texas, Arkansas, Kansas, Missouri and Illinois. Unlike in 1885, Gould was prepared: the railroads hired strikebreakers, obtained court injunctions, and received support from state militia, Texas Rangers, Pinkerton agents and federal courts. Violence flared at several points, most seriously at East St. Louis in April 1886, where deputies fired on a crowd, killing several bystanders.

Defeat and consequences

Knights of Labor Grand Master Workman Terence V. Powderly, who had opposed the strike, sought a settlement; Gould refused to recognise the union. By the autumn the strike had collapsed. The defeat — together with the backlash against labour after the Haymarket bombing of May 1886, with which the Knights were unfairly associated — began the rapid collapse of the Knights of Labor, whose membership fell from over 700,000 in 1886 to about 100,000 by 1890. Workers increasingly turned to the craft-union model of the new American Federation of Labor.
