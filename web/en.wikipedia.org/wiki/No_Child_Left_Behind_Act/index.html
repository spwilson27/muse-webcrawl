No Child Left Behind Act

The No Child Left Behind Act of 2001 (NCLB) was a United States Act of Congress that reauthorized the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965. It was signed into law by President George W. Bush on January 8, 2002, and was the most sweeping federal education reform in decades, built around standards-based testing and accountability.

Provisions

The law required states to develop assessments in basic skills and to test students annually in reading and mathematics in grades 3–8 and once in high school. Schools receiving federal Title I funding had to demonstrate "adequate yearly progress" (AYP) toward the goal of all students reaching proficiency by 2014, including disaggregated results for subgroups by race, income, disability and English proficiency. Schools that repeatedly missed AYP faced escalating sanctions, including offering transfers, supplemental tutoring, restructuring, or conversion to charter status.

Support and criticism

Supporters credited NCLB with exposing achievement gaps and raising math scores, particularly for younger students. Critics argued it encouraged teaching to the test, narrowed curricula, set an unrealistic universal-proficiency deadline, and was underfunded relative to its mandates. From 2011 the Obama administration granted waivers relieving states of some requirements in exchange for adopting reforms such as the Common Core standards.

Replacement

NCLB was replaced on December 10, 2015, by the Every Student Succeeds Act, which retained annual testing but returned much accountability design to the states.
