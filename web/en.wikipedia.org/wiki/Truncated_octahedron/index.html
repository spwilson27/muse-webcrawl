In geometry, the truncated octahedron is an Archimedean solid with 14 faces: 6 squares and 8 regular hexagons, with 36 edges and 24 vertices, one square and two hexagons meeting at each vertex. It is obtained by cutting off (truncating) the six vertices of a regular octahedron at one-third of the edge length so that the original triangular faces become regular hexagons. Its vertices can be given by all permutations of (0, ±1, ±2) in Cartesian coordinates, reflecting its identification with the permutahedron of order 4.

Properties

For an edge length a, the truncated octahedron has surface area A = (6 + 12√3)a² ≈ 26.78a² and volume V = 8√2a³ ≈ 11.31a³. It has full octahedral symmetry, and its dual is the tetrakis hexahedron.

Space-filling

The truncated octahedron is notable as the only Archimedean solid that can tile three-dimensional space by translation alone, forming the bitruncated cubic honeycomb. For this reason Lord Kelvin proposed in 1887 that a slightly curved version of it — the Kelvin cell — would be the most efficient way to partition space into equal-volume cells with minimal surface area; the conjecture stood for over a century until the Weaire–Phelan structure, with slightly less surface area, was found in 1993. The shape also appears as the Voronoi cell of the body-centred cubic lattice, and in chemistry as the sodalite cage found in zeolites, and as the structure of the hydrocarbon adamantane-like cages; it is also the familiar shape of the "Meffert's" style of a variety of puzzles and of some dice.
