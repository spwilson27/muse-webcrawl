Oceanic anoxic event

Oceanic anoxic events (OAEs) are periods in Earth's history when large parts of the oceans became severely depleted in dissolved oxygen, leading to the deposition of organic-rich black shales over wide areas. They occurred most prominently during the warm greenhouse climates of the Mesozoic Era, especially in the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods.

Characteristics

OAEs are recognised in the rock record by widespread black shale layers rich in organic carbon, accompanied by distinctive carbon isotope excursions indicating a major perturbation of the global carbon cycle. Burial of large quantities of organic matter drew down carbon dioxide and may ultimately have helped cool the climate. The events typically lasted several hundred thousand to over a million years.

Notable events

The Toarcian Oceanic Anoxic Event (about 183 million years ago, Early Jurassic) is associated with massive volcanism of the Karoo–Ferrar large igneous province and a marine mass extinction. Cretaceous events include OAE 1a (the Selli Event, about 120 million years ago) and OAE 2 at the Cenomanian–Turonian boundary (about 94 million years ago), associated with the Caribbean large igneous province and elevated extinction rates among marine organisms.

Causes

Proposed causes include large igneous province eruptions releasing carbon dioxide and nutrients, rapid global warming that slowed ocean circulation and increased biological productivity, and methane release. Anoxia itself can become self-reinforcing: low oxygen promotes recycling of phosphorus from sediments, fertilising further productivity and oxygen demand. Modern expanding oxygen-minimum zones make OAEs a subject of interest as analogues for future ocean deoxygenation.
