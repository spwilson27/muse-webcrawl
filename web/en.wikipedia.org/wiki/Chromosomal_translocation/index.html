Chromosomal translocation

In genetics, a chromosome translocation is a phenomenon that results in unusual rearrangement of chromosomes. This includes balanced and unbalanced translocation, with two main types: reciprocal (or non-Robertsonian) and Robertsonian translocation. Reciprocal translocation is a chromosome abnormality caused by exchange of parts between non-homologous chromosomes. Two detached fragments of two different chromosomes are switched.

Robertsonian translocations occur when two acrocentric chromosomes fuse at the centromere, losing their short arms. Carriers have 45 chromosomes instead of the usual 46, yet are usually phenotypically normal because the lost material is largely repeated ribosomal RNA genes.

Balanced versus unbalanced

A translocation is balanced when no genetic material is gained or lost overall; carriers are typically healthy but face increased risks of infertility and of producing offspring with unbalanced translocations, which can cause miscarriage or congenital abnormalities. An unbalanced translocation involves unequal exchange, resulting in extra or missing genes and usually serious effects.

Role in disease

Certain translocations are characteristically associated with cancers. The best known is the Philadelphia chromosome, a reciprocal translocation t(9;22)(q34;q11) that creates the BCR-ABL fusion gene, causing chronic myeloid leukaemia. Another classic example is t(8;14) in Burkitt lymphoma, which places the MYC oncogene under the control of an immunoglobulin enhancer. Some Robertsonian translocations involving chromosome 21 can cause familial Down syndrome.

Detection

Translocations are detected by cytogenetic techniques such as G-banded karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and increasingly by genome sequencing.
