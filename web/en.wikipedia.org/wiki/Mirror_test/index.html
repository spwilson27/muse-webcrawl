Overview

The mirror test, or mirror self-recognition (MSR) test, is a behavioural technique developed by psychologist Gordon Gallup Jr. in 1970 to investigate whether an animal possesses the capacity of visual self-recognition. An animal is marked with an odourless spot on a body part only visible via a mirror; if it then touches or investigates the mark while using the mirror, this is taken as evidence it recognises the reflection as itself.

Results and debate

Chimpanzees, bonobos and orangutans pass reliably; gorillas usually do not (with exceptions). Among non-primates, reported passes include bottlenose dolphins, Asian elephants (at least one individual), Eurasian magpies, and — controversially — cleaner wrasse fish. Human children typically pass at 18–24 months. Critics argue the test is biased toward visually oriented, hand-using species and that failure does not prove absence of self-awareness; Gallup links MSR to a self-concept and possibly theory of mind.
