Paavo Johannes Nurmi (13 June 1897 – 2 October 1973) was a Finnish middle-distance and long-distance runner. He was called the "Flying Finn" and dominated distance running in the early 20th century. Nurmi won nine gold and three silver medals at the Olympic Games between 1920 and 1928, making him one of the most successful Olympic athletes of all time, and set 22 official world records at distances between 1,500 metres and 20 kilometres.

Nurmi racing at the 1924 Paris Games, where he won five gold medals in six days.

Career

Born into a working-class family in Turku, Nurmi began running seriously as a teenager and developed a revolutionary training system with meticulous pace measurement—he famously raced with a stopwatch in hand. He made his Olympic debut at Antwerp 1920, winning gold in the 10,000 m and the cross-country events. At Paris 1924 he achieved one of the greatest feats in athletics history, winning the 1,500 m and 5,000 m finals less than two hours apart, and five gold medals overall. At Amsterdam 1928 he added a 10,000 m gold and two silvers.

Nurmi planned to end his career with the marathon at the 1932 Los Angeles Olympics, but shortly before the Games the IAAF declared him a professional—stemming from appearance payments on a 1929 American tour—and barred him, to lasting bitterness in Finland. He nevertheless returned to Olympic prominence when he lit the Olympic flame at the 1952 Helsinki Games.

Legacy

Nurmi is regarded as one of the greatest distance runners ever. His systematic, scientific approach to training and pacing transformed the sport. A statue of him stands outside Helsinki's Olympic Stadium, his image appeared on the Finnish 10-mark note, and the asteroid 1740 Paavo Nurmi is named after him. Later in life he became a successful businessman in construction and clothing; his chain of menswear shops still bears his name.
