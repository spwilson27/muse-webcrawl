Dexter electron transfer

Dexter electron transfer (also called Dexter electron exchange or Dexter energy transfer) is a fluorescence quenching and energy transfer mechanism in which an excited electron is transferred from one molecule (a donor) to a second molecule (an acceptor) via a non-radiative path. It was proposed by David L. Dexter in 1953.

Mechanism

Dexter transfer requires overlap of the donor and acceptor molecular orbitals: an electron in an excited state of the donor moves into the acceptor while a ground-state electron of the acceptor simultaneously moves to the donor — a correlated exchange of two electrons. Because it depends on orbital overlap, its rate falls off exponentially with distance, k ∝ exp(−2R/L), and is significant only at distances of about 1 nm or less.

Comparison with FRET

Förster resonance energy transfer operates through dipole–dipole coupling over much longer distances (1–10 nm) and transfers energy without electron motion; it also cannot change spin multiplicity. Dexter exchange, by contrast, can transfer triplet excitation, making it essential in phosphorescent organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) and in photosynthetic and photocatalytic systems.

Applications

The mechanism underlies triplet–triplet annihilation photon upconversion, sensitised phosphorescence, and aspects of charge and exciton transport in organic semiconductors.
