The Schlieffen Plan (German: Schlieffen-Plan) was a German operational strategy for a war against France and Russia, elaborated in memoranda of 1905–06 by Field Marshal Alfred von Schlieffen, Chief of the German General Staff from 1891 to 1906. Confronted with the prospect of a two-front war, the plan sought to defeat France rapidly — in about six weeks — with an overwhelming right wing sweeping through neutral Belgium and Luxembourg, enveloping Paris from the west, while holding defensively against Russia in the east; Germany would then transfer its forces east before Russian mobilisation was complete.

Design

Schlieffen's concept allocated roughly seven-eighths of the army in the west, with the strongest forces on the right wing advancing through Belgium as far as the Channel coast; the last man on the right was famously said to "brush the Channel with his sleeve." Schlieffen modelled the intended encirclement on Hannibal's victory at Cannae, the archetype of the battle of annihilation (Vernichtungsschlacht). The plan assumed Britain might be provoked by the violation of Belgian neutrality (guaranteed by the Treaty of London of 1839) but gambled that the war would be decided before this mattered.

Execution in 1914

Schlieffen's successor, Helmuth von Moltke the Younger, modified the deployment — strengthening the left wing in Alsace-Lorraine and the forces facing Russia and sparing the Netherlands. In August 1914 the German armies advanced through Belgium, meeting unexpected resistance and British intervention, while logistics and exhaustion strained the right wing. Alexander von Kluck's First Army turned inside Paris rather than west of it, exposing its flank; the French and British counterattack at the First Battle of the Marne (5–12 September 1914) halted the advance and set off the "Race to the Sea," crystallising the trench deadlock of the Western Front.

Historiography

The plan's very existence and content have been debated by historians: Gerhard Ritter published the 1905 memorandum in the 1950s, while Terence Zuber argued in Inventing the Schlieffen Plan (2002) that the canonical plan was largely a post-war construction of the General Staff's apologists. Most historians accept that a Schlieffen-style deployment plan guided German operations in 1914 while disagreeing about its rigidity and about whether it made the war's expansion inevitable.
