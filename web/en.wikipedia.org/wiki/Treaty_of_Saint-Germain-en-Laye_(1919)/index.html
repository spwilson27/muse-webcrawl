Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye (1919)

The Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye was the peace treaty signed on 10 September 1919 by the victorious Allies of World War I on the one hand and by the Republic of German-Austria on the other, at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, France.

Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye
Signed10 September 1919
LocationSaint-Germain-en-Laye, France
Effective16 July 1920
PartiesAllied Powers; Republic of German-Austria

Terms

The treaty confirmed the dissolution of Austria-Hungary, recognised the independence of Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Poland and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia), and prohibited union (Anschluss) between Austria and Germany without League of Nations consent. Austria lost South Tyrol and Trentino to Italy, parts of Carinthia and Styria, and Burgenland was largely transferred from Hungary. Its army was limited to 30,000 men and reparations were imposed.

Background

Following the collapse of the Habsburg monarchy in October–November 1918, German-Austria claimed German-speaking territories but the Allies drew borders largely on ethnic and strategic grounds. See also Treaty of Versailles and Austria-Hungary.

Consequences

The rump Austrian state, with about 6.5 million people, faced severe economic difficulties. The ban on Anschluss remained a central grievance in interwar politics.
