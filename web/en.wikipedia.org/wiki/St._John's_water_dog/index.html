St. John's water dog

The St. John's water dog, also known as the St. John's dog, the lesser Newfoundland, or the little Newfoundland, is an extinct breed of domestic dog from Newfoundland, Canada. It was the ancestor of most modern retrievers — including the Labrador Retriever, the Chesapeake Bay Retriever, the Curly-Coated and Flat-Coated Retrievers, and the Golden Retriever — and also contributed to the larger Newfoundland dog.

History

The breed developed in Newfoundland from dogs brought by European fishermen and settlers from the 16th century onwards. Working alongside the fishing fleets, the dogs hauled nets, retrieved lines and fetched fish and birds that fell into the sea; their short, dense, oily coats and webbed feet made them superb cold-water retrievers.

From the early 1800s, St. John's water dogs were shipped to England, where they attracted the attention of sportsmen developing new gundog breeds. Dogs imported by the Earl of Malmesbury and others formed the foundation of the Labrador Retriever; others crossed into the ancestors of the flat-coated, curly-coated, Chesapeake Bay and golden retrievers. A St. John's dog named Nell, photographed in 1856, is often cited as one of the earliest named examples of the type.

Extinction

In its homeland the breed declined through a combination of heavy dog taxes — imposed in Newfoundland, partly to protect sheep farming — and the British rabies quarantine law of 1885, which largely ended exports. By the middle of the 20th century few pure dogs remained; the last two known males, kept by an elderly fisherman in a remote part of the island, died in the early 1980s, and photographs of them were published in dog-historical literature. The breed's genes survive abundantly in its retriever descendants.

Characteristics

Contemporary accounts describe a medium-sized, strong and athletic dog, usually solid black but occasionally liver or black-and-white, with a short smooth coat, an "otter" tail used as a rudder, and a notably kind, biddable temperament. Richard Wolters and others emphasised their legendary love of water and their working partnership with Newfoundland fishermen.
