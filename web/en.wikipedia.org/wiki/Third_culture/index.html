Third culture


The third culture formed between two cultures

A third culture is a hybrid cultural identity that emerges when individuals who have spent significant developmental years outside their parents' culture combine elements of their culture of origin (the first culture) and the culture(s) in which they have lived (the second culture/s) into a distinct, shared culture of experience. People who build this identity are known as third culture individuals, most commonly studied in the form of third culture kids (TCKs).

Origins of the concept

The term was coined in the 1950s by American sociologists John and Ruth Useem, who studied families of American expatriates in India. Ruth Hill Useem later defined a third culture kid as a person who has spent a significant part of his or her developmental years outside the parents' culture, integrating elements of both origin and host cultures into a third culture.

Characteristics

  • Shared experience over shared nationality — TCKs often identify most strongly with others who have had similar mobile upbringings, regardless of passport.
  • Multilingualism and adaptability — frequent relocation often produces cross-cultural competence and ease in new environments.
  • Rootlessness and restlessness — many report difficulty answering "Where are you from?" and experience unresolved grief at repeated goodbyes.
  • Expanded worldview — exposure to multiple cultural frames from childhood tends to produce a three-dimensional view of world events.

Related usage

The phrase "third culture" has also been used (following C. P. Snow's "two cultures") to describe the bridging of the sciences and the humanities by popularizers of science, notably in John Brockman's 1995 book The Third Culture. In organizational communication, a third culture is also said to be built between interlocutors from different cultures as they create shared meaning.
