Solfeggio

Solfeggio (Italian: [solˈfeddʒo]), also called solfège (French) or solfége, is a music education method used to teach aural skills, pitch and sight-reading. It assigns syllables to the notes of a scale — most familiarly do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti — so that melodies can be sung and internalised by reference to those syllables.

The Guidonian hand, a mnemonic for solmisation

History

The syllables derive from the 11th-century theorist Guido d'Arezzo, who took the initial syllables of successive lines of the Latin hymn Ut queant laxis — ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la — each beginning a scale step higher. Si (later ti in English-language movable systems, to avoid confusion) was added for the seventh degree, and do replaced the awkward ut in the 17th century. Guido also devised the hexachord system sung with these syllables ("solmisation"), taught with the Guidonian hand.

Systems

  • Fixed do: syllables are bound to absolute pitches (C is always do), standard in Italy, France and much of Latin Europe.
  • Movable do: do is the tonic of the prevailing key, with chromatic inflections (di, ra, etc.) — used with the Kodály method and tonic sol-fa traditions.

Use in training

Solfeggio exercises progress from stepwise diatonic melodies to interval drills, dictation and atonal sight-singing. In conservatoires, especially in Italy and France, solfeggio also denotes a demanding course of rhythmic and melodic dictation. The famous song "Do-Re-Mi" from The Sound of Music popularised the syllables for English-speaking audiences.
