Collateralized mortgage obligation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Cash flows from a pool of mortgages are divided into tranches with different risk and maturity profiles.

A collateralized mortgage obligation (CMO) is a type of complex debt security that repackages and directs the payments of principal and interest from a collateral pool of mortgages to different types and maturities of securities, thereby meeting investor needs.

Litigated during the financial crisis of 2007–2008, CMOs were central to the expansion of the market for securitized mortgage debt in the 1980s and 1990s. First issued in 1983 by the investment banks Salomon Brothers and First Boston for the federal mortgage agency Freddie Mac, CMOs were designed to overcome the problem that ordinary pass-through mortgage-backed securities exposed investors to prepayment risk and uncertain maturities.

Structure

A CMO divides the cash flows generated by a pool of underlying mortgages into separate classes of bonds, known as tranches, each with its own rules for receiving principal and interest. In the simplest sequential-pay structure, all principal payments go first to the earliest tranche until it is retired, then to the next, and so on. More elaborate structures include planned amortization class (PAC) bonds, which offer scheduled payments protected by companion or support tranches that absorb variability, floating-rate tranches, and interest-only and principal-only strips.

Because the tranches differ in maturity, yield, and exposure to prepayment risk, a single mortgage pool can be marketed to investors with very different investment horizons and risk appetites, such as pension funds, banks, and insurance companies.

History

The first CMO was issued in June 1983. The market grew rapidly, and CMO technology was extended to other asset classes, producing collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) backed by corporate loans, credit card receivables, and other debt. During the 2000s housing boom, CMOs and CDOs backed by subprime and Alt-A mortgages proliferated; widespread defaults on the underlying loans and downgrades of the securities contributed significantly to the financial crisis of 2007–2008.

Risks

The principal risks of CMOs include prepayment risk (homeowners refinancing or paying off mortgages early when interest rates fall), extension risk (payments slowing when rates rise), credit risk on the underlying mortgages, and the complexity risk inherent in valuing highly structured cash flows.
