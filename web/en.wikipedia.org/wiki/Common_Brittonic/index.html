Common Brittonic

Common Brittonic
RegionGreat Britain (south of the Firth of Forth)

FamilyIndo-European → Celtic → Insular Celtic → Brittonic

Common Brittonic (also British, Common Brythonic, or *Brittonikā) is the name for the Celtic language spoken in Great Britain during the Iron Age and Roman era, and into the early medieval period before it diverged into the separate Brittonic languages: Old Welsh, Old Cornish, Old Breton, and Cumbric. It was spoken across the island south of the Firth of Forth, and in Brittany by emigrants from Britain.

History

Common Brittonic developed from Proto-Celtic via Insular Celtic, distinguishing itself from Goidelic (the ancestor of Irish and Scottish Gaelic) by sound changes such as the development of Proto-Celtic *kʷ into p — hence the classification of Brittonic as a "P-Celtic" language. During four centuries of Roman rule, British Latin coexisted with Brittonic, which absorbed many Latin loanwords (for example Welsh pont "bridge" from Latin pons, ffenestr "window" from fenestra). In the 5th and 6th centuries AD, sweeping phonological changes — loss of final syllables, vowel affection (i-affection), and accent shift — transformed the language and produced the distinct Neo-Brittonic languages.

Evidence

No connected texts in Common Brittonic survive. The language is reconstructed from personal and place names recorded by Greek and Latin writers (such as Camulodunum and river names still recognisably Celtic), a handful of inscriptions, coin legends, loanwords in English and Latin, and the comparative method applied to its descendant languages.

Legacy

Brittonic survives robustly in Welsh, more precariously in revived Cornish, and in Breton in Brittany. Its place-names blanket Britain — Dover, York (via reshaping), Carlisle, and countless river names such as Thames, Avon, and Derwent preserve Brittonic roots, evidence of the language once spoken where English now predominates.
