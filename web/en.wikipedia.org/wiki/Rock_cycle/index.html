The rock cycle is a basic concept in geology that describes the transitions through geologic time among the three main rock types: sedimentary, metamorphic, and igneous. Each type can be transformed into the others by processes such as melting, cooling, erosion, compaction and deformation, driven by Earth's internal heat and by surface forces powered by the Sun. The cycle has no beginning or end; rocks continually change form over millions of years.

History

The concept grew out of the work of James Hutton (1726–1797), often called the father of modern geology, who recognised that rock strata had been uplifted, eroded and re-formed over immense spans of time. The idea was developed further within the uniformitarian framework popularised by Charles Lyell and continues to be a standard teaching model in earth science.

The transitions

Igneous rocks

When magma (underground) or lava (at the surface) cools and solidifies, it forms igneous rock such as granite or basalt. Igneous rock exposed at the surface is broken down by weathering and erosion.

Sedimentary rocks

Weathered fragments are transported by water, wind, ice or gravity and deposited as sediment. Burial compacts the layers, and minerals precipitate to cement them — lithification — producing sedimentary rocks such as sandstone, shale and limestone.

Metamorphic rocks

Any rock subjected to great heat and pressure deep in the crust is transformed, without fully melting, into metamorphic rock: limestone becomes marble, shale becomes slate and schist, sandstone becomes quartzite.

Melting and the loop

If temperatures rise enough, rocks melt back into magma, completing the loop. Plate tectonics drives much of the cycle, subducting crust into the mantle and generating new volcanic rock at ridges and arcs. Any rock type can also bypass stages — for example, an igneous rock may be metamorphosed directly, or a metamorphic rock may weather straight into sediment.
