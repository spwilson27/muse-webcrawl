Abraham Harold Maslow (April 1, 1908 – June 8, 1970) was an American psychologist best known for creating Maslow's hierarchy of needs, a theory of psychological health predicated on fulfilling innate human needs in priority, culminating in self-actualization. A founder of humanistic psychology — the "third force" alongside behaviourism and psychoanalysis — he emphasised the positive qualities and potential of human beings rather than pathology.

Biography

Maslow was born in Brooklyn, New York, the eldest of seven children of Russian Jewish immigrants. He studied psychology at the University of Wisconsin, earning his PhD in 1934 under Harry Harlow, with experimental work on dominance in primates. He taught at Brooklyn College (1937–1951), where he encountered anthropologist Ruth Benedict and Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer, whose characters inspired his interest in self-actualising people. He later chaired the psychology department at Brandeis University (1951–1969). Maslow died of a heart attack in 1970.

Hierarchy of needs

In his 1943 paper "A Theory of Human Motivation" and the book Motivation and Personality (1954), Maslow proposed that human needs form a hierarchy, conventionally drawn as a pyramid: physiological needs, safety, love and belonging, and esteem (the "deficiency needs") must be substantially met before the "growth need" of self-actualization — realising one's full potential — can dominate behaviour. He later added cognitive, aesthetic and transcendence needs. The hierarchy has been enormously influential in management, education and marketing, though empirical research has found limited support for its strict ordering.

Self-actualization and peak experiences

Maslow studied psychologically healthy individuals — including historical figures such as Abraham Lincoln and Albert Einstein — whom he judged self-actualising, and described their transient moments of fulfilment as "peak experiences". He also coined, with others, the concepts explored in Toward a Psychology of Being (1962), and is associated with the remark that "if the only tool you have is a hammer, it is tempting to treat everything as if it were a nail".
