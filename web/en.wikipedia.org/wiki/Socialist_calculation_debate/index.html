Socialist calculation debate

The socialist calculation debate, also known as the economic calculation debate, was a discourse in economics in the 1920s and 1930s concerning how a socialist economy would perform economic calculation and allocate resources. The debate was initiated by Ludwig von Mises, who argued that without market prices for the means of production, rational economic calculation was impossible under socialism. Market socialists such as Oskar Lange responded with models in which a central planning board could simulate markets by trial and error.

The debate centred on whether planners could replicate the information conveyed by prices

Mises's argument

In his 1920 article "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" and his 1922 book Socialism, Mises argued that rational allocation of capital goods requires money prices formed by exchange of privately owned means of production. Where the state owns all productive resources, there is no market for them, hence no prices, and planners cannot compare the costs of alternative production plans. Calculation in kind, he argued, could not substitute for calculation in a common unit.

The knowledge problem

Friedrich Hayek developed the critique further in works such as "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945). Hayek argued that the knowledge needed for economic coordination is dispersed, tacit, and constantly changing, and that the price system aggregates this knowledge in a way no central authority could replicate. He characterised the debate as concerning not mere computation but the mobilisation of knowledge that is not given to anyone in totality.

Market socialist responses

Oskar Lange, in "On the Economic Theory of Socialism" (1936–37), proposed that a Central Planning Board could set prices parametrically and adjust them in response to shortages and surpluses, mimicking a Walrasian auctioneer, while plant managers followed rules to minimise cost and produce where price equalled marginal cost. Abba Lerner and Fred M. Taylor made related proposals. Lange conceded that Mises had performed a service by forcing socialists to address the pricing problem.

Later assessments

Historians of economics disagree about who "won" the debate. Some hold that the collapse of the Soviet Union vindicated Mises and Hayek; others note that large firms internally plan resource allocation on a vast scale, and that modern computing has revived proposals for computerised planning. The debate remains a touchstone in discussions of market socialism, mechanism design, and the informational role of prices.
