Deconstruction

Deconstruction influenced architecture, notably the deconstructivist style.

Deconstruction is a mode of critical analysis of texts and concepts, developed by the French philosopher Jacques Derrida from the 1960s, which examines the relationship between text and meaning by exposing the assumptions, hierarchies and internal tensions within a work. Deconstructive reading shows how a text's explicit claims may be undermined by its own rhetoric, and how meaning depends on differences between terms rather than on the full presence of any single one — Derrida's notion of différance.

Origins

Derrida introduced the term in works published in 1967, including Of Grammatology, borrowing and transforming Heidegger's idea of Destruktion and drawing on Saussure's structural linguistics. Against "logocentrism" — the privileging of speech and presence in Western metaphysics — Derrida analysed binary oppositions (speech/writing, nature/culture, presence/absence), showing that the supposedly subordinate term is indispensable to the dominant one. Later works extended deconstruction to ethics, law, hospitality and politics; Derrida insisted deconstruction was not a method, nor simple destruction, but an attentive mode of reading — "deconstruction is justice," he wrote in Force of Law.

Influence

Deconstruction profoundly influenced literary studies (notably the Yale School around Paul de Man), legal theory (Critical Legal Studies), theology and cultural studies. In the arts, its ideas informed deconstructivist architecture — a cousin of postmodern architecture — with fragmented forms by architects such as Peter Eisenman (who collaborated with Derrida), Zaha Hadid, Frank Gehry, Rem Koolhaas and Daniel Libeskind, showcased in a 1988 Museum of Modern Art exhibition.

Criticism

Deconstruction has been attacked as relativistic, obscurantist and nihilistic by philosophers including Jürgen Habermas and John Searle — whose 1977 exchange with Derrida became famous — and it featured prominently in the "science wars" of the 1990s, including the Sokal affair. Defenders reply that deconstruction demands unusually close, rigorous reading, and that its point is not that texts mean anything at all, but that their meaning is never fully closed or self-present.
