Acts of Andrew

The Acts of Andrew (Acta Andreae), is the earliest testimony of the acts and miracles of the Apostle Andrew. The surviving version is alluded to in a 3rd-century work, the Coptic Psalms of Mani, which provides a terminus ante quem for its composition. The Acts, along with the Acts of John, the Acts of Peter, the Acts of Paul and the Acts of Thomas, made up one of the major collections of apocryphal New Testament writings collectively known as the Leucian Acts.

Content

The text narrates Andrew's missionary journeys through Greece and Asia Minor, his miracles, healings and exorcisms, his imprisonment, and his martyrdom by crucifixion at Patras in Achaea. A prominent episode concerns Maximilla, the wife of the proconsul Aegeates, who converts to Christianity and to a life of chastity, provoking her husband's persecution of the apostle. The long farewell discourse Andrew delivers from the cross — addressing the cross itself in poetic terms — is among the most celebrated passages of the apocryphal acts.

Transmission and reception

The original text is largely lost; it survives in epitomes, translations and derivative works, including Gregory of Tours' 6th-century Latin Book of the Miracles of the Apostle Andrew, which summarises much of it while removing passages its author found objectionable. The Acts display strongly encratite tendencies — praising abstinence from marriage — which contributed to their exclusion from the orthodox canon, although they were read in Manichaean and Priscillianist circles. Modern scholars reconstruct the Greek original from Greek fragments, Coptic, Armenian and Latin witnesses.
