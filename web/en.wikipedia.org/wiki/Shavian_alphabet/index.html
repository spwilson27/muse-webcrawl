Shavian alphabet

The Shavian alphabet (also called the Shaw alphabet) is an alphabet designed as a replacement for the traditional spelling of English, with one letter for each of the language's phonemes. It was created by Ronald Kingsley Read in response to a competition funded by the will of the playwright George Bernard Shaw, a long-standing critic of English orthography.

Design

Shaw's will specified that the new alphabet must have at least 40 letters, be entirely distinct from the Latin alphabet, and represent each sound with a single symbol. Read's winning design contains 48 letters, organised in pairs by height: "tall" letters for voiceless sounds, "deep" letters for their voiced counterparts, and short letters for vowels, liquids and nasals. Letters are formed from a small set of strokes so that the alphabet can be written quickly without lifting the pen more than necessary.

History and use

To demonstrate the system, Shaw's play Androcles and the Lion was published in 1962 in a parallel-text edition in Shavian and conventional spelling. Read went on to develop the streamlined Quikscript alphabet from the same ideas. Shavian gained a Unicode block in 2003 (U+10450–U+1047F), and small communities have published translations and original texts in it, but it has never approached official adoption, remaining a curiosity of spelling-reform history.
