Community Charge

The Community Charge, commonly known as the poll tax, was a system of local taxation introduced by the government of Margaret Thatcher in the United Kingdom, replacing domestic rates. It was a fixed tax per adult resident, levied by each local authority, regardless of income or the value of the taxpayer's property. The tax was introduced in Scotland in 1989 and in England and Wales in 1990, and was abolished in 1993, replaced by the Council Tax.

Background

Domestic rates, based on the notional rental value of homes, had long been unpopular, and their revaluation in Scotland in 1985 caused sharp increases for some households. The government's 1986 Green Paper Paying for Local Government proposed replacing rates with a flat-rate charge so that every adult would contribute to the cost of local services, with the stated aim of making local councils more accountable to all their voters. Rebates were available for those on low incomes, but nearly everyone was required to pay at least 20 percent of the charge.

Opposition and riots

The charge was intensely unpopular: critics objected that a duke and a dustman would pay the same amount locally, and mass non-payment campaigns were organized under the slogan "Can't Pay, Won't Pay". On 31 March 1990, a large anti-poll tax demonstration in London escalated into the Poll Tax Riots in Trafalgar Square and the West End. Millions refused or fell behind in payment, overwhelming the courts and local authorities. The tax is widely considered a major factor in Thatcher's fall from power in November 1990.

Abolition and legacy

Thatcher's successor, John Major, announced the tax's abolition; the Council Tax, a banded property tax with a personal element, replaced it from 1993. The Community Charge episode remains a standard case study of a politically toxic tax, and the term "poll tax" is still invoked in British politics against flat per-head charges.
