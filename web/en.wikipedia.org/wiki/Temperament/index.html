Temperament

In psychology, temperament broadly refers to consistent individual differences in behaviour that are biologically based and are relatively independent of learning, system of values and attitudes. Temperament describes how a person behaves — the style and rhythm of behaviour — rather than what they do or why; it is distinguished from personality, which develops through the interaction of temperament with experience.

Historical models

The oldest temperament theory derives from the ancient Greek doctrine of the four humours, systematized by Hippocrates and Galen, which associated character types with bodily fluids: the sanguine (blood), choleric (yellow bile), melancholic (black bile) and phlegmatic (phlegm) temperaments. The scheme survived, in various forms, into the nineteenth century and influenced later typologies by Ernst Kretschmer and William Sheldon.

Modern research

Modern temperament research, pioneered by Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess in the New York Longitudinal Study, identified nine dimensions of infant temperament — including activity level, rhythmicity, adaptability, intensity of reaction and quality of mood — and grouped children into "easy", "difficult" and "slow-to-warm-up" categories. Mary Rothbart's model instead emphasizes reactivity and self-regulation, including the dimension of effortful control. Jerome Kagan's work linked behavioural inhibition in toddlers to later shyness and anxiety.

Temperament and personality

Temperament traits are observable from infancy, show moderate stability across the lifespan and have a genetic component demonstrated by twin studies. Adult personality models such as the Big Five are thought to build partly on temperamental foundations, but personality also encompasses goals, self-concept and learned habits that temperament alone does not explain.
