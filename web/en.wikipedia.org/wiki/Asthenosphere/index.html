Asthenosphere

The asthenosphere (from Greek asthenḗs, "weak") is the mechanically weak and ductile region of the upper mantle of Earth. It lies below the lithosphere, at depths between approximately 80 and 200 km (50 and 120 miles) below the surface, and may extend as deep as 700 km. The rigid lithospheric plates of plate tectonics rest and move upon it.

Properties

The asthenosphere consists of hot mantle rock close to its melting point. Seismic waves pass through it relatively slowly, so it is also characterised as a low-velocity zone. Although solid, the rock can flow plastically over millions of years — rather like very viscous pitch — allowing convection that helps drive plate motion. Partial melting of asthenospheric material rising at mid-ocean ridges produces the basaltic magma of new oceanic crust.

History of the concept

The term was coined in 1914 by the British geologist Joseph Barrell, who proposed a weak layer beneath the rigid crust to explain isostatic compensation. Its existence was confirmed by seismology in the mid-20th century, and it became a key element of the plate tectonic theory, explaining how rigid plates can move over the mantle and how the surface rebounds after the removal of loads such as ice sheets (glacial isostatic adjustment).
