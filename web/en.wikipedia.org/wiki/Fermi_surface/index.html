Fermi surface

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In condensed matter physics, the Fermi surface is the surface in reciprocal (momentum) space which separates occupied from unoccupied electron states at zero temperature. Within the Fermi–Dirac statistics of a metal, electrons fill the lowest available states up to the Fermi energy; the boundary of this filled region in k-space is the Fermi surface. Its shape is determined by the crystal's electronic band structure and governs most of a metal's electrical, thermal, magnetic, and optical properties, because only electrons near the Fermi surface can respond to external fields.

Shape and examples

For a hypothetical free-electron metal the Fermi surface is a sphere of radius kF = (3π²n)1/3, where n is the electron density. The alkali metals (sodium, potassium) approximate this closely. In real crystals, the periodic lattice potential distorts the sphere: in noble metals such as copper the sphere bulges out and touches the Brillouin zone boundaries, forming "necks." Multivalent and transition metals have complicated Fermi surfaces with several sheets — "hole pockets," "electron pockets," and open orbits running across the zone. In some quasi-one-dimensional and quasi-two-dimensional materials the surface consists of nearly flat sheets or cylinders, enabling nesting instabilities that drive charge-density-wave transitions.

Measurement

Fermi surfaces are mapped experimentally by several techniques:

  • De Haas–van Alphen effect — oscillations of magnetization as a magnetic field varies reveal the extremal cross-sectional areas of the Fermi surface perpendicular to the field. The related Shubnikov–de Haas effect appears in resistivity.
  • Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) — directly images the occupied band structure and Fermi contour, and is the principal tool for studying high-temperature superconductors.
  • Positron annihilation and Compton scattering — probe the electron momentum distribution in bulk samples.

Measurements are compared with first-principles band-structure calculations (density functional theory), and agreement between the two is a stringent test of electronic-structure theory.

Significance

Because transport and thermodynamic properties depend on the density of states and electron velocities at the Fermi surface, knowing its geometry explains why chemically similar metals can differ dramatically in conductivity, thermopower, and magnetism. Reconstruction of the Fermi surface signals phase transitions: in cuprate superconductors and heavy-fermion compounds, changes from large to small Fermi surfaces (counting with or without localized f-electrons) diagnose quantum critical points. The Luttinger theorem guarantees that the volume enclosed by the Fermi surface counts the total number of electrons, even in strongly interacting Fermi liquids.
