A regiment is a military unit. Its role and size vary markedly depending on the country, service and era. In many armies a regiment is composed of several battalions and is commanded by a colonel; in others — notably the British Army and armies modeled on it — the regiment is primarily an administrative and ceremonial organization that provides the identity, traditions and recruiting basis for one or more battalions, which are the actual tactical units.

Historically, regiments were raised and often personally maintained by their colonels, and were frequently known by the colonel's name before numbered and territorial titles became standard. The regimental system remains an important source of esprit de corps: soldiers often identify strongly with their regiment's battle honours, uniforms, marches and traditions.

History

The term derives from the Latin regimentum, a rule or system of government, via Middle French. Regiments emerged as permanent military formations in the early modern period, when European states began maintaining standing armies. By the 17th century, the regiment of infantry or cavalry — typically several hundred to a few thousand men — had become a standard building block of armies, organized into companies and commanded by a colonel assisted by a lieutenant colonel and major.

During the Napoleonic Wars and the 19th century, regiments grew and were grouped into brigades and divisions. Reforms such as Britain's Cardwell and Childers reforms linked regiments to geographic recruiting areas and paired their battalions, laying the foundation of the modern British regimental system.

Regiments in different armies

  • British Army — the regiment is an administrative and ceremonial grouping; infantry regiments provide battalions to brigades, and many were amalgamated in the 20th and 21st centuries.
  • United States Army — regiments historically fielded as tactical units; since the mid-20th century the battalion has been the key tactical unit, but regimental affiliations and designations are retained under systems such as the U.S. Army Regimental System.
  • Continental European armies — the regiment (Russian polk, German Regiment) typically remains a tactical formation of battalions under a brigade or division.

Organization

A typical tactical regiment consists of a headquarters, two to five battalions or equivalent sub-units, and supporting elements such as reconnaissance, engineers, signals and logistics. Cavalry and armored units in some armies use "regiment" for a battalion-sized formation. Artillery, engineer and signals units also commonly organize as regiments even where infantry practice differs.
