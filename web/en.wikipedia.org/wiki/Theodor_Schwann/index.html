Theodor Schwann

Born: 7 December 1810, Neuss, French Empire

Died: 11 January 1882, Cologne, German Empire

Field: Physiology, histology

Known for: Cell theory; Schwann cells; discovery of pepsin

Theodor Schwann (7 December 1810 – 11 January 1882) was a German physician and physiologist. His most significant contribution to biology is the extension of cell theory to animals, complementing the work of Matthias Schleiden on plants. He also discovered the enzyme pepsin, identified the role of yeast in alcoholic fermentation, and coined the term "metabolism".

Cell theory

Working in Berlin under Johannes Peter Müller, Schwann examined animal tissues microscopically and recognised that they share the cellular structure Schleiden had described in plants. In his Mikroskopische Untersuchungen (1839) he formulated the first two tenets of cell theory: that the cell is the basic unit of structure in both plants and animals, and that each cell has an individual life while contributing to the life of the whole organism. He classified tissues into five categories according to their cellular structure.

Other work

In 1836 Schwann isolated the digestive enzyme pepsin from the stomach lining. The following year he showed that fermentation depends on living yeast, and that putrefaction is caused by microorganisms in the air — experiments later built upon by Pasteur. His name is commemorated in the Schwann cells that form the myelin sheath around peripheral nerve fibres, which he described. From 1839 he held chairs at the Catholic University of Leuven and then at Liège, where he spent the remainder of his career.
