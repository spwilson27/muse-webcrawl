Grayanotoxin

Grayanotoxin I
ClassDiterpenoid neurotoxin
SourcesRhododendron, Pieris, Kalmia nectar and honey
MechanismBinds voltage-gated sodium channels, prevents inactivation

Grayanotoxins are a group of closely related neurotoxins named after Leucothoe grayana, a plant native to Japan from which the toxin was first isolated. They are found in species of Rhododendron and other plants in the family Ericaceae. They can be found in honey made from the nectar of these plants, and can cause a very rare condition known as grayanotoxin poisoning, honey intoxication, or "mad honey disease".

Mechanism

Grayanotoxins bind to voltage-gated sodium channels in cell membranes, preventing their inactivation. This keeps excitable cells in a state of depolarization, during which calcium entry into cells is increased. The resulting effects include stimulation of the vagus nerve, leading to slowed heart rate and low blood pressure.

Poisoning

Symptoms of grayanotoxin poisoning include excessive salivation, perspiration, vomiting, dizziness, weakness, low blood pressure and bradycardia. Cases are most often reported in Turkey and Korea, where honey from rhododendron-rich regions is produced. Symptoms typically resolve within 24 hours without treatment.

History

The intoxicating properties of mad honey were known in antiquity: Xenophon described the poisoning of Greek soldiers in Anatolia around 401 BC after they ate honey from rhododendron nectar, and mad honey was reportedly used as a weapon against Roman troops under Pompey in 67 BC.
