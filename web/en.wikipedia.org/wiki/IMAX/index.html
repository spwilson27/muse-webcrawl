IMAX (an acronym for Image MAXimum) is a proprietary system of high-resolution cameras, film formats, film projectors, and theatres known for having very large screens with a tall aspect ratio (approximately 1.43:1 or 1.90:1) and steep stadium seating. The format was developed in Canada by Graeme Ferguson, Roman Kroitor, Robert Kerr, and William Shaw, and the first IMAX film, Tiger Child, was shown at Expo 70 in Osaka, Japan.

Technical aspects

Traditional IMAX film uses 70 mm film run horizontally through the projector, with each frame 15 perforations long — a film area roughly ten times that of standard 35 mm film. This "15/70" format allows images of exceptional resolution and steadiness. IMAX cameras are correspondingly large and heavy, and the film stock moves at about 102.7 metres per minute during projection.

Digital IMAX, introduced in 2008, uses dual projectors and smaller screens in retrofitted multiplex auditoriums, while newer IMAX with Laser systems use dual 4K laser projectors capable of filling the full 1.43:1 giant screen.

History

The system grew out of multi-screen experiments at Expo 67 in Montreal. The first permanent IMAX theatre opened in 1971 at Ontario Place in Toronto. Originally used mainly for museum and science centre documentaries, IMAX expanded into feature films in the 2000s with the DMR (Digital Media Remastering) process, which converts conventional films to the IMAX format. Directors such as Christopher Nolan have shot portions of features — including The Dark Knight (2008) and Dunkirk (2017) — with IMAX 15/70 cameras.

Variants

  • IMAX Dome (originally OMNIMAX) projects onto a domed planetarium-style screen.
  • IMAX 3D uses two lenses to record and project left- and right-eye images.
  • IMAX GT (Grand Theatre) installations feature the largest screens, some over 20 metres tall.
