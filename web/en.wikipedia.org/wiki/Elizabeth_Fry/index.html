Elizabeth Fry (née Gurney; 21 May 1780 – 12 October 1845), sometimes referred to as Betsy Fry, was an English prison reformer, social reformer and, as a Quaker, a Christian philanthropist. She has been called the "angel of prisons". Fry was a major driving force behind new legislation to improve the treatment of prisoners, especially female prisoners, in the early 19th century.

Early life

Fry was born in Norwich into the wealthy Quaker Gurney family, bankers and philanthropists. In 1800 she married Joseph Fry, a London tea merchant and banker. Recorded as a Quaker minister in 1811, she began visiting prisons after hearing reports of the conditions endured by women and their children.

Prison reform

In 1813 Fry first visited Newgate Prison in London, where she found hundreds of women and their children crowded into filthy cells without bedding or adequate food. She began reading to the women and organised a school for their children. In 1817 she helped found the Association for the Improvement of the Female Prisoners in Newgate, and that year she gave evidence to a select committee of the House of Commons — the first woman to present evidence to Parliament — on the conditions of prisoners.

Fry toured prisons across Britain and Ireland, promoted education and rehabilitation, campaigned against capital punishment and for the humane treatment of transported convicts, and later extended her work to continental Europe, visiting prisons in France and Germany. Her famous intervention in 1813 is depicted in the painting Elizabeth Fry Reading to the Prisoners in Newgate.

Legacy

Fry's image appeared on the Bank of England £5 note between 2001 and 2016, shown reading to prisoners at Newgate. Numerous schools, charities and the Elizabeth Fry Society (Canada) bear her name, and she remains a symbol of humanitarian prison reform.
