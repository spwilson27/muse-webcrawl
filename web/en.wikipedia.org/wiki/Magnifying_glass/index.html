Magnifying glass

A magnifying glass is a convex lens used to produce a magnified image of an object. The lens is usually mounted in a frame with a handle. It works by creating an enlarged virtual image when the object is placed within the focal length of the lens, and is one of the oldest and simplest optical instruments.

History

Evidence of lens-like magnifiers dates to antiquity: "burning glasses" and polished crystal spheres are mentioned by Aristophanes and Seneca, and the Nimrud lens from Assyria dates to around 700 BC. In the 11th century, Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) described magnification by segments of glass spheres in his Book of Optics. Roger Bacon described the properties of a magnifying lens in 13th-century England, and reading stones — glass hemispheres laid on text — were used by monks and scholars before spectacles appeared around 1280 in Italy.

Optics

The magnification of a simple magnifier is approximately the ratio of the near-point distance of the eye (conventionally 25 cm) to the focal length of the lens. Typical hand magnifiers range from 2× to 6×; higher powers suffer from a small field of view and aberrations, for which compound designs such as the Coddington and Hastings triplet magnifiers were devised.

Uses

Magnifying glasses are used for reading fine print, examining stamps, coins, insects and circuit boards, igniting tinder by focusing sunlight, and as an iconic symbol of detective work (popularized by Sherlock Holmes) and of search and inspection in software interfaces.
