Prosecutor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A prosecutor is a legal representative of the prosecution in states with the common law adversarial system or the civil law inquisitorial system. Prosecutors are responsible for presenting the case against an individual accused of breaking the law, on behalf of the state or the people. They typically begin by deciding whether to file charges, based on the evidence gathered by police, and their office is responsible for the case from arrest through trial and appeals.

Organisation

Models differ widely: in the United States most local chief prosecutors (district attorneys) are elected, federal cases are handled by appointed US Attorneys, and special counsels are used for sensitive matters; England and Wales created the Crown Prosecution Service in 1985 to separate prosecution from policing; many European systems employ career prosecutors within a hierarchy under the ministry of justice (France's parquet), raising recurring independence questions; Japan's prosecutors famously achieve very high conviction rates, debated as both rigour and risk. International courts have their own prosecutors (the ICC's Prosecutor among the most scrutinised).

Ethics and debate

Prosecutors wield extraordinary discretion — charging, plea bargaining, sentencing recommendations — with limited review, making the office a focus of reform debates on wrongful convictions (including the duty to disclose exculpatory evidence), racial and socio-economic disparity, and mass incarceration. The professional ideal, in the classic formulation, is that the prosecutor's interest is not to win a case but to see that justice is done.
