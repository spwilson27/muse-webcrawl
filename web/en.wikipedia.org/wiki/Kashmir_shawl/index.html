The Kashmir shawl is a type of shawl woven in Kashmir from the fine downy undercoat of the changthangi (pashmina) goat. Finely patterned Kashmir shawls were luxury goods for centuries, prized at the courts of Mughal India and, from the late 18th century, in Europe, where they gave rise to the paisley motif in Western fashion.

Production

The raw fibre, called pashm, comes from goats grazing the high-altitude Changthang plateau. After cleaning and spinning — traditionally women's work — the yarn is woven by male weavers in Srinagar. Two techniques predominate: kani weaving, a twill tapestry method using small wooden bobbins (kanis) that allows intricate multicoloured patterns but may take months or years for a single shawl; and amlikar, in which patterns are embroidered in fine sozni needlework on a plainer woven ground. The finest shawls were said to pass through a ring — hence "ring shawls". The very finest historic shawls used shahtoosh, the down of the chiru antelope, now banned under wildlife-protection law.

History and influence

Shawl weaving in Kashmir was promoted under Sultan Zain-ul-Abidin in the 15th century and flourished under the Mughals; Akbar is said to have popularised wearing them doubled. Napoleonic-era campaigns in Egypt brought Kashmir shawls to France, where Empress Joséphine made them fashionable; the resulting European demand was met both by imports and by imitations woven in Paisley, Scotland — which lent its name to the buta motif. The Franco-Prussian War and changing fashions collapsed the export trade in the 1870s, devastating the industry.
