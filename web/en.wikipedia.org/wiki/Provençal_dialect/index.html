Provençal (Occitan: Prouvençau in Mistralian orthography, or Provençau in classical orthography) is a variety of Occitan, a Romance language spoken in southern France. It is spoken in the region of Provence, roughly east of the Rhône, as well as in parts of the southern Drôme and Gard. In a broader, older usage, "Provençal" was often used for the whole of the Occitan language, the literary language of the medieval troubadours.

Characteristics

Provençal is distinguished from neighbouring Occitan dialects such as Languedocien by features including the vocalisation of final and preconsonantal /l/ to [w], giving for example bal pronounced "baou", and by characteristic vowel developments. Two competing orthographies are used: the Mistralian norm, devised in the 19th century on French-based spelling conventions, and the classical norm, which follows medieval usage and is shared with the other Occitan dialects.

History

In the Middle Ages the literary koine of the troubadours was based largely on dialects of the south, and Provençal enjoyed great prestige across southern Europe. After the incorporation of Provence into the French crown (1481) and the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539), French progressively displaced it from administration and literature. A literary revival came in the 19th century with the Félibrige movement founded by Frédéric Mistral, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1904 and wrote his epic Mirèio in Provençal.

Current status

Like all regional languages of France, Provençal has no official status and the number of native speakers has declined sharply; UNESCO classifies it as definitely endangered. It is taught optionally in some schools in the Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur region, and cultural associations continue to promote it.
