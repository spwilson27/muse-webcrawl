Pueblo pottery is the ceramic tradition of the Pueblo peoples of the American Southwest — including the Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, Laguna, San Ildefonso, Santa Clara, and the Rio Grande pueblos of present-day New Mexico and Arizona. It descends from the pottery of the Ancestral Puebloans and remains a living art, made by hand without the potter's wheel.

History

Plain pottery appeared in the Southwest by about 500 AD, and painted black-on-white wares flourished among the Ancestral Pueblo (Anasazi) cultures of Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde. After the Spanish colonisation of the 17th century, pottery production contracted but survived in domestic use. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, railroad tourism and collectors created a market that revived and transformed the craft, and individual potters began to be recognised as artists.

Making

Traditional Pueblo pottery is formed by coiling ropes of locally dug clay, pinching and scraping the walls smooth with gourd tools, and slipping the surface with fine clay. Designs are painted with mineral and vegetal pigments using yucca-leaf brushes, and the ware is fired outdoors in open fires or kilns fuelled by wood or dung, which can produce characteristic "fire clouds." Styles are distinct to each pueblo: San Ildefonso is famed for black-on-black ware pioneered by Maria Martinez and her husband Julian; Acoma for thin-walled white ware with fine-line geometric painting; Santa Clara for deeply carved blackware; and Hopi-Tewa potter Nampeyo revived ancient Sikyátki designs in her polychrome ware.

Notable potters

Maria Martinez (1887–1980) of San Ildefonso became the most famous Pueblo potter, demonstrating at world's fairs and receiving national honours. Nampeyo (c. 1859–1942), the Martinez family, Lucy Lewis of Acoma, and generations of their descendants shaped the modern market and museum collections worldwide.

Cultural significance

Pottery remains central to Pueblo identity, economy, and ceremony. Certain forms and designs are restricted or sacred, and contemporary potters balance market production with community obligations and the transmission of techniques within families.
