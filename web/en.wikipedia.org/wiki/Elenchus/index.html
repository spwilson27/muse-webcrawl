Elenchus

In philosophy, the elenchus (Ancient Greek: ἔλεγχος, élenkhos, meaning "argument of disproof or refutation; cross-examining, testing, scrutiny") is the central technique of the Socratic method. An elenchus typically proceeds as follows: Socrates asks an interlocutor for a definition of some virtue or concept (for example, piety or courage); through a series of questions he secures agreement to further premises; and he then shows that those premises, together with the original definition, lead to a contradiction—thereby refuting the definition and leaving the interlocutor in a state of aporia, or perplexity.

Structure

The classic pattern, seen throughout Plato's early dialogues such as the Euthyphro, Laches, and Charmides, has three stages: the interlocutor states a thesis; Socrates elicits additional commitments through questions the interlocutor answers sincerely; and the conjunction of these commitments is shown to be inconsistent. A rule of the procedure is that the interlocutor must say what he actually believes, so that the refutation tests his own web of beliefs rather than a straw position.

Interpretation

Scholars debate the purpose of the elenchus. On a "constructivist" reading, associated with Gregory Vlastos, refuting false definitions clears the ground for true moral knowledge, and Socrates may legitimately infer that the negated thesis's opposite is established because the premises used are themselves true. On a purely "destructive" reading, the elenchus only exposes inconsistency and the pretence of knowledge—a result Socrates valued because recognizing one's ignorance is the precondition of philosophy. Vlastos famously posed the "problem of the elenchus": how can a method that only tests consistency show that any particular belief is false?
