Direct-to-consumer genetic testing

Direct-to-consumer (DTC) genetic testing allows consumers to access their genetic information — ancestry estimates, trait predictions and health-risk reports — without the involvement of a healthcare provider. Customers order a kit online, provide a saliva sample by post and receive results through a web portal. The industry grew rapidly after 2007, when companies such as 23andMe and deCODEme launched SNP-array-based tests; by the late 2010s tens of millions of people had been tested, mainly through 23andMe and AncestryDNA.

Services and uses

Products divide broadly into ancestry testing (ethnicity estimates and relative matching, which powers genetic genealogy) and health testing (carrier screening, disease risk such as late-onset Alzheimer's and Parkinson's risk variants, pharmacogenetic and wellness traits). Relative-matching databases have famously been used by genealogists and, controversially, by police: in 2018 investigators identified the Golden State Killer suspect by uploading crime-scene DNA to the open database GEDmatch, sparking a lasting debate over consent and genetic privacy.

Regulation and controversy

Regulators have wrestled with analytical validity, clinical validity and the risk of consumers misinterpreting results. In 2013 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration ordered 23andMe to stop marketing health reports until it obtained clearance; from 2015 the FDA authorised specific carrier tests and, in 2017–2018, genetic health-risk reports for conditions including Parkinson's disease and hereditary breast-cancer BRCA variants. Critics warn about false reassurance, limited variant panels, distress from unwelcome findings, insurer and law-enforcement access (U.S. genetic discrimination law, GINA, does not cover life insurance), and the sale or breach of customer data — a 2023 credential-stuffing breach at 23andMe exposed data of millions of users, and the company's 2025 bankruptcy raised further questions about the fate of its DNA database.
