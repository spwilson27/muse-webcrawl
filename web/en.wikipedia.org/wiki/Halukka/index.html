Halukka

The halukka (Hebrew: חלוקה, romanised ḥaluqah, "distribution") was an organised system for collecting and distributing charitable funds from Jewish communities in the diaspora to the Jewish community — the Old Yishuv — in Ottoman Palestine. From the 16th century until the early 20th century it provided the main livelihood of many Jews living in the four holy cities of Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias, most of whom were devoted to Torah study and prayer.

History

Systematic fund-raising for the Jews of the Land of Israel dates to the medieval period, but the halukka took its classic shape after the Ottoman conquest of Palestine in 1517 and the arrival of Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain. Communities abroad regarded supporting scholars and the poor in the Holy Land as a religious duty (mitzvah). Funds were raised locally and carried to Palestine by emissaries known as meshulachim (sing. meshulach), rabbis who travelled through the diaspora with letters of recommendation, collecting donations and often teaching and settling disputes along the way. From the 18th century, Ashkenazi funds were channelled through central committees such as the Council of Four Lands in Poland and the Pekidim and Amarkalim office in Amsterdam.

Organisation

Money arriving in Palestine was divided among the various kollels — associations organised by the recipients' country or community of origin — and then distributed to households, usually twice a year, in amounts calculated by family size and scholarly status. The system depended on a far-flung network of collectors and on the traditional charity box (pushke) placed in Jewish homes, including the blue boxes later adopted by the Zionist movement for a different purpose.

Decline

By the 19th century the halukka was attacked by maskilic (Jewish Enlightenment) and later Zionist critics, who saw it as perpetuating poverty and dependence rather than productive labour; the pioneers of the New Yishuv prided themselves on rejecting it. Philanthropists such as Moses Montefiore and the Rothschilds tried to redirect aid toward agriculture and crafts. The system was severely disrupted by the First World War, when remittances from enemy countries stopped, and it never regained its former importance, though halukka-style distribution to ultra-Orthodox study communities continues in modified form today.
