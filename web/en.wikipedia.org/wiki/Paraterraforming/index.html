Paraterraforming

Paraterraforming, also known as the "worldhouse" concept, is a proposed approach to making a planet or moon habitable by enclosing part or all of its surface under an artificial, pressurized shell rather than transforming the entire atmosphere and environment, as full terraforming would attempt. The concept was developed and named by the British engineer and writer Richard L. S. Taylor in the early 1990s.

Concept

A worldhouse would be a roofed-over world: a vast enclosed area — potentially growing over centuries to cover much of a small planet or moon — beneath which temperature, pressure, and atmosphere are controlled. The roof, supported by structures and the internal air pressure itself, would shield inhabitants from radiation and retain a breathable atmosphere while leaving the exterior environment largely unchanged.

Advantages

Advocates argue that paraterraforming avoids the greatest obstacles of planetary-scale terraforming. Bodies such as the Moon or Mercury lack sufficient gravity to hold a dense atmosphere over geological time, and thickening the atmosphere of Mars would require importing immense quantities of volatiles. A worldhouse, by contrast, confines atmosphere where it is needed, can start small and expand incrementally as settlement grows, and does not require destroying the native environment across the whole planet. The enclosed volume also receives the full benefit of terraforming-scale climate engineering at a fraction of the cost in materials.

Disadvantages

The concept still demands construction on a colossal scale, with questions about the strength of roofing materials, the consequences of a breach, and the maintenance of air, water, and nutrient cycles within the enclosure. A single-point failure or deliberate attack could be catastrophic for large populations. Critics also note that once a worldhouse encloses nearly an entire small body, it shades into terraforming itself, retaining most of its maintenance burdens.

In fiction

Covered or domed worlds appear frequently in science fiction, most famously in Kim Stanley Robinson's Mars trilogy, where tented canyons and roofed craters supplement planet-wide atmospheric change. The worldhouse idea is a staple of hard science fiction discussions of colonizing the Moon and asteroids.
