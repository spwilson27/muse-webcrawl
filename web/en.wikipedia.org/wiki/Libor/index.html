Libor (the London Inter-Bank Offered Rate) was a benchmark interest rate indicating the average rate at which major banks in London estimated they could borrow unsecured funds from one another in the interbank market. Published for several currencies and maturities, it became the world's most widely used reference rate, underpinning an estimated hundreds of trillions of dollars of loans, mortgages and derivatives.

Calculation

Each business day, a panel of banks submitted estimates of their borrowing costs; the rate was computed as a trimmed average of the submissions. Because submissions were judgements rather than records of actual transactions, the system depended on the honesty of the submitting banks.

Scandal and reform

From 2012, investigations revealed that traders at several banks had manipulated submissions for profit and, during the 2008 financial crisis, to disguise funding stress — the Libor scandal. Banks paid billions in fines and several individuals were prosecuted. Administration passed from the British Bankers' Association to ICE Benchmark Administration in 2014, but regulators concluded the underlying market was too thin to sustain the benchmark.

Discontinuation

Publication of most Libor settings ceased at the end of 2021, and the remaining US dollar settings ended on 30 June 2023. Replacement risk-free rates include SOFR (US dollar), SONIA (sterling), €STR (euro), TONA (yen) and SARON (Swiss franc), with synthetic Libor published temporarily to aid transition of legacy contracts.
