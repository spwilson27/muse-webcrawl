Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628

The Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628 was the last and most destructive of the long series of wars between the Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire and the Sasanian Empire of Persia. Lasting more than a quarter century, it saw Persia conquer the Levant, Egypt, and much of Anatolia before the emperor Heraclius's counteroffensives reversed the conquests — leaving both empires exhausted on the eve of the Arab Muslim conquests.

Origins

In 602 the Byzantine emperor Maurice was murdered by the usurper Phocas. The Sasanian shah Khosrow II, whom Maurice had restored to the Persian throne in 591, declared war to avenge his benefactor, invading Byzantine Mesopotamia.

Persian conquests

Persian armies under generals such as Shahrbaraz and Shahin overran Mesopotamia, Syria (Antioch fell in 611, Damascus in 613), and Palestine — Jerusalem fell in 614 and the True Cross was carried off to Ctesiphon. Egypt, the empire's granary, was conquered by 621. Persian forces reached Chalcedon, across the Bosporus from Constantinople, and in 626 the Persians, Avars, and Slavs besieged the capital itself — but the city's walls and the Byzantine navy held.

Heraclius's counterattack

Emperor Heraclius, who had overthrown Phocas in 610, rebuilt the army and from 622 led daring campaigns deep into Persian territory, striking through Armenia and Azerbaijan and destroying the great fire temple at Ganzak. In December 627 he crushed the Persian army at the Battle of Nineveh and advanced on Ctesiphon. Khosrow II was overthrown and executed in a palace coup in February 628, and his successor Kavadh II made peace.

Aftermath

The treaty restored the 591 borders and returned the captured provinces and the True Cross, which Heraclius restored to Jerusalem in 630. But the war had devastated both empires' finances, manpower, and frontier defenses — a crucial precondition for the rapid Arab conquests of the 630s and 640s, which destroyed Sasanian Persia entirely and stripped Byzantium of Syria and Egypt permanently.
