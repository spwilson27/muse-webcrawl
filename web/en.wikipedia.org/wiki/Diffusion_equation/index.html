The diffusion equation is a parabolic partial differential equation. In physics, it describes the macroscopic behavior of many micro-particles in Brownian motion, resulting from the random movements and collisions of the particles (see Fick's laws of diffusion). In mathematics, it is related to Markov processes, such as random walks, and applied in many other fields, such as materials science, information theory, and biophysics.

Statement

The equation is usually written as ∂φ(r,t)/∂t = ∇ · [ D(φ,r) ∇φ(r,t) ], where φ is the density of the diffusing material at location r and time t, and D is the (possibly position- and concentration-dependent) diffusion coefficient. When D is constant, the equation reduces to ∂φ/∂t = D∇²φ, the same form as the heat equation, with temperature in place of concentration. The fundamental solution for an instantaneous point source is a Gaussian whose variance grows as 2Dt per dimension — the signature of ordinary diffusion.

Derivation and applications

The equation follows from combining a continuity equation with Fick's first law, which states that flux is proportional to the negative concentration gradient. It models heat conduction, the spread of solutes and pollutants, neutron transport, semiconductor doping, population dispersal, and — via the Black–Scholes equation — option prices in finance. Einstein's 1905 analysis of Brownian motion provided its celebrated microscopic derivation, connecting the diffusion coefficient to molecular quantities.
