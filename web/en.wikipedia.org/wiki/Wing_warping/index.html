Wing warping

Wing warping was a system for lateral (roll) control of a fixed-wing aircraft developed by the Wright brothers between 1899 and 1905 for their early gliders and powered flyers. The technique, patented in 1906, consisted of twisting the wingtips of the aircraft in opposite directions by means of cables attached to the outer rear edges of the wings, thereby changing their angle of incidence and producing unequal lift on the two sides to roll the machine.

Wing warping on a Wright glider

History

Wilbur Wright conceived the idea in 1899 after observing birds in flight and twisting an inner-tube box in his bicycle shop, noticing that torsional twisting could alter the angle of a wing. The brothers tested the system on a five-foot biplane kite, then on their 1900, 1901, and 1902 gliders, where wing warping was combined with a movable rear rudder to solve the problem of adverse yaw. The system was central to their 1903 Wright Flyer, the first powered, controlled heavier-than-air aircraft.

Patent and litigation

The Wright brothers' 1906 patent described lateral control broadly, and the brothers sued numerous competitors who used ailerons, arguing that the patent covered any method of varying the angle of the wingtips. The resulting patent wars consumed much of their time and are often credited with slowing American aviation development until the patent pool of 1917.

Decline

As aircraft became faster and wings stiffer and more heavily loaded, twisting flexible wings became impractical and structurally dangerous, and hinged ailerons became the standard method of roll control. Wing warping survived in a few early designs, such as the Blériot XI and early Fokker types, but by the First World War it had largely been abandoned in favor of ailerons.
