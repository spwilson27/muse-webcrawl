Arctic amplification

Arctic amplification is the phenomenon, observed in the climate change of recent decades and reproduced in climate models, whereby the Arctic warms faster than the Earth as a whole. Over the satellite era the Arctic has warmed several times faster than the global average — recent assessments put the rate since 1979 at nearly four times the global rate. The effect is the high-latitude expression of polar amplification in general.

Causes

The principal driver is the ice–albedo feedback: as snow and sea ice melt, darker ocean and land surfaces are exposed, absorbing more solar radiation and causing further warming and melting. Additional contributors include the lapse-rate feedback (Arctic warming is concentrated near the surface beneath strong temperature inversions, so less energy escapes to space), increased transport of heat and moisture from lower latitudes, changes in clouds, and deposition of black carbon on snow and ice. The seasonal cycle matters: amplification is strongest in autumn and winter, when heat stored in newly ice-free ocean is released back to the atmosphere.

Observed effects

Arctic amplification accompanies the steep decline of summer sea ice, thawing of permafrost, increased mass loss from the Greenland ice sheet, longer growing seasons, and "Atlantification" of the Arctic Ocean. It affects Indigenous communities and ecosystems adapted to ice, including polar bears and ice-dependent seals.

Consequences and debate

Researchers have debated whether amplification weakens the equator-to-pole temperature gradient enough to make the jet stream "wavier", potentially influencing mid-latitude weather extremes such as cold outbreaks, heat waves, and stalled storm patterns. Assessments by the IPCC conclude that Arctic amplification itself is robust and primarily feedback-driven, while its influence on mid-latitude weather remains an area of active research with lower confidence.
