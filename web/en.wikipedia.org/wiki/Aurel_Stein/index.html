Sir Marc Aurel Stein, KCIE, FRAS, FBA (Hungarian: Stein Márk Aurél; 26 November 1862 – 26 October 1943) was a Hungarian-born British archaeologist, primarily known for his explorations and archaeological discoveries in Central Asia. He was also a professor at various Indian universities.

Early life and career

Stein was born in Budapest to a Jewish family and studied Sanskrit, Persian, and Indology at the universities of Vienna, Leipzig, and Tübingen, receiving his doctorate in 1883. In 1887 he moved to India, where he became principal of the Oriental College in Lahore and registrar of Punjab University. His translation of Kalhana's Rajatarangini, the chronicle of the kings of Kashmir, established his scholarly reputation and led him to advocate a programme of archaeological exploration in Chinese Turkestan, inspired by the earlier journeys of Sven Hedin.

Expeditions

Stein led four major expeditions to Central Asia (1900–01, 1906–08, 1913–16, and 1930), surveying ancient sites along the Silk Road in Xinjiang and Gansu. On the second expedition, in 1907, he persuaded the Daoist caretaker Wang Yuanlu to open the sealed Library Cave (Cave 17) at the Mogao Grottoes near Dunhuang, and acquired some 24 cases of manuscripts and 5 cases of paintings—including the Diamond Sutra of 868, the world's earliest dated printed book—for a small sum. His finds, which also included documents in Khotanese, Sogdian, Tibetan, and other languages, are now held principally by the British Library, the British Museum, and the National Museum in New Delhi.

Legacy and criticism

Stein's meticulous survey reports, including Ancient Khotan, Serindia, and Innermost Asia, transformed knowledge of the Silk Road. He was knighted in 1912. In China, however, he has been condemned as a plunderer of national heritage, and his Dunhuang acquisitions remain a prominent case in debates about the removal of cultural property. Stein died in Kabul in 1943 while preparing an expedition into Afghanistan, and was buried there in the Sherpur Cantonment.
