Saint Sophia Cathedral in Kyiv, Ukraine, is an outstanding architectural monument of Kievan Rus', named for the Hagia Sophia of Constantinople and symbolising Kyiv as a "new Constantinople". Built in the 11th century, it preserves the world's most complete ensemble of original mosaics and frescoes of that period and served for centuries as the principal church and burial place of the Kievan princes. It is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List together with the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra.

History

The chronicles give conflicting dates: the Primary Chronicle places the founding in 1037 by Yaroslav the Wise, after his victory over the Pechenegs, while the Novgorod First Chronicle gives 1011 under his father Vladimir the Great; much modern scholarship favours an early-11th-century start with completion under Yaroslav. The cathedral was looted during the Mongol invasion of 1240, declined in the later Middle Ages, and was restored in the 17th century under Metropolitan Peter Mohyla and Hetman Ivan Mazepa, who financed its rebuilding in the Ukrainian Baroque style — the source of its present pear-shaped gilded domes. The four-tier bell tower, 76 metres high, was built in 1699–1706. Under Soviet rule the cathedral was closed for worship and saved from planned demolition by being turned into a museum in 1934. Religious use has been a sensitive question since Ukrainian independence, and the cathedral functions as a state museum with occasional services.

Architecture and art

The core of the cathedral is a five-aisled, cross-domed Byzantine church crowned originally by thirteen domes. Inside, the apse is dominated by the six-metre mosaic of the Virgin Orans, the "Unbreakable Wall", set against gold; below it a mosaic of the Eucharist and figures of Church Fathers. The fresco cycle, including scenes of the life of Christ and the only surviving secular frescoes of Kievan Rus' — portraits of Yaroslav's family and court entertainments in the towers — covers some 3,000 square metres. The sarcophagus of Yaroslav the Wise stands in the northern gallery. Hundreds of medieval graffiti scratched into the plaster, including alphabets and prayers, are an important source for early East Slavic literacy.
