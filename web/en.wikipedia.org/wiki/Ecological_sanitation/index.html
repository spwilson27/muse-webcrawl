Ecological sanitation
Also calledEcoSan
ApproachSource separation, sanitisation, and reuse of excreta and greywater
Typical systemsUrine-diverting dry toilets (UDDTs), composting toilets, arborloos
GoalsPublic health, water saving, nutrient recycling, soil fertility

Ecological sanitation (often abbreviated EcoSan) is an approach to sanitation provision that aims to protect human health and the environment while recycling the nutrients and organic matter in human excreta and wastewater back into agriculture. Rather than treating excreta as waste to be flushed away with large volumes of water, ecosan systems separate flows at source, sanitise them, and return them safely to soils — "closing the loop" between sanitation and food production.

Principles and technologies

A central technology is the urine-diverting dry toilet (UDDT), which collects urine and faeces separately without flush water: urine, rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, can be stored and used as fertiliser, while faeces are dehydrated or composted in a vault, with ash or lime added to raise pH and kill pathogens. Other systems include composting toilets, arborloos (a shallow pit planted with a tree when full), fossa alterna double pits, and constructed wetlands for greywater treatment. Safe reuse follows multi-barrier principles consistent with the World Health Organization's 2006 guidelines on the safe use of wastewater, excreta, and greywater.

Applications and debate

Ecosan projects have been implemented at large scale in parts of China, sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America, and in eco-villages and emergency settings, where water scarcity, lack of sewers, and high fertiliser costs make the approach attractive. Challenges include user acceptance, the management logistics of collection and reuse, cultural attitudes to handling excreta, and ensuring reliable pathogen destruction; the field is promoted by networks such as the Sustainable Sanitation Alliance (SuSanA), founded in 2007.
