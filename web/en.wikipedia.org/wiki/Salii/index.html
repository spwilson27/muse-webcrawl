The Salii were the "leaping priests" of ancient Rome, two colleges of twelve patrician priests devoted to the war gods Mars and Quirinus. Their name derives from the Latin salire, "to leap" or "to dance". Dressed in archaic bronze armour, they processed through Rome each March — the opening of the campaigning season — carrying the sacred figure-eight shields (ancilia) and singing the archaic hymn known as the Carmen Saliare.

Origins

Roman tradition credited King Numa Pompilius with founding the original college, the Salii Palatini of Mars Gradivus, after a sacred shield was said to have fallen from heaven; eleven copies were made to protect the original. King Tullus Hostilius was said to have added the second college, the Salii Collini (or Agonenses), devoted to Quirinus, on the Quirinal Hill.

Rites

Throughout March the Salii carried the ancilia through the city, dancing in procession, striking their shields with staffs and singing their hymn — so ancient that by the classical period even the priests barely understood its language. They feasted lavishly each evening, giving rise to the proverbial expression Saliaris cena for a sumptuous dinner. Their rites closed with the Tubilustrium on 23 March, when the war trumpets were purified.

Membership

Salii were drawn from patrician families, appointed for life, though they were required to resign if they took certain higher offices. The office survived into the Empire; the emperor Marcus Aurelius was enrolled as a boy.
