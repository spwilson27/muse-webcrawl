Light therapy

Light therapy, or phototherapy, is a treatment involving exposure to bright artificial light, or to specific wavelengths of light, used to treat a variety of conditions. The best-established use is bright light therapy for seasonal affective disorder (SAD), a form of depression linked to the reduced daylight of winter.

Bright light therapy

Treatment typically uses a light box emitting about 10,000 lux of white light, far brighter than ordinary indoor lighting, with ultraviolet light filtered out. Patients sit near the box for roughly 30 minutes, usually in the morning, allowing light to reach the eyes indirectly. The therapy is believed to work by resetting circadian rhythms and influencing serotonin and melatonin pathways. Norman Rosenthal and colleagues at the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health first described SAD and the response to light in the early 1980s.

Other uses

  • Skin conditions: ultraviolet phototherapy (narrowband UVB, PUVA) for psoriasis, eczema and vitiligo
  • Neonatal jaundice: blue light phototherapy breaks down bilirubin in infants' skin
  • Sleep disorders: timed light exposure for circadian rhythm sleep disorders and jet lag
  • Acne and wound healing: blue and red light therapies of varying evidence

Safety

Side effects of bright light therapy are usually mild — eyestrain, headache, nausea, or agitation — but caution is advised for people with eye diseases, those taking photosensitising medications, and people with bipolar disorder, in whom light may trigger mania. UV phototherapy carries risks similar to sun exposure, including skin ageing and increased skin-cancer risk with long-term use.
