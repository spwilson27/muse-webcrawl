Internet cafe

An Internet café (also referred to as a cybercafé) is a type of small business that provides computers and other devices to access the Internet, usually for a fee based on the amount of time used. Internet cafés were a major means of accessing the Web during the 1990s and early 2000s, when home Internet access was far less common, and they remain important in parts of the developing world.

History

The first establishments offering public terminal or network access appeared in the late 1980s and early 1990s; the name is often traced to Café Cyberia, opened in London in 1994, and to early network cafés in South Korea and the United States. The spread of affordable dial-up and later broadband access made the café model widespread: customers could check email, browse the web, play networked games, and use office software without owning a computer.

In East and Southeast Asia, particularly South Korea and China, internet cafés — known as PC bang in Korea — evolved into centres for multiplayer online gaming, often providing high-end gaming PCs, fast connections, food service and comfortable seating. At their peak there were tens of thousands of such venues in China, although regulators later imposed restrictions including curfews for minors.

Decline and continuing role

From the mid-2000s onward, cheaper home broadband, laptops and smartphones sharply reduced demand in developed countries, and many cafés closed or converted into gaming centres, print shops or convenience-style access points. They nevertheless persist where home connectivity is expensive or unreliable, and they are widely used by travellers, migrant workers and people needing printing, scanning or document services.

Regulation

Internet cafés have been subject to varying regulation: licensing requirements, identity checks for users, content filtering, and restrictions on gaming by minors. In some jurisdictions operators must retain usage records, which has raised debates about privacy and surveillance.
