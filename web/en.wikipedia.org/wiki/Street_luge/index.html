Street luge

A street luger in the supine racing position.

Street luge is an extreme gravity-powered activity that involves riding a street luge board (sometimes referred to as a sled) down a paved road or course in the supine position. The sport evolved from downhill skateboarding in Southern California in the 1970s, when skateboarders found they could reach higher speeds by lying down on their boards. Street luge is not an Olympic sport, though it was contested at the X Games from 1995 until 2001 and remains part of the World Skate Games and international gravity sports festivals.

Equipment

A street luge board is typically longer and heavier than a skateboard — about 1.2 to 1.5 metres — made of wood, aluminium, steel, or composite materials, with a contoured pan for the rider's body and handles for steering leverage. It uses skateboard-style trucks and large polyurethane wheels (often 70–100 mm). Riders steer by shifting their weight and by leaning the trucks, and brake primarily with their feet, wearing shoes with pieces of rubber or brake soles attached; some boards have mechanical brakes.

Racing and safety

Riders wear full-face helmets, leather or Kevlar racing suits, gloves, and shoes with braking pads. In competition, riders race head-to-head in heats of four or six, or against the clock in time trials, on closed mountain roads with hay-bale protection. Speeds commonly exceed 80 km/h (50 mph) and top riders have surpassed 130 km/h (80 mph) on steep courses; the world speed record on a street luge is over 160 km/h.

Governing bodies have included the International Gravity Sports Association (IGSA), which ran a World Cup series, and the International Downhill Federation (IDF). Safety rules mandate leathers, helmets, and controlled courses, as the supine position places the rider head-first relative to obstacles at the feet — riders actually travel feet-first.

History

The activity traces to the Signal Hill downhill skateboard races in Long Beach, California, in the mid-1970s, where riders experimented with lying down. Through the 1980s the "buttboard" evolved into purpose-built luges. Street luge gained television exposure through the X Games, and a professional circuit flourished in the late 1990s before the event was dropped in 2001, after which the sport continued at the grassroots and world-championship level.
