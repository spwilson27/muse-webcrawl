Post-war immigration to Australia refers to the large-scale immigration programme undertaken by the Australian government after the Second World War. Driven by the conviction that Australia must "populate or perish", the programme transformed the country's demography: between 1945 and the mid-1960s Australia's population grew from about 7.4 million to well over 11 million, and the previously overwhelmingly British character of the population broadened to include large Italian, Greek, Dutch, German, Yugoslav and other communities.

Origins

The war, and in particular the threat of Japanese invasion in 1942, convinced policymakers that Australia's small population was a strategic vulnerability. The Chifley government created the Department of Immigration in 1945 under Arthur Calwell, its first minister. The initial target was to grow the population by 2 per cent per year, half from natural increase and half from immigration, with an expectation that migrants would be predominantly British.

Assisted passage schemes

The best-known scheme was the assisted passage for British migrants — popularly "Ten Pound Poms" — under which British adults paid £10 for their fare, with children travelling free. More than a million British migrants came under assisted passages. Agreements were also concluded with other countries, and from 1947 Australia accepted displaced persons from European refugee camps under an agreement with the International Refugee Organization; some 170,000 displaced persons arrived by 1951, settling the pattern for later non-British intakes. Migrant reception and training centres such as Bonegilla in Victoria processed new arrivals, and many migrants were directed to employment on major projects including the Snowy Mountains Scheme.

Social change and the end of White Australia

The programme initially operated within the framework of the White Australia policy, favouring northern Europeans and gradually extending to southern and eastern Europeans. From the 1960s the policy was progressively dismantled: the Holt government liberalised entry rules in 1966, and the Whitlam government formally ended race-based selection in 1973, paving the way for multicultural policies under the Fraser government and the arrival of Vietnamese and other Asian refugee communities from the late 1970s.

Legacy

Post-war migration remade Australian cities, cuisine, religion and language, and remains a defining feature of modern Australian identity. By the early twenty-first century roughly a quarter of Australians were born overseas.
