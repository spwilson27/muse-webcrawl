Intracoastal Waterway

The Intracoastal Waterway (ICW) is a 3,000-mile (4,800 km) inland waterway along the Atlantic and Gulf of Mexico coasts of the United States, running from Massachusetts around Florida to Brownsville, Texas. Consisting of natural inlets, saltwater rivers, bays and sounds linked by artificial canals, it provides a navigable route largely sheltered from the open sea.

Sections and construction

The Atlantic Intracoastal Waterway runs from the Norfolk area through the Carolinas, Georgia and Florida to Miami and Key West, while the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway continues from Florida's west coast to Texas. Although boaters had long used the sheltered passages, systematic federal development began with the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1910 and continued through the twentieth century; the modern waterway was largely complete by the 1940s. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers maintains the channel, generally dredged to a depth of about 12 feet (3.7 m) on the Atlantic section.

Use

The waterway serves commercial traffic — barges carrying petroleum products, chemicals, building materials and farm goods — and a large recreational fleet, including the seasonal migration of cruising boats ("snowbirds") between the northeastern United States and Florida. Bridges, locks and dredging schedules shape traffic, and the route is marked by distinctive magenta lines on nautical charts.
