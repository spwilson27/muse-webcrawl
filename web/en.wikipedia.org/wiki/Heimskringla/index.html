Heimskringla

Heimskringla (Old Norse: [ˈhɛimsˌkʰriŋla]) is a collection of sagas about Swedish and Norwegian kings, beginning with the legendary Yngling dynasty followed by accounts of historical Norwegian rulers from the 9th century to the 12th century. It was written in Old Norse in Iceland and is generally attributed to the Icelandic historian and poet Snorri Sturluson (1178/79–1241), around the year 1230.

The title Heimskringla comes from the first words of the work, kringla heimsins, "the orb of the world", and was first used in the 17th century.

Contents

The collection consists of sixteen sagas, of which the longest is the Saga of Olaf Haraldsson (Saint Olaf), occupying about a third of the whole work. It begins with the Ynglinga saga, which gives a euhemerised account of the Norse gods and traces the Swedish Yngling dynasty, and proceeds through the sagas of Halfdan the Black, Harald Fairhair, Haakon the Good, Olaf Tryggvason, the two Olafs, Magnus the Good, Harald Hardrada and others, concluding with the saga of Magnus Erlingsson, ending in 1177.

Sources and style

Snorri drew on earlier written works, including Ágrip, Fagrskinna and lost sagas, as well as skaldic poetry, whose intricate stanzas he treated as reliable historical testimony. His prose is noted for its clarity, dramatic dialogue and concise characterisation, making Heimskringla one of the masterpieces of medieval Scandinavian literature and a principal source for the history of early Norway.

Manuscripts and translations

The original manuscripts were largely destroyed in the Copenhagen fire of 1728, but transcripts made before the fire preserve the text. Heimskringla has been translated into many languages; the English translation by Samuel Laing (1844) was long standard, and a widely used modern translation is that of Lee M. Hollander (1964).
