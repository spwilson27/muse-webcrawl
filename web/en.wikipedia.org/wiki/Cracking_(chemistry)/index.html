Cracking (chemistry)

In petroleum geology and chemistry, cracking is the process whereby complex organic molecules such as kerogens or long-chain hydrocarbons are broken down into simpler molecules such as light hydrocarbons, by the breaking of carbon–carbon bonds in the precursors. Cracking is a key process in petroleum refining, converting heavy fractions of crude oil into more valuable gasoline, olefins and other products.

Methods

Thermal cracking, developed commercially in the early 20th century (the Burton process of 1913 and Vladimir Shukhov's earlier Russian work), uses high temperatures and pressures to break down heavy oils. Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC), introduced in the 1940s, circulates powdered zeolite catalyst through the reaction zone and is the dominant method for gasoline production. Steam cracking of naphtha or ethane at very high temperatures is the principal route to ethylene and propylene for the petrochemical industry. Hydrocracking uses hydrogen and catalysts under high pressure to make saturated products such as jet fuel and diesel with low sulfur content. Visbreaking and coking are milder and thermal resid-conversion processes, respectively.

Chemistry

Cracking reactions proceed mainly through free-radical mechanisms at high temperatures, or through carbocation intermediates on acidic catalysts. Long-chain alkanes split into shorter alkanes and alkenes; secondary reactions include isomerisation, cyclisation and aromatisation, which raise the octane number of the gasoline fraction.

Products

Products include refinery gas, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), light and heavy naphtha, kerosene, light cycle oil and coke. The balance of products can be shifted by choice of feedstock, catalyst and operating severity.
