The Alaska Purchase was the United States' acquisition of Alaska from the Russian Empire in 1867. The territory, comprising roughly 586,412 square miles (1,518,800 km²), was purchased for $7.2 million — about two cents per acre. The treaty was negotiated and signed by Secretary of State William H. Seward and Russian minister Eduard de Stoeckl, and was ratified by the U.S. Senate on April 9, 1867.

Background

By the mid-19th century, Russian America had become a financial burden for the Russian Empire. The colony's fur trade was declining, the territory was difficult to defend against British forces in nearby Canada, and Russia feared losing the colony without compensation in the event of war with Britain, especially after the Crimean War. Tsar Alexander II therefore instructed his minister in Washington to negotiate a sale.

Negotiations and treaty

Negotiations were conducted largely between Seward and de Stoeckl. The treaty was signed in the early hours of March 30, 1867, after an all-night session. The Senate approved it by a vote of 37 to 2. Funding, however, was delayed in the House of Representatives amid the impeachment proceedings against President Andrew Johnson; the appropriation finally passed in July 1868.

Transfer ceremony

The formal transfer took place on October 18, 1867, in Sitka (then called New Archangel). Russian and American soldiers paraded, the Russian flag was lowered, and the American flag was raised amid difficulties when the Russian flag became tangled on the flagpole. October 18 is now celebrated in Alaska as Alaska Day.

Reception and legacy

The purchase was initially mocked in parts of the American press as "Seward's Folly" or "Seward's Icebox," but opinion shifted after gold was discovered in the Klondike region and later after major oil discoveries at Prudhoe Bay in 1968. Alaska was organized as a district in 1884, became a territory in 1912, and was admitted to the Union as the 49th state on January 3, 1959.
