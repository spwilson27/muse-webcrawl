A frame story (also called a frame tale or frame narrative) is a literary device in which an introductory or main narrative sets the stage either for a more emphasised second narrative or for a set of shorter stories. The frame story leads readers from a first story into one or more other stories within it.

History and examples

The device is ancient: the Sanskrit Panchatantra embeds fables within framing tales, a structure transmitted westward through Persian and Arabic versions. In One Thousand and One Nights, Scheherazade postpones her execution by telling the king a new story each night, many of which nest further stories. Boccaccio's Decameron frames one hundred tales as told by young Florentines fleeing the plague, and Chaucer's Canterbury Tales presents its tales as pilgrims' storytelling on the road to Canterbury.

Later examples include Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, whose nested letters frame Victor's and the creature's narratives, Conrad's Heart of Darkness, told by Marlow aboard a boat on the Thames, and Emily Brontë's Wuthering Heights, narrated through Lockwood and Nelly Dean. Frame structures are also common in film and television, where a narrator recounts past events.

Functions

A frame can establish plausibility and distance, organise disparate tales under one roof, create dramatic irony between framing and embedded levels, or let an author comment on the act of storytelling itself. When frames nest repeatedly, the resulting recursion can blur the boundary between narrator and narrated.
