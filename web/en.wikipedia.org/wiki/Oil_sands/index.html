Oil sands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Open-pit oil sands mining near Fort McMurray, Alberta.

Oil sands, also known as tar sands, are a type of unconventional petroleum deposit consisting of a mixture of sand, clay, water and bitumen — a dense, viscous form of petroleum. Bitumen must be separated from the sand and upgraded before it can be refined into products such as gasoline and diesel. The world's largest deposits are in Canada (the Athabasca oil sands of Alberta) and Venezuela (the Orinoco Belt), with smaller deposits in Kazakhstan, Russia and elsewhere. Canada's proven reserves, overwhelmingly oil sands, are among the largest of any country, at roughly 165 billion barrels.

Extraction

Where deposits lie near the surface — under less than about 75 metres of overburden — they are extracted by open-pit mining: the sand is dug by giant shovels and trucks, crushed and mixed with hot water so the bitumen can be separated and floated off. Most reserves lie deeper and are recovered in situ, principally by steam-assisted gravity drainage (SAGD), in which steam injected through a horizontal well heats the bitumen so that it flows to a second, lower well. In-situ methods disturb far less land but consume large amounts of energy, usually by burning natural gas to raise steam.

Environmental issues

Oil sands production is energy- and water-intensive and produces higher greenhouse gas emissions per barrel than conventional crude — on the order of 5–25 per cent more across the fuel life cycle, depending on the method and study. Mining creates large tailings ponds of water, sand, clay and residual bitumen, and disturbs boreal forest and wetlands; reclamation of mined land is legally required but slow. The industry is also a major economic driver in Alberta and a recurring focus of debate over pipelines, Indigenous rights and Canada's climate commitments.

History

Indigenous peoples used bitumen from natural seeps to waterproof canoes long before European contact. Commercial development began in the twentieth century; the Great Canadian Oil Sands plant (now Suncor) opened near Fort McMurray in 1967, and large-scale expansion followed rising oil prices in the 1990s and 2000s, making Fort McMurray a boom town.
