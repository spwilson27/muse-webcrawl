Chocolate chip cookie

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A stack of chocolate chip cookies with melted chocolate pools, one broken open]
A stack of chocolate chip cookies with melted chocolate pools, one broken open

The chocolate chip cookie is a drop cookie featuring chocolate chips or chunks as its distinguishing ingredient. It was invented in the United States in 1938 by Ruth Graves Wakefield, who ran the Toll House Inn in Whitman, Massachusetts — hence its original name, the Toll House Chocolate Crunch Cookie. It is the most popular cookie in America and a global baking icon, its recipe printed on every bag of Nestlé Toll House morsels.

Invention and spread

Wakefield — a trained dietitian and cookbook author — chopped a Nestlé semi-sweet bar into a butter cookie dough (legend says she expected it to melt through; she, a professional, knew it would hold its shape). The recipe's 1938 publication and wartime popularity (Massachusetts soldiers shared them from care packages) led to her deal with Nestlé: the recipe printed on the wrapper, in exchange, by tradition, for a lifetime supply of chocolate. Nestlé began scoring bars and selling pre-cut 'morsels' in 1939–41.

Variations and culture

The cookie canonically balances crisp edges and a soft centre; canonical disputes include brown versus white sugar ratios, chill time, salt flakes and chip versus chopped chocolate. State cookie of Massachusetts (1997), subject of endless 'perfect cookie' science journalism, and base of the cookie-cake and skillet-cookie industries, it is America's default homemade bake — the recipe by which the country's kitchens are judged.
