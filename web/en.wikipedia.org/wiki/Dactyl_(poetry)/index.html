Dactyl (poetry)

A dactyl (from Greek δάκτυλος, dáktylos, "finger") is a metrical foot in poetry consisting of one stressed syllable followed by two unstressed syllables — in quantitative verse, one long syllable followed by two short ones. The pattern (— ‿ ‿) resembles the three joints of a finger. English examples include words such as "poetry", "elephant", and "manhattan".

Usage

The dactyl is the defining foot of classical epic verse: the dactylic hexameter of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey and Virgil's Aeneid is built from six dactylic feet, with the spondee permitted as a substitute in most positions. In English poetry, sustained dactylic metre is rarer, but it appears in works such as Longfellow's Evangeline ("This is the forest primeval...") and in Alfred, Lord Tennyson's "The Charge of the Light Brigade", whose galloping dactyls mimic cavalry. Robert Browning's "The Lost Leader" is another frequently cited example.

Related feet

The dactyl is related to the anapaest (two unstressed followed by one stressed), its mirror image, and contrasts with the two-syllable feet such as the iamb and trochee.
