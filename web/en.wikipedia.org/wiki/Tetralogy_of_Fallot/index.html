Tetralogy of Fallot

Specialty: Cardiology, paediatrics

Symptoms: Cyanosis, "tet spells", clubbing, murmur

Treatment: Surgical repair

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is a congenital heart defect involving four anatomical abnormalities of the heart. Although only four defects are counted, the condition in fact arises from a single developmental error — anterior displacement of the infundibular (conal) septum. It is the most common cause of the "blue baby syndrome", and was first described systematically by Étienne-Louis Arthur Fallot in 1888.

The four defects

  1. Pulmonary stenosis — narrowing of the right ventricular outflow tract, usually at or below the pulmonary valve
  2. Overriding aorta — the aorta sits over the ventricular septal defect, receiving blood from both ventricles
  3. Ventricular septal defect (VSD) — a large hole between the two ventricles
  4. Right ventricular hypertrophy — thickening of the right ventricular wall from pumping against the obstruction

Signs and symptoms

The degree of cyanosis depends on the severity of the pulmonary stenosis. Infants may experience "tet spells" (hypercyanotic episodes) — sudden deepening of cyanosis with rapid breathing and distress, often after crying or feeding; older children characteristically squat, which increases systemic vascular resistance and improves oxygenation. Other features include a heart murmur, clubbing of fingers and toes, and poor growth. Diagnosis is typically by echocardiography.

Treatment and prognosis

Definitive treatment is open-heart surgical repair, usually performed in the first year of life, involving closure of the VSD with a patch and relief of the outflow obstruction. Temporary palliative shunts (such as the Blalock–Taussig shunt, first performed in 1944) were historically used to increase pulmonary blood flow before repair. With modern surgery, most patients survive to adulthood, though lifelong cardiology follow-up is needed because of risks such as pulmonary regurgitation and arrhythmia.
