1985 diethylene glycol wine scandal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The 1985 diethylene glycol wine scandal was a major adulteration scandal in which diethylene glycol — a toxic industrial chemical used in antifreeze — was illegally added to some Austrian wines, chiefly by producers making sweet wines for the German market, to imitate the body and sweetness of higher-quality late-harvest wines.

Discovery and course

In early 1985, German laboratory testing of Austrian imports found the chemical; authorities seized stocks, and by July the affair was public. In Austria, the Burgenland wine-growing region around the Neusiedler See — famous for sweet wines — stood at the centre of the trade. Investigations found that one broker had sold enormous quantities of diethylene glycol to winemakers. Several producers and merchants were prosecuted and imprisoned; the ringleader eventually took his own life before sentencing.

The amounts present in most wines were small and no deaths were directly attributed to drinking them, but the scandal devastated Austria's wine industry: exports collapsed, German supermarkets destroyed stocks, and Austrian wine's reputation was ruined for years.

Aftermath

Austria responded with some of the strictest wine laws in the world, emphasising origin, traceability, and dry quality wines. The industry reinvented itself around quality reds and crisp dry whites such as Grüner Veltliner, and export values eventually surpassed pre-scandal levels — a turnaround often cited as the "miracle" that forced Austrian wine to modernise.
