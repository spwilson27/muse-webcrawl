John Coltrane

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: John Coltrane playing soprano saxophone in 1963: eyes closed, in the year of his classic quartet's maturity]

John William Coltrane (23 September 1926 – 17 July 1967) was an American jazz saxophonist and composer, among the most influential musicians of the twentieth century. In a career of barely twelve recording years he moved from bebop sideman to the 'sheets of sound' of Giant Steps (1960), modal explorations with his classic quartet, and the spiritual free jazz of his final period — a trajectory of relentless seeking that made him a saint to many listeners, literally: the St John Coltrane Church in San Francisco canonised him.

His landmark albums include Blue Train (1957), Giant Steps (1960), My Favorite Things (1961), A Love Supreme (recorded 1964) — his four-part suite of devotion, among the most revered recordings in jazz — and Ascension (1965).

Sideman to leader

Born in Hamlet, North Carolina, Coltrane served in a navy band, then played with Dizzy Gillespie, Johnny Hodges and Thelonious Monk, and twice with Miles Davis, whose quintet (1955–57, 1958–60) made his name; heroin addiction cost him the first engagement, and his 1957 recovery — a spiritual awakening he credited in A Love Supreme's liner poem — refounded his art. The 1957 Monk quartet residency at the Five Spot is legend among jazz apprenticeships.

The quartet and the last years

With McCoy Tyner, Jimmy Garrison and Elvin Jones (1961–65), Coltrane built the classic quartet: modal vamps, torrential improvisation, standards remade ('My Favorite Things' on soprano sax). After 1965 he pushed into atonality and collective improvisation with a second band including his wife Alice Coltrane, dividing audiences as he had at every step. Liver cancer killed him at 40 in 1967. The Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award and a Pulitzer Prize special citation (2007) mark official canonisation.
