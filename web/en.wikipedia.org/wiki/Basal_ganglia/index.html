The basal ganglia (or basal nuclei) are a group of subcortical nuclei, of varied origin, in the brains of vertebrates. They are situated at the base of the forebrain and strongly connected with the cerebral cortex, thalamus, and brainstem. The main components are the striatum (comprising the caudate nucleus and putamen), the globus pallidus, the subthalamic nucleus, and the substantia nigra.

Function

The basal ganglia are primarily involved in action selection: the initiation and suppression of voluntary movements, procedural and habit learning, eye movements, and reward-related and emotional behaviour. Cortical inputs excite the striatum, whose output reaches the thalamus through direct (movement-facilitating) and indirect (movement-suppressing) pathways. Dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta modulates both pathways, balancing the initiation of desired actions against the inhibition of competing ones.

Clinical significance

Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra causes Parkinson's disease, characterised by tremor, rigidity and bradykinesia. Loss of striatal neurons in Huntington's disease produces involuntary, dance-like movements (chorea). The basal ganglia are also implicated in Tourette syndrome, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and addiction, and are a target for deep brain stimulation in movement disorders.

History

Thomas Willis described parts of the complex in the 17th century; the term "basal ganglia" came into use in the 20th century, though modern anatomists prefer "basal nuclei" since "ganglion" properly refers to the peripheral nervous system.
