Marburg is a university town in the German state of Hesse, capital of the Marburg-Biedenkopf district. Spread along the valley of the river Lahn and crowned by the Landgrave's castle (Marburger Schloss), its steep, narrow lanes and half-timbered old town are among the best preserved in Germany.

History

Marburg grew around a castle of the Thuringian landgraves and is inseparable from Saint Elizabeth of Hungary, who lived and worked charitably there from 1228 until her death in 1231; the Elisabethkirche (begun 1235) built over her tomb is one of the earliest purely Gothic churches in Germany and made Marburg a pilgrimage centre. In 1527 Landgrave Philip the Magnanimous founded the University of Marburg, the world's oldest Protestant university still in existence. In 1529 the town hosted the Marburg Colloquy, Philip's failed attempt to reconcile Luther and Zwingli on the Eucharist.

University and culture

The University of Marburg (some 22,000 students) dominates town life; its scholars have included the Brothers Grimm, who studied there and collected fairy tales in the region, chemist Robert Bunsen, and philosopher Martin Heidegger, who taught there in the 1920s. The town's Marburg School of neo-Kantian philosophy (Hermann Cohen, Paul Natorp) was influential around 1900.

Marburg virus

The town gave its name to the Marburg virus: in 1967 laboratory workers in Marburg (and Frankfurt and Belgrade) handling Ugandan green monkeys developed a haemorrhagic fever, killing seven — the first recognised filovirus outbreak. Marburg remains a major vaccine-research centre, home to a BSL-4 laboratory and the vaccine manufacturer that grew out of the Behringwerke founded by Emil von Behring, winner of the first Nobel Prize in Medicine (1901), who developed diphtheria antitoxin in the town.
