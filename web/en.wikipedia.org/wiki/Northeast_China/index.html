Northeast China

Northeast China (simplified Chinese: 东北; traditional Chinese: 東北; pinyin: Dōngběi), historically known in the West as Manchuria, is a geographical region of China comprising the three provinces of Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang, and sometimes the eastern part of Inner Mongolia. It is separated from the Russian Far East by the Amur and Ussuri rivers and from North Korea by the Yalu and Tumen rivers.

Geography

The region centres on the Northeast China Plain (the Manchurian Plain), one of the largest plains in China, framed by the Greater Khingan Range to the west, the Lesser Khingan Range to the north, and the Changbai Mountains to the east. The Songhua, Liao, and Nen rivers drain the plain. Winters are long, cold, and dry under the Siberian High, while summers are warm and rainy; major cities include Shenyang, Dalian, Changchun, and Harbin.

History

The region was the homeland of peoples including the Manchus, whose Qing dynasty conquered China in the 17th century. In the 20th century Northeast China was contested by Russia and Japan; Japan established the puppet state of Manchukuo in 1932. After 1949 the region became China's first industrial base—its "rust belt"—centred on steel, machinery, automobiles, and the Daqing oilfield. Since the early 2000s it has faced industrial decline and population outflow, prompting the government's "Revitalize Northeast China" programme. The region is also a major grain-producing area, especially for maize, rice, and soybeans from its fertile black soils. See also Manchuria, Heilongjiang, and Harbin.
