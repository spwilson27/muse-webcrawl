Islamic modernism

Islamic modernism is a movement that has sought to reconcile the Islamic faith with modern values such as democracy, civil rights, rationality, science and progress. Emerging in the 19th century in response to European colonial expansion and the perceived decline of the Muslim world, Islamic modernists argued that Islam, properly understood through renewed interpretation (ijtihad), was fully compatible with modernity.

Leading modernists included Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh.

Key figures

Prominent modernists include Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838–1897), his disciple Muhammad Abduh (1849–1905), Grand Mufti of Egypt; Sir Syed Ahmad Khan in British India, founder of the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College at Aligarh; Muhammad Iqbal; and Rashid Rida, whose later work moved toward Salafism. Modernists promoted education reform, reinterpretation of the Quran in light of reason, and the rejection of blind imitation (taqlid) of medieval scholarship.

Legacy

Islamic modernism influenced reform movements across the Middle East and South and Southeast Asia and remains a reference point in contemporary debates over Islam, democracy and human rights, though it has been criticised both by traditionalist scholars and by Islamist movements.
