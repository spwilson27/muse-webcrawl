Battle of Anzio

The Battle of Anzio was a battle of the Italian campaign of World War II that took place from 22 January 1944 to 5 June 1944. Under Operation Shingle, the US VI Corps under Major General John P. Lucas landed at Anzio and Nettuno, on the coast about 50 km south of Rome, hoping to outflank the German Winter Line and force the Germans to abandon their defences around Monte Cassino. The landings achieved complete surprise, but Lucas consolidated rather than advancing rapidly, and German forces under Field Marshal Albert Kesselring and General Eberhard von Mackensen contained the beachhead for four months in a bitter attritional struggle before the Allies broke out in May 1944 as part of Operation Diadem.

Landings

Championed by Winston Churchill, Operation Shingle landed the British 1st Infantry Division, the US 3rd Infantry Division, Rangers, Commandos and paratroops on 22 January 1944 with virtually no opposition. A jeep patrol even reached the outskirts of Rome. Lucas, mindful of the near-disaster at Salerno and ordered to secure the beachhead, dug in — a decision that has been debated ever since. Kesselring rushed reserves south and by early February the Germans had ringed the shallow beachhead, subjecting it to constant artillery and air attack; the hospital ships and the harbour were repeatedly hit.

German counterattacks and stalemate

In February the German Fourteenth Army launched major counterattacks (notably Operation Fischfang, 16–20 February) that nearly split the beachhead but failed with heavy losses on both sides. Lucas was replaced by Lucian Truscott. Through the spring the beachhead remained a static, muddy battlefield reminiscent of the First World War, with the Allies supplied entirely by sea.

Breakout

On 23 May 1944, as the main Allied offensive (Diadem) broke the Gustav Line at Cassino, VI Corps attacked out of the beachhead toward Valmontone in Operation Buffalo, threatening to cut off the retreating German Tenth Army. Mark Clark instead directed the main effort toward Rome, which fell on 4 June — two days before D-Day in Normandy overshadowed the achievement. The Germans escaped to fight on in the Gothic Line, a decision historians continue to debate.
