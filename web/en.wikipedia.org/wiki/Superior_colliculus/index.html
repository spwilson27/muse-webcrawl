The superior colliculus (Latin for "upper hill") is a structure on the roof of the mammalian midbrain. In non-mammalian vertebrates, its homologue is known as the optic tectum. It is a paired structure forming part of the corpora quadrigemina, together with the smaller inferior colliculi below.

The superior colliculi on the dorsal midbrain

Structure

The superior colliculus is a layered structure, conventionally divided into seven alternating layers of grey and white matter. The superficial layers receive direct input from the retina and visual cortex and are organised purely visually, whereas the deeper (intermediate and deep) layers also receive auditory and somatosensory input and contain motor-related neurons.

Function

The superior colliculus is best known for its role in generating saccadic eye movements. Retinotopic maps in its superficial layers register the location of visual stimuli, and activity in the deeper layers codes the direction and amplitude of a gaze shift, integrating visual, auditory, and somatosensory signals into a common frame of reference. It also contributes to orienting movements of the head and body toward novel stimuli and has been implicated in aspects of visual attention and in the residual vision ("blindsight") seen after damage to the primary visual cortex.
