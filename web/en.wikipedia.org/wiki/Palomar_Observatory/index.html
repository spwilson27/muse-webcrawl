Palomar Observatory is an astronomical research observatory in San Diego County, California, in the Palomar Mountain Range. It is owned and operated by the California Institute of Technology (Caltech). The observatory was conceived by astronomer George Ellery Hale, whose 1928 grant from the Rockefeller Foundation funded its construction.

Telescopes

The observatory's centerpiece is the Hale Telescope, a 200-inch (5.1 m) reflecting telescope named for Hale, which saw first light in 1948 (dedicated 1949) and was the largest effective telescope in the world until 1976. Its 200-inch Pyrex mirror was a landmark of optical engineering. Other instruments include the 48-inch Samuel Oschin Schmidt telescope, famous for the Palomar Observatory Sky Survey (POSS) photographic atlases and now equipped for the Zwicky Transient Facility; the 60-inch telescope; and the Palomar Testbed Interferometer (decommissioned).

Research

Palomar instruments have been central to discoveries ranging from supernova surveys and the mapping of large-scale cosmic structure to the identification of quasars' cosmological distances and the discovery of dwarf planet Eris and the distant object Sedna using the Samuel Oschin telescope. The Zwicky Transient Facility, begun in 2018, scans the northern sky every few nights for transient events such as supernovae and asteroids.

History

Hale selected the Palomar site for its clear, dark skies above coastal fog. Construction proceeded through the 1930s despite the challenge of casting and polishing the great mirror and transporting it by train and truck up the mountain. The observatory remains an active research facility and a popular visitor destination, though growing light pollution from Southern California cities is mitigated by local lighting ordinances.
