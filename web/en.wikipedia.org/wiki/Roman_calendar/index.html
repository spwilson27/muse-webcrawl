Roman calendar

The Roman calendar was the calendar used by the Roman Kingdom and Roman Republic. Although the term often includes the Julian calendar established by Julius Caesar in 46 BC, the earlier Republican calendar was a lunisolar system of 355 days in twelve months, kept in step with the seasons by irregular insertion of an intercalary month, Mercedonius. Its scrambled state by the late Republic was a byword for confusion before Caesar's reform.

Structure

Legend credited Romulus with a ten-month calendar beginning in March, and Numa Pompilius with adding January and February. The Republican year had months of 29 or 31 days, with February at 28. Days were counted backwards, inclusively, from three fixed points: the Kalends (1st), Nones (5th or 7th) and Ides (13th or 15th) — the origin of the English word "calendar" (from kalendae). Days were classed as fasti (lawful for public business) or nefasti, and marked with a cycle of market days (nundinae) every eight days.

Intercalation and reform

The pontifices were responsible for inserting Mercedonius after 23 February in some years, but they manipulated intercalation for political advantage — lengthening allies' terms of office or tax contracts — so that by the 40s BC the calendar was months adrift from the seasons. Caesar, as pontifex maximus, reformed it with the help of the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes: a 365-day year with a leap day every four years, after the extraordinary 445-day "year of confusion" (46 BC).

Legacy

The Julian calendar, with month names and lengths largely inherited from the Republican system, remained in use in Europe for over 1,600 years, and its successor, the Gregorian calendar, still uses Roman month names such as September ("seventh") and October ("eighth"), fossils of the old March-start year.
