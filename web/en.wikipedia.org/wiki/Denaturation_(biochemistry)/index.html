Denaturation (biochemistry)

Denaturation

AffectsProteins and nucleic acids

Denaturation is a process in which proteins or nucleic acids lose the quaternary, tertiary and secondary structure present in their native state, when exposed to an external stress or compound such as a strong acid or base, a concentrated inorganic salt, an organic solvent, agitation, radiation or heat. If proteins in a living cell are denatured, cell activity is disrupted and the cell may die. Denatured proteins can exhibit a wide range of properties, from conformational change and loss of solubility to aggregation, because the hydrophobic groups normally buried in the interior become exposed.

Protein denaturation

A protein's function depends on its three-dimensional fold. Denaturation unfolds the chain without breaking peptide bonds, so the primary structure — the amino acid sequence — remains intact while biological activity is lost. Everyday examples include egg white, in which the albumin proteins turn from clear and runny to white and firm when cooked, and the curdling of milk by acid. Christian Anfinsen's classic experiments with ribonuclease A showed that, under suitable conditions, denaturation by urea could be reversed: when the denaturant was removed the protein spontaneously refolded into its active form, demonstrating that the information for folding resides in the amino acid sequence — a conclusion known as Anfinsen's dogma. In many cases, however, denaturation is effectively irreversible because unfolded chains aggregate.

Nucleic acid denaturation

Denaturation of nucleic acids, such as DNA, occurs when the hydrogen bonds between base pairs break and the double helix separates into single strands — commonly called melting. It can be induced by high temperature, extremes of pH, or low salt concentration. The melting temperature (Tm) at which half the DNA is denatured depends on GC content, since G–C pairs have three hydrogen bonds. Denaturation and its reversal (renaturation or annealing) underpin the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), Southern and Northern blotting, and DNA sequencing library preparation.

Denaturants and applications

Common laboratory denaturants include urea and guanidinium chloride (chaotropic agents), the detergent SDS used in SDS-PAGE, and organic solvents. Industrially, denaturation is exploited in food processing, in the production of denatured alcohol (ethanol made undrinkable by additives), in permanent hair waving, and in disinfection, since heat and alcohol kill microbes largely by denaturing their proteins. Protein denaturation in the human body is normally lethal at sustained high fevers, and enzymes cease to function when the pH or temperature departs far from physiological ranges.
