Squid

Scientific classification
Kingdom:Animalia
Phylum:Mollusca
Class:Cephalopoda
Superorder:Decapodiformes
Order:Teuthida

A bigfin reef squid, Sepioteuthis sepioidea complex.

A squid is a mollusc with an elongated soft body, large eyes, eight arms, and two tentacles in the superorder Decapodiformes, though many other molluscs within the broader Neocoleoidea are also called squid. Like all other cephalopods, squid have a distinct head, bilateral symmetry, and a mantle. They are mainly soft-bodied, like octopuses, but have a small internal skeleton in the form of a rod-like gladius or pen, made of chitin.

Anatomy

Squid swim by jet propulsion, drawing water into the mantle cavity and expelling it through a siphon. Two fins along the mantle aid steering and slow swimming. The arms bear suckers, and the two longer feeding tentacles end in clubs used to seize prey and draw it to a horny beak. Squid have three hearts, blue copper-based blood, and a highly developed nervous system with a large brain. Their skin contains chromatophores that allow rapid colour change for camouflage and communication, and many species are bioluminescent.

Size and diversity

About 300 species are known, ranging from tiny pygmy squid a few centimetres long to the giant squid (Architeuthis dux) and colossal squid (Mesonychoteuthis hamiltoni), which can exceed 10 metres in total length and possess the largest eyes in the animal kingdom. Squid are abundant in all oceans, from surface waters to the deep sea, and are a crucial food source for fish, seabirds, marine mammals, and humans.

As food

Squid are an important commercial fishery worldwide, eaten as calamari, sushi, and in Mediterranean and Asian cuisines. Major fisheries target species such as the Argentine shortfin squid, the Humboldt squid, and various loliginids.
