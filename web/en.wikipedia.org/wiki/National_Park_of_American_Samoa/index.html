National Park of American Samoa

The National Park of American Samoa is a national park in the United States territory of American Samoa, distributed on three islands: Tutuila, Ofu, and Taʻū. It preserves and protects coral reefs, tropical rainforest, and Samoan culture. Authorized by Congress in 1988 and formally established in 1993 after lease agreements were concluded with Samoan villages, the park covers about 13,500 acres (5,500 ha), of which roughly 8,000 acres are offshore waters.

Land arrangements

Uniquely among U.S. national parks, the land is not owned by the federal government. Because nearly all land in American Samoa is communally owned by villages, the National Park Service leases the park lands from village councils under 50-year agreements, respecting the Samoan way of life, fa'a Samoa.

Natural features

The park protects some of the most pristine coral reefs in the United States, including the large coral colonies (Porites) of Taʻū, lowland and montane rainforest, and habitat for flying foxes (fruit bats), which play a vital role as pollinators and seed dispersers in Samoan forests. Pago Pago Harbor, adjacent to the Tutuila unit, is one of the deepest natural harbours in the South Pacific.

Culture and visiting

Samoan villages within and near the park maintain traditional customs, and visitors are encouraged to observe village protocols. The park is remote — American Samoa lies about 2,600 miles southwest of Hawaii — and annual visitation is only a few thousand people, making it one of the least-visited U.S. national parks.
