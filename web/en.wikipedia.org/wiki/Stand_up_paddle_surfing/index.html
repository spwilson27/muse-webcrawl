Stand up paddle surfing

Stand up paddle surfing or stand up paddleboarding (SUP) is an offshoot of surfing, in which the participant stands on a floating board and uses a long-shafted paddle to propel themselves through the water. It can be practised in the ocean — including riding waves — on lakes, rivers and canals, and is one of the fastest-growing water sports of the 21st century.

History

Standing and paddling on a board has ancient roots in several cultures, including Peruvian reed craft, Polynesian voyaging and standing gondoliers. The modern sport began in Hawaii, where surfers at Waikiki — the "Beach Boys" — stood on large boards and used outrigger canoe paddles to position themselves to photograph tourists. In the early 2000s, big-wave surfer Laird Hamilton popularised stand up paddle surfing on oversized boards, and the sport spread from Hawaii to California and worldwide, expanding beyond surf into flatwater recreation, racing, touring, yoga and whitewater disciplines.

Equipment

  • Board: longer and wider than a standard surfboard, typically 9–12 feet; solid epoxy or inflatable construction
  • Paddle: a single-bladed paddle with a bent shaft, sized so the handle reaches roughly wrist height with the arm raised
  • Leash and flotation: a coiled or straight leash is standard in surf; life jackets may be legally required on flat water in some jurisdictions

Disciplines

SUP surfing, flatwater touring and fitness paddling, downwind paddling, river running, and competitive racing over sprint and distance courses. Governing bodies have included the International Surfing Association, which sanctions SUP world championships.
