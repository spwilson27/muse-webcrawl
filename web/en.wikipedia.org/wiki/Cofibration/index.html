Cofibration

In mathematics, particularly homotopy theory, a cofibration is a map between topological spaces that satisfies the homotopy extension property (HEP): given a map defined on the subspace and a homotopy starting at its restriction, the homotopy can be extended over the whole space. Cofibrations are one of the three classes of maps — together with fibrations and weak equivalences — in a Quillen model category, such as the standard model structure on topological spaces.

Definition

A map i : A → X is a cofibration if, for every topological space Y, every map f : X → Y and every homotopy H : A × [0,1] → Y with H(a, 0) = f(i(a)), there exists an extension of H to a homotopy X × [0,1] → Y starting at f. Intuitively, what happens on the subspace cannot obstruct deformations defined on the whole space.

Examples

The inclusion of a subcomplex into a CW complex is a cofibration, as is the inclusion of the boundary sphere into a disc, and the inclusion X → X × [0,1] embedding a space as the bottom of its cylinder. For well-behaved (cofibrant) spaces — for example, CW complexes — maps out of them behave homotopically as expected, which is why homotopy theorists often replace arbitrary spaces by cofibrant approximations. The mapping cylinder construction factors any map into a cofibration followed by a homotopy equivalence.

Relation to model categories

Daniel Quillen's axiomatisation puts cofibrations at the centre of abstract homotopy theory: in a model category, cofibrations are the maps with the left lifting property against trivial fibrations. In the standard model structure on spaces, the cofibrations are retracts of relative cell complexes, and every object obtained by attaching cells is cofibrant. Dualising the definition (replacing extension by lifting) gives the notion of a fibration, defined by the homotopy lifting property, of which Serre and Hurewicz fibrations are the classical examples.
