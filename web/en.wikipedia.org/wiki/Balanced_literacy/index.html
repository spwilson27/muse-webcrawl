Balanced literacy

Balanced literacy is an approach to reading and writing instruction that combines elements of whole language and phonics. Emerging in the 1990s, notably through the work of educators such as Irene Fountas and Gay Su Pinnell and the Reading Recovery tradition of Marie Clay, it became the dominant model in many North American school districts. It seeks a middle path in the "reading wars" between meaning-centred and code-centred instruction.

Components

A balanced literacy classroom typically includes read-alouds, shared reading, guided reading in small groups using leveled texts matched to each child's reading level, independent reading, word study, and a writers' workshop modelled on the process approach to writing. Teachers use running records to assess progress through text levels. A central—and controversial—technique is the three-cueing system, which encourages beginning readers to use meaning, sentence structure, and partial visual information to identify words.

Criticism and decline

From the 2010s, advocates of the "science of reading" argued that balanced literacy under-emphasised systematic phonics and that three-cueing teaches guessing strategies inconsistent with research on skilled reading. Investigations such as the 2022 podcast Sold a Story intensified scrutiny of prominent balanced-literacy programmes. Many U.S. states have since passed laws mandating evidence-based reading instruction, and major districts, including New York City in 2023, moved away from balanced literacy units toward structured phonics-based curricula. Supporters respond that the approach was often implemented unevenly and that its emphasis on rich texts and writing remains valuable.
