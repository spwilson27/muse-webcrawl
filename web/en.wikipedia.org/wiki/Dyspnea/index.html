Dyspnea (from Latin dyspnoea, from Greek dyspnoia, "disordered breathing"), also known as shortness of breath or breathlessness, is the subjective sensation of difficulty or discomfort in breathing. It is a symptom, not a disease in itself, and can arise from disorders of the lungs, heart, airways, blood or metabolism, as well as from anxiety and deconditioning.

Normal breathing is largely unconscious; dyspnea occurs when the demand for ventilation is mismatched with the body's ability to respond, or when respiratory sensations are amplified. Its severity is commonly graded with scales such as the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale or the Borg scale.

Causes

The most frequent causes of acute dyspnea include asthma exacerbation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pneumonia, heart failure with pulmonary edema, pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism and acute coronary syndromes. Chronic dyspnea is most often due to asthma, COPD, interstitial lung disease, heart failure, obesity and physical deconditioning. Other causes include anemia, hyperthyroidism, metabolic acidosis, neuromuscular weakness, upper-airway obstruction and panic attacks.

Mechanism

Dyspnea is thought to arise when afferent signals from chemoreceptors, mechanoreceptors in the lungs and chest wall, and the respiratory muscles conflict with the motor command sent to breathe — a phenomenon sometimes called "air hunger" or neuromechanical dissociation. Carbon dioxide retention, hypoxemia and increased work of breathing all intensify the sensation.

Evaluation

Assessment begins with vital signs and pulse oximetry, followed by a history and examination directed at distinguishing cardiac from pulmonary causes. Investigations may include chest radiography, electrocardiography, blood tests (including natriuretic peptides and D-dimer), arterial blood gas analysis, spirometry and echocardiography. Related terms include orthopnea (breathlessness when lying flat), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, platypnea (worse when upright) and trepopnea (worse lying on one side).

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying cause: bronchodilators for obstructive airway disease, diuretics for heart failure, antibiotics for pneumonia and anticoagulation for pulmonary embolism. Supplemental oxygen is given when blood oxygen is low. For refractory breathlessness in advanced disease, opioids at low doses, fan therapy directed at the face, pulmonary rehabilitation and breathing techniques can provide relief.
