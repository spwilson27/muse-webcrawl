Expansion card

In computing, an expansion card (also called an expansion board, adapter card, or accessory card) is a printed circuit board that can be inserted into an electrical connector, or expansion slot, on a computer motherboard to add functionality to a computer system. One edge of the card holds the contacts (the edge connector or pin header) that fit exactly into the slot, establishing contact between the card's electronics and the motherboard's bus.

Purpose

Expansion cards allow a computer's capabilities to be extended or upgraded without replacing the whole system. Common categories include:

  • Graphics cards (GPUs) — accelerate rendering and computation.
  • Sound cards — provide audio input and output beyond integrated audio.
  • Network interface cards — add Ethernet, Wi-Fi, or other networking.
  • Storage controllers — add SATA, SAS, RAID, or NVMe connectivity.
  • TV tuner cards and video capture cards — receive and record video signals.
  • USB and serial/parallel port cards — add additional ports.

History and standards

Early microcomputers such as the Altair 8800 (S-100 bus) and the Apple II popularized expansion slots. The IBM PC introduced the ISA bus, followed by MCA, EISA, VESA Local Bus, PCI, AGP (for graphics), and finally PCI Express (PCIe), which became the dominant standard in the 2000s. Laptop computers used smaller form factors such as PCMCIA/CardBus and ExpressCard, and later internal M.2 and Mini PCIe slots. The number of available slots and their bandwidth constrain how many cards a system can support.

Form factors

Desktop expansion cards are built to standardized heights and lengths; low-profile cards fit slim cases using shorter brackets. Cards may require auxiliary power connectors from the power supply, especially high-performance graphics cards, and large cards are sometimes supported by retention brackets to prevent flexing or damage to the slot.

Modern trends

As more functions — audio, networking, graphics, storage control — have been integrated onto motherboards and processors, the need for expansion cards has declined for typical users. Expansion slots remain important for professional workstations, servers, gaming systems, and specialized applications such as data acquisition, industrial control, and high-speed networking.
