Prudentius

Aurelius Prudentius Clemens (348 – c. 413 AD) was a Roman Christian poet, born in the province of Tarraconensis in northern Spain (probably at Calagurris, modern Calahorra). He is generally considered the greatest of the early Christian Latin poets, and his allegorical poem the Psychomachia was one of the most influential works of the Middle Ages.

Life

Prudentius practised law and rose in the imperial administration, serving at the court of the emperor Theodosius I before retiring around the age of 57 to devote himself to religious poetry. In the preface to his collected works, written in 405, he resolved to spend his remaining years composing hymns and poems in praise of God and against paganism.

Works

  • Psychomachia ("Battle of the Soul") — the first fully personification allegory in Western literature, in which the Virtues battle the Vices for the human soul; it profoundly influenced medieval allegory and art.
  • Cathemerinon — twelve hymns for daily prayer and festivals, some still sung today.
  • Peristephanon — fourteen poems in honour of Spanish and Roman martyrs.
  • Contra Symmachum — a polemic against the pagan senator Symmachus, defending the removal of the Altar of Victory from the Roman Senate.
  • Apotheosis and Hamartigenia — doctrinal poems against heresies and on the origin of sin.
  • Dittochaeon — 49 four-line epigrams on biblical scenes, possibly inscriptions for church paintings.

Legacy

Prudentius modelled his metres on Horace and Virgil while filling them with Christian content. His works were copied extensively throughout the Middle Ages — over 300 manuscripts survive — and the Psychomachia's framework of Virtues and Vices shaped medieval literature from the morality plays to later allegories.
