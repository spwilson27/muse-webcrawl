Cataphract

A cataphract was a form of armoured heavy cavalryman found in much of Eurasia from antiquity through the late Middle Ages. The word derives from the Greek kataphraktos, "completely enclosed [in armour]". Rider and horse were both covered in scale or lamellar armour, and the cataphract typically fought as a shock cavalryman armed with a long lance (the kontos), charging in dense formation to break enemy infantry.

Origins and spread

Heavily armoured cavalry first developed among the Iranian peoples of the Central Asian steppe — Massagetae, Sarmatians and Alans — and was perfected by the Parthian Empire, whose cataphracts, combined with horse archers, destroyed a Roman army at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BC. The Sasanian Empire inherited the tradition, and Rome adopted cataphract units (notably the clibanarii) from the 2nd century AD onward. In the Byzantine Empire the cataphract became the decisive battlefield arm, described in the military manuals of Maurice and Leo VI, and remained important until the Komnenian period.

Equipment and tactics

The rider wore a helmet and corselet of scale, lamellar or chain mail, often with arm and leg defences; the horse wore a Chamfron and body armour of fabric, leather or metal scales. The principal weapon was the long two-handed kontos; bows, swords and maces served as secondary arms. Cataphracts ideally charged after enemy formations had been disordered by archery or infantry action, the weight of the armoured mass delivering an irresistible blow.
