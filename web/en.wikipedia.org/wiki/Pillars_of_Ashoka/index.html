Pillars of Ashoka

The Pillars of Ashoka are a series of monolithic columns dispersed throughout the Indian subcontinent, erected or at least inscribed with edicts by the Mauryan emperor Ashoka during his reign, approximately 268 to 232 BCE. The pillars, often 12 to 15 metres (40 to 50 feet) high and weighing up to 50 tons, carry some of the earliest decipherable inscriptions of India, proclaiming Ashoka's policies of dhamma.

Construction and capitals

Each pillar consists of a single piece of buff-coloured sandstone, quarried at Chunar and given a lustrous polish characteristic of Mauryan art. The pillars are topped by sculpted capitals — lions, bulls, elephants, and the wheel (dharmachakra) — carved from a single block. The most famous capital, the four lions of Sarnath, was adopted as the national emblem of India in 1950.

Inscriptions

The pillar edicts and minor pillar edicts are written mainly in Prakrit in the Brahmi script (and in the northwest in Greek and Aramaic). They exhort subjects to respect parents and elders, show kindness to servants and animals, practise tolerance among religious sects, and avoid violence. The decipherment of Brahmi by James Prinsep in 1837, largely through these inscriptions, founded the modern study of early Indian history.
