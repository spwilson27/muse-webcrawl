Interleukin

Interleukins are cytokines that mediate communication among leukocytes and other cells.

Interleukins (ILs) are a group of cytokines (secreted proteins and signal molecules) that are expressed and secreted by white blood cells (leukocytes) as well as some other body cells. They mediate communication between cells of the immune system, regulating cell growth, differentiation, and motility, and are particularly important in stimulating immune responses such as inflammation.

Nomenclature

The name "interleukin" was chosen in 1979 to describe molecules that act as messengers between leukocytes. Human interleukins are numbered in the order of their discovery, from IL-1 onward; more than three dozen have been designated, although some, such as IL-14 and IL-16, were later found to be unrelated molecules or renamed. Many interleukins are grouped into families based on shared receptor subunits and structural features, such as the IL-1 family, the IL-6 family, and the common gamma chain family (IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-9, IL-15, IL-21).

Clinical significance

Dysregulated interleukin production contributes to autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, allergies, and immunodeficiency. Interleukins and their receptors are important drug targets: recombinant IL-2 (aldesleukin) has been used in cancer immunotherapy, while monoclonal antibodies blocking interleukin signalling — such as tocilizumab against the IL-6 receptor and dupilumab against the IL-4 receptor alpha chain — are used in rheumatoid arthritis, atopic dermatitis, asthma, and other conditions. IL-1 blockade (anakinra, canakinumab) is used in autoinflammatory syndromes.
