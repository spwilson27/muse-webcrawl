Pannonian Avars

The Pannonian Avars were an alliance of nomadic peoples of mixed origin who established the Avar Khaganate, a powerful state centred on the Pannonian Basin (in present-day Hungary), between the late 6th and early 9th centuries. At its height the khaganate dominated the Carpathian Basin and exacted tribute from the Byzantine Empire, playing a central role in the history of early medieval Europe and in the settlement of Slavic peoples in the Balkans.

Origins and arrival in Europe

The Avars appeared on the Byzantine frontier in 557–558, sending an embassy to Constantinople. Their identity is debated: Byzantine authors connected them with the Rouran Khaganate of Mongolia, destroyed by the Göktürks in the 550s, though the European Avars were probably a composite group that absorbed many peoples during their westward migration. Under the khagan Bayan I they allied with the Lombards to destroy the Gepid kingdom in 567, then occupied the whole Carpathian Basin when the Lombards departed for Italy in 568.

The khaganate

From their fortified centre — the "Ring" of the Avars, a system of camps in the Hungarian plain — the khagans raided the Balkans and extracted enormous tributes of gold from Byzantium. Together with Slavic allies and subjects, Avar armies besieged Constantinople in 626 in coordination with Sassanid Persia; the failure of that siege marked the peak of Avar power abroad. Slavic settlers spread through the Balkans under and beyond Avar rule during this period.

Decline and fall

Internal revolts, including the uprising of the Slavic merchant Samo (623–658), and Bulgar secession weakened the khaganate. In the 790s Charlemagne's Frankish armies, with Lombard and Slav allies, destroyed the Avar Ring and seized its accumulated treasure; the khaganate disintegrated, and the remaining Avars were absorbed into the Frankish, Bulgar and Slavic worlds. Avars last appear in sources in the early 9th century.

Legacy

Avar-period archaeology in Hungary is rich — tens of thousands of graves with distinctive belt fittings, stirrups (which the Avars may have introduced to Europe) and horse burials. The modern Avars of Dagestan in the Caucasus are a distinct people and are not generally considered descendants of the Pannonian Avars.
