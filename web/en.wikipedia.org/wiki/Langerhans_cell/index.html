Langerhans cell

Langerhans cells (LCs) are tissue-resident macrophages of the skin that form a dense network in the epidermis. They are dendritic, antigen-presenting cells that play a central role in cutaneous immune responses, sampling antigens that penetrate the skin barrier and migrating to draining lymph nodes to activate T cells. They are named after the German physician Paul Langerhans, who first described them in 1868 while still a medical student.

Characteristics

Langerhans cells are characterized by the expression of the C-type lectin langerin (CD207) and by the presence of distinctive cytoplasmic organelles called Birbeck granules, which appear under the electron microscope as rod- or tennis-racket-shaped structures. Birbeck granules are involved in the endocytic processing of antigens captured by langerin. LCs also express CD1a and high levels of MHC class II molecules.

Development

Unlike most dendritic cells, epidermal Langerhans cells are largely derived from embryonic precursors—yolk-sac macrophages and fetal-liver monocytes—that seed the skin before birth and maintain themselves by local self-renewal throughout life. After severe inflammation or injury, bone-marrow-derived monocytes can replenish the population.

Function

Langerhans cells continuously extend and retract their dendrites between keratinocytes to survey the environment for pathogens and damage. Upon capturing antigen and receiving activation signals, they mature, upregulate chemokine receptors such as CCR7, and migrate via afferent lymphatics to skin-draining lymph nodes, where they present antigen to naive T cells. Depending on the context, they can promote immunity or contribute to tolerance; they are implicated in allergic contact dermatitis, in the skin's defence against viruses such as HIV, and in graft-versus-host disease.

Clinical significance

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare disorder involving the clonal proliferation of cells with Langerhans-like features, often driven by activating mutations in the MAPK pathway such as BRAF V600E. LCH can affect a single organ, commonly bone or skin, or present as multisystem disease. Langerhans cells are also studied in the context of psoriasis, atopic dermatitis and skin cancer immunotherapy.
