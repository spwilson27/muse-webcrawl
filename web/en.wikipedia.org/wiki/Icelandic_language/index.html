Icelandic (Icelandic: íslenska) is a North Germanic language spoken by about 350,000 people, mostly in Iceland, where it is the national and official language. It is most closely related to Faroese and the western dialects of Norwegian, and descends from the Old Norse spoken by Norse settlers who colonised Iceland in the ninth and tenth centuries.

History

Icelandic has changed remarkably little in its written form over a millennium: modern Icelanders can read the medieval sagas and Eddas with only modest difficulty. While the pronunciation shifted considerably, the conservative spelling and morphology were preserved, partly through deliberate language policy. Icelandic retains a full system of four noun cases and three grammatical genders, lost or reduced in mainland Scandinavian languages.

Alphabet and phonology

The Icelandic alphabet has 32 letters, including the distinctive characters Ð/ð (eth), Þ/þ (thorn), and Æ/æ. Letters such as c, q, w and z do not appear in native words. Vowel length and quality are both phonemic, and the language has preaspiration of stops in certain positions.

Vocabulary and purism

Icelandic is noted for linguistic purism: rather than borrowing foreign words, new terms are coined from native roots. Examples include tölva ("computer", blending tala, number, and völva, prophetess) and sími ("telephone", an old word for thread or cord). The Icelandic Language Council advises on neologisms.

Grammar

Nouns decline in four cases (nominative, accusative, dative, genitive) and have three genders. Verbs conjugate for person, number, tense, mood and voice. Word order is typically subject–verb–object, with the flexibility case marking allows.
