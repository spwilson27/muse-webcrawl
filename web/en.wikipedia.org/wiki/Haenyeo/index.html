Haenyeo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The haenyeo (Korean: 해녀, "sea women") are female divers of Jeju Island, South Korea, who harvest seafood by freediving, without breathing apparatus. For centuries the haenyeo have gathered abalone, conch, sea urchin, sea cucumber and octopus from the seabed, working in a tradition passed from mothers to daughters that shaped Jeju's distinctive semi-matriarchal family structure, in which women were often the main breadwinners.

The work

Haenyeo train from childhood, typically beginning around age eleven and becoming fully accomplished divers by eighteen. Working in groups for safety, they dive to depths of roughly 10 metres and stay submerged for up to several minutes at a time, surfacing with a characteristic whistling breath known as sumbisori. Each diver carries a round float (tewak) with a net for the catch, along with simple tools such as a weeding hoe and a knife. In the past they wore white cotton suits; since the 1970s these have been replaced by rubber wetsuits.

History and decline

Diving for seafood in Korea is ancient, but records of women outnumbering male divers (haenam) appear from the seventeenth century, and by the eighteenth century women dominated the trade, in part because men were drawn away by war, boat fishing or corvée labour and because women's bodies, with greater fat reserves, tolerate cold water better. Under Japanese rule many haenyeo resisted colonial exploitation, including a major protest in 1932. In the 1960s there were some 23,000 haenyeo; as educational and economic opportunities for women expanded, recruitment collapsed, and by the 2010s only a few thousand remained, most of them over sixty years old.

Recognition

In 2016 the "Culture of Jeju Haenyeo" was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising the divers' contribution to women's status, community identity and sustainable, low-impact fishing. The Haenyeo Museum in Jeju documents the tradition.
