Western (genre)

[Image: A Western film still — a lone rider on horseback crossing a desert valley beneath towering red rock buttes.]

The Western is a genre of fiction typically set in the American frontier ("the Old West") during the second half of the nineteenth century, featuring cowboys, outlaws, lawmen, Native Americans, cattle drives, gunfights and the conflict between wilderness and civilisation. It is one of the defining genres of American popular culture, flourishing in novels, film and television.

History

The Western grew from dime novels, Wild West shows and the frontier fiction of Owen Wister (The Virginian, 1902), Zane Grey and later Louis L'Amour. In cinema it is as old as the medium itself: The Great Train Robbery (1903) was an early hit. John Ford's Stagecoach (1939) elevated the genre artistically and made John Wayne its emblematic star; the 1940s and 1950s brought psychological and morally ambiguous classics — Red River, High Noon, Shane, The Searchers — and television series such as Gunsmoke and Bonanza.

Later developments

In the 1960s Sergio Leone's Italian-made "Spaghetti Westerns" with Clint Eastwood stripped the genre to a stylised, violent minimalism. The "revisionist Westerns" of the late 1960s and 1970s — The Wild Bunch, McCabe & Mrs. Miller — questioned the founding myths, a line continued by Unforgiven (1992) and later neo-Westerns such as No Country for Old Men. Themes of honour, justice outside the law, and the closing frontier continue to be reworked across media, including video games.
