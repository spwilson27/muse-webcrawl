Co-determination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Co-determination gives employees a formal voice in company governance, most developed in Germany (Mitbestimmung).

Co-determination (German: Mitbestimmung) is the right of a company's workers to participate in the management of that company through elected representatives. It is most fully developed in Germany, where by law employees elect a substantial share of the members of the supervisory boards of large companies, and through works councils (Betriebsräte) that hold consultation and veto rights over workplace matters.

German model

The modern system grew out of the Works Councils Act of 1920, post-1945 arrangements in the British occupation zone, and the Coal and Steel Co-determination Act of 1951, which granted parity representation in coal and steel firms. The Co-determination Act of 1976 (Mitbestimmungsgesetz) extended near-parity to companies with more than 2,000 employees: half of supervisory board seats go to employee representatives, though the shareholder-elected chair holds a casting vote. Firms with 500–2,000 employees give workers one-third of seats. Works councils, elected by all employees, negotiate working hours, overtime, holidays, and workplace rules, though they generally may not call strikes.

Elsewhere

Forms of co-determination exist across much of the European Union; EU law requires employee board representation in European Companies (SEs) where it existed before. Sweden, Austria, Denmark, and the Netherlands have significant worker board representation. In the United States and United Kingdom the model is rare, and proposals to adopt it periodically surface in political debate.

Debate

Supporters argue co-determination produces more stable firms, longer investment horizons, better training, and smoother restructuring. Critics argue it can entrench insiders, slow decision-making, and deter capital. Empirical studies generally find modest effects—slightly lower shareholder returns in some periods, offset by productivity and employment stability gains.
