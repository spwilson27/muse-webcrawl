A beehive hut, known in Irish as a clochán, is a dry-stone hut with a corbelled roof, found mainly in the southwest of Ireland. The huts take their English name from their resemblance to an old-fashioned skep beehive. They are among the most distinctive survivals of early medieval Irish building.

Construction

Beehive huts are built entirely without mortar. Successive courses of flat stones are laid in rings, each course projecting slightly inward over the one below — a technique called corbelling — until the rings converge at a capstone at the top. The resulting walls are very thick and shed rain effectively, since water runs down the stepped exterior face. Most huts are circular in plan, stand 2–5 metres high, and have a single low doorway with a stone lintel; some have small windows or internal wall niches.

History and distribution

The finest examples are the monastic cells on Skellig Michael, a rocky island off County Kerry settled by monks probably between the 6th and 8th centuries and now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Groups of clocháns also survive on the Dingle Peninsula, notably near Fahan, where several hundred have been recorded. Dating is difficult because the dry-stone technique changed little over centuries; huts were used as dwellings, monastic cells, hermitages, and later as stores and animal shelters. Similar corbelled structures occur elsewhere in Atlantic Europe, such as the bories of Provence and the trulli of southern Italy.
