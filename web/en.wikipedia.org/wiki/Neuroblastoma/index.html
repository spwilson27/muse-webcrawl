Neuroblastoma

SpecialtyPaediatric oncology
Usual onsetUnder 5 years of age
TypesLow, intermediate and high risk
CausesGenetic mutations in developing nerve tissue
PrognosisVaries widely by risk group

Neuroblastoma is a type of cancer that forms in certain types of nerve tissue. It most frequently starts from one of the adrenal glands but can also develop in the head, neck, chest, abdomen, or spine. The cancer forms from neural crest cells and is overwhelmingly a disease of early childhood: it is the most common cancer in the first year of life and the most common extracranial solid tumour of childhood.

Symptoms may include a lump or mass in the abdomen, neck, or chest, bone pain, bulging eyes, dark circles around the eyes ("raccoon eyes"), and, when the tumour presses on the spinal cord, weakness or paralysis. About half of cases have already spread (metastasised) at the time of diagnosis, most often to the lymph nodes, liver, bones, and bone marrow.

Causes and biology

Neuroblastoma arises from immature nerve cells (neuroblasts) of the sympathetic nervous system that fail to differentiate normally. In a small minority of cases the condition is inherited, usually through mutations in the ALK or PHOX2B genes. The most important biological marker is amplification of the MYCN oncogene, which is associated with aggressive behaviour and a poorer prognosis. Tumours in infants may also show hyperdiploidy, a favourable feature, whereas deletions of chromosome arms 1p and 11q are unfavourable.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by biopsy of the tumour together with measurement of catecholamine breakdown products (vanillylmandelic acid and homovanillic acid) in the urine, which are elevated in most patients. Imaging with CT, MRI, and MIBG scans defines the extent of disease, and bone marrow aspirates and biopsies check for spread. Staging uses the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS), and patients are stratified into low, intermediate, and high risk groups based on age, stage, histology, and genetic features.

Treatment and prognosis

Low-risk tumours may be observed without treatment or removed by surgery alone, and some tumours in infants regress spontaneously — a striking feature of this disease, exemplified by stage 4S disease in babies, where even widespread spread to the liver, skin, and bone marrow often resolves on its own. Intermediate-risk disease is treated with moderate chemotherapy. High-risk neuroblastoma requires intensive multimodal therapy: induction chemotherapy, surgical resection, high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell transplant, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy with anti-GD2 antibodies such as dinutuximab, together with the differentiating agent isotretinoin.

Survival exceeds 95% for low-risk disease but historically was below 50% for high-risk disease, though outcomes have improved with modern immunotherapy. See also neurotoxin and neurodegenerative disease for unrelated topics in neuroscience.
