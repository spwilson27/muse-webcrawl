Spandrel

A spandrel is the roughly triangular space between the top of an arch and a rectangular frame enclosing it, or the space between two adjacent arches in an arcade. In architecture there are several related uses of the term, including the surface between the extrados of an arch and the mouldings above it, and the triangular areas formed where a dome on pendentives meets its supporting structure.

Architecture

Spandrels became prominent decorative fields in Gothic, Renaissance, and Baroque architecture, where they were filled with carving, painting, inscriptions, or roundels. The spandrels of the pendentives beneath the dome of St Mark's Basilica in Venice, famously used by Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin as an analogy in evolutionary biology, are decorated with mosaics of the four evangelists. In modern curtain-wall construction, "spandrel" also denotes the opaque panel area of a building façade between floors, concealing slabs, beams, and services.

Evolutionary biology

In their 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm", Gould and Lewontin criticized adaptationist reasoning in evolutionary biology, arguing that some traits are non-adaptive byproducts — "spandrels" — of architectural or developmental constraints, rather than direct products of natural selection. The term has since entered wide use in debates about adaptation and exaptation.
