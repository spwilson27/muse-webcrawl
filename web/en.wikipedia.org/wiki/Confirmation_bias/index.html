Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for, interpret, favour, and recall information in a way that confirms or supports one's prior beliefs or values. It is a type of cognitive bias and a major subject in psychology, decision science, and the study of misinformation.

Types

  • Biased search — asking one-sided questions and seeking confirmatory evidence (e.g., Peter Wason's 2-4-6 task).
  • Biased interpretation — evaluating the same evidence differently depending on prior views, as in Lord, Ross & Lepper's capital punishment study (1979).
  • Biased memory — selectively recalling schema-consistent information.
  • Belief perseverance and the backfire effect debates in political contexts.

Explanations

Explanations include cognitive ease and positive-test strategies, motivated reasoning (defending identity and desired conclusions), and Bayesian accounts in which limited updating can sometimes be rational given priors — a point of ongoing debate.

Consequences

Confirmation bias affects scientific research (experimenter bias, publication bias), medicine, law (tunnel vision in investigations), investing, and online echo chambers and filter bubbles.

Mitigation

Strategies include considering the opposite, pre-registration, blind analysis, red-teaming, structured analytic techniques, and devil's advocacy.
