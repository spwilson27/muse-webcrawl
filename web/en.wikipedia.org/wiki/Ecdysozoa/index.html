The Ecdysozoa are a superphylum of protostome animals united by a shared mode of growth: they periodically shed their outer cuticle in a process called ecdysis (moulting), controlled by steroid hormones called ecdysteroids. The group includes the arthropods (insects, spiders, crustaceans and their kin) — the most species-rich phylum on Earth — and the nematodes (roundworms), as well as the velvet worms (Onychophora), tardigrades, horsehair worms (Nematomorpha), priapulids, kinorhynchs and loriciferans. Together the ecdysozoans comprise the majority of described animal species.

Evidence and history

The clade was proposed by Anna Marie Aguinaldo and colleagues in 1997 on the basis of 18S ribosomal RNA sequences, which grouped arthropods with nematodes rather than with annelids — overturning the older "Articulata" hypothesis that had united arthropods and segmented worms. Subsequent molecular phylogenies and shared features of the moulting machinery have broadly supported the grouping, though some early opposition came from morphologists who doubted the RNA trees. A morphological synapomorphy is the three-layered cuticle containing chitin (in most groups) and the loss of locomotory cilia.

Classification

Ecdysozoa is usually divided into two great assemblages:

  • Scalidophora — Priapulida, Kinorhyncha and Loricifera, with an eversible, spine-bearing introvert.
  • Nematoida — Nematoda and Nematomorpha, long unsegmented worms with a collagenous cuticle.
  • Panarthropoda — Onychophora, Tardigrada and Arthropoda, animals with paired, jointed or lobopod appendages and a chitinous cuticle.

Panarthropoda contains by far the greatest diversity; arthropods alone have well over a million described species, and nematodes may rival them in total numbers. Fossil ecdysozoans, including lobopodians and priapulid-like worms, are prominent in Cambrian deposits such as the Burgess Shale, indicating that the group's body plans diversified early in animal evolution.
