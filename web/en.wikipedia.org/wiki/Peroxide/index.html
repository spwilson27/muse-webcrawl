Peroxide

Peroxide
General structureR–O–O–R′
Functional groupO–O single bond
ExamplesHydrogen peroxide, benzoyl peroxide, sodium peroxide
UsesBleaching, disinfection, polymerisation initiators, oxidisers

In chemistry, peroxides are a group of compounds with the structure R−O−O−R′, where R and R′ are hydrogen, organic groups, or metals. The O−O group, the peroxide group or peroxy group, is a divalent −O−O− structure. Aside from hydrogen peroxide, some other major classes of peroxides include peroxy acids, metal peroxides, organic peroxides, and main group peroxides.

Most peroxides are powerful oxidising agents and are widely used as bleaches and disinfectants. The O–O bond is relatively weak (about 146 kJ/mol for the bond dissociation energy in hydrogen peroxide), which makes peroxides reactive and, in concentrated form, potentially explosive.

Properties

The peroxide group contains a single oxygen–oxygen bond. The characteristic O–O bond length is about 1.45 Å, and the R–O–O angles are near 110°. The relatively weak O–O bond readily undergoes homolytic cleavage to give free radicals, a property exploited in radical polymerisation, where peroxides such as benzoyl peroxide serve as initiators.

Hydrogen peroxide

The simplest peroxide is hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a pale blue, slightly more viscous liquid than water. Dilute solutions are used as antiseptics and hair bleaches, while concentrated solutions serve as oxidisers in rocketry and in the production of other chemicals. Hydrogen peroxide is produced industrially principally by the anthraquinone process.

Organic peroxides

Organic peroxides contain carbon bonded to the peroxy group. They include hydroperoxides (R–O–O–H), dialkyl peroxides (R–O–O–R), and peroxyesters. Many are used as radical initiators; others, such as tert-butyl hydroperoxide, are oxidising agents in synthesis. Because of the weak O–O bond, organic peroxides are thermally unstable and are handled with care.

Safety

Concentrated peroxides can decompose violently, releasing oxygen and heat. Mixtures of peroxides with fuels can be explosive. Peroxide-forming solvents such as diethyl ether and tetrahydrofuran must be tested and inhibitor-stabilised during storage.
