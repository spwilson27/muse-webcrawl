Bank War

The Bank War was a political struggle in the United States during the 1830s over the fate of the Second Bank of the United States. President Andrew Jackson, who distrusted the Bank as an unconstitutional and corrupting monopoly, vetoed its recharter in 1832 and withdrew federal deposits in 1833, provoking a fierce contest with the Bank's president, Nicholas Biddle, and congressional supporters such as Henry Clay.

Period1832–1836
Key figuresAndrew Jackson, Nicholas Biddle, Henry Clay
OutcomeSecond Bank's charter expired, 1836

Background

The Second Bank, chartered in 1816 for twenty years, acted as the federal government's fiscal agent and regulated state banks by redeeming their notes. Jacksonian Democrats viewed it as an engine of Eastern financial privilege; its supporters saw it as essential to a stable currency.

The veto and removal of deposits

In 1832 Congress passed a recharter bill four years early, hoping to make the Bank an election issue; Jackson vetoed it with a message asserting presidential authority to judge constitutionality. After re-election he ordered federal deposits removed to selected state banks — the so-called "pet banks" — dismissing two Treasury secretaries before Roger B. Taney carried out the order.

Consequences

The Senate censured Jackson in 1834. The Bank's federal charter lapsed in 1836, and a speculative boom fed by state-bank lending was followed by the Panic of 1837. The fight helped define the emerging Democratic and Whig parties.
