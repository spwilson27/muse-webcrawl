Sex assignment

Sex assignment (sometimes called gender assignment) is the classification of an infant at birth as male or female, usually based on the appearance of the external genitalia, sometimes confirmed prenatally by ultrasound or genetic testing. The assigned sex is recorded on the birth certificate and typically determines the legal sex and the gender in which the child is raised. The phrase "assigned female at birth" (AFAB) and "assigned male at birth" (AMAB) are commonly used in medical, legal, and social contexts.

Process

In most births the genitalia are unambiguous and assignment is immediate. When an infant is born with intersex traits — variations in chromosomes, gonads, hormones, or genital anatomy — assignment may involve a multidisciplinary team and the parents, and practices have changed considerably over time. In the mid-20th century, the prevailing "optimal gender" model associated with psychologist John Money favoured early surgical normalisation and unambiguous rearing; from the 1990s onward, intersex advocacy groups and medical bodies increasingly questioned routine infant surgery, favouring informed consent, delay of irreversible procedures, and involvement of the person concerned.

Relation to gender identity

For most people, gender identity aligns with the sex assigned at birth (cisgender). For transgender people, gender identity differs from the assignment, and many jurisdictions provide procedures for legal gender recognition and amendment of birth records. Sex assignment is therefore distinguished analytically from both biological sex characteristics and from gender identity, though the terms overlap in everyday use.

Legal aspects

Some jurisdictions permit a third or unspecified marker (such as "X") on identity documents, and rules differ widely concerning registration of intersex births, later amendment, and recognition of non-binary identities. Disputes over sex classification in areas such as sport, prisons, and single-sex services have made assignment and its legal consequences the subject of continuing public and judicial debate.
