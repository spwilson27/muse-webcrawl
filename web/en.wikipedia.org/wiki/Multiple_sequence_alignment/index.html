Multiple sequence alignment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A multiple sequence alignment (MSA) is an alignment of three or more biological sequences — protein, DNA or RNA — generally assumed to share an evolutionary relationship. By arranging the sequences in rows and inserting gaps so that homologous residues line up in columns, an MSA reveals conserved regions, motifs and variable positions that hint at structure, function and phylogeny.

Methods

Exact alignment by dynamic programming generalises the pairwise algorithm but becomes computationally infeasible (NP-hard) beyond a handful of sequences, so practical tools rely on heuristics. Progressive alignment methods, such as ClustalW and its successors, first build a guide tree from pairwise distances and then align the most similar sequences progressively. Iterative methods (e.g. MUSCLE, MAFFT) repeatedly refine an initial alignment. Consistency-based methods (e.g. T-Coffee) combine information from many pairwise alignments. Hidden Markov model approaches, implemented in HMMER and used to build the Pfam database of protein families, align sequences against profile models.

Uses

Multiple alignments are used to identify conserved domains and active sites, predict secondary and tertiary structure, design primers and probes, and construct phylogenetic trees. Sequence logos provide a visual summary of conservation at each column.

Assessment

Because different methods can produce substantially different alignments of divergent sequences, benchmarks such as BAliBASE are used to evaluate accuracy, and alignment quality — especially gap placement in variable regions — remains an active research problem.
