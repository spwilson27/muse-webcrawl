The 54th Massachusetts Infantry Regiment was an infantry regiment in the Union Army during the American Civil War. It was the first military unit consisting of Black soldiers to be raised in the North during the war, and one of the first official African American units in the United States Armed Forces. Recruited by abolitionists and led by Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, the regiment gained fame for its assault on Fort Wagner, South Carolina, on July 18, 1863.

Formation

Following the Emancipation Proclamation, Massachusetts Governor John Albion Andrew authorized the raising of a regiment of Black volunteers. Recruits came from across the North and included two sons of abolitionist Frederick Douglass, Charles and Lewis Douglass. Shaw, the son of a prominent abolitionist family, accepted command. The men initially were promised the same pay as white soldiers, but the War Department paid them less; the regiment refused all pay until Congress equalized wages in 1864.

Fort Wagner

On July 18, 1863, the 54th led the assault on Battery Wagner, a Confederate fort guarding Charleston Harbor on Morris Island. The regiment charged over open sand against heavy fire; Shaw was killed and the regiment lost roughly 270 of some 600 men engaged. Sergeant William Harvey Carney, who rescued the national colors, later became the first African American to be awarded (for an action) the Medal of Honor. The regiment's valor at Fort Wagner helped spur further enlistment of Black troops — by war's end some 180,000 African Americans served in the Union forces.

Later service and legacy

The regiment later fought at the Battle of Olustee in Florida, at Honey Hill, and at Boykin's Mill, and was mustered out in August 1865. Augustus Saint-Gaudens's memorial to Shaw and the 54th, unveiled on Boston Common in 1897, is regarded as a masterpiece of American public sculpture. The regiment's story was dramatized in the 1989 film Glory.
