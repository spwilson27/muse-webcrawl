Orphan work

An orphan work is a copyright-protected work whose owner cannot be identified or located by someone who wishes to use the work in a manner requiring the owner's permission. Because licences cannot be obtained, would-be users — libraries, archives, filmmakers, publishers and scholars — face legal risk if they proceed, and frequently abandon the use. The problem is especially acute for photographs, unpublished letters and manuscripts, early recordings and films, and works whose creators are unknown or whose rights passed through defunct publishers.

Unattributed photographs are a classic category of orphan works.

Scale of the problem

Studies by the British Library and others have estimated that a large share of in-copyright items in cultural collections — in some categories a majority — may be orphaned, because copyright lasts for decades after an author's death, no comprehensive rights registry exists, and much creative material was published anonymously or without rights documentation. Mass digitisation projects such as Google Books brought the issue to prominence, since library collections contain millions of in-copyright but out-of-print and possibly orphaned books.

Legal responses

Several jurisdictions have adopted mechanisms to unlock orphan works. The European Union's Orphan Works Directive (2012/28/EU) permits publicly accessible cultural institutions, after a documented "diligent search", to digitise and make available orphan works in their collections, with compensation owed if a rights holder reappears. Canada operates a licensing scheme through its Copyright Board for unlocatable owners. In the United States, the Copyright Office's 2006 report proposed limiting remedies for users who performed a reasonably diligent search; bills introduced in 2006 and 2008 (the Orphan Works Acts and the Shawn Bentley Orphan Works Act) failed to pass, opposed by photographers and illustrators who feared their works would be wrongly treated as orphaned.

Criticism and alternatives

Proposals are debated between cultural institutions, which seek legal certainty for digitisation and access, and creators' groups, which warn that diligent-search standards may be weak in practice and that mass commercial use could follow. Alternative approaches include extended collective licensing (used in Nordic countries), limitation-on-remedies schemes, and improved rights registries.
