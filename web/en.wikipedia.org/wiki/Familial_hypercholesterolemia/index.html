Familial hypercholesterolemia

SpecialtyEndocrinology, medical genetics, cardiology
SymptomsHigh LDL cholesterol, tendon xanthomas, early heart disease
CausesMutations in LDLR, APOB, or PCSK9 genes
Frequency~1 in 250 (heterozygous); ~1 in 300,000–1,000,000 (homozygous)

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is a genetic disorder characterised by high cholesterol levels, specifically very high levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, from birth, and by early cardiovascular disease. Because individuals with FH have had elevated LDL throughout life, their risk of coronary heart disease is substantially higher than that of the general population with similar cholesterol levels acquired later.

Genetics

FH is most often inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern and is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the LDLR gene, which encodes the LDL receptor that clears LDL particles from the blood. Less commonly, mutations in APOB (the ligand that binds LDL to its receptor) or gain-of-function mutations in PCSK9 are responsible. People with one mutated copy (heterozygotes) have roughly half the normal number of functioning LDL receptors; those with two (homozygotes) have almost none and far more severe disease.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with heterozygous FH have no symptoms for decades. Physical signs can include tendon xanthomas (cholesterol deposits, classically in the Achilles tendon and knuckles), xanthelasma around the eyelids, and corneal arcus at a young age. Untreated, about half of men with heterozygous FH develop coronary disease by age 50; homozygous FH can cause heart attacks in childhood.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on very high LDL cholesterol, a family history of early heart disease, physical signs, and genetic testing; criteria sets include the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network and Simon Broome criteria. Cascade screening of relatives is recommended because the condition runs predictably in families. Treatment aims at large LDL reductions: high-intensity statins, ezetimibe, and PCSK9 inhibitors such as evolocumab and alirocumab; severe homozygous cases may need lipoprotein apheresis or the drug lomitapide.
