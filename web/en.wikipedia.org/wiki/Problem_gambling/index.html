Problem gambling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Problem gambling (or gambling disorder, formerly often called pathological gambling or compulsive gambling) is an addictive disorder characterised by persistent and recurrent gambling behaviour that disrupts personal, family or vocational pursuits. In the DSM-5, gambling disorder is classified with the substance-related and addictive disorders, reflecting evidence that it engages brain reward systems in ways similar to drugs of abuse.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic criteria include preoccupation with gambling, needing to gamble with increasing amounts of money to achieve excitement, repeated unsuccessful efforts to cut back, restlessness or irritability when trying to stop, gambling to escape problems ("chasing" losses), lying to conceal involvement, jeopardising relationships or employment, and relying on others for money to relieve desperate financial situations. Severity is graded by the number of criteria met.

Prevalence and risk factors

Estimates of past-year problem gambling among adults typically range from about 0.1% to several percent depending on the jurisdiction and instrument used. Risk factors include early exposure to gambling, impulsivity, co-occurring substance use and mood disorders, and easy availability — including online gambling and electronic gaming machines. Men are affected more often than women, though rates among women have risen.

Consequences

Consequences include severe indebtedness, bankruptcy, family breakdown, job loss, crime to finance gambling, and elevated rates of depression and suicide.

Treatment and support

Cognitive behavioural therapy and motivational interviewing have the best evidence base. Self-help fellowships such as Gamblers Anonymous (founded 1957, modelled on Alcoholics Anonymous) offer peer support. Many jurisdictions fund helplines, self-exclusion schemes and treatment services, and in the United States the National Problem Gambling Helpline (1-800-GAMBLER) operates nationwide.
