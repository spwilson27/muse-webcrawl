Area code

An area code is a part of a telephone number that designates a geographic region or a special service. The term is most often used in the North American Numbering Plan (NANP), where a three-digit Numbering Plan Area (NPA) code precedes the seven-digit local number, giving the familiar pattern (NPA) NXX-XXXX in the United States, Canada, and parts of the Caribbean.

History

The NANP was created by AT&T and Bell Laboratories in 1947 to enable operator toll dialling and, later, direct distance dialling across the continent. The original 86 area codes were assigned so that the most populous areas got codes that were quickest to dial on rotary phones: New York City received 212, Chicago 312, Los Angeles 213, while rural regions received high-effort codes such as 915. Originally the middle digit was restricted to 0 or 1, and state and provincial boundaries largely defined the areas.

Splits, overlays, and exhaustion

Growth in population, fax machines, pagers, second lines, and mobile phones exhausted the numbering resources of many codes. Relief took two forms: geographic splits, dividing an area so part of it received a new code, and overlays, in which a new code covers the same territory, making ten-digit dialling mandatory. Since 1995 new codes may use any middle digit, greatly expanding the pool. Special codes exist for non-geographic services, notably toll-free numbers (800, 888, 877, 866, 855, 844, and 833) and premium-rate 900 numbers.

Cultural significance

Area codes have become markers of local identity, celebrated in popular music and branding — for example 212 for Manhattan, 310 for Los Angeles, 416 for Toronto, and 504 for New Orleans — and residents sometimes resist changes that would surrender a prestigious legacy code.

Analogues elsewhere

Many other countries use geographic dialling codes of variable length — called STD codes in the United Kingdom and Ireland, trunk codes, or Vorwahlen in Germany — though the fixed term "area code" is characteristically North American.
