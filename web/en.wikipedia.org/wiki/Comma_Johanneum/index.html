The Comma Johanneum (Latin: Comma Johanneum), also called the Johannine Comma, is an interpolated phrase (comma) in the First Epistle of John at 1 John 5:7–8. In the King James Version the passage reads: "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth, the spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one." The italicised first sentence — the Comma — is the most explicit statement of the doctrine of the Trinity in the traditional text of the New Testament.

Textual history

The Comma is absent from virtually all Greek manuscripts before the 14th century and from the earliest versions and the quotations of the Greek Church Fathers — who, engaged in Trinitarian controversies, never cite it. It appears to have arisen as a Latin gloss, perhaps in Spain or North Africa in the 4th–5th centuries (a similar form appears in the writings associated with Priscillian's circle), and entered the Latin Vulgate tradition over the early Middle Ages. When Erasmus omitted it from his 1516 and 1519 Greek New Testaments, critics objected; after a late Greek manuscript containing it (Codex Montfortianus) was produced, Erasmus included it in his 1522 third edition — the often-repeated story that he had rashly promised to do so is regarded as probably legendary. From Erasmus and the later Textus Receptus it passed into the King James Version.

Modern scholarship

Modern critical editions of the Greek New Testament omit the Comma, and most modern translations either omit it or footnote it, while it is retained in translations based on the Textus Receptus and in traditionalist circles. Isaac Newton wrote a notable early critique of the passage (published posthumously, 1754) as part of his work on textual corruptions of Scripture.
