Schmidt camera

A large Schmidt camera used for sky surveys

A Schmidt camera, also referred to as a Schmidt telescope, is a catadioptric astronomical telescope designed to provide very wide fields of view with low aberrations. It was invented by the Estonian/German optician Bernhard Schmidt in 1930.

Design

The Schmidt camera uses a spherical primary mirror and a thin aspheric corrector plate (the Schmidt corrector) placed at the centre of curvature of the mirror, where the aperture stop sits. This arrangement eliminates coma and astigmatism, leaving only field curvature: the focal surface is curved, so photographic plates or films had to be bent into a spherical shape in a special holder. Spherical aberration is corrected by the shaped corrector plate.

Applications

Schmidt cameras are used almost exclusively for survey photography, since they can image large areas of sky sharply at fast focal ratios (f/1 to f/3). Famous examples include the Samuel Oschin telescope (48-inch) at Palomar Observatory, which produced the Palomar Observatory Sky Survey, and the UK Schmidt Telescope in Australia, which surveyed the southern sky. Space-based variants include the Kepler photometer, a Schmidt telescope used to detect exoplanets.

Related designs

The Schmidt concept underlies the Schmidt–Cassegrain telescope widely used by amateur astronomers, and the Schmidt–Newtonian variant.
