Septic tank

A septic tank and soil absorption field.

A septic tank is an underground chamber, made of concrete, fiberglass, or plastic, through which domestic wastewater (sewage) flows for basic treatment. It is the central component of a septic system, a small-scale, on-site sewage treatment arrangement used where properties are not connected to a municipal sewer, most commonly in rural areas and low-density suburbs. Settling and anaerobic digestion reduce the matter in the wastewater; the treated liquid effluent is then disposed of through a drain field (leach field) in the surrounding soil.

Operation

Wastewater from toilets, sinks, baths, and laundry enters the tank, which holds it long enough for solids and oils to separate by gravity. Heavier solids sink to the bottom, forming a layer of sludge; lighter oils and grease float, forming a layer of scum; and a clarified liquid layer remains in the middle. Naturally occurring anaerobic bacteria digest a portion of the organic matter, reducing the volume of accumulated solids. Baffles or T-shaped outlet fittings prevent scum and sludge from leaving with the effluent, which flows to the drain field, where it percolates through soil that provides further treatment by filtering and microbial action.

Multi-compartment tanks and effluent filters improve performance, and some modern systems add aeration units or disinfection for sensitive sites.

Maintenance

Sludge and scum accumulate over time and must be removed by pumping, typically every three to five years for a household, depending on tank size and usage. Neglect can allow solids to escape into the drain field, clogging the soil and causing system failure, surface pooling of effluent, and contamination of groundwater and nearby wells. Inspections generally check sludge and scum depth, baffles, and the condition of the distribution box and drain field.

Environmental considerations

Properly functioning septic systems are an effective and economical means of wastewater treatment. Poorly sited, aging, or overloaded systems can release nitrogen, phosphorus, and pathogens into groundwater and surface water, contributing to well contamination and the nutrient pollution of lakes and coastal waters. Many jurisdictions regulate tank design, minimum distances from wells and water bodies, and inspection upon property transfer.

History

The septic tank is credited to Jean-Louis Mouras, who built a prototype in France in the 1860s and patented a design in 1881; similar systems spread to the United States and Britain in the following decades. The name derives from the Greek septikos, "putrefying," referring to the anaerobic decomposition inside. Septic systems now serve a substantial share of homes in many countries — roughly a fifth of households in the United States.
