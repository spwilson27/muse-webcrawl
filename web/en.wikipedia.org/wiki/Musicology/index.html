Musicology

Musicology (from Greek mousikē, "music," and -logia, "domain of study") is the scholarly study of music. Musicology traditionally divides into three main branches: historical musicology, systematic musicology, and ethnomusicology. The field's umbrella organisation is the International Musicological Society, founded in 1927, and its leading national body is the American Musicological Society (1934).

Branches

Historical musicology studies the history of Western art music: composers' lives, sources, notation, editions and style. Ethnomusicology, coined by Jaap Kunst around 1950 from the earlier "comparative musicology," studies music in its cultural context worldwide, using fieldwork. Systematic musicology covers music theory and analysis, acoustics, psychoacoustics and the psychology of music, the sociology of music, aesthetics and music cognition. The organisational scheme derives from Guido Adler's influential 1885 essay "The Scope, Method, and Aim of Musicology."

History

Interest in music as an object of scholarship is ancient — Pythagoras and Aristoxenus in Greece, treatises in China and India, and medieval theorists such as Guido d'Arezzo. Modern musicology arose in 19th-century Germany and Austria with scholars such as Friedrich Chrysander (who edited Handel's works) and Philipp Spitta (biographer of Bach). In the 1980s and 1990s, the "new musicology" — associated with Joseph Kerman, Susan McClary and others — brought feminist, critical and postcolonial approaches to the discipline, questioning the traditional focus on autonomous works and great composers.
