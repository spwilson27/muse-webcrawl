Flash (photography)

TypePhotographic lighting device
PurposeBrief, intense burst of artificial light
Modern formXenon flashtube (electronic flash / speedlight)

A flash is a device used in photography to produce a brief burst of artificial light — typically lasting from about 1/1000 to 1/200 of a second — at a colour temperature of roughly 5,500 K, in order to illuminate a scene. It is used to photograph dark scenes, to freeze fast motion, and as fill light to soften shadows in daylight.

History

Early photographers used flash powder — a mixture of magnesium powder and an oxidiser — ignited on a tray, a dangerous and smoky method. Single-use flashbulbs, introduced in the late 1920s, contained a filament and magnesium foil in an oxygen-filled glass bulb. The electronic flash, based on Harold Edgerton's work with gas-discharge tubes in the 1930s, stores charge in a capacitor and discharges it through a xenon tube; reusable and compact, it became the standard "speedlight" from the 1960s onward.

Technique

Flash exposure is governed by the flash's guide number, the distance to the subject, and the lens aperture; unlike ambient exposure it is largely independent of shutter speed up to the camera's flash synchronisation speed. Through-the-lens (TTL) metering automates output by measuring light reflected from the subject and quenching the flash when enough light has been delivered. Off-camera flash, bounce flash (aiming the head at a ceiling or wall), diffusers, and multiple-flash setups are used to control the direction and harshness of the light.

Drawbacks

Direct on-camera flash can produce flat lighting, harsh shadows, and red-eye, caused by light reflecting from the retina when the flash is close to the lens axis. Many museums and venues prohibit flash to protect artworks and avoid disturbing others.
