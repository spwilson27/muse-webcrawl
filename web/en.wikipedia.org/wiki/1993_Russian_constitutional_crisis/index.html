The 1993 Russian constitutional crisis, also known as the 1993 October Coup, Black October, or the Storming of the White House, was a political stand-off between Russian president Boris Yeltsin and the Russian parliament that was resolved with military force. It lasted from 21 September to 4 October 1993 and culminated in tanks shelling the White House, the seat of parliament in Moscow.

Background

Throughout 1992 and 1993, Yeltsin's radical economic reforms — price liberalisation and privatisation — produced hyperinflation and hardship, and the Congress of People's Deputies and its standing body, the Supreme Soviet, led by speaker Ruslan Khasbulatov, repeatedly blocked his programme. The Soviet-era constitution left the division of power between president and parliament ambiguous, and both sides claimed legal supremacy. Vice President Alexander Rutskoy broke with Yeltsin and joined the opposition.

Crisis

On 21 September 1993, Yeltsin issued Decree No. 1400 dissolving the Congress and Supreme Soviet, in defiance of the constitution and a Constitutional Court ruling. Parliament responded by impeaching Yeltsin and swearing in Rutskoy as acting president. Deputies and their supporters barricaded themselves inside the White House, which government forces cordoned off, cutting power and water.

On 3 October, pro-parliament demonstrators broke through police lines, seized the Moscow mayor's office and attacked the Ostankino television centre, where dozens were killed. Yeltsin declared a state of emergency and, on the morning of 4 October, ordered the army to storm the White House. Tanks shelled the building's upper floors — images broadcast worldwide — and by the afternoon the defenders surrendered. Official figures put the death toll at 187, though some estimates are considerably higher.

Aftermath

Yeltsin suspended the Constitutional Court and ruled by decree until a referendum on 12 December 1993 approved a new constitution that created a strongly presidential ("superpresidential") system with a new, weaker parliament, the Federal Assembly. The crisis ended the uneasy dual power of Russia's transition and remains a defining and divisive episode of the post-Soviet era.
