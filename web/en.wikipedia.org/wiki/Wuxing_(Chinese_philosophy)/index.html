Wuxing (Chinese philosophy)

Wuxing (Chinese: 五行; pinyin: wǔxíng), usually translated as Five Phases or Five Elements, is a fivefold conceptual scheme used in many traditional Chinese fields of thought to explain a wide array of phenomena, including cosmic cycles, the interactions between internal organs, the succession of political regimes, and the properties of medicines and foods. The five phases are wood (木), fire (火), earth (土), metal (金) and water (水).

The cycles

The phases are related by two principal cycles. In the generating (sheng) cycle, wood feeds fire, fire produces earth (ash), earth bears metal, metal enriches water (condensation), and water nourishes wood. In the overcoming or controlling (ke) cycle, wood parts earth, earth absorbs water, water quenches fire, fire melts metal, and metal chops wood.

History

The scheme was systematised during the Warring States period and is associated with the philosopher Zou Yan (c. 305–240 BCE) and the Yinyang school. It was incorporated into Han dynasty correlative cosmology, notably by Dong Zhongshu, and influenced calendrics, astronomy, medicine and statecraft. Under the theory of dynastic succession, each imperial dynasty was associated with a phase whose virtue succeeded that of the previous dynasty.

Applications

Wuxing correspondences structure traditional Chinese medicine (the zangfu organs, flavours, emotions and seasons), feng shui, Chinese astrology, music theory and martial arts such as Xingyiquan, whose five basic fists correspond to the five phases.
