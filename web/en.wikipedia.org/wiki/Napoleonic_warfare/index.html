Napoleonic warfare

Napoleonic warfare denotes the military methods of the era of the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars (roughly 1792–1815), characterised by mass conscript armies, rapid manoeuvre, combined arms and decisive battle. Building on reforms begun under the French monarchy and theorists such as Guibert and du Teil, it transformed the scale and tempo of European war.

Characteristics

The levée en masse of 1793 gave France armies of unprecedented size. Organised under Napoleon into self-contained army corps — each with infantry, cavalry and artillery able to fight alone for a day — French forces marched dispersed to live off the land and concentrated rapidly for battle (manoeuvre sur les derrières). Battles sought the destruction of the enemy's main army through envelopment, as at Ulm and Jena-Auerstedt, with artillery massed in grand batteries and infantry attacking in column supported by skirmish lines of voltigeurs.

At sea the period saw the climax of the sailing navy, decided by Nelson's tactics at Trafalgar (1805). On land, Spain's guerrilla war and Russia's scorched-earth retreat in 1812 exposed the system's logistical limits; by 1813–1815 the other powers had adopted corps organisation, conscription and general staffs of their own, defeating Napoleon at Leipzig and Waterloo. The era's lessons, codified by Jomini and Clausewitz, shaped military thought for a century.
