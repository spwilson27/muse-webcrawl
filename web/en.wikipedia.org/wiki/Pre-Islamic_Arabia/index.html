Pre-Islamic Arabia

Pre-Islamic Arabia refers to the Arabian Peninsula before the rise of Islam in the 630s CE. Islamic tradition calls the preceding period the Jahiliyyah ("age of ignorance"), but the peninsula in fact hosted sophisticated kingdoms, long-distance trade networks, and diverse religious communities for millennia.

South Arabian kingdoms

The fertile southwest (modern Yemen) was home to the kingdoms of Saba (Sheba), Ma'in, Qataban, Hadhramaut, and Himyar. These states grew wealthy on the incense trade in frankincense and myrrh and on irrigation engineering such as the great Marib Dam. Himyar, the last of them, adopted a monotheistic faith influenced by Judaism in the fourth century CE and fell to Aksumite and then Sasanian invasion in the sixth century.

Central and northern Arabia

Central Arabia was dominated by nomadic and semi-nomadic Bedouin tribes organised by kinship, alongside oasis towns such as Mecca and Yathrib (Medina). In the north, Arab client states buffered the great empires: the Ghassanids served Byzantium, the Lakhmids of al-Hira served Sasanian Persia, and earlier the Nabataeans, with their capital at Petra, prospered from caravan trade before Roman annexation in 106 CE.

Religion

Most Arabs practised a polytheistic religion centred on deities such as al-Lat, al-Uzza, and Manat, with the Kaaba in Mecca housing numerous idols. Substantial Jewish tribes lived in the Hijaz oases, Christian communities (Monophysite, Nestorian, and others) flourished in the south and northeast, and Zoroastrianism was present in the east; individual monotheistic seekers known as hanifs are also remembered in tradition.

Culture

Pre-Islamic Arabia produced a celebrated oral poetry tradition — the great odes known as the Mu'allaqat — celebrating honour, hospitality, warfare, and desert life; poetry served as the tribal register of genealogy and glory. Written records survive in tens of thousands of inscriptions in scripts such as Safaitic, Hismaic, and the South Arabian monumental script.
