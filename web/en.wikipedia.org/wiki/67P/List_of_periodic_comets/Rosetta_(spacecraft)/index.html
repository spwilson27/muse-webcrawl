Rosetta (spacecraft)

Rosetta photographed with comet 67P behind it.

Rosetta was a European Space Agency cornerstone mission: the first spacecraft to rendezvous with, orbit, and deploy a lander onto a comet. Built around a large orbiter with two 14-metre solar wings, it was launched on 2 March 2004 and spent a decade chasing comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko through the inner Solar System.

Journey and operations

Rosetta's trajectory used gravity assists at Earth (2005, 2007, 2009) and Mars (2007), with flybys of asteroids 2867 Šteins and 21 Lutetia en route, followed by 31 months of hibernation beyond Jupiter's distance. It awoke on schedule in January 2014 and entered orbit around the comet on 6 August 2014, revealing a rubber-duck-shaped nucleus of two joined lobes. In November 2014 it released the Philae lander, then spent almost two more years monitoring the comet's activity through perihelion and beyond, finally impacting the surface in the Sais region on 30 September 2016.

Science results

The mission's eleven orbiter instruments measured the nucleus's density (surprisingly low, like cork), mapped its surface chemistry, and analyzed coma gases, finding molecular oxygen, nitrogen, noble gases, and organic molecules including glycine and phosphorus — ingredients for life — while its measurement of the comet's water isotope ratio disfavoured Jupiter-family comets as the source of Earth's oceans. Rosetta is named for the Rosetta Stone, just as Philae is named for the Philae obelisk; both keys unlocked ancient scripts, as the mission was intended to unlock the early Solar System.
