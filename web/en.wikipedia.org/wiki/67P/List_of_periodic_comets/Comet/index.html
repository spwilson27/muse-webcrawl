Comet

Hale–Bopp, the Great Comet of 1997, showing its two tails.

A comet is an icy small Solar System body that, when passing close to the Sun, warms and begins to release gases — a process called outgassing — producing a glowing coma and sometimes two tails of gas and dust millions of kilometers long pointing away from the Sun. The word comes from the Greek komētēs, "long-haired", for the hair-like appearance of the tails.

Physical characteristics

At a comet's heart lies the nucleus, a solid body of ice, dust, and rocky material typically a few hundred meters to tens of kilometers across, often very dark and loosely consolidated — "a dirty snowball", in Fred Whipple's famous phrase. Near the Sun, sublimating ice drags dust outward to form the coma, which can swell larger than a planet. Solar radiation pressure sweeps dust into a broad, curved, yellowish tail, while the solar wind drives ionized gas into a straighter, blue ion tail. Each perihelion passage erodes the nucleus, and comets eventually fade, split, or disintegrate.

Orbital characteristics

Short-period comets originate in the Kuiper belt and scattered disc beyond Neptune and return in less than 200 years; Jupiter-family comets are the most numerous known group, while Halley-type comets have periods of 20–200 years. Long-period comets fall in from the Oort cloud, a vast spherical shell of icy bodies up to a light-year from the Sun, and may take millions of years to complete an orbit. Periodic comets that have been confirmed at two returns receive permanent numbers, such as 1P/Halley and 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, the target of the Rosetta mission.

Comets in history

Comets were long read as omens; Halley's appears on the Bayeux Tapestry, and the Great Comets of 1811, 1843, and 1997 (Hale–Bopp) drew worldwide attention. Spacecraft have now flown past several nuclei, returned dust samples (Stardust), impacted one (Deep Impact), and orbited and landed on one (Rosetta and Philae).
