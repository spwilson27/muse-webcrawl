Comet

The twin-lobed nucleus of comet 67P, visited by the Rosetta and Philae spacecraft.

A comet is a small body of ice, dust, and rock orbiting the Sun. Distant from the Sun it is a dark, inert nucleus only kilometers wide; as it approaches the inner Solar System, sunlight sublimates its ices, and escaping gas and dust form an atmosphere (the coma) and one or two tails that can stretch hundreds of millions of kilometers — making comets among the most spectacular naked-eye sights in the sky.

Structure

The solid nucleus is a fragile aggregate of water ice, frozen carbon dioxide and other volatiles, dust, and organic compounds; densities measured at comets such as 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko are lower than that of water, implying a porous, rubble-like interior. Surface vents open and close with the seasons of the comet's day and orbit, producing jets. The coma can grow to 15 times Earth's diameter, and its hydrogen envelope, visible in ultraviolet, can exceed the Sun in size. Two tails form: a curved, whitish dust tail pushed by radiation pressure, and a straight, blue ion tail carried by the solar wind along the magnetic field.

Families and origins

Comets are grouped by their orbits. Jupiter-family comets, with periods under 20 years and flat orbits, come chiefly from the scattered disc. Halley-type comets, with periods of 20–200 years at any inclination, and long-period comets, some with periods of millions of years, arrive from the Oort cloud. A few comets become sungrazers, passing within a hair's breadth of the Sun, and others turn dormant, masquerading as asteroids.

Exploration

An armada met Halley's Comet in 1986; since then spacecraft have flown by Borrelly, Wild 2 (returning dust samples to Earth), Tempel 1 (which Deep Impact excavated with a projectile), and Hartley 2. The high point remains ESA's Rosetta mission, which orbited comet 67P for two years and set down the Philae lander on its surface in November 2014, directly sampling a comet for the first time.
