Rosetta (spacecraft)

Rosetta
ESA comet orbiter
Launched: 2 March 2004
Mission end: 30 September 2016
Target: 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko.

Rosetta was a European Space Agency robotic spacecraft built to rendezvous with and study the comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko. Launched in 2004 on an Ariane 5 rocket, it travelled for ten years, using gravity assists from Earth and Mars and passing asteroids Šteins and Lutetia, before arriving at the comet in August 2014. It was the first spacecraft to orbit a comet nucleus and to accompany one around the Sun.

Mission

Rosetta carried the Philae lander, which descended to the comet’s surface on 12 November 2014—the first controlled landing on a comet nucleus. Although Philae came to rest in an unplanned position and operated for a limited time on battery power, it returned important measurements. The orbiter mapped the comet’s two-lobed “rubber duck” shape, measured its density and outgassing, identified complex organic compounds and observed dramatic changes as 67P approached perihelion in 2015.

Findings and end of mission

Rosetta showed that the comet’s water has a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio quite different from Earth’s oceans, weakening the idea that Jupiter-family comets like 67P supplied most of Earth’s water. Instruments detected molecular oxygen, glycine and phosphorus in the coma. As the comet receded from the Sun and power fell, ESA ended the mission by deliberately impacting Rosetta onto the surface on 30 September 2016, near a pit later scrutinised in final images.

The mission was named after the Rosetta Stone, reflecting the hope that a comet—preserved material from the early Solar System—would help decode planetary origins.
