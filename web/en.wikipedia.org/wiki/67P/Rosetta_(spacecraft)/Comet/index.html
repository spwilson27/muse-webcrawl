Comet

Comet
Icy small Solar System body
Parts: nucleus, coma and tails
Famous examples: Halley, Hale–Bopp, 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko.

A comet is an icy small body that orbits the Sun and, when it comes close enough for solar heating, releases gas and dust to form a glowing atmosphere called a coma and one or more tails. Comets are remnants from the formation of the Solar System, preserving volatile compounds and dust from the early solar nebula. Their tails always point generally away from the Sun: a bluish ion tail shaped by the solar wind and a broader, curved dust tail reflecting sunlight.

Structure and orbits

The solid nucleus is typically only a few kilometres across and consists of water ice, frozen carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide, dust and organic compounds. Short-period comets originate mainly in the Kuiper belt and scattered disc beyond Neptune, while long-period comets arrive from the distant Oort cloud. When a comet repeatedly passes the Sun it loses volatiles, may fragment or become dormant, and can supply meteoroid streams that produce meteor showers when Earth crosses their debris.

Observation and exploration

Bright comets have been recorded since antiquity and were once treated as omens. Edmond Halley recognised that the comets of 1531, 1607 and 1682 were the same object, whose 1758 return confirmed Newtonian gravitation. Spacecraft have flown by and visited several comets: Giotto imaged Halley’s nucleus in 1986, Deep Impact struck Tempel 1 in 2005, Stardust returned dust from Wild 2, and ESA’s Rosetta orbited 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko in 2014–2016.

Significance

Comets are studied for clues to the origin of Earth’s water and organic chemistry, although measurements at 67P suggested that Jupiter-family comets were probably not the dominant source of terrestrial oceans. They also remain relevant to impact hazards and to understanding how planetesimals formed and migrated in the young Solar System.
