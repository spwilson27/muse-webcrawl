Philae (spacecraft)

Philae
ESA comet lander carried by Rosetta
Landed on 67P: 12 November 2014
First controlled landing on a comet nucleus.

Philae was a robotic lander carried by the European Space Agency’s Rosetta spacecraft to comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko. On 12 November 2014 it separated from Rosetta and descended to the surface, achieving the first controlled touchdown on a comet nucleus. The lander was named after Philae island in the Nile, where an obelisk helped decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs—an analogy for the mission’s aim of decoding comet science.

Landing

The landing did not go as planned. Philae’s harpoons and hold-down thruster failed to secure it, and the lander bounced twice across the weak-gravity surface before settling in a shadowed crevice later named Abydos, roughly a kilometre from the intended Agilkia site. Its exact position was unknown for nearly two years. Because its solar panels received little light, Philae worked through its planned science sequence on battery power for about 57 hours and then fell silent.

Results and rediscovery

Despite the difficult landing, Philae returned panoramic images, measured the surface and subsurface, sampled gases and detected organic compounds. It found the surface at the final resting place to be unexpectedly hard beneath a thin dust layer. A brief contact occurred in June 2015 as the comet neared the Sun, but communications could not be sustained. In September 2016, Rosetta images finally revealed Philae wedged in a crack, closing one of the mission’s best-known mysteries shortly before Rosetta itself was crashed into the comet.

Philae’s landing was a major public event and demonstrated both the feasibility and the difficulty of operating on a small, irregular, low-gravity body.
