Rosetta (spacecraft)

Rosetta, the first spacecraft to orbit a comet's nucleus.

Rosetta was a space probe built and launched by the European Space Agency to perform a detailed study of comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko. Launched on 2 March 2004 aboard an Ariane 5 rocket, it reached the comet on 6 August 2014 after a ten-year journey of more than 6 billion kilometers, becoming the first spacecraft to rendezvous with and orbit a comet.

Mission profile

Because no launcher could send Rosetta directly to the comet, the probe used four gravity assists (Earth three times, Mars once), flew by the asteroids 2867 Šteins (2008) and 21 Lutetia (2010), and spent 957 days in deep-space hibernation before waking in January 2014. After arriving, it mapped the comet's double-lobed nucleus, selected a landing site, and on 12 November 2014 released the Philae lander, which made history's first soft landing on a comet. Rosetta then escorted the comet through its August 2015 perihelion, studying the growth and decay of its coma and tails.

Findings and end of mission

Rosetta's instruments showed that the comet's water has a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio quite different from Earth's oceans, arguing against Jupiter-family comets as the main source of Earth's water, and detected molecular oxygen and complex organic compounds, including the amino acid glycine. With the comet receding from the Sun and power dwindling, controllers deliberately crashed Rosetta onto the nucleus on 30 September 2016, ending the mission close to its lander. The probe is named after the Rosetta Stone.
