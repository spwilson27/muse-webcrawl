Opioid antagonist

An opioid antagonist, or opioid receptor antagonist, is a receptor antagonist that acts on one or more of the opioid receptors. They bind to opioid receptors with high affinity but do not activate them, thereby blocking or reversing the effects of opioid agonists such as morphine, heroin and fentanyl.

Examples and uses

  • Naloxone — a short-acting antagonist used in emergency medicine to rapidly reverse opioid overdose and respiratory depression. It is available as an injection and as a nasal spray, and is widely distributed to first responders and laypeople.
  • Naltrexone — a longer-acting, orally available antagonist used in the treatment of opioid use disorder and alcohol use disorder, including a monthly extended-release injection.
  • Nalmefene — used for overdose reversal and, in some countries, for reduction of alcohol consumption.
  • Peripherally acting antagonists — such as methylnaltrexone, naloxegol and naldemedine, which are designed not to cross the blood–brain barrier and are used to treat opioid-induced constipation without reversing central analgesia.

Pharmacology

Most clinically used opioid antagonists are competitive antagonists at the μ-opioid receptor, with varying activity at the κ- and δ-opioid receptors. Administration to a person dependent on opioids can precipitate acute withdrawal, because the antagonist displaces agonist from the receptors. Naloxone's short half-life (roughly 30–90 minutes) means that a person revived from overdose of a long-acting opioid may relapse into respiratory depression and requires observation.

History

Nalorphine, an early mixed agonist–antagonist, was used in the 1950s to counter opioid respiratory depression. Pure antagonists followed: naloxone was synthesised in 1960 and approved in 1971, and naltrexone in the 1980s.
