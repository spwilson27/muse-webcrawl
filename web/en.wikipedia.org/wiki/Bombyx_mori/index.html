Bombyx mori

Bombyx mori
Family: Bombycidae
Common name: domestic silkmoth

Bombyx mori, commonly called the domestic silk moth, is an insect from the moth family Bombycidae. It is the closest relative of Bombyx mandarina, the wild silk moth. The silkworm is the larva (or caterpillar) of the domestic silk moth. It is an economically important insect, being a primary producer of silk.

Domestication

The domestic silk moth was domesticated from wild populations in China more than 5,000 years ago and has been so thoroughly bred in captivity that it can no longer survive without human care. Adults have lost the ability to fly effectively, and the larvae depend entirely on humans for food and protection. The preferred food plants are white mulberry trees (Morus alba).

Life cycle

The female moth lays 300–500 tiny eggs, which hatch into larvae that feed on mulberry leaves for about a month, moulting four times. The mature larva spins a cocoon of a single continuous silk thread roughly 300–900 m long, secreted from its salivary glands. Inside the cocoon it pupates; in sericulture the pupa is usually killed by heat before the adult can break the cocoon and damage the thread.

Silk production

Sericulture — the rearing of silkworms — remains a major industry in China, India, and other countries. Silk fibroin from B. mori is also used in biomedical applications, and the moth serves as a model organism in genetics and insect physiology; its genome was sequenced in the early 2000s.

Other uses

In Korea, silkworm pupae (beondegi) are eaten as a snack, and silkworm droppings are used in traditional Chinese medicine.
