Conservation of momentum

[Hero image placeholder: pool balls colliding, illustrating momentum transfer]

In physics, the principle of conservation of momentum states that for an isolated system — one not subject to any net external forces — the total momentum of the system remains constant over time. Momentum is a vector quantity, defined for a particle as the product of its mass and velocity:

p = mv

It is one of the fundamental conservation laws of nature and applies universally, from subatomic particle interactions to the motion of galaxies.

Newtonian mechanics

The conservation of momentum follows directly from Newton's laws of motion. By Newton's third law, forces between interacting particles occur in equal and opposite pairs, so the total rate of change of momentum of a closed system is zero when no external force acts. The principle governs both elastic collisions, in which kinetic energy is also conserved, and inelastic collisions, in which it is not.

Noether's theorem

In 1918 Emmy Noether proved that conservation of momentum is a consequence of the homogeneity of space — the fact that the laws of physics are the same at every point in space. Under Noether's theorem, every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity; translational symmetry yields momentum conservation, just as rotational symmetry yields conservation of angular momentum.

Applications

Conservation of momentum explains rocket propulsion, the recoil of firearms, jet engines, and the analysis of collisions in particle physics. In a rocket, exhaust gases carry momentum backward, so the rocket gains equal momentum forward. Conservation of momentum also holds in relativistic mechanics and quantum mechanics, where momentum is generalized to include the momentum carried by electromagnetic fields and photons.

History

The concept developed from the medieval theory of impetus and the work of John Philoponus and Jean Buridan. René Descartes proposed in 1644 that the total "quantity of motion" in the universe is conserved, and John Wallis, Christopher Wren, and Christiaan Huygens correctly formulated the laws of elastic collision in papers presented to the Royal Society in 1668.
