Microgravity

Microgravity is the condition in which people or objects appear to be weightless. The effects of microgravity are seen when astronauts and objects float in space. More precisely, the term describes an environment in which the apparent acceleration forces are very small compared with Earth's gravity — of the order of one millionth (10−6) of normal gravity, hence "micro".

Weightlessness and free fall

A spacecraft in orbit is in continuous free fall towards Earth; everything inside falls together, so occupants experience weightlessness. The gravitational force at the altitude of the International Space Station is about 90% as strong as at the surface — the station is not "beyond gravity" but perpetually falling around the planet. Residual accelerations from atmospheric drag, tidal (gravity-gradient) forces, and crew activity reduce the environment from perfect free fall to microgravity.

Creating microgravity on Earth

Brief periods of microgravity are produced by parabolic aircraft flights (such as NASA's KC-135 "Vomit Comet" and ESA's parabolic campaigns), drop towers (for example the Bremen drop tower in Germany, which gives several seconds of free fall), and sounding rockets, which provide minutes of microgravity.

Effects and research

Microgravity research spans fluid physics, combustion, materials science, crystallography, and biology. Long-term exposure in humans causes bone density loss, muscle atrophy, fluid redistribution, and vision changes, which are countered by exercise regimes aboard the space station. Protein crystals grown in microgravity can be larger and better ordered, aiding drug design.
