Standard precautions (medicine)

Standard precautions are the minimum infection-control practices that apply to all patient care in healthcare settings, regardless of the suspected or confirmed infection status of the patient. They are based on the principle that all blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions (except sweat), non-intact skin, and mucous membranes may contain transmissible infectious agents. Standard precautions replaced the earlier system of "universal precautions" (introduced in the 1980s in response to the HIV/AIDS epidemic) and body substance isolation, and were formalized by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in its 1996 and 2007 isolation precaution guidelines.

Components

  • Hand hygiene — washing with soap and water or using alcohol-based hand rub before and after patient contact and after contact with potentially infectious material.
  • Personal protective equipment — gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection selected according to the anticipated exposure.
  • Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette — covering coughs, masking symptomatic patients, and spatial separation in waiting areas (added in 2007).
  • Safe injection practices — single use of needles and syringes and single-dose vials where possible.
  • Sharps safety — careful handling and disposal of needles and other sharp instruments.
  • Cleaning and disinfection of equipment and environmental surfaces, and safe handling of soiled linen.

Transmission-based precautions

When a patient is known or suspected to be infected with a pathogen requiring additional controls, standard precautions are supplemented by transmission-based precautions: contact precautions (e.g., for MRSA or Clostridioides difficile), droplet precautions (e.g., for influenza and meningococcal disease), and airborne precautions (e.g., for tuberculosis, measles, and chickenpox), the latter requiring a fit-tested respirator and an airborne infection isolation room.
