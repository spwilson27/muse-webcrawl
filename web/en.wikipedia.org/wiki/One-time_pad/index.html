One-time pad

The one-time pad (OTP) is an encryption technique that cannot be cracked, provided it is used correctly. In this technique, a plaintext is paired with a random secret key (also referred to as a one-time pad). Then, each bit or character of the plaintext is encrypted by combining it with the corresponding bit or character from the pad using modular addition.

Perfect secrecy

In 1949, Claude Shannon proved that if the key material is truly random, never reused in whole or in part, and kept completely secret, the one-time pad provides perfect secrecy: the ciphertext gives an attacker no information about the plaintext, because every possible plaintext of the same length is equally likely. The key must be at least as long as the message itself.

History and use

The technique is related to the Vernam cipher, patented by Gilbert Vernam in 1919, and was used in the form of paper pads by intelligence services and special forces during the Second World War and the Cold War, including on the Washington–Moscow hotline. Its principal drawback is the practical difficulty of generating and securely distributing large quantities of truly random key material; reuse of key material, as in the Soviet traffic exploited by the Venona project, destroys its security.
