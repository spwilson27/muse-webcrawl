Endocrine disruptor

Endocrine disruptors, sometimes also referred to as hormonally active agents, endocrine disrupting chemicals, or endocrine disrupting compounds, are chemicals that can interfere with endocrine (or hormonal) systems. These disruptions can cause numerous adverse outcomes, including developmental malformations, interference with reproduction, increased cancer risk and disturbances in the immune and nervous system function.

Endocrine disruptors can mimic or block natural hormones at their receptors.

Mechanisms

Endocrine disruptors may mimic naturally occurring hormones such as estrogens and androgens, bind to hormone receptors and either activate or block them, or alter the synthesis, transport and elimination of natural hormones. Because hormones act at very low concentrations, even small exposures during critical developmental windows can have significant effects.

Examples

Well-studied examples include bisphenol A (BPA), used in plastics and epoxy resins; phthalates, used as plasticisers; certain pesticides such as DDT; polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs); and some flame retardants. The synthetic estrogen diethylstilbestrol (DES), prescribed to pregnant women in the mid-20th century, is a classic case demonstrating transgenerational effects.

Regulation

The European Union, the United States Environmental Protection Agency and other bodies have programmes to screen and regulate endocrine disrupting chemicals, though scientific debate continues over low-dose effects and non-monotonic dose responses.
