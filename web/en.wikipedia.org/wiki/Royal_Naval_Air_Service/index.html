Royal Naval Air Service

The Royal Naval Air Service (RNAS) was the air arm of the Royal Navy, under the direction of the Admiralty's Air Department, which existed formally from 1914 to 1918. It was one of the two British military aviation services of the First World War, alongside the army's Royal Flying Corps. On 1 April 1918 the RNAS and the RFC were amalgamated to form the Royal Air Force, the world's first independent air force.

Origins

British naval aviation began with the first naval pilots trained in 1911 and the formation of the Naval Wing of the RFC in 1912. Dissatisfied with the arrangement, the Admiralty formalised its own service: the RNAS was officially recognised on 1 July 1914. Under the energetic direction of Commodore (later Sir) Murray Sueter and the Churchill-era Admiralty, the service expanded rapidly in aircraft, airships, seaplane carriers and armoured cars.

First World War

At the outbreak of war in August 1914 the RNAS had more aircraft under its control than the RFC. Its duties included fleet reconnaissance, coastal patrol, anti-submarine and anti-Zeppelin work, and attacks on enemy naval targets. RNAS aircraft carried out some of the first strategic bombing raids of the war, against Zeppelin sheds at Düsseldorf, Cologne and Friedrichshafen, and the famous Cuxhaven Raid of Christmas Day 1914, in which seaplanes carried by ships attacked the Zeppelin base near Cuxhaven.

RNAS squadrons served in France and Belgium, at Gallipoli, in the Mediterranean, East Africa and Mesopotamia. The service pioneered the aircraft carrier, torpedo-bombing aircraft such as the Sopwith Cuckoo, and large flying boats. Its armoured car squadrons operated in France, Russia and the Middle East. Famous RNAS pilots included Raymond Collishaw and Roderick Dallas. At the time of the merger in 1918 the RNAS had grown to some 55,000 personnel and nearly 3,000 aircraft, together with over a hundred air stations.

Legacy

Naval aviation returned to Admiralty control with the creation of the Fleet Air Arm in 1924, formally restored to the Royal Navy in 1937, which inherited the RNAS tradition of shipborne flight.
