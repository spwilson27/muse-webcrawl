Abdülmecid I

Abdülmecid I (Ottoman Turkish: عبد المجيد اول; Turkish: I. Abdülmecid; 25 April 1823 – 25 June 1861) was the 31st sultan of the Ottoman Empire, reigning from 1839 until his death. Succeeding his father Mahmud II as a sixteen-year-old, he presided over the launch of the Tanzimat — the great programme of administrative, military, legal, and educational reform intended to modernize the empire — and led it through the Crimean War, in which Britain and France fought as Ottoman allies.

Reign and reforms

Abdülmecid inherited a crisis: an Egyptian army under Ibrahim Pasha had just destroyed the Ottoman army at Nezib, and the Ottoman fleet had defected to Alexandria. Diplomacy by the European powers restored the lost provinces. On 3 November 1839, weeks after his accession, the sultan's government proclaimed the Edict of Gülhane, drafted with the foreign minister Mustafa Reşid Pasha, which promised security of life, honour, and property to all subjects regardless of religion, regular taxation, and fair conscription — the founding charter of the Tanzimat. Under a succession of reforming grand viziers, including Reşid, Mehmed Emin Âli, and Keçecizade Fuad Pasha, the empire acquired new penal and commercial codes, a reorganized army and bureaucracy, secular schools, telegraph lines, and the first railways.

The Imperial Reform Edict of 1856, issued at the close of the Crimean War partly under European pressure, went further, formally promising equality of Muslim and non-Muslim subjects. Abdülmecid was also the first sultan to listen directly to public grievances on regular tours, and he founded institutions including the first Ottoman theatre productions at court and the Imperial School of Medicine.

Foreign affairs

The sultan granted refuge to Hungarian and Polish revolutionaries after 1848 — including Lajos Kossuth — refusing Russian and Austrian demands for their extradition. The dispute over the Holy Places in Palestine contributed to the outbreak of the Crimean War (1853–1856), which the empire survived with its territory intact under the Treaty of Paris, gaining admission to the Concert of Europe.

Personal life and death

Abdülmecid received a modern education, spoke French, and played Western classical music on the piano. He was an enthusiastic builder — Dolmabahçe Palace, completed in 1856, became the new imperial residence, and the Ortaköy Mosque and the Hırka-i Şerif Mosque bear his patronage. His enormous household and court spending deepened the empire's debt, which began in his reign with the first foreign loans of 1854. Frail and tubercular, he died on 25 June 1861 aged 38, leaving the throne to his brother Abdülaziz. Four of his sons later became sultans, the last four rulers of the empire.
