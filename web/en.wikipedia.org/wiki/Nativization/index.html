Nativization
FieldLinguistics (contact linguistics, creolistics, second-language acquisition)
Related conceptsIndigenization, loanword adaptation, creole genesis

In linguistics, nativization (also spelled nativisation) is the process by which a language, dialect, or linguistic feature is adapted to, or develops within, a local speech community so as to become native to it. The term is used in several related senses across sociolinguistics, creole studies, and the study of world Englishes.

Senses of the term

Loanword nativization refers to the phonological and morphological adaptation of borrowed words to the patterns of the borrowing language, so that they come to be treated as native vocabulary. Varietal nativization describes how a transplanted language — such as English in India, Nigeria, or Singapore — develops stable local norms of pronunciation, grammar, and usage, a process central to Braj Kachru's model of world Englishes. In creole studies, nativization denotes the acquisition of native speakers by a pidgin, accompanied by expansion and regularisation of its grammar, producing a creole; linguist Derek Bickerton's language bioprogram hypothesis controversially proposed that children drive this process using innate grammatical capacities. In second-language acquisition, the term may also refer to learners' first language becoming more deeply entrenched, or to a learner variety stabilising ("fossilizing").

Examples

Classic cases include the development of Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea from a trade pidgin into a creole with native speakers, and the deep structural and lexical localisation of English in South Asia, where nativized varieties such as Indian English have their own standard norms and literature.
