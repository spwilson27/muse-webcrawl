Cicuta

Cicuta (water hemlock) in flower.

Cicuta, commonly known as water hemlock, is a genus of four species of highly poisonous plants in the family Apiaceae. They are perennial herbaceous plants that grow in wet meadows, marshes, and along stream banks in temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. Cicuta species are among the most violently toxic plants native to North America and Europe, and are frequently confused with edible relatives such as wild carrot and parsnip.

Description

Water hemlocks grow from 1 to 2.5 metres tall, with hollow, often purple-streaked stems, pinnately compound leaves with toothed leaflets whose veins characteristically run to the notches between the teeth, and umbrella-shaped inflorescences (umbels) of small white flowers. The thickened rootstock contains horizontal chambers — a diagnostic feature visible when the root is cut lengthwise.

Toxicity

All parts of the plant, especially the roots, contain cicutoxin, a potent unsaturated alcohol that acts as a noncompetitive antagonist of the neurotransmitter GABA in the central nervous system. Ingestion — even of a small piece of root — can cause nausea, violent convulsions, and death from respiratory failure within hours. Livestock are commonly poisoned in spring when the roots are exposed in soft ground. Water hemlock should not be confused with poison hemlock (Conium maculatum), the plant of Socrates' execution, which contains different alkaloids; both are dangerous.

Species

  • Cicuta bulbifera — bulb-bearing water hemlock
  • Cicuta douglasii — western water hemlock
  • Cicuta maculata — spotted water hemlock
  • Cicuta virosa — northern water hemlock, cowbane
