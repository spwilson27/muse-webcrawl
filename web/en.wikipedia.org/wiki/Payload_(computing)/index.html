Payload (computing)

In computing and telecommunications, the payload is the part of transmitted data that is the actual intended message. Headers and metadata, sent solely to facilitate delivery, are considered overhead and are not part of the payload. The term is borrowed from transportation, where payload denotes the load carried by a vehicle that earns revenue or performs the mission, as distinct from the vehicle and its fuel.

In networking

In a network packet, the payload is the data carried within the packet after the headers of each protocol layer. Under encapsulation, the entire packet of a higher-layer protocol becomes the payload of the layer below: a TCP segment is the payload of an IP packet, which is itself the payload of an Ethernet frame. The payload fraction of a transmission determines its efficiency; excessive header overhead relative to payload reduces effective throughput.

In programming

In programming, the term commonly refers to the substantive part of data being exchanged — for example, the JSON body of an API request or response, as opposed to the HTTP headers or status line. It is also used for the data embedded in structured formats, such as the content of a JWT (JSON Web Token), whose middle structure is literally called its payload.

In computer security

In computer security, the payload is the part of malware that executes the intended harmful (or beneficial, in penetration testing) action. It is distinguished from the exploit that delivers it: a worm or virus may propagate using an exploit vector, but the payload might be a backdoor, ransomware encryption routine, keylogger, or a simple message. Frameworks such as Metasploit organize their code around standardized payloads (e.g., reverse shells) paired with many different exploits.
