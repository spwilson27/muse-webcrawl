The history of music covers the development of musical traditions in all cultures, from prehistoric bone flutes and ancient ritual song to the global popular music industry. In the Western tradition it is conventionally divided into the Medieval, Renaissance, Baroque, Classical, Romantic and Modern periods.

Music is a universal human activity; its written history begins with notation in the ancient and medieval worlds.

Prehistoric and ancient music

Bone flutes from sites such as Hohle Fels in Germany date back more than 40,000 years. Music was central to ritual in ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, India and Greece. The oldest known complete song is the Hurrian Hymn No. 6 (c. 1400 BC). Greek theorists such as Pythagoras studied the mathematics of intervals, and Greek modes influenced later theory. Ancient Indian and Chinese traditions developed sophisticated systems of melody (raga; pentatonic scales) that persist today.

Medieval and Renaissance

In medieval Europe, Gregorian chant dominated sacred music, and the notation developed by Guido d'Arezzo made transmission far more precise. Polyphony grew from organum at Notre Dame (Léonin and Pérotin) through the motets of Machaut. Renaissance composers such as Josquin des Prez, Palestrina and Byrd perfected imitative polyphony, while printing (Petrucci, 1501) spread music widely. Secular forms — madrigals, chansons and dances — flourished.

Baroque and Classical

Around 1600, opera was born in Florence, the basso continuo became standard, and tonality crystallised. Bach, Handel and Vivaldi mark the Baroque's culmination. The Classical period of Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven established the symphony, string quartet and sonata form, with clearer textures and public concerts replacing court patronage.

Romantic and modern

Nineteenth-century Romanticism prized expression and nationalism: Schubert's songs, Chopin's piano music, Wagner's operas, and the vast symphonies of Mahler. Around 1900, Debussy's impressionism, Stravinsky's rhythm and Schoenberg's atonality and twelve-tone technique broke with tradition. Meanwhile, recording technology (from Edison's phonograph of 1877) transformed music's reach.

Popular music

In the 20th century, African-American traditions gave rise to ragtime, blues, jazz, rhythm and blues, rock and roll, soul, funk and hip hop; country, folk revivals and electronic dance music developed alongside. The Beatles and other recording artists made the album a major art form. Digital production and streaming in the 21st century globalised genres from K-pop to Afrobeats, while classical and folk traditions continue to evolve.
