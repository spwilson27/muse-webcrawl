Transgender hormone therapy

Transgender hormone therapy, also called gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) or hormone replacement therapy (HRT), is a form of medical treatment in which sex hormones and other hormonal medications (such as androgen blockers) are administered to transgender or gender nonconforming individuals for the purpose of more closely aligning their secondary sexual characteristics with their gender identity.

Feminizing therapy

Feminizing hormone therapy typically combines an estrogen, most often estradiol, with an antiandrogen such as spironolactone or cyproterone acetate, or with a GnRH analogue to suppress testosterone production. Effects include breast development, redistribution of body fat, softening of the skin and reduced body hair growth.

Masculinizing therapy

Masculinizing hormone therapy uses testosterone, given by injection, gel or other preparations. Effects include deepening of the voice, growth of facial and body hair, increased muscle mass and cessation of menstruation. Many effects are gradual, developing over months to years, and some are irreversible.

Access and guidelines

Hormone therapy is provided under guidelines such as the Standards of Care of the World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH) and the clinical practice guidelines of the Endocrine Society. Access models vary by country and range from assessment-based gatekeeping to informed consent. In adolescents, puberty blockers may be used to suppress puberty before hormone therapy is considered.
