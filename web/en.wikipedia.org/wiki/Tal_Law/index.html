Tal Law

The Tal Law was an Israeli law passed by the Knesset on 23 July 2002 granting, under specified conditions, a deferral of military service to ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) yeshiva students. It is named after retired judge Zvi Tal, who headed the public committee whose recommendations formed the basis of the legislation.

Background

Since the founding of Israel, full-time yeshiva students had received deferments from conscription under the Torato Omanuto ("Torah study is his profession") arrangement, which originally applied to only a few hundred students but by the late 1990s covered tens of thousands. In 1998 the Supreme Court of Israel ruled that the defence minister had no authority to grant such blanket deferments and that primary legislation was required. The Tal Committee was appointed in 1999 to propose a settlement.

Provisions

The law allowed a yeshiva student who had deferred service to take, at age 22, a "decision year" during which he could work without being drafted; thereafter he could choose between abbreviated military service, civilian national service, or returning to the yeshiva with continued deferment. The stated aims were to regularise the deferments in law and gradually increase Haredi participation in military or civilian service and in the workforce. In practice, only a small proportion of eligible men chose service under the law's terms.

Invalidation and aftermath

On 21 February 2012 the Supreme Court ruled, by six votes to three, that the Tal Law was unconstitutional because it violated the principle of equality in sharing the burden of service, and that it could not be extended in its existing form. The law expired on 1 August 2012. Attempts to legislate a replacement — including the 2014 conscription law passed by the Yesh Atid-led coalition, its subsequent amendment, and further annulment by the Court in 2017 — kept the question of Haredi conscription at the centre of Israeli politics for years afterwards.
