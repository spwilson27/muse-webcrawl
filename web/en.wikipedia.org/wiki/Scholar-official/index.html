Scholar-official

A scholar-official of imperial China

Scholar-officials, also known as scholar-gentry or literati (Chinese: 士大夫, shìdàfū), were the educated elite who dominated government and society in imperial China for roughly two thousand years. They were civil servants appointed by the emperor, selected — at least in principle — for their mastery of Confucian learning, demonstrated through the imperial examinations.

History

The scholar-official class took shape during the Han dynasty, when officials began to be recruited on the basis of Confucian scholarship rather than purely by birth. The maturation of the examination system under the Sui and Tang dynasties, and its full flowering under the Song, made literary education the principal ladder to office, wealth, and prestige. Scholar-officials administered the empire at every level, from county magistrates to grand chancellors.

Culture

The ideal scholar-official was expected to combine administrative competence with literary and artistic refinement: poetry, calligraphy, and painting (the "three perfections") were marks of the cultivated gentleman. In retirement or disgrace, many withdrew to their estates, where the gentry families they headed dominated local society as landlords, teachers, and arbiters of custom.

Decline

The examination system was abolished in 1905, and the fall of the Qing dynasty in 1912 ended the scholar-officials as an institution, though their cultural prestige long outlasted them. Comparable examination-based elites existed in Korea (the yangban) and Vietnam.
