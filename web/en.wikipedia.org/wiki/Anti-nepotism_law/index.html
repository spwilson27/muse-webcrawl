Anti-nepotism law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anti-nepotism law
Type: Ethics and employment legislation
Purpose: Prohibit officials from employing or advancing relatives
Key example: U.S. federal anti-nepotism statute, 5 U.S.C. § 3110 (1967)
A government building: anti-nepotism laws restrict public officials from appointing relatives to positions in the agencies they control.

Anti-nepotism laws are laws and regulations that prohibit public officials and other decision-makers from employing, appointing, promoting, or advocating for their relatives. They are the principal legal instrument against nepotism in the public sector, and are usually paired with conflict-of-interest rules.

United States

The principal federal anti-nepotism statute, 5 U.S.C. § 3110, was enacted in 1967 as part of the Postal Revenue and Federal Salary Act. It is often called the "Bobby Kennedy law" because it followed President John F. Kennedy's appointment of his brother Robert as Attorney General in 1961. The statute bars a public official from appointing, employing, promoting, or advancing a relative in the agency in which the official serves or over which the official exercises control, and prohibits payment from appropriated funds for such appointments. The Office of Legal Counsel concluded in 2017 that the statute does not bar presidential appointments to White House staff positions, reading a separate appointment authority as an exception. Most U.S. states have their own anti-nepotism statutes for state and local government, and the House and Senate ethics rules restrict the employment of relatives in members' offices.

Other jurisdictions

Many countries regulate the employment of relatives in public administration through civil service codes and anti-corruption law. In the Philippines, the Administrative Code and anti-graft law prohibit appointments of relatives within the third civil degree in the same office. In India and elsewhere, recruitment rules and court decisions on equality of opportunity in public employment (Article 16 of the Indian Constitution) serve similar purposes.

Private sector

In the private sector, nepotism is generally lawful, and anti-nepotism policies are a matter of company governance rather than statute: many employers restrict relatives from supervising one another or working in the same chain of command, mainly to manage conflicts of interest, favouritism, and morale.
