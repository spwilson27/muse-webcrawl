The Anatolian beyliks (Turkish: Anadolu beylikleri) were Turkmen principalities (beyliks) established in Anatolia during the 11th to 13th centuries, first on the frontiers of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum and later flourishing after its decline following the Mongol victory at the Battle of Köse Dağ (1243) and the fragmentation of Ilkhanid authority in the 1330s. Each beylik was ruled by a bey and sustained by Turkmen tribal forces; together they transformed Anatolia from a predominantly Greek-speaking Christian land into a Turkish-speaking Muslim one.

History

First period

After the Seljuk victory at Manzikert in 1071, Turkmen bands pushed across Anatolia. Early frontier principalities included the Danishmends in north-central Anatolia, the Saltukids in Erzurum, the Mengujekids, Artukids in the southeast, and the Beylik of Çubukoğulları. Most of these were absorbed by the Sultanate of Rum during the 12th and early 13th centuries.

Second period

The Mongol invasion and the decline of Seljuk power after 1243 opened the way for a second, richer flowering of beyliks, especially along the Byzantine frontier in western Anatolia. The Karamanids of south-central Anatolia were the strongest, repeatedly challenging first the Ilkhanids and then the Ottomans. Maritime beyliks such as Aydın, Menteşe, Saruhan and Karesi raided the Aegean islands and Greek coasts; the Aydınid Umur Bey became famous for his naval expeditions. In the northeast, the Beylik of Eretna and its successor, Kadı Burhan al-Din's state, ruled Sivas and Kayseri, while the Dulkadir and Ramadanid beyliks formed buffer states between the Ottomans and Mamluk Egypt.

Ottoman absorption

The Ottoman principality, itself once a small frontier beylik around Söğüt, gradually annexed its neighbours — first by purchase and marriage (Germiyan, Hamid), then by conquest. Bayezid I annexed most western beyliks in the 1390s, but Timur's victory at Ankara (1402) briefly restored many of them. Through the 15th century Mehmed II and his successors re-annexed the beyliks one by one; Karaman, the last major rival, was absorbed in the 1470s, and the Ramadanids held out under Ottoman suzerainty until 1608.

Legacy

The beyliks left a rich architectural legacy of mosques, madrasas, caravanserais and tombs in towns such as Konya, Karaman, Birgi, Milas, Kastamonu and Beyşehir. Their courts patronised Turkish-language literature, helping establish Turkish as a language of administration and letters in Anatolia.
