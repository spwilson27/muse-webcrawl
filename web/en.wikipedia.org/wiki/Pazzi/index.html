The Pazzi were a noble Florentine family in the Middle Ages and the Renaissance. Originally from the Arno valley, they became wealthy bankers and prominent citizens of Florence, where they were the chief rivals of the Medici. Their name is remembered above all for the Pazzi conspiracy of 1478 and for the Pazzi Chapel, a masterpiece of Renaissance architecture.

History

Family legend traced the Pazzi to Pazzino de' Pazzi, a crusader said to have been among the first to scale the walls of Jerusalem in 1099 and to have brought back flints from the Holy Sepulchre used in Florence's Easter Scoppio del Carro ritual. By the fifteenth century the family ran a successful bank with branches abroad and intermarried with other great houses; Andrea de' Pazzi commissioned Filippo Brunelleschi to design the chapter house for the Franciscans of Santa Croce — the Pazzi Chapel — one of the earliest and purest works of Renaissance architecture.

Pazzi conspiracy

Rivalry with the Medici sharpened under Lorenzo de' Medici, whose laws curtailed Pazzi interests. On 26 April 1478, during High Mass in Florence Cathedral, conspirators led by Francesco de' Pazzi attacked Lorenzo and his brother Giuliano. Giuliano was stabbed to death; Lorenzo escaped with a wound and took refuge in the sacristy. The plot, backed by Pope Sixtus IV and involving Archbishop Francesco Salviati, collapsed within hours: enraged Florentines lynched or executed scores of conspirators, including Francesco, who was hanged from a window of the Palazzo della Signoria. The Pazzi were exiled, their property confiscated, their arms defaced, and the family name banned from public use.

Later history

The surviving Pazzi were permitted to return to Florence after Lorenzo's death in 1492, and members of the family later distinguished themselves in letters, diplomacy, and the church, though the family never recovered its former power. Maria Maddalena de' Pazzi (1566–1607), a Carmelite mystic, was canonised in 1669.
