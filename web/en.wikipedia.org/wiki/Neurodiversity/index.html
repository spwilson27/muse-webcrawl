Neurodiversity

The rainbow infinity symbol is widely used by the neurodiversity movement.

Neurodiversity is a framework for understanding human brain function and mental-behavioural traits that regards differences in brain function as normal variations within the population. It originated in the autism rights movement of the 1990s and holds that conditions such as autism, ADHD, dyslexia and others are not necessarily deficits to be cured, but forms of human diversity comparable to biodiversity, whose difficulties often arise from a mismatch between the individual and their social environment — an application of the social model of disability.

History

The term is commonly attributed to the Australian sociologist Judy Singer, who developed it in her 1998 honours thesis, and to journalist Harvey Blume, who used "neurodiversity" in The Atlantic in 1998 ("Neurodiversity may be every bit as crucial for the human race as biodiversity is for life in general"). Autistic self-advocates, including Jim Sinclair and the Autism Network International, laid the movement's foundations, rejecting cure-oriented agendas.

The neurodiversity paradigm

Writer Nick Walker formalized the "neurodiversity paradigm" in three principles: neurodiversity is a natural and valuable form of human diversity; the idea that there is one "normal" or "right" kind of mind is a culturally constructed fiction; and the social dynamics around neurodiversity parallel those of other forms of diversity and power. Individuals whose cognition fits societal norms are often called neurotypical, while those who diverge are neurodivergent (a term credited to activist Kassiane Asasumasu).

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that the paradigm may understate the needs of people with high support needs or severe impairments, and that it fits less severe conditions better than conditions involving profound intellectual disability. Supporters respond that neurodiversity is compatible with providing support and treatment, opposing only coercive normalization. The movement has influenced education, employment programs and mental-health discourse.
