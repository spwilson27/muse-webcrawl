Ring homomorphism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a ring homomorphism is a structure-preserving function between two rings. More explicitly, if R and S are rings, then a ring homomorphism is a function f : R → S such that for all a, b in R:

  • f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b)
  • f(a · b) = f(a) · f(b)
  • f(0R) = 0S

Authors who require rings to have a multiplicative identity usually also require f(1R) = 1S. A bijective ring homomorphism is a ring isomorphism; a homomorphism from a ring to itself is a ring endomorphism, and an isomorphism to itself an automorphism.

Properties

Ring homomorphisms map zero to zero and satisfy f(−a) = −f(a) and f(a−1) = f(a)−1 for units a. The kernel of f — the set of elements mapped to zero — is an ideal of R, and the image is a subring of S. A homomorphism is injective if and only if its kernel is trivial.

Fundamental theorem

The first isomorphism theorem for rings states that R/ker f is isomorphic to the image of f. This parallels the corresponding theorems for groups and modules, reflecting the fact that rings and their homomorphisms form a well-behaved algebraic category.

Examples

  • The identity map and the zero map (the latter, between unital rings, is excluded by the identity-preserving convention).
  • Complex conjugation, an automorphism of ℂ.
  • The quotient map R → R/I onto a quotient ring.
  • Evaluation of polynomials: for a commutative ring R and element r, the map R[x] → R sending a polynomial to its value at r.
  • The inclusion ℤ → ℚ → ℝ → ℂ.
