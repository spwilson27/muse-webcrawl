Eleatics

The Eleatics were a school of pre-Socratic philosophers in the early 5th century BC centred at Elea, a Greek colony in southern Italy (Magna Graecia). The school took its name from the city and was founded by Parmenides. Other members included Zeno of Elea and Melissus of Samos. Xenophanes is sometimes associated with the school as a precursor, though his connection is debated among scholars.

Philosophy

The Eleatics are famous for their radical monism and their use of deductive argument. Parmenides, in his poem On Nature, argued that reality is a single, ungenerated, indestructible, indivisible and unchanging sphere of Being; change, motion and plurality are illusions of the senses, since what-is cannot come from what-is-not. This made the Eleatics the first philosophers to privilege pure reason and logical argument over sensory experience.

Zeno's paradoxes

Zeno of Elea developed a series of paradoxes — including Achilles and the tortoise, the dichotomy, and the arrow — designed to defend Parmenides' doctrine by showing that the assumption of plurality and motion leads to absurdity. The paradoxes have provoked philosophical and mathematical discussion for over two millennia and were only fully addressed with the modern concepts of convergent series and the calculus.

Melissus and legacy

Melissus of Samos, the school’s later systematiser, argued that Being is infinite in extent and incorporeal. The Eleatic challenge — to explain how a world of change can arise — set the agenda for the pluralist responses of Empedocles, Anaxagoras and the atomists Leucippus and Democritus, and profoundly influenced Plato, whose dialogue Parmenides stages a meeting between the young Socrates and the Eleatic master.
