Gruyère (French: gruyère) is a hard Swiss cheese that originated in the district of Gruyères in the canton of Fribourg, Switzerland. Made from unpasteurised cow's milk, it is named after the town of Gruyères and has been produced in the region since at least the 12th century. Since 2001 it has been protected in Switzerland as an appellation d'origine protégée (AOP).

Characteristics

Gruyère is sweet but slightly salty, with a flavour that varies widely with age: young cheeses (aged about five months) are creamy and nutty, while fully aged wheels — some matured 12 months or more and sold as réserve or surchoix — are earthier, more complex and develop small crunchy crystals and fine cracks. The cheese is ivory to pale yellow, dense and smooth; unlike Emmental, it has few or no holes. A wheel typically weighs 25–40 kg and is made from around 400 litres of milk.

Production

Raw milk is heated in a copper vat, rennet is added, and the curd is cut, cooked and pressed into moulds, then brined and aged on spruce planks in cellars. Production is governed by strict AOP rules covering the delimited area (the cantons of Fribourg, Vaud, Neuchâtel, Jura and parts of Bern), the cows' feed (no silage) and traditional methods; alpine Gruyère (Gruyère d'alpage) is made in mountain chalets during the summer grazing season.

Uses

Gruyère is one of the classic cheeses of fondue (traditionally blended with Vacherin Fribourgeois in the moitié-moitié), of the croque-monsieur, French onion soup and quiche Lorraine, and is widely used in gratins and as a table cheese. In 2022 a US federal appeals court ruled that "gruyere" is a generic term in the United States and not protectable as a certification mark there, a decision criticised by Swiss and French producers.
