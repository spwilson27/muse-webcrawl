Etruscans

The Etruscans were an Italic people of ancient Italy, centred in Etruria — roughly modern Tuscany, western Umbria and northern Lazio — whose civilisation flourished from about 900 BC until its gradual assimilation into the Roman Republic in the late 1st century BC. The Etruscans called themselves Rasenna; the Greeks called them Tyrrhenians, the source of the name of the Tyrrhenian Sea.

Origins

The origins of the Etruscans were debated in antiquity: Herodotus claimed they migrated from Lydia in Anatolia, while Dionysius of Halicarnassus argued they were indigenous to Italy. Modern archaeology and genetic studies generally support substantial continuity with the earlier Villanovan culture of Iron Age Italy.

Civilisation

The Etruscans formed a loose league of independent city-states, including Veii, Tarquinii, Cerveteri, Vulci and Clusium. They were accomplished metalworkers, engineers, traders and artists, famous for bucchero pottery, bronze work, gold jewellery, tomb painting and temple architecture. Etruscan religion, with its elaborate divination practices such as haruspicy (reading entrails), strongly influenced Roman religion.

Language

The Etruscan language, written in an alphabet derived from Greek, is not Indo-European and is only partially understood, since few long texts survive. It disappeared as a spoken language around the early centuries AD.

Decline

Etruscan power waned from the 5th century BC under pressure from Celts in the north, Greeks in the south and the rising Romans. Rome conquered Veii in 396 BC, and city after city fell or allied itself to Rome; the last Etruscan cities were absorbed in the 1st century BC. Nevertheless, early Rome owed much to the Etruscans — tradition held that Rome's last three kings were Etruscan, and Roman symbols such as the fasces and the triumph have Etruscan roots.
