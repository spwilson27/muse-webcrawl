Choice modelling

Field: Econometrics, marketing research, transport planning

Key figure: Daniel McFadden

Core concept: Random utility theory

Choice modelling attempts to model the decisions of individuals or groups among a set of discrete alternatives, such as which product to buy, which transport mode to use, or which job to accept. It is used in marketing, transport, environmental economics and health economics, both to understand behaviour and to predict demand for new alternatives.

Theoretical basis

Most choice models rest on random utility theory, developed from work by Thurstone and formalised by Daniel McFadden in the 1970s. The utility of an alternative is split into an observable component — a function of the alternative's attributes (price, travel time, quality) and the decision-maker's characteristics — and an unobserved random component. Assuming decision-makers choose the alternative with the highest utility, different assumptions about the random term give different models; the best-known, the multinomial logit model, follows from independently Gumbel-distributed errors.

Models

The multinomial logit is valued for its closed-form choice probabilities but imposes the restrictive independence of irrelevant alternatives property. More flexible models include the nested logit, mixed logit (random parameters logit), which allows taste variation and arbitrary substitution patterns, and latent class models. McFadden received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2000 in part for this work.

Data and applications

Choice models are estimated from revealed-preference data (observed real-world choices) or stated-preference data from choice experiments, in which respondents choose among designed hypothetical alternatives; conjoint analysis is a related stated-preference technique. Applications include forecasting rail and road demand, valuing environmental amenities and health states, pricing and product design, and estimating willingness to pay for attributes.
