Cycle of erosion

The cycle of erosion is an idealized model that explains the development of relief in landscapes. The model starts with the rapid uplift of a landscape by tectonic processes, followed by a very gradual removal of material by agents of erosion, leading eventually to a low, featureless plain — a peneplain — close to sea level (the base level). Renewed uplift can restart the cycle.

The Davisian model

The American geographer William Morris Davis proposed the model between 1884 and 1899, describing landforms in terms of "structure, process and stage". In the youthful stage, streams cut deep V-shaped valleys into the uplifted surface, with waterfalls and lakes, and broad undissected uplands between valleys. In maturity, relief is at its greatest, slopes decline, drainage becomes integrated and floodplains widen. In old age, the landscape is worn down to a peneplain with gentle slopes, broad floodplains and isolated residual hills called monadnocks.

Criticism and alternatives

The model was dominant in geomorphology for half a century but was later criticised for assuming prolonged tectonic stability and for its qualitative, descriptive character. Walther Penck proposed an alternative model emphasising simultaneous uplift and denudation; later work by Lester King (pediplanation in arid landscapes) and John Hack (dynamic equilibrium) led to modern process-based and quantitative approaches. Nevertheless, Davis's terminology of youthful, mature and old-age landscapes remains in common descriptive use.
