Human sacrifice

PracticeRitual killing of human beings as offerings to deities or spirits
AttestedPrehistoric and ancient societies worldwide; persisted into the early modern period

Human sacrifice is the act of killing one or more humans as part of a ritual, usually intended as an offering to a god, gods, or ancestral spirits. It has been practised in many cultures throughout human history, and its forms have ranged from retainer sacrifice — the killing of servants to accompany a ruler in death — to sacrifices of war captives, slaves, children, or volunteers, offered for purposes such as consecrating buildings, ensuring fertility, averting calamity, or divination.

Ancient world

Retainer sacrifice is documented in early dynastic Egypt, in the royal burials of Ur in Mesopotamia, and in ancient China, where it was largely replaced by tomb figures such as the Terracotta Army. In the Mediterranean world, the Carthaginians were accused by Greek and Roman writers of sacrificing children to Baal Hammon and Tanit; interpretation of the Carthage tophet remains debated. Greek and Roman religion mostly rejected human sacrifice, though Rome buried foreign couples alive in exceptional crises early in its history and banned the practice by senatorial decree in 97 BC.

The Americas

Human sacrifice was central to several Mesoamerican religions. The Aztecs offered captives and others on temple pyramids, extracting the heart to nourish the sun god Huitzilopochtli; the scale of Aztec sacrifice, reported in the tens of thousands under some rulers, is debated by historians. The Maya also practised sacrifice, including offerings at the Sacred Cenote of Chichen Itza. In the Andes, the Inca performed capacocha ceremonies in which children were sacrificed at high mountain shrines; several remarkably preserved mummies, such as the Llullaillaco children, have been recovered.

Asia and Europe

Ancient Chinese and Shang-dynasty practice included large-scale sacrifice of captives; Vedic India knew the purushamedha at least symbolically. In pre-Christian northern Europe, accounts describe sacrifices at sites such as Uppsala, and some Iron Age "bog bodies," including Tollund Man, may have been ritual victims. In Japan, the custom of junshi — retainers following their lord in death — persisted in attenuated form for centuries.

Decline

Human sacrifice declined as universalizing religions — including Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism — and expanding states suppressed it, though isolated cases persisted into the 19th and even the 20th century, notably in parts of West Africa and in criminal or occult contexts in the modern era. Modern law treats ritual killing as murder, and the subject remains important to archaeology and to debates about violence and social stratification in early states.
