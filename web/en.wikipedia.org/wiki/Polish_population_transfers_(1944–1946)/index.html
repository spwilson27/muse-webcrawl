Polish population transfers (1944–1946)

The Polish population transfers of 1944–1946 were the forced migrations of ethnic Poles from the former eastern territories of Poland (the Kresy), annexed by the Soviet Union after the Second World War, to the new Polish state whose borders had been shifted westward. About 1.5 to 2 million Poles were moved, most of them resettled in the so-called Recovered Territories taken from Germany. The transfers were part of the wider redrawing of the map of Central and Eastern Europe agreed by the Allies at Yalta and Potsdam.

Background

Under the Curzon Line settlement confirmed at the Yalta Conference (1945), Poland's eastern border moved west; the cities of Wilno (Vilnius) and Lwów (Lviv), and the regions of Eastern Galicia and Volhynia, passed to the Soviet republics of Lithuania, Belarus and Ukraine. "Repatriation" agreements signed in 1944 between the Soviet-sponsored Polish Committee of National Liberation and the three Soviet republics provided for population exchanges.

The transfers

Transports began in late 1944. Many Poles fled or were pressured to leave, especially from Ukrainian territories where the Volhynia massacres of 1943–1944 had already driven Polish communities westward. Repatriants travelled in overcrowded trains, often in cattle wagons, bringing only what they could carry. On arrival, most were settled in Silesia, Pomerania and other territories from which the German population was being expelled. Conditions were chaotic: housing and land allocation were improvised, and some people were displaced repeatedly.

Scale and aftermath

Historians estimate roughly 1.18 million Poles were transferred under the 1944 agreements by 1946, with additional movements bringing the total to around 1.5–2 million. Hundreds of thousands of Poles remained in the Soviet Union; further "repatriations" followed in 1955–1959. The transfers made postwar Poland one of the most ethnically homogeneous countries in Europe and left a lasting mark on Polish collective memory, particularly around the loss of Lwów and Wilno.
