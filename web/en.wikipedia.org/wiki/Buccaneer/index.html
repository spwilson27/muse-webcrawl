Buccaneer

Buccaneers were a kind of privateer or pirate active in the Caribbean Sea during the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. The word derives from the French boucan, a frame used to smoke meat, after the hunters of Hispaniola and Tortuga who cured wild cattle and pig meat that way.

Origins

The first buccaneers were French, English, and Dutch hunters and runaway servants on Hispaniola who, driven off by Spanish forces, turned to raiding Spanish shipping and settlements from bases such as Tortuga and Port Royal, Jamaica. Bands known as the Brethren of the Coast elected their captains and divided plunder by agreed shares.

Golden age and decline

Under leaders such as Henry Morgan, buccaneers sacked wealthy Spanish towns including Porto Bello, Maracaibo, and Panama City in 1671. European governments at first tolerated or sponsored them as a weapon against Spain, but as colonial trade grew more valuable than plunder, treaties and naval patrols suppressed them; by the 1690s buccaneering had largely merged into outright piracy or disappeared.

In culture

Alexandre Exquemelin's 1678 account The Buccaneers of America shaped the popular image of the buccaneer, later carried into novels, films, and the name of the Tampa Bay Buccaneers football team.
