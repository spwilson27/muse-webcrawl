Trench warfare

Trench warfare is a type of land warfare using occupied lines of trenches and other fortifications, in which combatants are well protected from small arms fire and substantially sheltered from artillery. It became archetypically associated with the Western Front of the First World War (1914–1918), where a system of trenches eventually stretched roughly 475 miles from the North Sea to the Swiss frontier.

Development

Field entrenchment had appeared in the American Civil War and the Russo-Japanese War, but the lethality of modern rifles, machine guns, and quick-firing artillery made defence dominant after the "Race to the Sea" in autumn 1914. A typical system comprised three parallel lines—front, support, and reserve—connected by communication trenches, fronted by barbed wire and a contested strip of no man's land.

Life in the trenches

Units rotated through front-line duty, enduring mud, flooding, lice, trench foot, and the threat of snipers, raids, mining, and poison gas. Major offensives such as Verdun and the Somme in 1916 and Passchendaele in 1917 gained little ground at enormous cost. New tactics—infiltration by stormtroopers, creeping barrages, tanks, and combined-arms methods—gradually restored mobility in 1918.

Later use

Trenches recurred wherever mobility stalled: the Korean War's static phase, the Iran–Iraq War, the Eritrean–Ethiopian War, and in parts of the Russo-Ukrainian War from 2014 onward, where minefields, artillery, and drones have recreated aspects of the First World War deadlock.
