Milky Way

The Milky Way is the galaxy that includes the Solar System, with the name describing the galaxy's appearance from Earth: a hazy band of light seen in the night sky formed from stars that cannot be individually distinguished by the naked eye. It is a barred spiral galaxy with a diameter of roughly 100,000 to 200,000 light-years and is estimated to contain 100 to 400 billion stars and at least that many planets.

The Solar System lies about 26,000 light-years from the Galactic Center, on the inner edge of the Orion Arm. At the centre sits a supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A*, of about four million solar masses, whose imaging by the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration — telescopes coordinated worldwide and publicised by institutions including NASA — was released in 2022.

Etymology and mythology

The name is a translation of Latin via lactea, itself from Greek for "milky circle". Greek myth explained the band as milk spilled from the breast of the goddess Hera. Many cultures saw a road, river or path in the sky — the Chinese "Silver River", for example. Galileo Galilei resolved the band into individual stars with his telescope in 1610, and philosophers such as Immanuel Kant speculated that it was a disc of stars seen from within.

Appearance

From Earth, the Milky Way appears brightest toward the constellation Sagittarius, where the Galactic Center lies behind dense clouds of interstellar dust that obscure it in visible light. It is best seen from dark-sky sites far from light pollution — the kind of view shown in today's featured picture on the main page. Up to a few thousand individual stars, plus planets and the band itself, are visible to the unaided eye on a clear moonless night.

Structure

The galaxy consists of a central bar-shaped core of stars surrounded by a disc of gas, dust and stars organised into spiral arms, all enclosed in a vast halo of old stars, globular clusters and dark matter, which accounts for most of its mass. The disc's rotation curve — stars far from the centre orbit almost as fast as those close in — was key evidence, developed by Vera Rubin and others, for dark matter's existence.

Neighbourhood

The Milky Way belongs to the Local Group of galaxies, dominated by it and the Andromeda Galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away. The two are approaching each other and are expected to collide and merge in roughly 4–5 billion years, forming a new elliptical galaxy sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda". Satellite galaxies include the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, visible from the southern hemisphere.

Formation and history

The Milky Way began forming soon after the Big Bang, about 13.6 billion years ago, growing by accreting smaller galaxies whose streams of stars still cross the halo. Data from the European ESA Gaia mission, which has measured positions and motions for well over a billion stars, have revolutionised understanding of this history, revealing past mergers such as the one with the Gaia-Enceladus dwarf galaxy around 8–11 billion years ago.
