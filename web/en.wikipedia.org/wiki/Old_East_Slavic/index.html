Old East Slavic (also called Old Russian, though the term is disputed) was the language of the East Slavs during roughly the 9th to 14th centuries, used in Kievan Rus' and its successor states. It is the common ancestor of modern Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian.

History

Old East Slavic emerged from Proto-Slavic as the East Slavic tribes settled the forest and forest-steppe zones of Eastern Europe. Written records begin after the Christianisation of Rus' in 988, which brought literacy in the form of Old Church Slavonic texts written in Cyrillic; the local East Slavic vernacular increasingly influenced writing, producing a distinctly East Slavic literary language.

From the 14th century, political division — between the Grand Duchy of Lithuania in the west and the northeastern principalities that became Muscovy — accelerated dialect divergence toward separate Ruthenian (ancestor of Ukrainian and Belarusian) and Russian traditions.

Sources

Important texts include:

  • The Primary Chronicle (Tale of Bygone Years), compiled in Kyiv in the early 12th century.
  • The Tale of Igor's Campaign, an epic poem of the late 12th century.
  • Russkaya Pravda, the legal code of Kievan Rus'.
  • The Novgorod birch bark letters — more than a thousand everyday documents (letters, notes, exercises) scratched on birch bark from the 11th century onward, invaluable for reconstructing the spoken Novgorod dialect, which differs notably from the Kyiv standard.
  • The Ostromir Gospels (1056–57), the oldest dated East Slavic manuscript.

Features

Old East Slavic is characterised by pleophony (polnoglasie) — the development of Proto-Slavic *TorT sequences into ToroT (e.g. *gordъ "town" → gorodъ) — distinguishing East Slavic from South and West Slavic. It preserved nasal vowels only vestigially, merged the yers (ъ, ь) in weak positions (their fall, around the 12th–13th centuries, reshaped the syllable structure of all East Slavic languages), and had a dual number alongside singular and plural.
