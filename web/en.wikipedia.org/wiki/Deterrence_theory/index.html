Deterrence theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Deterrence theory is a theory of international relations and security that holds that an adversary can be dissuaded from an action — especially attack — by the credible threat of unacceptable retaliation. It became the organising doctrine of the nuclear age: Bernard Brodie observed in 1946 that the purpose of military establishments had shifted from winning wars to averting them. Thomas Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict (1960) formalised deterrence as bargaining under risk, introducing concepts of commitment, brinkmanship and the "threat that leaves something to chance".

Nuclear deterrence and beyond

Cold War practice elaborated massive retaliation, flexible response, second-strike survivability and mutual assured destruction (MAD), with arms control partly designed to stabilise deterrence (protecting retaliatory forces, banning missile defences in the ABM Treaty). Deterrence theory has been extended — to conventional, cyber, space and cross-domain threats, and to non-state actors — and criticised on empirical (deterrence successes are hard to prove), psychological (leaders are not unitary rational actors) and moral grounds (threatening mass killing). Contemporary debates include deterring limited aggression under the nuclear shadow ("stability–instability paradox"), extended deterrence's credibility for allies, and the effects of missile defence and precision strike on strategic stability.
