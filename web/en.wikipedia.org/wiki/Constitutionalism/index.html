Constitutionalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Constitutionalism is the idea — and the set of practices realising it — that government authority derives from, and is limited by, a fundamental law: a constitution. It is "limited government" in the strong sense: not merely that a state has a constitution (nearly all do, including authoritarian ones, whose "sham" or "window-dressing" constitutions scholars distinguish), but that the constitution actually constrains power-holders through enforceable rules, independent courts, free elections and protected rights.

History and debates

Its intellectual sources run from medieval notions of fundamental law and resistance rights, through Locke and the American and French revolutions, to the post-1945 and post-1989 waves of constitution-making in which rights-protecting courts became standard. Concepts within constitutionalism include the rule of law, separation of powers, democratic self-government and federalism — sometimes in tension (the counter-majoritarian difficulty) and lately tested by "abusive constitutionalism": elected governments using constitutional amendment and court-packing to entrench themselves, studied in Hungary, Venezuela, Poland and elsewhere. Comparative scholarship (Ginsburg, Hirschl) examines constitutional endurance, judicial empowerment's political origins, and whether constitutionalism can be detached from liberalism ("authoritarian constitutionalism" in Singapore or China discussions).
