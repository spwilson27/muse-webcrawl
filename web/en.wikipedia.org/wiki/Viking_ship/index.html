Viking ships were the wooden vessels built by the Norse peoples of Scandinavia during the Viking Age and its aftermath. Built with overlapping (clinker) planking on a true keel, rigged with a single square sail and rowed when necessary, they ranged from small open boats to great warships. Fast, flexible, and able to operate in very shallow water, they underpinned Norse exploration and raiding across the North Atlantic — reaching Iceland, Greenland, and North America — as well as river journeys deep into Eastern Europe.

Construction

Viking ships were built shell-first: a keel plank grown into curved stem and stern posts, with strakes of oak (or pine) overlapped and riveted with iron roves. Frames were inserted afterward and lashed or treenailed to the planking, giving a hull that was light and flexible enough to work in heavy seas rather than resist them. A side-hung rudder on the right ("steerboard") side — the origin of the word starboard — was controlled by a tiller. A single mast carried a square sail of wool or linen, often depicted as striped; the shallow draught, sometimes under a metre, let ships beach directly and penetrate far up rivers.

Famous finds

Several ships survive thanks to burial customs and waterlogging. The Oseberg ship (buried 834) is richly carved; the Gokstad ship (c. 890) is a seaworthy karvi whose replica crossed the Atlantic in 1893; the five Skuldelev ships, sunk as a blockade in Roskilde Fjord in the 11th century, include both a great longship built in Dublin and ocean-going knarrs. Replicas such as Draken Harald Hårfagre have demonstrated the type's sailing qualities in modern voyages.

Ship burials

High-status Norse burials sometimes placed the dead in a ship with grave goods — as at Oseberg, Gokstad, Tune, and in the boat graves of Sutton Hoo (Anglo-Saxon England) — reflecting the ship's central place in Norse life and beliefs about the journey to the afterlife.
