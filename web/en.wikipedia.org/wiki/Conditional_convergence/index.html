Conditional convergence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Partial sums of the alternating harmonic series, the classic conditionally convergent series

In mathematics, a series or integral is said to be conditionally convergent if it converges, but does not converge absolutely. That is, a series Σan converges conditionally if Σan exists (as a finite value) while Σ|an| diverges to infinity.

Examples

The classic example is the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + 1/5 − ⋯, which converges to ln 2, while the corresponding harmonic series of absolute values diverges. More generally, alternating series whose terms decrease monotonically to zero converge by the alternating series test, but often only conditionally.

Riemann series theorem

The striking feature of conditional convergence is captured by the Riemann series theorem: the terms of a conditionally convergent series of real numbers can be rearranged to converge to any desired value, or to diverge. This happens because the positive and negative parts of the series each diverge, so judicious ordering can steer the partial sums anywhere. Absolutely convergent series, by contrast, are unaffected by rearrangement.

Integrals and higher dimensions

An improper integral is conditionally convergent if it converges but the integral of the absolute value diverges; the Dirichlet integral ∫ sin x / x dx is a standard example. Analogous notions apply to series in Banach spaces, where unconditional convergence is stronger than convergence and coincides with absolute convergence only in finite dimensions.
