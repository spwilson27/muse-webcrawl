Squid (weapon)

Squid
Type:Anti-submarine mortar
Place of origin:United Kingdom
In service:1943–1977
Wars:Second World War, Cold War

A Squid mortar aboard a preserved Second World War escort vessel.

Squid was a British World War II ship-mounted anti-submarine weapon. It consisted of a three-barrelled mortar which launched depth charges ahead of the ship. It was developed during the Second World War and entered service in 1943, replacing the earlier Hedgehog spigot mortar. In 1955 the twin-barrelled Limbo system began to supersede it, though Squid remained in service with several navies into the 1970s.

Design

The weapon comprised three smooth-bore barrels, 12 inches (305 mm) in calibre, mounted in a slightly splayed frame so that the three projectiles landed in a triangular pattern around the target. Each bomb contained about 94 kg (207 lb) of Minol explosive and sank at roughly 13.3 m/s. Unlike contact-fused weapons such as Hedgehog, Squid's bombs were fitted with clockwork time fuses set automatically by signals from the ship's sonar, so all three detonated at the depth of the target submarine. Firing was also automatic, triggered when the sonar range matched the predicted position, allowing the attacking ship to keep the U-boat in sonar contact right up to the moment of attack rather than losing it in the blind spot astern that plagued depth-charge attacks.

Effectiveness

Squid was credited with a markedly higher kill probability than conventional depth charges; post-war analysis suggested a success rate several times better. It was first used in combat in 1944 and was credited with a share in the destruction of several U-boats, including U-333 and U-972.
