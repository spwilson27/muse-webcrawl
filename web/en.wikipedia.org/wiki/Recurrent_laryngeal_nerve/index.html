Recurrent laryngeal nerve

The recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) is a branch of the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) that supplies all the intrinsic muscles of the larynx, with the exception of the cricothyroid muscles. There are two recurrent laryngeal nerves, right and left. The right and left nerves are not symmetrical, with the left nerve looping under the aortic arch and the right nerve looping under the right subclavian artery and travelling upward on the opposite side.

Structure

The recurrent laryngeal nerves branch from the vagus nerve, relative to which they get their name: "recurrent" from the Latin re- (back) and currere (to run), because the nerve fibre path goes inferiorly and then superiorly to the opposite side to the larynx.

On the right side, the nerve loops under and behind the subclavian artery, then ascends in the tracheoesophageal groove. On the left side, the nerve loops under the aortic arch distal to the ductus arteriosus (ligamentum arteriosum), then ascends similarly in the tracheoesophageal groove. Both nerves enter the larynx deep to the inferior constrictor muscle, passing just deep to the cricothyroid joint, where they are termed the inferior laryngeal nerves.

Function

The recurrent laryngeal nerves control all intrinsic muscles of the larynx except for the cricothyroid muscle. These muscles act on length, tension, and opening of the vocal cords. The nerves also carry sensory information from the mucous membranes of the larynx below the lower surface of the vocal fold.

Clinical significance

The nerves are vulnerable to injury during surgery of the head and neck, especially thyroid and parathyroid surgery, and during anterior cervical spine surgery. Injury can cause vocal cord paralysis, resulting in hoarseness, a weak voice, and in bilateral injury, potentially life-threatening airway obstruction. The left nerve, with its longer course through the chest, also may be affected by intrathoracic disease, such as an enlarged left atrium (Ortner's syndrome) or aortic aneurysm.

Evolutionary significance

The recurrent laryngeal nerve is often cited as evidence of evolution. In fish, the branchial arch nerves pass behind the corresponding arterial arches; in tetrapods the arteries were remodelled during neck elongation, carrying the nerve with them. The extreme case is the giraffe, in which the left recurrent laryngeal nerve travels several metres down the neck and back up, even though its direct path would be only a few centimetres.
