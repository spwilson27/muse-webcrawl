Magic (illusion)

Magic, also known as stage magic, conjuring or illusion, is a performing art in which audiences are entertained by tricks, effects and illusions that appear impossible or supernatural. The magician achieves these effects through natural means — sleight of hand, misdirection, mechanical devices, and psychology — while presenting them as extraordinary feats.

Categories of effects

Magic effects are commonly classified as productions (making something appear), vanishes, transformations, transpositions, levitations, escapes, penetrations and predictions. Performance styles include close-up magic performed at arm's length, parlour magic for small groups, stage magic for large audiences, and grand illusion involving large apparatus, such as sawing a woman in half or making landmarks appear to vanish, as in David Copperfield's televised illusion with the Statue of Liberty.

History

Conjuring is ancient — the cups and balls routine is depicted in Egyptian tomb paintings and was described by Seneca — but modern stage magic developed in the 19th century with Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin, who brought magic from fairgrounds into theatres and established the modern image of the evening-dressed magician. The Golden Age of magic around 1900 produced stars such as Harry Houdini, famous for escapes, and Howard Thurston. In the later 20th and 21st centuries, performers such as David Blaine and Derren Brown popularised street magic and mentalism on television.

Ethics and exposure

Magicians traditionally guard their methods under a code of secrecy, and organisations such as the Magic Circle (founded 1905 in London) and the Society of American Magicians promote the art while discouraging casual exposure of secrets, though debate continues about televised "masking" and internet revelations.
