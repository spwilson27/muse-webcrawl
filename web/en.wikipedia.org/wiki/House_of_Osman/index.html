House of Osman

The House of Osman (Ottoman Turkish: Âl-i Osman), also known as the Ottoman dynasty, was the ruling family of the Ottoman Empire from its foundation around 1299 until the abolition of the sultanate in 1922. Founded by Osman I, a Turkish tribal leader in northwestern Anatolia, the dynasty produced 36 sultans in an unbroken male line over more than six centuries.

History

Osman I (died 1323/24) established a small principality (beylik) around Söğüt that expanded at the expense of the Byzantine Empire under his son Orhan. Later sultans — Murad I, Bayezid I, Mehmed II "the Conqueror", who took Constantinople in 1453, Selim I and Suleiman the Magnificent — transformed the state into a world empire spanning southeastern Europe, western Asia and North Africa. From the 16th century the sultans also held the title of Caliph.

Succession and court

Succession was not governed by primogeniture: in the early centuries royal sons competed for the throne, often by open fratricide, which Mehmed II's law code formally sanctioned. From the 17th century, succession passed to the eldest male dynast, and princes were confined to the palace in the kafes ("cage"). The dynasty's power was mediated through the imperial harem, where the valide sultan (queen mother) could wield great influence.

End of the dynasty

The last sultan, Mehmed VI, left Istanbul in November 1922 after the Grand National Assembly of Turkey abolished the sultanate; his cousin Abdülmecid II served briefly as caliph only, until the caliphate too was abolished in 1924 and the dynasty's members were exiled. They were permitted to return to Turkey decades later. The head of the house in the 21st century is Harun Osman (born 1932), a great-great-grandson of Sultan Abdulmejid I.
