Startle response

The startle response is a largely unconscious defensive response to sudden or threatening stimuli, such as sudden noise or sharp movement. It is associated with negative affect. Usually the onset of the startle response is a startle reflex reaction. The startle reflex is a brainstem reflectory reaction (reflex) that serves to protect vulnerable parts, such as the back of the neck (whole-body startle) and the eyes (eyeblink), and facilitates escape from sudden stimuli. It is found across the lifespan of many species. A variety of responses may occur because of the individual's emotional state, posture, preparation, and the presence of competing reflexes.

Startle reflex

The acoustic startle reflex is mediated by a short pathway through the pons: auditory input reaches the cochlear nuclei and then the caudal pontine reticular formation, which activates spinal motor neurons to produce a rapid whole-body flinch, typically within tens of milliseconds. In humans the most reliable component measured is the eyeblink, recorded by electromyography of the orbicularis oculi muscle.

Neuropsychology

The magnitude of the startle response is modulated by emotional state: it is potentiated during fear and anxiety and attenuated during pleasant states, a phenomenon known as fear-potentiated startle. This modulation is mediated by the amygdala and is widely used in research on anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, and schizophrenia, where prepulse inhibition of startle is often reduced.

Prepulse inhibition

When a weak stimulus (a prepulse) precedes the startling stimulus by a short interval, the response is reduced — an effect called prepulse inhibition, used as an operational measure of sensorimotor gating.

In infants

A related primitive reflex in infants is the Moro reflex, an involuntary response to a sensation of falling or a loud noise, which normally disappears within the first months of life.
