Fauna of New Zealand

The fauna of New Zealand is shaped by the archipelago's long isolation. New Zealand separated from the supercontinent Gondwana about 85 million years ago, and its animals evolved for millions of years in the near-absence of land mammals. The result was an ecosystem dominated by birds, many of them flightless, together with ancient reptiles and invertebrates found nowhere else.

Birds

Birds occupied niches taken by mammals elsewhere. The largest were the moa, nine species of giant flightless ratites, the biggest standing over three metres tall; they were hunted to extinction within a few centuries of Polynesian settlement around the 13th–14th centuries, and their predator, the enormous Haast's eagle, died out with them. Surviving icons include the five species of kiwi, the nocturnal, flightless kākāpō — the world's heaviest parrot — the weka, takahē, and kea, an alpine parrot noted for its curiosity and intelligence. Seabirds are exceptionally diverse: New Zealand hosts many species of penguins, albatrosses, and petrels, and is sometimes called the seabird capital of the world.

Other animals

The only native land mammals are bats — the long-tailed bat and the lesser short-tailed bat, the latter spending much time foraging on the ground. The tuatara, sole survivor of an order of reptiles that flourished 200 million years ago, survives on offshore islands. Native frogs of the genus Leiopelma are among the most primitive frogs alive. Invertebrates include the giant wētā, tree-sized relatives of crickets, giant land snails (Powelliphanta), and the peripatus or velvet worm. Marine mammals are abundant, including Hector's dolphin, one of the smallest and rarest dolphins.

Introduced species and conservation

Humans brought rats, stoats, possums, deer, and other mammals, which devastated birds that had evolved without mammalian predators; many species survive only on predator-free offshore islands or in fenced mainland sanctuaries. Conservation efforts, led by the Department of Conservation, include ambitious eradication programmes under the Predator Free 2050 goal and world-leading island restoration projects.
