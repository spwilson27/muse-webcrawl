Georg Lukács

Georg Lukács

Born: 13 April 1885, Budapest, Austria-Hungary

Died: 4 June 1971, Budapest, Hungary

Notable work: History and Class Consciousness (1923)

Georg Lukács (Hungarian: Lukács György; 13 April 1885 – 4 June 1971) was a Hungarian Marxist philosopher, literary historian and critic. A founder of the tradition known as Western Marxism, he is best known for his theory of reification and class consciousness, and for major contributions to the theory of the novel and literary realism.

Life

Born into a wealthy Budapest banking family, Lukács studied in Budapest, Berlin and Heidelberg, where he was influenced by Max Weber and Georg Simmel. His early works, Soul and Form (1911) and The Theory of the Novel (written 1914–15, published 1920), predate his conversion to Marxism in 1918–19, when he joined the Hungarian Communist Party and served as a commissar in Béla Kun's short-lived Hungarian Soviet Republic. After its fall he lived in exile in Vienna, Berlin and Moscow, returned to Hungary in 1945, briefly served as Minister of Culture in Imre Nagy's government during the 1956 Hungarian Revolution, and was deported to Romania before being allowed home.

Philosophy

History and Class Consciousness (1923) developed the concept of reification (Verdinglichung) — the process by which social relations come to appear as relations between things — and argued that the proletariat could become the conscious subject-object of history. The book profoundly influenced 20th-century critical theory, including the Frankfurt School, though it was condemned by the Communist International and disavowed by Lukács himself under pressure. His later work championed the realist novel of Balzac, Tolstoy and Thomas Mann against modernism.
