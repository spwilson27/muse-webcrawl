Arginine is an α-amino acid used in the biosynthesis of proteins. It contains an α-amino group, an α-carboxylic acid group and a side chain ending in a guanidino group, which is protonated at physiological pH, making arginine the most basic of the standard amino acids. It is encoded by six codons (CGU, CGC, CGA, CGG, AGA and AGG) — more than any other amino acid.

Biological roles

Arginine is conditionally essential: healthy adults synthesise it (chiefly via the intestinal–renal axis, from citrulline), but demand may exceed synthesis in growth, pregnancy, trauma and illness. It is a key intermediate of the urea cycle — the enzyme arginase splits it into urea and ornithine — and it is the substrate of nitric oxide synthases, which produce the signalling molecule nitric oxide essential to vascular tone and immune function. Arginine is also a precursor of creatine, agmatine and polyamines, and is abundant in histones and protamines, where its positive charge binds DNA.

Sources and uses

Dietary sources include meat, seafood, dairy, nuts, seeds and legumes. Arginine was first isolated in 1886 by Ernst Schulze from lupin seedlings, and its structure was established within the following decade. It is marketed as a dietary supplement for sports performance and cardiovascular health, though clinical evidence for many claims is weak; medically it is used in the diagnosis of growth-hormone deficiency and in treating certain urea-cycle disorders (where citrulline is generally preferred).
