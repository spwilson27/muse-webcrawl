Rhoticity in English

The distinction between rhotic and non-rhotic accents is one of the best-known classifications of English pronunciation. In a rhotic accent, the historical /r/ sound is pronounced in all positions, including after a vowel and before a consonant or pause, as in hard and car. In a non-rhotic accent, /r/ is pronounced only when it is followed by a vowel (including across word boundaries, as "linking R"), so car sounds like "cah" in isolation.

Distribution

Rhotic accents include most varieties of American English (General American), Canadian English, Irish English, Scottish English, and the English of the West Country and parts of Lancashire. Non-rhotic accents include Received Pronunciation and most accents of England outside the north and west, Welsh English, Australian and New Zealand English, South African English, and traditionally the accents of New York City, Boston and the American South, some of which are becoming more rhotic.

History

English was originally fully rhotic. The loss of post-vocalic /r/ began in south-eastern England in the 15th century and spread through the prestige speech of London during the 18th century, becoming a marker of educated southern British speech. American varieties settled before the change was complete largely retained rhoticity, which is why rhotic speech became standard in the United States and Canada.

Related phenomena

Non-rhotic accents typically feature linking R ("law[r] and order") and sometimes intrusive R ("law[r] and order" where no historical r exists, as in "idea[r] of it"). The vowel mergers associated with r-loss produced distinct vowel qualities in words like nurse, start and north.
