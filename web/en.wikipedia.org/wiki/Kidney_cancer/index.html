Kidney cancer, also known as renal cancer, is a group of cancers that start in the kidney. The great majority of cases in adults are renal cell carcinoma (RCC), which arises from the lining of the proximal tubules; cancers of the renal pelvis are usually transitional cell (urothelial) carcinoma, while the main childhood kidney cancer is Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma). Kidney cancer accounts for roughly 2–3 per cent of adult malignancies worldwide, with the highest incidence in North America and Europe, and about twice as many cases in men as in women.

Signs and symptoms

Early kidney cancer often causes no symptoms and is increasingly discovered incidentally on scans performed for other reasons. The classic triad of visible blood in the urine (haematuria), flank pain and a palpable abdominal mass is now uncommon and suggests advanced disease. Other features include unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats, fatigue, anaemia and paraneoplastic syndromes such as high blood calcium or excess red cell production from tumour-secreted hormones.

Causes and risk factors

Established risk factors include cigarette smoking, obesity and hypertension. Long-term dialysis and acquired cystic kidney disease raise risk, as do occupational exposures such as trichloroethylene. Hereditary syndromes account for a small proportion: von Hippel–Lindau disease, caused by mutation of the VHL tumour-suppressor gene, predisposes to clear cell RCC — the commonest subtype — and studying it revealed the hypoxia-response (HIF) pathway that drives most sporadic cases as well.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on contrast-enhanced CT or MRI imaging, which characterises the renal mass and stages spread to veins, lymph nodes, lungs and bone; biopsy is used selectively. Localised disease is treated surgically, by radical or (increasingly) partial nephrectomy, sometimes laparoscopically or robotically, with thermal ablation or active surveillance for small tumours in frail patients. Metastatic RCC, historically resistant to chemotherapy, is now managed with VEGF-pathway tyrosine kinase inhibitors, mTOR inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors, alone or in combinations, which have substantially improved survival since the 2000s.
