Roman temple


Best preserved: Maison Carrée (Nîmes), Pantheon (Rome), Temple of Bacchus (Baalbek)
Features: High podium, frontal steps, deep porch, engaged columns

Roman temples were among the most important building types of ancient Rome, housing the cult image of a deity and serving as the ceremonial focus of Roman religion — though sacrifice and worship took place on an altar outside, in front of the temple, rather than inside. Roman temple design drew on Etruscan and Greek precedents but developed distinctive features: a high podium, a single frontal staircase, a deep columned porch (pronaos), and columns engaged into the walls of the cella (pseudoperipteral arrangement), so that the building was emphatically directional, designed to be viewed and approached from the front.

Architecture

A typical Roman temple stood on a podium about 3–4 metres high, with the cella behind a porch of free-standing columns in one of the classical orders — Tuscan, Ionic, Corinthian (the Roman favourite), or Composite. The templum in the technical sense was the inaugurated sacred space defined by augurs, of which the building was the physical expression. Temples typically faced onto a forum or sacred precinct, often aligned with a theatre or processional route.

Famous examples

  • Pantheon, Rome — Hadrian's rebuilding (c. 118–125 AD) of Agrippa's temple to all gods, with its vast concrete dome and oculus; converted to a church in 609.
  • Maison Carrée, Nîmes — an Augustan temple (c. 7 BC) dedicated to Gaius and Lucius Caesar, among the best preserved anywhere.
  • Temple of Bacchus, Baalbek — one of the largest and best-preserved temples of the empire.
  • Temple of Portunus and the Temple of Hercules Victor in the Forum Boarium, Rome — early survivals, the latter a rare round temple.
  • Temple of Antoninus and Faustina, Temple of Saturn, and Temple of Vesta in the Roman Forum.

Survival

Many temples survived because they were converted into churches in late antiquity; others were quarried or buried. Roman temple forms profoundly influenced Renaissance and Neoclassical architecture, from Palladio onward, and remain the model for courthouses, museums, and civic buildings throughout the Western world.
