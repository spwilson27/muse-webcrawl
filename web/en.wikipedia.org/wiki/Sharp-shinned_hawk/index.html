Sharp-shinned hawk

The sharp-shinned hawk (Accipiter striatus) is a small hawk, with short, broad wings and a long tail, found across North and Central America and the Greater Antilles. It is the smallest hawk in Canada and the United States and is an ambush predator of small birds, famously frequenting bird feeders in winter in pursuit of sparrows and finches.

OrderAccipitriformes
FamilyAccipitridae
GenusAccipiter
Length24–35 cm (males smaller than females)
Wingspan43–58 cm

Description

Adults are slate grey above and pale below with fine reddish-orange barring; juveniles are brown above and streaked below. The legs are long, thin, and yellow with notably "sharp" shins, and the tail is often notched or squared at the tip. The species is notoriously difficult to distinguish from the slightly larger Cooper's hawk, which has a more rounded tail and a larger, blockier head.

Behaviour and diet

Sharp-shinned hawks specialize in catching other birds in flight, darting through dense cover at speed; they also take small rodents, bats, and large insects occasionally. Prey is often plucked at a "plucking post" before being eaten. They breed in coniferous and mixed forests, building a stick nest, and northern populations migrate south in autumn, concentrating at well-known hawk-watch sites. Females (the larger sex, as in most raptors) lay typically 4–5 eggs. Like other North American raptors, the species declined during the DDT era and subsequently recovered.
