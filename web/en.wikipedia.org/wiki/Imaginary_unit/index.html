Imaginary unit

The imaginary unit i on the complex plane

The imaginary unit or unit imaginary number (i) is a solution to the quadratic equation x2 + 1 = 0. Although there is no real number with this property, i can be used to extend the real numbers to what are called complex numbers, using addition and multiplication. A simple example of the use of i in a complex number is 2 + 3i.

Imaginary numbers are an important mathematical concept; they extend the real number system to the complex number system, in which at least one root for every nonconstant polynomial exists (see Algebraic closure and Fundamental theorem of algebra). Here, the term "imaginary" is used because there is no real number having a negative square.

Definition

By definition, the imaginary unit i is one solution (the other solution is the square root of −1, i.e. −i) of the quadratic equation x2 + 1 = 0 or equivalently x2 = −1. Since the equation has no real solutions, if we assume that the familiar arithmetic laws (addition, multiplication, commutativity, associativity and distribution) apply to the imaginary unit, the equation must then be taken as defining i.

The imaginary unit is sometimes written √−1 in advanced mathematics contexts (as well as in less advanced popular texts). However, great care needs to be taken when manipulating formulas containing square roots. The radical sign notation is reserved either for the principal square root function, which is defined for only real x ≥ 0, or for the principal branch of the complex square root function.

History

Although the Greek mathematician and engineer Heron of Alexandria is noted as the first to have conceived these numbers, it was Rafael Bombelli who first set down the rules for the multiplication of complex numbers in 1572. The concept had appeared in print earlier, in the work of Gerolamo Cardano. At the time, imaginary numbers, just like zero and negative numbers, were poorly understood and regarded by some as fictitious or useless. Many other mathematicians were slow to adopt the use of imaginary numbers, including René Descartes, who wrote about them in La Géométrie, in which he coined the term "imaginary" and meant it to be derogatory.

Powers of i

The powers of i repeat in a cycle of length 4: i0 = 1, i1 = i, i2 = −1, i3 = −i, i4 = 1, and so on. This follows because i4 = (i2)2 = (−1)2 = 1.
