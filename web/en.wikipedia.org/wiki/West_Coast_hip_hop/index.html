West Coast hip hop is a regional genre of hip hop music that encompasses any artists or music that originated in the West Coast of the United States. It began to emerge as a distinct scene in Los Angeles in the early 1980s, initially dominated by electro-hop and dance-oriented acts, before gangsta rap groups such as N.W.A brought the region to national prominence in the late 1980s.

History

Early West Coast hip hop grew out of Los Angeles party and club culture, with DJs such as Uncle Jamm's Army and the World Class Wreckin' Cru playing electro-funk records. Ice-T is widely credited with pioneering gangsta rap on the West Coast with his 1986 track "6 in the Mornin'". In 1988, N.W.A released Straight Outta Compton, whose stark depictions of life in South Los Angeles made the coast a center of the genre and sparked national controversy.

In the early 1990s, Dr. Dre's The Chronic (1992) introduced G-funk, a style built on slow, layered Parliament-Funkadelic samples and synthesizer melodies. Artists including Snoop Dogg, Tupac Shakur, Warren G and Tha Dogg Pound defined the sound, and Death Row Records became one of the most successful labels in the genre. The mid-1990s were marked by the East Coast–West Coast hip hop rivalry, which ended tragically with the deaths of Shakur in 1996 and The Notorious B.I.G. in 1997.

Later developments

In the 2000s and 2010s, the West Coast scene diversified. The Bay Area's hyphy movement, led by artists such as E-40 and Mac Dre, emphasized energetic dance culture, while Los Angeles produced a new generation including Kendrick Lamar, Schoolboy Q, Tyler, the Creator and Vince Staples, whose work brought renewed critical acclaim to the region.
