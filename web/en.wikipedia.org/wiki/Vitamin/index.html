Vitamin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A vitamin is an organic compound that is an essential nutrient — required in small amounts, obtainable from the diet (or, for vitamin D, from sunlight acting on skin, and for some, from gut bacteria), whose absence causes a specific deficiency disease. Thirteen vitamins are recognised for humans: A, C, D, E, K and the eight B vitamins. The name (Casimir Funk, 1912) blends 'vital' and 'amine', though not all vitamins are amines.

Vitamins act as cofactors for enzymes (B vitamins in energy metabolism), antioxidants (C, E), hormone-like regulators (D, in calcium balance) and structural essentials (A for vision, K for clotting). Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are stored and can accumulate to toxicity; water-soluble ones are more readily excreted.

Deficiency and excess

Scurvy, rickets, beriberi and night blindness are the textbook deficiencies, largely conquered where diets are adequate and fortification (iodised salt, folic-acid flour fortification preventing neural-tube defects) is practised. Excess is a real risk mainly from supplements — vitamin A toxicity was recorded in Arctic explorers who ate polar-bear liver.

Supplements debate

Trials of routine multivitamins in well-nourished adults show little benefit for major disease endpoints, while targeted supplementation (folate in pregnancy, B12 for vegans and older adults, D where sun is scarce) is well supported — a case study in nutrition evidence being population-specific.
