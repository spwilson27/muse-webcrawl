The current Constitution of France was adopted on 4 October 1958. It is typically called the Constitution of the Fifth Republic, and it replaced the Constitution of the Fourth Republic of 1946. Charles de Gaulle was the main driving force in introducing the new constitution and inaugurating the Fifth Republic, while the text was drafted by Michel Debré.

The Constitution establishes France as a secular, democratic republic, proclaims the principles of national sovereignty, and incorporates by reference the rights contained in the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789 and the preamble of the 1946 Constitution.

Provisions

The Constitution creates a semi-presidential system: executive power is shared between the President of the Republic, elected by direct universal suffrage for five years, and a Prime Minister appointed by the President but responsible to Parliament. The President appoints the government, can dissolve the National Assembly, call referendums, and wields wide-ranging emergency powers under Article 16.

Constitutional Council

The Constitutional Council (Conseil constitutionnel) reviews the constitutionality of legislation. Since the 2008 constitutional reform, courts may refer questions of constitutionality raised in litigation (the question prioritaire de constitutionnalité, QPC), greatly expanding citizens' ability to challenge laws.

Amendments

The Constitution has been amended many times, notably in 1962 to provide for direct election of the President, in 2000 to reduce the presidential term from seven to five years, and in 2008 in a major modernisation package. Amendments require either a referendum or a three-fifths majority of both houses meeting in Congress at Versailles.
