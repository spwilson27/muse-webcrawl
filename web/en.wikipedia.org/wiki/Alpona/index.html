Alpona

Traditional alpona floor painting
MediumRice-paste (and pigment) painting on floor or wall
RegionBengal (West Bengal, India, and Bangladesh)
OccasionReligious festivals, weddings, harvest rites

Alpona (Bengali: আলপনা, also spelled alpana) is a traditional folk art of Bengal, in eastern India and Bangladesh, in which ritual designs are painted by hand — traditionally by women — on floors, courtyards, and walls using a paste of rice flour and water. The white motifs are drawn freehand, often with the fingers or a small wad of cotton, and may be coloured with natural pigments such as turmeric yellow and vermilion red. The art form is closely related to the rangoli and kolam traditions of other parts of India, but is distinguished by its flowing, white, line-based motifs.

Motifs and occasions

Alpona motifs are mostly drawn from nature and agrarian life: creepers and leaves, lotus and other flowers, the sun, fish, peacocks, footprints of the goddess Lakshmi, and the sheaf of paddy. A central medallion is often surrounded by borders of creepers. Alponas are made for religious observances (vratas) — especially the worship of Lakshmi, goddess of wealth and harvest — for weddings and other auspicious ceremonies, and for seasonal festivals such as Poush Sankranti. Because the rice paste is perishable, the designs are ephemeral, renewed for each occasion; in Santhal tribal communities a related mural tradition decorates house walls.

Modern practice

In the twentieth century, artists associated with Rabindranath Tagore's university at Santiniketan, including Nandalal Bose, elevated alpona into a taught art form and spread its vocabulary across public decoration in Bengal. Today alponas are painted on streets and public spaces for festivals and cultural events, sometimes on a monumental scale, and contemporary artists adapt the motifs to textiles, ceramics, and graphic design.
