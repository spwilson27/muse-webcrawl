Galilean transformation

In physics, a Galilean transformation is used to transform between the coordinates of two reference frames which differ only by constant relative motion within the constructs of Newtonian physics. These transformations, together with spatial rotations and translations in space and time, form the Galilean group.

For frames in standard configuration moving at velocity v along the x-axis, the transformation is x′ = x − vt, y′ = y, z′ = z, t′ = t. Velocities add simply: u′ = u − v.

History and limits

Named after Galileo Galilei, who articulated the principle of relativity in 1632, the transformations hold at speeds far below the speed of light. At relativistic speeds they are superseded by the Lorentz transformations of special relativity, which preserve the speed of light in all inertial frames.

Galilean invariance—the idea that the laws of mechanics are the same in all inertial frames—is a cornerstone of classical mechanics.
