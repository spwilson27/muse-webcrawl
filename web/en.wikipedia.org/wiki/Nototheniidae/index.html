Nototheniidae, commonly called notothens or cod icefishes, is a family of ray-finned fishes of the suborder Notothenioidei, native to the cold waters of the Southern Ocean around Antarctica and the sub-Antarctic islands, with some species off southern South America, New Zealand and southern Australia. Notothenioids dominate the Antarctic fish fauna, and nototheniids are their most diverse family.

Adaptations to cold

Notothens live in water that can reach −1.8 °C, near the freezing point of seawater. Many produce antifreeze glycoproteins in their blood and tissues, which bind to ice crystals and prevent them from growing — an adaptation evolved as the Southern Ocean cooled over the last tens of millions of years. Having largely lost the swim bladder of other perch-like fishes, notothens achieve buoyancy through lipid-rich tissues and reduced bone mineralisation. The related icefishes (Channichthyidae) have gone further, losing haemoglobin entirely.

Diversity and ecology

The family includes the giant Antarctic toothfish (Dissostichus mawsoni) and Patagonian toothfish (D. eleginoides) — marketed as "Chilean sea bass" and the subject of major commercial fisheries regulated by CCAMLR — as well as small benthic genera such as Trematomus, Notothenia and Gobionotothen. Most species are bottom-dwellers feeding on krill, amphipods and other fish, though a few, like the Antarctic silverfish (Pleuragramma antarcticum), are pelagic.

Evolution

Notothenioids are a textbook example of adaptive radiation: from a single bottom-dwelling ancestor they diversified into nearly every ecological niche of the Antarctic shelf as competing fish groups were eliminated by cooling. Because their antifreeze proteins evolved convergently with those of northern cod — from different ancestral genes — they are also a celebrated case of convergent evolution.
