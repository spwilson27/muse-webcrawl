History of the United States (1849–1865)

The history of the United States from 1849 to 1865 covers the era from the aftermath of the Mexican–American War and the California Gold Rush through the sectional crisis over slavery's expansion, the collapse of the second party system, secession, and the American Civil War, ending with Union victory, emancipation, and the assassination of President Abraham Lincoln in April 1865.

The Battle of Gettysburg (July 1863), the war's turning point in the East.

Compromise and crisis, 1849–1854

Gold discovered at Sutter's Mill (1848) brought California statehood in 1850 as part of the Compromise of 1850, which paired California's admission as a free state with a stricter Fugitive Slave Act and popular sovereignty in Utah and New Mexico. The deaths of the Whig Party's leaders and the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 — which repealed the Missouri Compromise line — shattered the political order, provoking "Bleeding Kansas" violence and the founding of the anti-slavery Republican Party.

The coming of war, 1855–1861

The Dred Scott decision (1857) declared Congress powerless to bar slavery from the territories. John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry (1859) inflamed both sections. Abraham Lincoln's election in November 1860 triggered the secession of South Carolina and ten other slave states, which formed the Confederate States of America under Jefferson Davis. The firing on Fort Sumter on 12 April 1861 began the war.

The Civil War, 1861–1865

The Union's advantages in population, industry and rail met Confederate defensive skill and hopes of European recognition. Early Confederate victories in the East (First and Second Bull Run, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville) were balanced by Union successes in the West (Shiloh, Vicksburg). Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation (1 January 1863) made abolition a war aim and authorised Black enlistment — some 180,000 served. Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 1863) turned the tide; Ulysses S. Grant's Overland Campaign and Sherman's March to the Sea broke Confederate capacity. Robert E. Lee surrendered at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865. The war cost roughly 620,000–750,000 military deaths. Lincoln was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth on 14 April 1865, five days after Appomattox.

Legacy

The Thirteenth Amendment (ratified December 1865) abolished slavery nationwide. The war settled the questions of secession and slavery, centralised federal power, and set the stage for the contested Reconstruction era.
