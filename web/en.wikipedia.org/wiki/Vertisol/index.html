Vertisol

ClassificationSoil order (USDA soil taxonomy)
Key featureHigh shrink–swell clay content (>30%)
Typical regionsIndia (Deccan), Sudan, Texas, eastern Australia

Vertisols are an order of soils in USDA soil taxonomy characterised by a high content of expansive clay minerals, such as montmorillonite, that swell when wet and shrink and crack deeply when dry. The name comes from the Latin vertere, "to turn", referring to the continual churning of soil material as cracks open and close.

Properties

During dry seasons, wide, deep cracks form at the surface; loose surface material falls in, so that when the soil re-wets and expands, the subsoil is pushed upward and outward, producing polished shear planes called slickensides, wedge-shaped structural aggregates, and microrelief of mounds and depressions known as gilgai. Vertisols are typically dark, fertile and rich in calcium, but their physical behaviour makes them difficult to work: they are sticky when wet, rock-hard when dry, and their movement can damage foundations, roads and irrigation structures.

Distribution and use

Vertisols cover roughly 2–3% of the world's ice-free land, with large areas in India (the black cotton soils of the Deccan), the Gezira of Sudan, Texas, South Africa and Australia. They support cotton, sorghum, wheat and pasture, and are often used for grazing because cropping is constrained by the narrow window of workable moisture.
