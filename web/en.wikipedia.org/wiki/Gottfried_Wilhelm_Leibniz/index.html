Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Portrait of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in later life, wearing a large wig, after the portrait by Christoph Bernhard Francke]
Leibniz (1646–1716)

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) was a German polymath active as a mathematician, philosopher, scientist, inventor and diplomat. He independently developed differential and integral calculus at the same time as Isaac Newton, and his notation for calculus is the one still used today. He is also known for the philosophy of monads, the principle of sufficient reason, and the claim that this is 'the best of all possible worlds'.

Mathematics

Leibniz published the first account of calculus in 1684, introducing the integral sign ∫ and the d notation for differentials. The later priority dispute with Newton's supporters was one of the most bitter in the history of science; the modern consensus is that both invented it independently. He also developed binary arithmetic and worked on determinants and logic.

Philosophy

In the Monadology (1714) Leibniz argued that reality consists of simple, windowless substances called monads, coordinated by a pre-established harmony. His Theodicy addressed the problem of evil by arguing that God created the best of all possible worlds — a view satirised by Voltaire in Candide.

Other work

Leibniz designed a stepped calculating machine, corresponded with hundreds of scholars, served the House of Hanover as historian and librarian, and pursued projects from mining engineering to a universal formal language (characteristica universalis).
