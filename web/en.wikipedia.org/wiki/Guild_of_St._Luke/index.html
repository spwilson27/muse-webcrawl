The Guild of Saint Luke was the most common name for a city guild of painters and other artists in early modern Europe, especially in the Low Countries. It took its name from Saint Luke the Evangelist, who by tradition had painted a portrait of the Virgin Mary and was therefore the patron saint of artists. Guilds of St Luke regulated training, standards and the sale of art in their cities from the late Middle Ages until their abolition during the upheavals of the late 18th century.

Organisation and functions

A typical guild controlled entry into the profession: an apprentice trained for several years with a master, then worked as a journeyman before submitting a masterwork and paying fees to become a master entitled to open a workshop and sell work. The guild inspected quality, regulated the import and sale of works by outsiders (often confining them to annual fairs), ran lotteries and outlets, cared for members' widows and orphans, and maintained an altar or chapel. Membership commonly embraced more than painters: depending on the city, the Antwerp guild — one of the most famous, associated with the cathedral of Our Lady and later the Pand — included sculptors, engravers, printers, booksellers, art dealers, and makers of frames, harpsichords and other decorated goods.

Famous guilds

Notable guilds included those of Antwerp (recorded from 1382), Bruges, Ghent, Brussels, Delft — where Johannes Vermeer twice served as headman — Haarlem, and Utrecht, where the painters seceded in 1611 under Joachim Wtewael to form a guild of their own. In Florence the painters belonged to the Arte dei Medici e Speziali before the founding of the Accademia di San Luca model spread; Rome's Accademia di San Luca, founded in the 1590s, became the prototype of the art academy that displaced the guild system.

Decline

As academies and direct patronage grew and guild monopolies came under economic and political attack, the guilds of St Luke lost their powers; most were dissolved in the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic era or shortly after.
