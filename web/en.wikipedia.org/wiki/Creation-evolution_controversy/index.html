Creation–evolution controversy

The creation–evolution controversy centres on the origins of the Earth and of life.

The creation–evolution controversy (also termed the creation vs. evolution debate or the origins debate) involves an ongoing, recurring cultural, political, and theological dispute about the origins of the Earth, of humanity, and of other life. Within it, creationism, the religious belief that the universe and life originated from specific acts of divine creation, is set against evolution, the scientific model by which living organisms diversify and change over successive generations.

History

The controversy began in Europe and North America in the late 18th and 19th centuries, when geological discoveries showed that the Earth was far older than a literal reading of the biblical chronology suggested, and when Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859. In the United States the dispute became particularly prominent in public education, with a series of legal cases concerning the teaching of evolution in public schools, beginning with the Scopes Trial of 1925.

In 1968, the Supreme Court of the United States ruled in Epperson v. Arkansas that bans on teaching evolution violated the Establishment Clause. In Edwards v. Aguillard (1987), the Court struck down a Louisiana law requiring that "creation science" be taught alongside evolution. In Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District (2005), a federal court ruled that intelligent design is a religious view and not science, and that teaching it in public school science classes was unconstitutional.

Positions

Positions in the debate range from young Earth creationism, which holds that the Earth and life were created in their present forms within roughly the last ten thousand years, through old Earth creationism and theistic evolution, to the position of the scientific community, which overwhelmingly accepts evolution as the best-supported explanation for the diversity of life. The vast majority of scientists reject creationism as a scientific alternative; major scientific organisations state that evolution is a fact and a well-confirmed theory.

Public opinion

Surveys show considerable variation in public acceptance of evolution by country and by religious affiliation. Acceptance tends to be higher in Europe and lower in the United States than in other developed countries, though it varies substantially between groups within the United States as well.
