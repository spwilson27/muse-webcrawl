Essential thrombocythemia

Other name: Essential thrombocytosis
Field: Haematology
Class: Myeloproliferative neoplasm (BCR-ABL1–negative)
Key feature: Persistently elevated platelet count (≥450 × 109/L)
Driver mutations: JAK2 V617F (~55%), CALR (~25%), MPL (~5%)
Main risks: Thrombosis and bleeding; rare progression to myelofibrosis or acute leukaemia

Essential thrombocythemia (ET), also called essential thrombocytosis, is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm characterised by the overproduction of platelets by megakaryocytes in the bone marrow, resulting in a persistently elevated platelet count in the blood. It is one of the classic BCR-ABL1–negative myeloproliferative neoplasms, alongside polycythemia vera and primary myelofibrosis. The word "essential" indicates that the cause lies within the marrow itself, as opposed to reactive (secondary) thrombocytosis caused by infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, or splenectomy.

Signs and symptoms

Many patients are asymptomatic and are diagnosed incidentally on a routine blood count. When symptoms occur they are often vasomotor: headaches, dizziness, visual disturbances, and erythromelalgia — burning pain and redness of the hands and feet. The serious complications are thrombosis (arterial events such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and transient ischaemic attacks; venous events including deep vein thrombosis and the unusual-site Budd–Chiari syndrome) and, paradoxically, bleeding, especially at extremely high platelet counts where acquired von Willebrand syndrome develops. The spleen may be mildly enlarged.

Cause and diagnosis

ET is a clonal stem-cell disorder driven by acquired mutations that activate the JAK-STAT pathway: JAK2 V617F in roughly half of patients, insertions/deletions in CALR (calreticulin) in about a quarter, and MPL mutations in a small minority; a small fraction are "triple-negative". Diagnosis follows WHO criteria: platelet count ≥450 × 109/L, a bone marrow biopsy showing increased enlarged mature megakaryocytes without significant increases in other lineages, exclusion of other myeloid neoplasms and of reactive causes, and presence of a clonal marker.

Treatment and prognosis

Management is guided by thrombotic risk (age over 60 and previous thrombosis define high risk). Low-risk patients are usually treated with low-dose aspirin alone; high-risk patients receive cytoreductive therapy, most often hydroxycarbamide (hydroxyurea), with anagrelide or interferon alfa as alternatives — interferon being preferred in younger patients and in pregnancy. Life expectancy is near normal for many patients, but ET can progress over decades to post-ET myelofibrosis or, uncommonly, to acute myeloid leukaemia.
