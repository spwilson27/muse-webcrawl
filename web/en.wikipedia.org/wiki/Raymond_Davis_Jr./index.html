Raymond Davis Jr.

Raymond Davis Jr. (October 14, 1914 – May 31, 2006) was an American chemist and physicist. He is best known for leading the Homestake experiment, which was the first experiment to detect neutrinos emitted from the Sun, and for the work that established the solar neutrino problem. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2002 for his contributions to the detection of cosmic neutrinos.

Early life and career

Davis was born in Washington, D.C., and graduated from the University of Maryland. He earned his PhD in physical chemistry from Yale University in 1942 and spent the war years on weapons-related work, including at Dugway Proving Ground. After the war he joined Brookhaven National Laboratory, where he spent most of his career working on neutrino physics.

The Homestake experiment

Building on Bruno Pontecorvo's proposal, Davis constructed a detector consisting of a large tank of perchloroethylene (cleaning fluid) deep underground in the Homestake gold mine in Lead, South Dakota, where the overlying rock shielded it from cosmic rays. Solar neutrinos occasionally converted chlorine-37 atoms in the fluid into radioactive argon-37, which Davis extracted and counted. Beginning in the late 1960s, the experiment consistently detected only about one third of the neutrinos predicted by John Bahcall's solar model — the "solar neutrino problem". The discrepancy was ultimately explained by neutrino oscillation, the phenomenon for which Masatoshi Koshiba's and Davis's results were recognised by the 2002 Nobel Prize.

Legacy

Davis's painstaking radiochemistry — extracting a handful of argon atoms from hundreds of thousands of gallons of fluid — founded the field of neutrino astronomy. The Homestake site later became part of the Sanford Underground Research Facility.
