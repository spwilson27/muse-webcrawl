Mettā

Mettā (Pali) or maitrī (Sanskrit) is a term from Buddhism meaning benevolence, loving-kindness, goodwill, friendliness and an active interest in the welfare of others. It is one of the ten pāramīs (perfections) of Theravāda Buddhism and the first of the four brahmavihāras or "divine abodes", together with karuṇā (compassion), muditā (sympathetic joy) and upekkhā (equanimity).

In the scriptures

The most famous exposition is the Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta of the Sutta Nipāta, which describes the wish that all beings be happy and safe: "May all beings be happy and secure; may their hearts be wholesome." The sutta compares the protective feeling of mettā to that of a mother watching over her only child. The Visuddhimagga of Buddhaghosa gives detailed instructions for its cultivation.

Practice

In mettā meditation (mettā bhāvanā), the practitioner directs well-wishing phrases first towards themselves, then in widening circles: a benefactor, a dear friend, a neutral person, a difficult person, and finally all beings without distinction. The traditional formula runs: "May I (you, all beings) be happy; may I be safe; may I be healthy; may I live with ease." The "near enemy" of mettā is said to be attachment or selfish affection, and its "far enemy" is ill will.

Modern research

Since the early 2000s, loving-kindness meditation has been studied in clinical and contemplative psychology, with research reporting associations with reduced stress, increased positive emotion and changes in brain activity in regions involved in empathy. It is taught widely in both traditional retreat settings and secular mindfulness programmes.
