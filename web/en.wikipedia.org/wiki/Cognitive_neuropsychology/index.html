Cognitive neuropsychology

Cognitive neuropsychology is a branch of cognitive psychology that aims to understand how the structure and function of the brain relates to specific psychological processes. It studies the cognitive effects of brain injury or neurological illness in order to infer the organisation of normal cognitive function, drawing on detailed case studies and group studies of patients with lesions, as well as on evidence from healthy participants.

Approach and assumptions

The discipline rests on several working assumptions. Modularity holds that cognitive systems are composed of relatively independent components. Transparency assumes that a damaged system is the normal system minus the impaired components, rather than a wholesale reorganisation. Universality supposes that the basic cognitive architecture is broadly the same across individuals, so findings from single cases can generalise. A key method is the double dissociation: showing that one patient is impaired on task A but not B, while another shows the reverse pattern, which licenses the inference that the tasks depend on separable processes.

Domains of study

Cognitive neuropsychologists have investigated acquired disorders of language (aphasia and acquired dyslexia), object and face recognition (agnosia and prosopagnosia), memory (the amnesic syndrome, famously illustrated by patient H.M.), attention and spatial neglect, number processing (acalculia), and executive function after frontal lobe damage. Classic cases — from Broca's patient "Tan" to Phineas Gage — continue to frame questions in the field.

Relationship to other fields

Cognitive neuropsychology is closely related to cognitive neuroscience, but traditionally distinguishes itself by emphasising the cognitive level of explanation and by its scepticism about whether localisation alone explains function. Its findings inform cognitive rehabilitation, and the single-case methodology has been debated against group-study and computational modelling approaches, including connectionist models that simulate lesion effects in artificial neural networks.
