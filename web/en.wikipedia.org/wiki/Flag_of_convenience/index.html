A flag of convenience (FOC) is a business practice whereby a ship's owners register a merchant ship in a ship register of a country other than that of the ship's owners, and the ship flies the civil ensign of that country, called the flag state. The term is often used pejoratively, and although the practice is common and lawful, in many cases it is associated with attempts to reduce operating costs or avoid the regulations of the owner's country.

History

Open ship registration has its roots in the age of sail, when merchants sought flags offering protection or neutrality in wartime. The modern system grew after the First World War, when American shipowners began registering tankers in Panama to circumvent Prohibition-era rules and crewing laws. Liberia established its maritime registry in 1948 in cooperation with American interests, and together Panama and Liberia pioneered the model in which registry is essentially a commercial service offered to foreign shipowners, administered for revenue rather than as an extension of a national merchant fleet.

By the early twenty-first century, the three largest registries in the world — Panama, Liberia and the Marshall Islands — were all open registries, and a large majority of the world's commercial tonnage sailed under a flag different from the nationality of its beneficial owners.

Reasons for the practice

Shipowners cite a range of economic and operational motives: lower registration fees and tonnage taxes; the ability to employ multinational crews at wages set by negotiation rather than by the labour law of the owner's home state; fewer restrictions on crew nationality; and simplified corporate structures. Flag states market their registries on administrative efficiency, mortgage law favourable to lenders, and around-the-clock registration services.

Criticism

The International Transport Workers' Federation (ITF) has campaigned against flags of convenience since 1948, arguing that some open registries permit substandard conditions, low wages, long hours and poor enforcement of safety and environmental rules. Critics also link weak flag-state control to maritime casualties, oil pollution and difficulty in identifying beneficial owners after accidents or sanctions violations. Defenders of open registries respond that the leading registries maintain high inspection standards, appear on the white lists of port state control regimes, and that ship quality correlates more strongly with owners and classification societies than with the flag itself.

Legal framework

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, every state may fix the conditions for granting nationality to ships, but a "genuine link" must exist between the state and the ship. In practice the content of the genuine-link requirement has never been precisely defined, and flag states exercise primary jurisdiction over ships on the high seas, supplemented by port state control inspections in foreign ports.
