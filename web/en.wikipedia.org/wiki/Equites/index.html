Equites

The equites (Latin: equitēs, singular eques; literally "horsemen" or "knights") were the second of the two property-based classes of ancient Rome, ranking below the senatorial class. Membership required a property qualification — under the late Republic and Empire a census of 400,000 sesterces — and was marked by distinctive insignia: a gold ring (anulus aureus) and a toga with a narrow purple stripe (angustus clavus), as opposed to the broad stripe of a senator.

Origins

The class originated in the cavalry of the early Roman army: citizens wealthy enough to provide their own horses were organized into centuries of equites and received a horse at public expense (equus publicus). As Rome's armies professionalized and cavalry service passed to allied auxiliaries, the equestrian order became a status group defined by wealth rather than military function. Each year the order paraded in the transvectio equitum before the censors, who reviewed membership and could expel the unworthy.

Role in the Republic

From the second century BC the equites became a political force. Gaius Gracchus gave equestrian juries control of the extortion court, and equestrian publicani syndicates bid for tax-farming and public contracts across the provinces. Conflict between senators and equites over courts, contracts and provincial profits was a recurring theme of late-republican politics; Cicero championed the "harmony of the orders" (concordia ordinum) as a political ideal.

The Empire

Augustus formalized the equestrian order as a career service for the emperor. Equites staffed a growing series of posts: prefectures (of Egypt, the praetorian guard, the grain supply, the watch), procuratorships administering imperial finances and provinces, and senior military posts such as legionary tribunate and camp prefect. The standardized cursus of equestrian militiae gave capable administrators a route of advancement outside the senatorial career, and emperors increasingly governed through them; a few equites, like Sejanus, amassed extraordinary power. In the third century AD, equestrians largely displaced senators from military command, and the emperor Gallienus formalized this shift.
