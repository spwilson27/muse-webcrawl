The Legal Services Corporation (LSC) is a nonprofit corporation established by the United States Congress in 1974 to provide financial support for civil legal aid to low-income Americans. It is the single largest funder of civil legal aid in the country, distributing grants to more than 130 independent nonprofit legal aid programmes across all 50 states, the District of Columbia and U.S. territories.

History

LSC was created by the Legal Services Corporation Act of 1974, signed by President Richard Nixon, succeeding the legal services programme of the Office of Economic Opportunity established during the War on Poverty. President Ronald Reagan sought to abolish the corporation in the 1980s and its funding was cut substantially, but Congress retained it. In 1996, Congress imposed restrictions on grantees, including prohibitions on class-action lawsuits, challenges to welfare reform, and representation in certain abortion- and redistricting-related matters; some restrictions were struck down by the Supreme Court in Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez (2001), which invalidated the bar on challenging existing welfare laws.

Operations

LSC is governed by an eleven-member board of directors appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Its grantees handle civil (not criminal) matters such as eviction defence, domestic violence protection, consumer fraud, access to benefits and veterans' and disaster-related legal needs, closing on the order of three-quarters of a million cases in a typical recent year. LSC research on the "justice gap" has estimated that low-income Americans receive no or inadequate legal help for the great majority of their substantial civil legal problems.

Funding

The corporation is funded almost entirely by congressional appropriation, which has been on the order of several hundred million dollars annually in recent years; a number of presidential administrations have proposed eliminating or sharply reducing it, but Congress has continued to fund it with bipartisan support.
