Komuso

A komusō begging monk, his face hidden by the tengai basket hat

Komuso (Japanese: 虚無僧, komusō, "priest of nothingness" or "emptiness monk") were begging monks of the Fuke school of Japanese Zen Buddhism during the Edo period (1603–1868). They were recognisable across Japan by the deep woven basket hats (tengai) that completely concealed their faces and by the shakuhachi bamboo flute, which they played as a form of meditation and as their sole means of requesting alms.

Practice

The komuso's flute-playing was not performance but discipline: the solo pieces known as honkyoku were practised as suizen, "blowing Zen", in which breath, tone, and emptiness of mind were the substance of meditation. Because the monks were forbidden to speak, they communicated and begged by playing specific recognised pieces in front of houses.

History

The Fuke sect traced its lineage to the Chinese Zen master Puhua (Fuke in Japanese) and was organised in Japan around temples such as Myōan-ji in Kyoto and Reihō-ji in Edo. Many komuso were masterless samurai (rōnin) displaced by the peace of the Tokugawa shogunate, and the sect received extraordinary privileges: komuso alone could travel freely throughout the country when movement was otherwise restricted. This freedom was abused — government spies reportedly used the disguise, and criminals hid among the monks — and after the Meiji Restoration the Fuke sect was abolished in 1871 and playing the shakuhachi as alms-begging was banned. The honkyoku repertoire survived through lay schools of shakuhachi, and today performers in komuso garb still appear at temples and festivals.

Appearance

Besides the tengai, komuso wore a sash and carried the flute and a small begging bag. Tradition held that the basket hat abolished identity — the monk was officially "no-one" — which underpinned the legal fiction that a komuso could not be identified, questioned, or made to testify.
