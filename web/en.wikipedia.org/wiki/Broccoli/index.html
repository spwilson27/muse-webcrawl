Broccoli

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A fresh head of broccoli with tight dark-green florets]

Broccoli is a cruciferous vegetable, Brassica oleracea var. italica, eaten for its immature flower heads — dense clusters of green buds on thick stalks. Developed in Italy from wild cabbage (its name is the diminutive of brocco, "little sprout"), it was known in Roman times in early forms, refined in Calabria — the standard heading type is still called Calabrese — and remained an Italian speciality until the 20th century. The D'Arrigo brothers commercialised it in California from the 1920s, shipping it east on ice, and broccoli rode the late-century health-food wave to global ubiquity. China and India now grow the most.

Rise and reputation

Broccoli's ascent was marketing as much as agriculture: it arrived in American cities as Italian immigration grew, survived Prohibition-era farming changes, and boomed as nutrition science praised crucifers. Few vegetables have enemies in high places: President George H. W. Bush banned it from Air Force One in 1990, declaring "I do not like broccoli... and I haven't liked it since I was a little kid" — broccoli growers promptly sent tonnes of it to the White House, and his son's presidency had to field the same question. The moment fixed broccoli as the archetypal "good for you" vegetable in American political theatre.

Nutrition and cooking

Broccoli is rich in vitamin C, vitamin K, folate and fibre, and is the showcase source of sulforaphane, a compound formed when the plant's glucoraphanin meets the enzyme myrosinase during chopping and chewing; studies of sulforaphane's effects on detoxification enzymes drive much of the research interest. Cooking is a compromise: steaming keeps more sulforaphane and bite than boiling, which leaches nutrients into the water. Broccolini, a 1990s cross with Chinese gai lan, and purple sprouting broccoli extend the family.
