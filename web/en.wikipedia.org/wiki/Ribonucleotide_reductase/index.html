Ribonucleotide reductase

Ribonucleotide reductase
EC number: 1.17.4.1
Reaction: ribonucleotide → deoxyribonucleotide
Classes: I, II, III (by radical chemistry)

Ribonucleotide reductase (RNR), also called ribonucleoside diphosphate reductase, is the enzyme that catalyses the formation of deoxyribonucleotides from ribonucleotides — the chemical step that converts the building blocks of RNA into those of DNA. It reduces the 2′ hydroxyl group of the ribose ring, producing the deoxyribonucleotides (dADP, dGDP, dCDP and dUDP) needed for DNA replication and repair. Ribonucleotide reductase is found in all living organisms and is thought to have been essential to the origin of DNA-based life.

Classes

RNRs are divided into three classes according to how they generate the radical that initiates catalysis. Class I enzymes, found in eukaryotes and some bacteria and viruses, use a di-iron centre and a tyrosyl radical in their small subunit and require oxygen. Class II enzymes use adenosylcobalamin (vitamin B12) and function with or without oxygen. Class III enzymes use an iron–sulfur cluster and S-adenosylmethionine to form a glycyl radical and are inactivated by oxygen, occurring in anaerobic bacteria.

Regulation

Because unbalanced deoxyribonucleotide pools are mutagenic, RNR is tightly regulated by allosteric effectors: ATP and the four dNTPs bind specificity and activity sites to balance production of the four products. In eukaryotes the enzyme is also controlled across the cell cycle, peaking in S phase.

Medical significance

RNR is a target of anticancer and antiviral drugs: hydroxyurea quenches its radical, and gemcitabine inhibits it, starving dividing cells of DNA building blocks. Mutations in the human small subunit gene RRM2B cause mitochondrial DNA depletion syndromes.
