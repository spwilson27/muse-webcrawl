Enlightenment in Buddhism

The English term enlightenment is the common Western translation of several Buddhist terms, most notably bodhi and vimutti. The abstract noun bodhi (from the root budh-, "to awaken") means the knowledge or wisdom by which a person becomes a buddha, an awakened one. Enlightenment is understood in Buddhism as the complete ending of suffering (dukkha) and of the cycle of rebirth (samsara), through insight into the true nature of reality.

Bodhi

In early Buddhism, bodhi is attained when the Four Noble Truths are fully understood and the three poisons — delusion, attachment and aversion — are extinguished, so that craving ceases and nirvana is realised. The Buddha's own awakening at Bodh Gaya, after a night of meditation resisting the temptations of Mara, is the paradigmatic account. Those who attain it by following the Buddha's teaching are called arhats in the Theravada tradition.

Mahayana developments

Mahayana Buddhism expanded the concept: the bodhisattva path leads to the full buddhahood of a samyaksambuddha, accompanied by insight into emptiness (śūnyatā) and the doctrine that awakening is not separate from samsara. In East Asian traditions such as Zen (Chan), enlightenment may be described in terms of kenshō or satori — a sudden seeing into one's true nature — while Tibetan traditions analyse it through the five wisdoms and the stages of the path (bhūmis).

Related concepts

Buddhist texts also speak of prajñā (wisdom/insight), vimoksha (liberation), and the attainment of stream-entry and other stages of awakening. Western interpreters during the 19th century adopted the word "enlightenment", with its associations of light and reason, to render these ideas.
