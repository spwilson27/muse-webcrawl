Anti-radiation missile

Type: Air-to-surface (and some surface-to-surface) missile

Guidance: Passive radar homing

Targets: Air-defence radars, jammers, emitters

An anti-radiation missile (ARM) is a missile designed to detect and home in on an enemy radio emission source, typically a radar of an air-defence system. ARMs are the principal weapon of suppression of enemy air defences (SEAD) missions, flown by specialised aircraft known in the US Air Force as Wild Weasels.

Operation

The missile's passive seeker listens for radar emissions and guides the missile toward the strongest or pre-selected signal. Even when the warhead misses, forcing the enemy radar to shut down ("blink") suppresses the air-defence battery and protects friendly strike aircraft. Modern ARMs carry inertial and GPS backup guidance so they can continue to the radar's last known position if it switches off, and some remember emitter locations throughout the engagement.

History

The first operational ARM was the American AGM-45 Shrike, rushed into service during the Vietnam War to counter Soviet-supplied SA-2 radars. It was followed by the AGM-78 Standard ARM and the much faster AGM-88 HARM, which became the standard Western ARM from the 1980s, used extensively in Libya, Iraq and the Balkans. Other designs include the British ALARM — which could loiter under a parachute waiting for a radar to switch on — the French Martel, and the Russian Kh-31 and Kh-58 families. Anti-radiation seekers have also been fitted to loitering munitions and drones.

Countermeasures

Radar operators counter ARMs by emitting intermittently, using decoy emitters, employing passive sensors, or shutting down and relocating. Missile-defence guns and short-range SAMs may also engage the incoming missile or its launch aircraft.
