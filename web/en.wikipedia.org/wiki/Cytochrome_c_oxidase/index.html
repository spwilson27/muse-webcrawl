Cytochrome c oxidase

Cytochrome c oxidase (also Complex IV) is a large transmembrane protein complex found in the inner mitochondrial membrane of eukaryotes and in the plasma membrane of many bacteria. It is the last enzyme in the respiratory electron transport chain, transferring electrons from cytochrome c to molecular oxygen and thereby driving the production of ATP.

Reaction

The complex receives four electrons from four molecules of cytochrome c and uses them to reduce one molecule of oxygen completely to two molecules of water, taking up four "substrate" protons from the mitochondrial matrix in the process. Simultaneously, it pumps four additional protons across the membrane into the intermembrane space, contributing to the electrochemical gradient used by ATP synthase.

Structure

The mammalian enzyme contains 13 subunits, the three largest encoded by mitochondrial DNA. Its catalytic core holds two heme groups (a and a3) and two copper centres (CuA and CuB). The binuclear centre formed by heme a3 and CuB is where oxygen binds and is reduced. Cyanide, carbon monoxide, and azide inhibit the enzyme by binding at this centre — the basis of cyanide's acute toxicity.

Genetics and disease

Because many of its subunits and assembly factors are essential, mutations affecting Complex IV cause mitochondrial diseases, including forms of Leigh syndrome and infantile encephalomyopathies. Certain subunits of the enzyme — notably the mitochondrial gene COX1 encoding subunit I — are widely used as DNA barcodes for species identification in animals.
