Fire clay

Fire clay
TypeRefractory clay
Main mineralsKaolinite, hydrous aluminium silicates
Firing rangeResists temperatures above ~1,500 °C
UsesFire bricks, crucibles, kiln linings, metallurgy

Fire clay is a range of refractory clays used in the manufacture of ceramics, especially fire bricks and other high-temperature products. Fire clay has a high content of aluminium oxide (alumina) and silica and can withstand temperatures in excess of 1,500 °C without melting or deforming substantially. Because of its refractory qualities, it has historically been mined extensively in coal-bearing strata, where it commonly occurs as a seat earth beneath coal seams.

Composition and properties

Fire clays are composed mainly of hydrous aluminium silicates, principally kaolinite, with variable amounts of free silica (quartz), iron oxide, lime, magnesia and alkalis. High-alumina fire clays approach 40% or more alumina and have the greatest refractoriness; lower grades contain more fluxing impurities, which reduce the softening temperature. The plasticity of fire clay varies — flint clays are hard and non-plastic, while plastic fire clays can be moulded easily and are used to bond less plastic materials.

Occurrence

Fire clay is found in many coalfields, including the Stourbridge district of England (famous for glass-making pots and fire bricks), the Pennine and South Wales coalfields, and in the United States in Pennsylvania, Ohio, Kentucky, Missouri and Colorado. Underclays beneath coal seams are interpreted as ancient soils in which the original swamp vegetation grew.

Uses

  • Manufacture of fire bricks for furnaces, kilns, fireplaces and boiler settings
  • Crucibles and glass pots
  • Refractory mortars, castables and ceramic kiln furniture
  • Sanitary ware, stoneware pipes and other heavy clay products (lower grades)
  • Complex clay liners for chimneys and metallurgical vessels
