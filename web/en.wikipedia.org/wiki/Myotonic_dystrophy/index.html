Myotonic dystrophy (DM) is a type of muscular dystrophy, a group of long-term genetic disorders that impair muscle function. It is the most common form of muscular dystrophy that begins in adulthood. Symptoms include gradually worsening muscle loss and weakness, prolonged muscle contractions (myotonia) with delayed relaxation, cataracts, and abnormalities of the heart's electrical conduction system. The condition also affects many other organs, including the brain, eyes, endocrine glands, and gastrointestinal tract.

There are two main types. Type 1 (DM1), or Steinert disease, is caused by an expanded CTG trinucleotide repeat in the DMPK gene on chromosome 19. Type 2 (DM2) is caused by an expanded CCTG repeat in the CNBP gene on chromosome 3 and is generally milder. Both are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern and show genetic anticipation: the repeat tends to lengthen in successive generations, producing earlier and more severe disease.

Facial and neck muscle wasting is characteristic of myotonic dystrophy type 1.

Signs and symptoms

In DM1, weakness preferentially affects the face, jaw, neck flexors, and distal limb muscles, in contrast to the proximal pattern of many other dystrophies. Myotonia — delayed relaxation after contraction — is shown by difficulty releasing a handshake and by percussion myotonia. Other features include early posterior subcapsular cataracts, frontal balding, cardiac arrhythmias and heart block, excessive daytime sleepiness, insulin resistance and diabetes, testicular atrophy, dysphagia, and gastrointestinal dysmotility.

A severe congenital form of DM1 occurs in babies of affected mothers, with profound hypotonia, respiratory failure, club foot, and intellectual disability. Childhood-onset disease may present mainly with learning difficulties.

Genetics

DM1 results from expansion of a CTG repeat in the 3′ untranslated region of DMPK (dystrophia myotonica protein kinase). Normal alleles have 5–34 repeats; alleles with more than 50 repeats cause disease, and expansions may exceed several thousand repeats in congenital cases. DM2 results from a CCTG expansion in intron 1 of CNBP (formerly ZNF9).

Mechanism

The expanded repeats are transcribed into toxic RNA that accumulates in nuclear foci and sequesters splicing regulators of the muscleblind-like (MBNL) family, while upregulating CELF proteins. The resulting widespread mis-splicing of many transcripts — including the chloride channel CLCN1 (explaining myotonia) and the insulin receptor — accounts for the multisystem character of the disease.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is confirmed by genetic testing demonstrating the repeat expansion. Electromyography may show characteristic myotonic discharges ("dive-bomber" sounds). Slit-lamp examination detects cataracts, and an ECG is used to screen for conduction disease. Genetic counselling is recommended because of anticipation and the 50% recurrence risk to offspring.

Treatment

There is no cure and no approved disease-modifying therapy; management is supportive and surveillance-based. Regular cardiac monitoring with ECG (and pacemaker or defibrillator implantation when indicated) reduces sudden death. Mexiletine may improve myotonia. Other measures include cataract surgery, screening for diabetes, physiotherapy, ankle–foot orthoses for foot drop, and treatment of excessive daytime sleepiness, sometimes with modafinil. Antisense oligonucleotide and other RNA-targeted therapies are under investigation.

Prognosis and epidemiology

DM1 affects roughly 1 in 8,000 people and is particularly common in the Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean region of Quebec because of a founder effect. Life expectancy is reduced, mainly by respiratory failure and cardiac arrhythmia. DM2 is usually milder, lacks a congenital form, and has a later onset.

History

The disease was described independently in 1909 by Hans Steinert and by Frederick Batten and George Gibb. The genetic basis — an unstable CTG repeat expansion — was identified in 1992, making DM1 one of the first disorders attributed to a trinucleotide repeat expansion.
