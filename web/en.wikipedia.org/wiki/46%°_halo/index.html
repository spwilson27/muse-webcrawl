A 46° halo is a rare atmospheric optical phenomenon in the form of a large ring of light with a radius of about 46 degrees centred on the Sun or Moon. It is produced, like its much commoner counterpart the 22° halo, by sunlight refracting through hexagonal ice crystals in cirrus clouds, but through faces of the crystal that form a 90° prism rather than a 60° one.

Formation

Hexagonal ice crystals act as prisms. Rays passing through two side faces of a crystal column are bent by about 22°, giving the familiar halo; rays entering a side face and leaving through an end face (the basal face) encounter a 90° prism and are deviated by a minimum of about 46°. For a distinct 46° halo the crystals must be randomly oriented, which happens in only some cirrus conditions, and the resulting ring is broad, faint and diffuse compared with the 22° halo.

Observation

Because it is faint and easily drowned out by brighter halo phenomena, the true 46° halo is seen far less often than its frequency of occurrence might suggest, and many reported sightings are actually the bright supralateral arc, which lies in a similar position but is produced by oriented column crystals and shows stronger spectral colours. When seen completely, the 46° halo appears as a pale ring roughly twice the diameter of the 22° halo, sometimes with reddish colouring on its inner edge. It can also accompany other displays including parhelia, the circumzenithal arc and tangent arcs.
