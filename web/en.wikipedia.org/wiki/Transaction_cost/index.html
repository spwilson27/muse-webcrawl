Transaction cost

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, a transaction cost is a cost incurred in making an economic exchange — the cost of participating in a market. Transaction costs include search and information costs (finding and evaluating trading partners), bargaining and decision costs (negotiating and concluding a contract), and policing and enforcement costs (ensuring the other party performs and remedying breaches).

Origins

Ronald Coase introduced the concept in "The Nature of the Firm" (1937), arguing that firms exist because organising production internally can be cheaper than repeatedly contracting in the market. In "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960), Coase showed that when transaction costs are zero, resources flow to their most valuable use regardless of the initial assignment of rights — the condition underlying the Coase theorem — but that in reality positive transaction costs make institutions and legal rules matter.

Transaction cost economics

Oliver Williamson developed transaction cost economics into a theory of governance, holding that transactions differ in asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency, and that firms, markets, and hybrid forms (such as long-term contracts and franchising) are chosen to economise on transaction costs. Highly specific assets create hold-up risks that favour vertical integration. Coase and Williamson both received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (1991 and 2009 respectively).
