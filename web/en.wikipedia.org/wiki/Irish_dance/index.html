Irish dance refers to a group of traditional dance forms originating in Ireland, which can broadly be divided into social dancing and performance dancing. Irish social dancing includes céilí dances and set dances, performed in groups, while performance dancing is dominated by Irish step dance, characterised by a rigid upper body and intricate, rapid footwork. The global success of the show Riverdance, first performed as an interval act at the 1994 Eurovision Song Contest, propelled Irish dance to worldwide popularity.

History

Evidence for dance in early Ireland is sparse, but group dances accompanied by song are recorded in accounts of medieval and early modern Ireland. The modern forms developed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries through the work of itinerant dancing masters, who travelled between villages teaching steps and standardising figures. Crossroads dances — outdoor dances at rural crossroads — were a noted feature of Irish life, later romanticised in Eamon de Valera's famous (and probably apocryphal) vision of "cosy homesteads" and "the laughter of happy maidens".

The Gaelic League, founded in 1893, promoted Irish dance as part of the cultural revival, organising the first céilí in London in 1897. In 1927 the Irish Dancing Commission, An Coimisiún le Rincí Gaelacha (CLRG), was established to regulate competitive step dancing and remains the largest governing body.

Step dance

Competitive step dancing is performed solo in soft shoes ("ghillies" or pumps) for light dances — reels, light jigs, slip jigs and single jigs — and in hard shoes, similar to tap shoes with fibreglass tips and heels, for heavy dances such as the hornpipe and treble jig. Dancers keep the torso and arms still, a feature variously explained by tradition, etiquette or legend. Competitions are called feiseanna (singular feis), culminating in the World Irish Dancing Championships (Oireachtas Rince na Cruinne), held annually since 1970 and drawing thousands of dancers from the Irish diaspora and beyond.

Céilí and set dancing

Céilí dances are group dances for couples arranged in lines or circles, with named traditional figures such as "The Walls of Limerick" and "The Siege of Ennis". Set dancing, descended from French quadrilles adapted in the early nineteenth century, is danced by four couples in a square ("a set"), particularly in the south and west of Ireland, and enjoyed a major popular revival from the 1980s.

Sean-nós dance

Sean-nós ("old style") dance is a relaxed, improvisational solo style from Connemara, with low-to-the-ground footwork closely following the music's rhythm, contrasting with the formal posture of competition step dance.
