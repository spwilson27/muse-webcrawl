Primary progressive aphasia (PPA) is a type of neurological syndrome in which language capabilities slowly and progressively become impaired. As with other types of aphasia, the symptoms that accompany PPA depend on which parts of the left hemisphere are significantly damaged. However, unlike most other aphasias, PPA results from continuous deterioration in brain tissue, typically from a neurodegenerative disease, rather than from stroke or head injury. The syndrome was described by the neurologist M. Marsel Mesulam in 1982.

Diagnostic criteria require that language difficulty be the most prominent symptom and the principal cause of impaired daily activities for the initial phase of the illness, with memory, visuospatial skills, and personality relatively preserved early on. Over years the deficits broaden, and most patients eventually develop a more global dementia.

Variants

International consensus criteria (Gorno-Tempini et al., 2011) recognize three variants:

Semantic variant (svPPA)

Also called semantic dementia. Fluent speech with loss of word meaning and object knowledge, poor single-word comprehension, and surface dyslexia; associated with anterior temporal lobe atrophy and usually TDP-43 pathology.

Nonfluent/agrammatic variant (nfvPPA)

Effortful, halting speech with agrammatism and/or apraxia of speech, with relatively preserved single-word comprehension; associated with left posterior frontal and insular atrophy and often tau pathology (frontotemporal lobar degeneration).

Logopenic variant (lvPPA)

Impaired word retrieval and sentence repetition with phonological errors, slow but grammatical speech; associated with left temporoparietal atrophy and most often underlain by Alzheimer's disease pathology.

Causes and pathology

PPA is not a single disease but a clinical syndrome caused by neurodegeneration of the language network. The underlying pathologies are most commonly frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD-tau or FTLD-TDP) and, for the logopenic variant, Alzheimer's disease. A minority of cases are genetic, involving mutations in GRN, MAPT, or C9orf72.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines clinical language assessment with structural MRI (focal atrophy), functional imaging, and increasingly biomarkers to identify the underlying pathology. There is no disease-modifying treatment; management centres on speech-language therapy, compensatory communication strategies (including augmentative devices), and support for patients and families. Cholinesterase inhibitors may be used when Alzheimer's pathology is suspected, though evidence in PPA specifically is limited.
