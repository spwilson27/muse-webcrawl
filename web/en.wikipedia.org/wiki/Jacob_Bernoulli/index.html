Jacob Bernoulli (also James or Jacques; 6 January 1655 – 16 August 1705) was one of the many prominent mathematicians in the Bernoulli family of Swiss mathematicians. An early proponent of Leibnizian calculus, he sided with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz during the Leibniz–Newton calculus controversy. He is known for his numerous contributions to calculus and, along with his brother Johann Bernoulli, was one of the founders of the calculus of variations.

Life

Bernoulli was born in Basel, Switzerland, the son of Nicolaus Bernoulli, a councillor and magistrate. Against his father's wishes, who wanted him to study theology, Jacob pursued mathematics and astronomy. He obtained a degree in theology in 1676 but devoted himself to science, travelling through France, England, and the Netherlands. In 1687 he was appointed professor of mathematics at the University of Basel, a position he held for the rest of his life, teaching among others his brother Johann and his nephew Nicolaus I Bernoulli.

Contributions

Bernoulli's most famous work is the Ars Conjectandi ("The Art of Conjecturing"), published posthumously in 1713, a landmark in probability theory. It contains his proof of the law of large numbers for Bernoulli trials — the first version of that fundamental theorem — and introduces the Bernoulli distribution and Bernoulli numbers. He also derived the mathematical constant e through the study of compound interest, studied the catenary, the lemniscate of Bernoulli, and the logarithmic spiral, which he admired so much that he asked for it to be carved on his tombstone with the motto Eadem mutata resurgo ("Although changed, I arise the same"). Famously, the stonemason carved an Archimedean spiral instead.

Family rivalry

Jacob's relationship with his younger brother Johann deteriorated into bitter public rivalry over problems such as the brachistochrone and isoperimetric problems, even though their competition spurred important advances in the calculus of variations.
