Automatic Train Control

Automatic train control (ATC) is a general class of train protection systems for railways that involves a speed control mechanism in response to external inputs. For example, a system could effect an emergency brake application if the driver does not react to a signal at danger. ATC systems tend to integrate various cab signalling technologies and they evolved from air brake- or track circuit-based systems.

History

Train control systems emerged from the need to prevent collisions and to enforce safe speeds on increasingly busy railways. Early forms relied on mechanical trips and audible warnings in the cab. In the United States, the Interstate Commerce Commission required railroads to install automatic train stop or cab signal equipment on major lines following a series of accidents in the early twentieth century. The Pennsylvania Railroad developed an early continuous cab signal system in the 1920s that displayed the track condition in the locomotive cab.

Operation

ATC typically works by transmitting information from the track to the train, using track circuits, inductive loops, or radio. Onboard equipment continuously supervises the train's speed against the permitted maximum for the section ahead. If the driver fails to acknowledge a restrictive indication or the train exceeds the permitted speed, the system makes an automatic brake application, in severe cases an emergency stop that cannot be released until the train has halted.

Variants

Related systems include automatic train stop (ATS), which applies the brakes if a signal is passed at danger, and automatic train operation (ATO), which can drive the train itself. In Japan, ATC has been used extensively, including on the Shinkansen, where digital ATC replaced the earlier analogue system and permits smoother braking curves calculated continuously by onboard equipment. In Europe, national ATC variants gave way progressively to the European Train Control System (ETCS) under the European Rail Traffic Management System.
