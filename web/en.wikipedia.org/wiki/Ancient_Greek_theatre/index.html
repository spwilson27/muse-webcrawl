Ancient Greek theatre

Theatre of ancient Greece
Period: c. 6th century BC – Hellenistic era
Genres: Tragedy, comedy, satyr play
Key figures: Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes
Centre: Athens (Theatre of Dionysus)

Ancient Greek theatre (or Greek drama) flourished in ancient Greece between about 550 and 220 BC, centred on Athens, where tragedy and comedy were performed at festivals of the god Dionysus such as the City Dionysia. It is the foundation of Western theatre.

Origins and structure

Drama grew from choral dithyrambs sung for Dionysus; tradition credits Thespis (c. 534 BC) as the first actor to step out of the chorus. Plays combined actors wearing masks with a chorus that sang, danced, and commented on the action. The theatre building comprised the circular orchestra, the hillside theatron (seeing place), and the skene (stage building), with devices such as the mechane crane for gods.

Genres and playwrights

Tragedy dramatised myths of gods and heroes: Aeschylus added a second actor, Sophocles a third, and Euripides probed psychology and convention. Surviving works include the Oresteia, Oedipus Rex, and Medea. Old Comedy, exemplified by Aristophanes (Lysistrata, The Frogs), satirised public life; later New Comedy (Menander) turned to domestic situations. Short satyr plays followed tragic trilogies at festivals.

Legacy

Aristotle's Poetics analysed tragedy and coined lasting critical vocabulary — mimesis, catharsis, hamartia. Stone theatres such as Epidaurus, famous for its acoustics, hosted performances across the Greek world, and Greek forms shaped Roman drama and, through the Renaissance, modern theatre.
