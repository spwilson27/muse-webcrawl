Backdoor (computing)

A backdoor is a method, often secret, of bypassing normal authentication or security controls in a computer system, network, or software application. Backdoors may be installed deliberately by developers or manufacturers (for maintenance or recovery), inserted by attackers as malware, or created through compromised credentials.

Types and examples

Backdoors range from undocumented hard-coded passwords in firmware, to hidden administrative accounts, to sophisticated implants. The 1980s Morris worm exploited debugging features left in software; in Ken Thompson's famous 1984 lecture "Reflections on Trusting Trust", he described a compiler backdoor that could invisibly insert a login backdoor into compiled programs. In 2024, the xz Utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094) — malicious code inserted into a widely used compression library by a contributor who had gained maintainer trust over several years — was discovered before it reached most production systems and became a prominent example of a supply-chain backdoor.

Deliberate backdoors and policy debates

Governments have repeatedly debated mandating "lawful access" backdoors or key-escrow arrangements in encryption products, notably during the 1990s Clipper chip controversy. Security experts generally argue that any deliberately weakened access mechanism can be discovered and exploited by adversaries, and therefore cannot be made safe for only authorised parties. Disclosed incidents involving router firmware and telecom equipment have kept the issue prominent.

Detection and defence

Defences include code review and reproducible builds, software bill-of-materials practices, firmware auditing, network monitoring for anomalous connections, and prompt patching. Rootkits frequently include backdoor components to preserve attacker access.
