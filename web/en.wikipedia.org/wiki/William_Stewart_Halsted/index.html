William Stewart Halsted

William Stewart Halsted, M.D. (September 23, 1852 – September 7, 1922) was an American surgeon who emphasised strict aseptic technique during surgical procedures and is considered a founder of modern surgery. One of the "Big Four" founding professors of Johns Hopkins Hospital, he established the first surgical residency training programme in the United States and pioneered the radical mastectomy for breast cancer.

Career

Halsted graduated from Yale in 1874 and from Columbia's College of Physicians and Surgeons in 1877, then studied surgery in Europe under Theodor Billroth and others. Practising in New York in the early 1880s, he was among the first American surgeons to embrace Listerian antisepsis. His research on cocaine as a local anaesthetic led to a personal addiction that dogged his career; after treatment he moved to Baltimore in 1886 at the invitation of William Henry Welch.

Johns Hopkins

When Johns Hopkins Hospital opened in 1889 Halsted became its first surgeon-in-chief and later professor of surgery. He introduced thin rubber gloves (originally to protect his scrub nurse Caroline Hampton's hands from antiseptic dermatitis), championed careful haemostasis and gentle tissue handling, and developed operations for hernia, thyroid disease and breast cancer — the radical mastectomy bearing his name was the standard treatment for decades. His gruelling pyramidal residency trained most of the first generation of American academic surgeons, including Harvey Cushing.
