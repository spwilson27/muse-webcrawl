Pastis (Provençal: pastís, "mash-up") is an anise-flavoured spirit and apéritif traditionally from France, typically containing around 40–45% alcohol by volume and under 100 g/L of sugar. It is most closely associated with Provence and the south of France, where it is the quintessential apéritif of café terraces.

A glass of pastis, cloudy after the addition of water.

History

Pastis was first commercialised by Paul Ricard in 1932 and soon became France's favourite apéritif. Its rise followed the banning of absinthe in 1915: producers such as Henri Bardouin and Ricard developed anise-based substitutes that could legally capture the absinthe market, and in the early 1930s the French government authorised the sale of aniseed spirits at 40% strength, later raised to 45%. Brands such as Ricard, Pernod and Pastis 51 (created by Pernod in 1951, named for the year of its launch) came to dominate the category.

Production and serving

Pastis is made by macerating star anise, liquorice root and aromatic herbs of Provence in neutral alcohol, sometimes with distillation. By law it must contain at least 2 g/L of anethole and a minimum proportion of liquorice. It is almost always served diluted — roughly five parts cold water to one part pastis — which causes the anethole to emulsify and the drink to turn an opaque milky yellow-white, an effect known as louching. It is traditionally poured over ice in a small tumbler during the apéritif hour, often accompanied by variants with syrups: a perroquet (with mint syrup), a tomate (with grenadine) and a mauresque (with orgeat).

Cultural significance

Pastis is deeply embedded in the culture of southern France, evoking pétanque games, summer heat and the novels of Marcel Pagnol. It remains one of France's best-selling spirits by volume, with consumption peaking in the summer months.
