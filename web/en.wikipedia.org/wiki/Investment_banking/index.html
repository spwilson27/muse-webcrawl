Investment banking is an advisory-based financial service for companies, governments and institutions. Unlike commercial banking, it does not take deposits from the public; instead, investment banks help clients raise capital in financial markets, advise on mergers and acquisitions (M&A) and other strategic transactions, and trade securities.

Activities

The core functions of a typical investment bank include underwriting — purchasing newly issued shares or bonds from an issuer and reselling them to investors, as in an initial public offering (IPO); advisory work on mergers, acquisitions, divestitures and restructurings; sales and trading, in which the bank makes markets in securities and executes trades for clients and for its own account; asset management for institutional and wealthy clients; and research on companies and markets.

History

Merchant banks in Europe financed trade and sovereign borrowing from the early modern period onward. In the United States, firms such as J.P. Morgan & Co. and Kuhn, Loeb & Co. financed railroads and industrial consolidation in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The Glass–Steagall Act of 1933 separated commercial and investment banking in the US after the 1929 crash; its partial repeal in 1999 (Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act) allowed universal banks to recombine the businesses. The 2008 financial crisis transformed the industry: Bear Stearns and Merrill Lynch were acquired, Lehman Brothers failed, and the remaining large standalone investment banks, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, converted to bank holding companies.

Structure and criticism

Large "bulge bracket" banks serve the biggest clients globally, while "boutique" firms specialise in advisory work. Investment banking has been criticised for conflicts of interest — for example, between underwriting and research — for its role in the 2008 crisis, and for very high compensation, but it remains central to how large companies raise money and restructure.
