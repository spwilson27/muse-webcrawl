Macadamia

Macadamia
Kingdom:Plantae
Order:Proteales
Family:Proteaceae
Genus:Macadamia F.Muell.
Species:9 recognised species

Macadamia is a genus of nine species of flowering plants in the family Proteaceae. They are native to eastern Australia, New Caledonia and Indonesia (Sulawesi). They are small to large evergreen trees growing to 2–12 m (7–40 ft) tall. The fruit is a very hard, woody, globose follicle with a pointed apex, containing one or two seeds. The seeds — particularly those of Macadamia integrifolia and Macadamia tetraphylla — are edible and are known commercially as macadamia nuts.

Description

The leaves are arranged in whorls of three to six, lanceolate to obovate or elliptical in shape, 6–30 cm long and 3–13 cm broad, with an entire or spiny-serrated margin. The flowers are produced in a long, slender, simple raceme 5–30 cm long; the individual flowers are 10–15 mm long, white to pink or purple, with four tepals. The fruit is a very hard, woody, globose follicle with a pointed apex, containing one or two seeds.

Taxonomy

The genus was named by Ferdinand von Mueller in 1857 after his friend John Macadam, a noted scientist and secretary of the Philosophical Institute of Victoria. The nine accepted species include M. integrifolia, M. tetraphylla, M. ternifolia and M. jansenii.

Cultivation and uses

Only two species, Macadamia integrifolia and M. tetraphylla, produce nuts of commercial importance; hybrids of the two are also widely grown. The nuts are a high-energy food, rich in monounsaturated fats. Commercial production is centred in Hawaii, Australia, South Africa, Kenya, Guatemala and Brazil. Trees take seven to ten years to begin bearing in quantity, and the extremely hard shells require mechanical cracking.

Toxicity

Some species, such as M. ternifolia, produce bitter nuts containing cyanogenic glycosides and are not eaten; selective breeding of commercial cultivars removed these compounds from edible varieties. Macadamia nuts are toxic to dogs.
