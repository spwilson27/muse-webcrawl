Edsger W. Dijkstra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Edsger Dijkstra, portrait photograph, bearded, in later life]

Edsger Wybe Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist, programmer, software engineer, and science essayist. A theoretical physicist by training, he was one of the most influential figures in the young discipline, receiving the Turing Award in 1972 for fundamental contributions to programming as a discipline.

Working at the Centrum Wiskunde & Informatica in Amsterdam from 1952, Dijkstra designed the graph algorithm that bears his name (1956, published 1959) for shortest paths, contributed to the Algol 60 compiler (the first complete implementation), and helped design the THE multiprogramming system (1968), an early layered operating system.

Influence

His 1968 letter 'Go To Statement Considered Harmful' — title supplied by editor Niklaus Wirth — became a founding document of structured programming. He introduced semaphores and the mutual-exclusion problem (Dekker's algorithm context), the dining philosophers problem, and the banker's algorithm; his 1976 book A Discipline of Programming developed predicate transformers and program correctness proofs (weakest preconditions).

Dijkstra was famous for aphorisms ('Computer science is no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes') and for handwritten manuscripts circulated as numbered 'EWDs'. He held the Schlumberger Centennial Chair at the University of Texas at Austin from 1984 until his retirement in 1999.

Legacy

The Dijkstra Prize in distributed computing is named for him. His insistence that programming is a mathematical activity shaped curricula and the formal-methods tradition, even where industry chose more pragmatic paths.
