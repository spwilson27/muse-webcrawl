New Kingdom of Egypt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The great hypostyle hall of Karnak temple, forest of carved columns]

The New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BC), also called the Egyptian Empire, was the third and most celebrated period of ancient Egyptian civilisation, following the Middle Kingdom. Its pharaohs of the 18th, 19th and 20th Dynasties — Ahmose, Hatshepsut, Thutmose III, Akhenaten, Tutankhamun, Seti I, Ramesses II and Ramesses III among them — ruled an empire from Nubia to the Euphrates, built the temples of Karnak and Luxor and the tombs of the Valley of the Kings, and corresponded as equals with the great powers of Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni and the Hittites, whose diplomatic archive survives in the Amarna Letters.

Empire

Ahmose I expelled the Hyksos and reunified Egypt (c. 1550 BC); Thutmose III (r. 1479–1425 BC), at last free of Hatshepsut's co-reign, campaigned seventeen times in Asia, winning at Megiddo (c. 1457 BC) the first battle in history recorded in detail, and made Egypt the dominant power of the Levant. Wealth from Nubian gold and Asiatic tribute funded Amun-Ra's priesthood and the vast temple estates that increasingly rivalled the crown.

Decline

After the Amarna episode and the Ramesside age — Ramesses II's Kadesh and the first peace treaty, Ramesses III's repulse of the Sea Peoples (c. 1175 BC) — the 20th Dynasty waned amid tomb robbery, strikes by the royal workmen of Deir el-Medina (history's first recorded strikes, c. 1152 BC), Libyan incursions and priestly power at Thebes. By 1070 BC Egypt split between Tanite kings in the north and Amun's high priests in the south, opening the Third Intermediate Period.
