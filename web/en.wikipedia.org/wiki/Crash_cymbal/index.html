Crash cymbal

A crash cymbal is a type of cymbal that produces a loud, sharp "crash" and is used mainly for occasional accents, as opposed to a ride cymbal. It can be played on a drum kit or as part of an orchestral percussion section, where pairs of crash cymbals are clashed together by hand.

Characteristics

Crash cymbals in a kit are typically 14 to 18 inches in diameter, thin to medium-thin in weight, and mounted on stands angled towards the drummer. A stroke near the edge with the shoulder of the stick produces the characteristic explosive wash, used to punctuate fills, mark the start of a chorus, or end a song, often in combination with a bass drum stroke. Players frequently "choke" the cymbal by grabbing it to cut the sound short. Repeated crashing on the edge in rock music sometimes leads to cracks, so technique and glancing strokes are used to prolong cymbal life.

Variants

Related types include the smaller, brighter splash cymbal, the fast-decaying china cymbal with its upturned edge and trashy sound, and hybrid "crash/ride" cymbals. In orchestral music, matched pairs of heavier crash cymbals (also called clash cymbals) are struck together or scraped, suspended cymbals are played with mallets, and composers from Berlioz to Mahler wrote prominent parts for them.
