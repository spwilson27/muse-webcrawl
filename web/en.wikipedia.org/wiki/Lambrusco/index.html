Lambrusco

Lambrusco is the name of both a family of red wine grape varieties and the lightly sparkling red wine made from them in the Emilia-Romagna and Lombardy regions of northern Italy, principally around the cities of Modena, Parma, Reggio Emilia and Mantua. The wines, which range from bone dry (secco) to sweet (amabile), are made to be drunk young and are traditionally served with the rich cuisine of Emilia.

Grapes and styles

The principal varieties include Lambrusco Salamino, Lambrusco Grasparossa and Lambrusco di Sorbara, each with its own DOC. Sorbara wines are the palest, lightest and most aromatic; Grasparossa, from the hills of Castelvetro, gives the darkest, fullest-bodied and most tannic wines; Salamino, named for the salami-like shape of its bunches, is the most widely planted. The fizz is traditionally obtained by a secondary fermentation — historically in bottle, nowadays usually by the Charmat (tank) method.

History

Wild and cultivated vines of the Lambrusco type were known to the Etruscans and Romans; Cato and Pliny remarked on vines in the region. In the 1970s and 1980s, sweet, industrially produced Lambrusco — notably Riunite — became one of the best-selling imported wines in the United States, which for a generation fixed the name's reputation as cheap and sweet. Since the 1990s, artisan producers have revived dry, bottle-fermented and single-variety Lambruscos, winning recognition for the traditional style.

Appellations

DOC wines include Lambrusco di Sorbara, Lambrusco Grasparossa di Castelvetro, Lambrusco Salamino di Santa Croce, Reggiano Lambrusco and Lambrusco Mantovano.
