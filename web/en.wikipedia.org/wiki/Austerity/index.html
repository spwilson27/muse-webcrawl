Austerity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Austerity is a set of political-economic policies that aim to reduce government budget deficits through spending cuts, tax increases, or a combination of both. Austerity programmes are typically adopted when a government cannot or will not service rising debt, when creditors or currency-union rules demand consolidation, or when policymakers believe deficit reduction will restore confidence and growth ("expansionary fiscal contraction", in the doctrine associated with Alesina and others).

Record and argument

The defining modern episodes are the British consolidation after 2010, the euro-area programmes imposed on Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Italy during the debt crisis (with IMF participation and later IMF reassessment that fiscal multipliers had been underestimated), and Latin American stabilisations of earlier decades. Supporters cite debt sustainability, market confidence, and successful consolidations (Canada in the 1990s, Ireland's export-led recovery); critics (Krugman, Blyth's Austerity: The History of a Dangerous Idea) cite depressed demand, hysteresis, worsened debt ratios through shrinking GDP, and concentrated social costs — the distributional argument becoming the populist argument of the 2010s. The pandemic suspended austerity everywhere at once; its post-2022 return, renamed as "fiscal consolidation" under high interest rates, resumed the old contest on new terrain.
