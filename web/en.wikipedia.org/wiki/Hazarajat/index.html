Hazarajat

The Hazarajat is a mountainous region in the central highlands of Afghanistan, inhabited predominantly by the Hazara people. It spans all or part of the provinces of Bamyan, Daykundi, Ghor, Ghazni, Uruzgan, Parwan and Maidan Wardak, among others. The region is centred on the Hindu Kush and the Koh-i-Baba mountain ranges.

Geography

The Hazarajat is high, rugged and largely arid, with elevations commonly between 2,000 and 3,500 metres. Winters are long and severe, and many areas are cut off by snow for months. Rivers rising in the region include the Helmand, Hari Rud, Kunduz and Balkh.

History

The region was once a flourishing centre of Buddhism, evidenced by the Buddhas of Bamiyan, colossal statues carved in the sixth century and destroyed in 2001. It later came under Mongol influence, and the Hazara emerged as the region's principal inhabitants. In the 1890s the region was conquered by Amir Abdur Rahman Khan after campaigns that killed or enslaved a large share of the Hazara population and redistributed much of their land.

Economy and society

Agriculture and livestock herding are the main livelihoods, with wheat, potatoes and almonds among the principal crops. Bamyan province is a centre of tourism within Afghanistan, known for the Band-e Amir lakes and the former Buddha niches.
