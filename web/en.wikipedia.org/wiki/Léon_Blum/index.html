Léon Blum (1872–1950) was a French socialist politician and writer who led the Popular Front government of 1936–37 and served three times as Prime Minister of France. He was the first socialist, and first Jewish, prime minister of France.

Popular Front

After the electoral victory of the Popular Front coalition in May 1936, amid a wave of sit-down strikes, Blum's government enacted the Matignon Agreements: the 40-hour week, paid holidays, collective bargaining rights, and wage increases. His government also dissolved far-right leagues, nationalised the arms industry and reformed the Bank of France. Faced with economic difficulties and the Spanish Civil War — in which he adopted a policy of non-intervention — he resigned in June 1937.

War and postwar

Imprisoned by the Vichy regime and tried at the Riom Trial (1942), Blum was deported to Buchenwald. He survived and, after liberation, briefly headed a provisional government (December 1946 – January 1947), negotiated American loans (the Blum–Byrnes agreements) and led the French delegation at UNESCO's founding.
