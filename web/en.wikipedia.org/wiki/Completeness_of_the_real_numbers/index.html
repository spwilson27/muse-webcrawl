Completeness of the real numbers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The real line: completeness means there are no "gaps" in it

The completeness of the real numbers is the property of the real number system that distinguishes it from the rational numbers and underlies much of real analysis. Intuitively, completeness means that there are no "gaps" or "missing points" in the real line: every point that ought to be there is there. The rational numbers fail this property — for example, the set of rationals whose square is less than 2 has no least upper bound within the rationals, because its supremum would be √2, which is irrational.

Forms of completeness

Several equivalent statements express completeness for an ordered field:

  • Least-upper-bound property — every nonempty subset of the reals that is bounded above has a least upper bound (supremum) that is itself a real number.
  • Cauchy completeness — every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges to a real number.
  • Monotone convergence — every bounded monotone sequence of real numbers converges.
  • Nested interval property — every nested sequence of closed intervals whose lengths shrink to zero contains exactly one point.
  • Dedekind completeness — every Dedekind cut of the reals is produced by a real number.

In the standard axiomatic treatment, the real numbers are characterized as the unique (up to isomorphism) complete ordered field; one of the equivalent forms above is adopted as the completeness axiom.

Consequences

Completeness is what makes calculus work over the reals. The intermediate value theorem, the extreme value theorem, the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, and the convergence of bounded monotone sequences all rest on it. Constructions of the real numbers — by Dedekind cuts of rationals (Dedekind, 1872) or by equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences (Cantor, 1872) — produce a complete field from the incomplete rationals, and any two such constructions yield isomorphic structures.

Generalizations

The idea generalizes beyond the reals: a metric space is complete if every Cauchy sequence converges in the space, and completion of a metric space can be constructed by the Cauchy-sequence method. Uniform spaces and ordered fields admit their own notions of completeness, which need not coincide.
