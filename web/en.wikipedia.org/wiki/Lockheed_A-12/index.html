The Lockheed A-12 is a retired high-altitude, Mach 3+ reconnaissance aircraft built for the United States Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) by Lockheed's Skunk Works, based on the designs of Clarence "Kelly" Johnson. Developed under the top-secret Oxcart programme as the successor to the U-2, the single-seat A-12 was faster and flew higher than any other manned aircraft of its era, and it was the direct ancestor of the better-known SR-71 Blackbird.

Development

Concerned that the subsonic U-2 spyplane was becoming vulnerable to Soviet surface-to-air missiles—a fear confirmed when Francis Gary Powers was shot down in 1960—the CIA sought an aircraft that could survive by speed and altitude alone. Lockheed's design, the twelfth in its internal "Archangel" series (hence A-12), combined two Pratt & Whitney J58 turbojet/ramjet engines with a titanium airframe able to withstand the heat of sustained flight above Mach 3.2, chines and radar-absorbent materials to shrink its radar cross-section, and a ceiling above 85,000 feet. Because the Soviet Union controlled much of the world's titanium supply, the CIA quietly acquired the metal through shell companies. The first A-12 was flown in secret from Groom Lake (Area 51) in April 1962.

Operational history

The A-12's only operational deployment, codenamed Operation Black Shield, began in 1967 from Kadena Air Base on Okinawa. A-12s flew missions over North Vietnam and Laos during the Vietnam War, photographing surface-to-air missile sites and supporting the search for the captured USS Pueblo off North Korea in 1968. No A-12 was ever shot down, although North Vietnamese gunners came close. Despite its success, the programme was cancelled in 1968 in favour of the Air Force's two-seat SR-71, and the A-12 fleet was retired and placed in storage. Surviving aircraft are now displayed in museums across the United States; one two-seat trainer variant, the M-21 mothership for the D-21 drone, is preserved at the Museum of Flight in Seattle.

Specifications

The A-12 was about 31 m (101 ft) long, carried a single pilot, and could exceed Mach 3.2 (roughly 3,500 km/h) at altitudes above 85,000 ft (25,900 m), with an unrefuelled range of about 4,000 km. In official record attempts, its SR-71 derivative—not the still-classified A-12—set the public speed and altitude records for air-breathing manned aircraft.
