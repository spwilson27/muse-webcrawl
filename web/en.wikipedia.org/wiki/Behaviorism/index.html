Behaviorism

Behaviorism
FieldPsychology
Key figuresJohn B. Watson, Ivan Pavlov, B. F. Skinner, Edward Thorndike
Key conceptsClassical conditioning, operant conditioning, reinforcement

Behaviorism is a systematic approach to understanding the behaviour of humans and animals. It assumes that behaviour is either a reflex evoked by environmental stimuli or a consequence of an individual's history of reinforcement and punishment, and it dominated much of psychology in the first half of the 20th century.

History

John B. Watson launched the school with his 1913 paper "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It", arguing psychology should study only observable behaviour rather than introspected mental states. Ivan Pavlov's work on classical conditioning in dogs, and Edward Thorndike's law of effect, supplied its experimental foundations. B. F. Skinner developed radical behaviorism and the analysis of operant conditioning, using the "Skinner box" to study how reinforcement schedules shape behaviour; his books include The Behavior of Organisms (1938) and Walden Two (1948).

Concepts

Classical conditioning pairs a neutral stimulus with one that naturally evokes a response, as in Watson's controversial "Little Albert" experiment (1920). Operant conditioning modifies behaviour through rewards and punishments. Behaviourists explained learning without reference to internal mental representations.

Criticism and legacy

From the 1950s the cognitive revolution — associated with figures such as Noam Chomsky, whose 1959 review savaged Skinner's account of language — restored the mind to scientific psychology. Nevertheless behaviourist methods survive in applied behaviour analysis, behaviour therapy, education and animal training, and its insistence on observable, measurable outcomes reshaped experimental psychology permanently.
