Ideasthesia

Ideasthesia is a neuropsychological phenomenon in which activation of concepts (semantic information) evokes perception-like experiences. The term, from the Greek idea (concept) and aisthesis (sensation), was introduced by neuroscientist Danko Nikolić and colleagues to reframe the study of synesthesia: whereas synesthesia is traditionally described as one sensory stimulus triggering another sensation, ideasthesia emphasizes that it is the meaning of the stimulus — the concept it activates — that induces the concurrent experience.

Evidence

Several findings motivated the concept. In grapheme–colour synesthesia, the colour experienced for a letter depends on its conceptual identity: the same ambiguous character elicits different colours depending on whether it is interpreted as the letter S or the numeral 5. Newly learned symbols acquire synesthetic colours once they acquire meaning, and colours can shift when a symbol's meaning changes. Lexical–gustatory synesthesia likewise shows that it is the concept of a word, not its sound alone, that evokes tastes. Experiments with the "Balkan" style of studies — in which participants learn novel characters as letters — demonstrated that mere exposure is insufficient; conceptual categorization drives the effect.

Theoretical significance

Ideasthesia suggests that perception-like experiences can be generated at the level of semantic representation, blurring the boundary between sensing and thinking. Nikolić proposed that ideasthesia underlies aspects of ordinary cognition as well: metaphors, in which abstract concepts are experienced through sensory ones, and even the phenomenality of consciousness itself, through the theory of "practopoiesis." The framework has influenced research on how meaning shapes perception, including cross-modal correspondences in non-synesthetes, such as the bouba/kiki effect.

Relation to synesthesia

Ideasthesia is not a separate condition but a proposed re-description of synesthetic phenomena: synesthesia is a special, unusually vivid case of a general principle by which concepts evoke experiences. Researchers debate whether all forms of synesthesia are meaning-driven or whether some, such as certain sound–colour pairings in absolute-pitch musicians, are better explained by direct sensory cross-activation.
