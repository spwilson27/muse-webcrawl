Meissner effect

The Meissner effect (or Meissner–Ochsenfeld effect) is the expulsion of a magnetic field from a superconductor during its transition to the superconducting state when it is cooled below the critical temperature. Discovered by Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld in 1933, the effect shows that superconductivity is not merely perfect conductivity: a perfect conductor would trap whatever flux was present when it cooled, whereas a superconductor actively expels the field from its interior.

[Image: A magnet levitating above a high-temperature superconductor cooled in liquid nitrogen]
Magnetic levitation produced by the Meissner effect and flux pinning.

Explanation

Within BCS theory and the phenomenological London equations, screening currents flow in a thin surface layer (the London penetration depth, typically tens to hundreds of nanometres) that exactly cancel the applied field inside the material. The Meissner state persists up to a critical field; in type-II superconductors, flux enters between the lower and upper critical fields in quantised vortices.

Applications

The effect underlies magnetic levitation demonstrations and, with flux pinning, maglev bearings and levitating trains. It is also the working principle of sensitive magnetometry based on superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs).
