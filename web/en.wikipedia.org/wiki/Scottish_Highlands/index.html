Scottish Highlands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Scottish Highlands are a mountainous region encompassing the northern and western parts of Scotland, separated from the Lowlands by the Highland Boundary Fault and by a distinct cultural identity shaped by Gaelic language and clan society. The Highlands include the highest ground in the British Isles — Ben Nevis (1,345 m) — and some of Europe's oldest rocks, scoured by ice into glens, corries and long freshwater lochs such as Loch Ness, home of the fabled monster, and Loch Lomond.

History and culture

For centuries the Highlands were governed through the clan system, with allegiance to chiefs rather than distant kings; Gaelic (Gàidhlig) was the majority language into the early modern era. Highland support for the exiled Stuarts produced the Jacobite risings of 1689, 1715 and 1745 — the last ending at Culloden (1746), after which the British state disarmed the clans, banned tartan dress (until 1782) and dismantled heritable jurisdictions. From the late 18th century the Highland Clearances evicted thousands of tenants for sheep farming, driving emigration to Canada, Australia and New Zealand and depopulating glens whose ruins still mark the landscape. In the 19th century Queen Victoria's love of Balmoral repackaged the Highlands as romantic wilderness — tartan, whisky, stalking and Highland games — an image now central to Scottish tourism. Today the region faces familiar rural challenges (housing, depopulation, land ownership concentrated in large estates) alongside rewilding projects and a Gaelic revival centred on Inverness, the "capital of the Highlands".
