Fraunhofer lines

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fraunhofer lines are a set of dark spectral absorption lines in the optical spectrum of the Sun. They are named after the German physicist Joseph von Fraunhofer, who systematically observed and catalogued them from 1814 onward. The lines are produced when light from the Sun's hot interior passes through cooler gases in its outer atmosphere, whose atoms absorb light at characteristic wavelengths.

The solar spectrum, showing the dark Fraunhofer absorption lines against the rainbow continuum.

Discovery

The English chemist William Hyde Wollaston first noticed a few dark lines in the solar spectrum in 1802, but it was Fraunhofer, using a precision spectroscope of his own design, who mapped some 574 lines and labelled the most prominent with the letters A through K — a notation still in use today. In 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen showed that each chemical element produces a unique pattern of spectral lines, identifying the Fraunhofer lines with elements such as sodium, calcium, iron and hydrogen in the solar atmosphere.

Importance

Fraunhofer lines became reference standards for wavelength measurement and the foundation of astronomical spectroscopy. Because the same lines appear in starlight, shifted by the Doppler effect, they allow astronomers to measure stellar composition, temperature and radial velocity. The calcium H and K lines and the hydrogen Balmer lines remain everyday tools of solar and stellar physics, and the relative strengths of Fraunhofer features underpin the classification of stellar spectra.
