Apparent death

Apparent death is a state in which a living person exhibits signs so close to death — imperceptible pulse and breathing, cold skin, fixed pupils — that they may be mistaken for a corpse. Known in older medical literature by the Latin vita minima and related to the phenomena of thanatosis in animals, apparent death was a major cultural anxiety of the 18th and 19th centuries.

Causes

Conditions that can produce apparent death include catalepsy, hypothermia, severe shock, drowning, electrical injury, poisoning, and certain metabolic or neurological disorders. In animals, tonic immobility ("playing dead") is an evolved defensive behaviour seen in opossums, some sharks, and many insects.

Historical concern

Fear of premature burial led to waiting mortuaries in Germany, where corpses were observed for signs of putrefaction before burial, and to patented "safety coffins" fitted with bells, flags, and breathing tubes. Edgar Allan Poe drew on these fears in "The Premature Burial" (1844). Modern determination of death by neurological criteria and monitoring has greatly reduced, though not entirely eliminated, diagnostic uncertainty.
