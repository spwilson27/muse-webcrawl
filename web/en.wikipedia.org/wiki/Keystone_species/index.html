Keystone species

A keystone species is a species whose effect on its ecosystem is disproportionately large for its abundance — like the keystone of an arch, its removal causes collapse of much of the structure. The concept is central to ecology and Conservation biology.

Origins and examples

Robert Paine coined the term in 1969 after removing the starfish Pisaster from Pacific shores: mussels then monopolised space and diversity crashed. Classic cases: sea otters keep sea urchins from destroying kelp forests; wolves reshape deer behaviour and vegetation in Yellowstone; elephants maintain Savanna by toppling trees; beavers engineer wetlands wholesale.

Use

Protecting keystone species is an efficient conservation strategy, and reintroducing them (‘trophic rewilding’) can restore whole food webs. The concept is sometimes stretched too loosely, so ecologists insist on demonstrated disproportionate impact.
