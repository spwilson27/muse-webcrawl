Liberal arts

Liberal arts education (from Latin liberalis, "free", and ars, "art or principled practice") is the traditional programme of higher education in Western history. The term liberal arts may refer either to academic disciplines — such as literature, languages, philosophy, history, mathematics, and science — or to a philosophy of education that takes a broad, interdisciplinary approach rather than narrow vocational training. In classical antiquity the liberal arts were the subjects considered essential for a free person to know in order to take an active part in civic life.

History

The concept originated in ancient Greece and Rome. In late antiquity the seven liberal arts were codified, and through the medieval period they formed the core of university education: the trivium of grammar, logic (dialectic), and rhetoric, followed by the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. Writers such as Martianus Capella and Boethius transmitted the scheme to the Middle Ages, where personifications of the seven arts became a standard motif in art.

During the Renaissance the studia humanitatis — grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry, and moral philosophy — reshaped liberal education toward the study of classical texts. In the modern era the liberal arts ideal underlies the liberal arts colleges of the United States, which emphasise small classes and broad undergraduate study across the humanities, social sciences, and natural sciences.

Modern usage

Today "liberal arts" commonly denotes the academic disciplines of the humanities and the basic sciences, as distinct from professional, vocational, or technical curricula. Advocates argue that such an education develops critical thinking, communication, and adaptability; critics question its cost and direct vocational value. The model has spread beyond its Western origins, with liberal arts institutions founded in Europe, Asia, and elsewhere in recent decades.
