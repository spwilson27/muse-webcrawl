Meadow

A meadow is an open habitat or field, vegetated by grasses, herbs, and other non-woody plants (grassland). Trees or shrubs may sparsely populate meadows, as long as these areas maintain an open character. Meadows are a type of nearly natural grassland, often used for agriculture or grazing. Meadows attract a multitude of wildlife, and support flora and fauna that could not thrive in other habitats. They are ecologically important as they provide areas for animal courtship displays, nesting, food gathering, pollinating insects, and plants.

Types

Meadows may be naturally occurring — as in alpine meadows above the tree line, flood meadows along rivers, and coastal meadows — or created and maintained by human activity, such as hay meadows mown annually to produce hay and water meadows deliberately flooded in early spring to encourage early growth. Wet meadows lie on saturated soils, while urban meadows are planted in parks and gardens for biodiversity and amenity.

Ecology

Traditionally managed species-rich meadows are among the most biodiverse habitats in temperate regions, supporting diverse communities of wildflowers, grasses, butterflies, bees, and ground-nesting birds. The decline of traditional hay-making and the conversion of meadows to intensive farmland or development have caused widespread losses of meadow habitat, prompting conservation programs based on late mowing, low nutrient input, and grazing.
