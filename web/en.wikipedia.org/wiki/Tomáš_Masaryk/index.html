Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk (7 March 1850 – 14 September 1937) was a Czechoslovak statesman, political activist and philosopher who served as the first president of Czechoslovakia, holding office from the state's founding in 1918 until his resignation in 1935. Revered as the "President Liberator" (Prezident Osvoboditel), he is regarded as the founding father of the Czechoslovak state.

Early life and academic career

Masaryk was born in Hodonín, Moravia, the son of a Slovak coachman and a German-speaking Moravian mother. He studied philosophy at the University of Vienna, receiving his doctorate in 1876, and later became a professor at the Czech Charles-Ferdinand University in Prague. As an academic he championed critical thinking, famously challenging the authenticity of supposedly ancient Czech manuscripts and defending a Jewish man, Leopold Hilsner, against a blood-libel charge — stances that made him unpopular with nationalist opinion.

Path to independence

During the First World War, Masaryk went into exile and campaigned across Britain, France, Russia and the United States for the dismemberment of Austria-Hungary and the creation of an independent Czechoslovak state. He helped organise the Czechoslovak Legion in Russia and secured Allied recognition of the Czechoslovak National Council as a government-in-exile.

Presidency

Czechoslovakia was proclaimed independent on 28 October 1918, and Masaryk was elected its first president the following month, being re-elected in 1920, 1927 and 1934. A deeply respected moral authority, he presided over the only functioning democracy in interwar Central Europe. Ill health forced his resignation in December 1935; he died at Lány Castle in 1937, a year before the Munich Agreement dismantled his republic.
