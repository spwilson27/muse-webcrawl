Whaling in the Faroe Islands

Whaling in the Faroe Islands centres on the drive hunting of long-finned pilot whales, known in Faroese as the grindadráp (often shortened to "the grind"). The hunt, documented since the 16th century and with roots possibly reaching back to the earliest Norse settlement, involves herding pods of whales and dolphins into shallow bays, where they are beached and killed, and the meat and blubber shared among the local community.

History

Regulations concerning the division of whale catches appear in the "Sheep Letter" of 1298, and statistics of drive hunts have been kept since 1584, making the Faroese record one of the longest-running wildlife statistics in the world. Whale meat and blubber historically formed an important part of the islanders' diet.

The hunt

When a pod is sighted, boats form a semicircle and drive the animals into an approved bay. A specially designed spinal lance and blunt hook are now used under Faroese regulations intended to shorten the killing process; only licensed participants may take part in the killing, and the catch is distributed free of charge according to traditional shares rather than sold commercially.

Scale

Annual catches vary considerably, averaging several hundred pilot whales in typical years; in some years none are taken. The long-finned pilot whale is not classified as endangered, and supporters of the hunt argue it is sustainable and a non-commercial community tradition.

Controversy

The practice is strongly criticised by animal-welfare and conservation organisations, which object to the killing of cetaceans and to the spectacle of the drives; a 2021 drive in which more than 1,400 Atlantic white-sided dolphins were killed prompted international criticism and a Faroese government review, after which a provisional annual quota for dolphins was introduced. Health authorities have also debated the safety of eating pilot whale meat because of mercury and PCB contamination, and Faroese health officials have advised limiting consumption.
