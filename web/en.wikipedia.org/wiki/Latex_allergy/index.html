Latex allergy

Latex allergy
SpecialtyAllergy and immunology
SymptomsItchy skin, hives, runny nose, wheezing; in severe cases anaphylaxis
Risk factorsRepeated exposure to natural rubber latex; health care work; spina bifida
PreventionAvoidance of latex; use of nitrile or vinyl alternatives

Latex allergy is a medical term encompassing a range of allergic reactions to the proteins present in natural rubber latex, a product derived from the milky sap of the rubber tree Hevea brasiliensis. Reactions range from mild skin irritation to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Latex became a major occupational health issue in the 1980s and 1990s, when the adoption of universal precautions during the HIV/AIDS epidemic dramatically increased the use of latex gloves among healthcare workers; prevalence among healthcare workers has been estimated at up to about 10 per cent, compared with roughly 1–6 per cent in the general population. Since then, reduced use of powdered, high-protein latex gloves has lowered rates in many countries.

Powdered latex gloves were a major source of sensitisation among healthcare workers.

Types of reaction

  • Irritant contact dermatitis: the most common reaction — dry, itchy, irritated skin caused by friction, detergents, and glove powder. It is not a true allergy.
  • Allergic contact dermatitis (Type IV): a delayed, T-cell-mediated reaction to chemical additives used in rubber manufacture, appearing as an eczematous rash 24–48 hours after contact.
  • Immediate (Type I) allergy: an IgE-mediated reaction to natural latex proteins, causing hives, itching, rhinitis, conjunctivitis, asthma, and, in severe cases, anaphylaxis within minutes of exposure.

Risk factors and cross-reactivity

People at increased risk include healthcare workers, rubber industry workers, and patients who have undergone many surgical procedures — especially children with spina bifida, who historically experienced very high rates of sensitisation. Many people with latex allergy also react to certain fruits and vegetables containing cross-reactive proteins, a phenomenon known as the latex–fruit syndrome; commonly implicated foods include banana, avocado, kiwifruit, chestnut, and papaya.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is by clinical history supported by blood tests for latex-specific IgE or by skin prick testing. There is no cure; management is strict avoidance of natural rubber latex and substitution with synthetic alternatives such as nitrile, neoprene, or vinyl for gloves, and non-latex materials for catheters, balloons, condoms, and elastic. People with severe allergy may need to carry an adrenaline auto-injector and wear medical identification. Hospitals increasingly maintain latex-free environments and policies for sensitised patients and staff.
