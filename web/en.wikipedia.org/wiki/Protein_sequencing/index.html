Protein sequencing

Protein sequencing is the practical process of determining the amino acid sequence of all or part of a protein or peptide. This may serve to identify the protein or characterize its post-translational modifications. Typically, partial sequencing of a protein provides enough information (one or more sequence "tags") to identify it by reference to databases of protein sequences derived from the conceptual translation of genes.

Methods

Edman degradation

The Edman degradation, developed by Pehr Edman, sequentially labels and cleaves amino acids from the N-terminus of a peptide. Phenyl isothiocyanate reacts with the free N-terminal amino group; the terminal residue is cleaved as a phenylthiohydantoin derivative and identified, and the cycle repeats. The method is reliable for roughly 30–50 residues but requires a free N-terminus and relatively pure samples.

Mass spectrometry

The two major direct methods of protein sequencing by mass spectrometry are Edman degradation and mass spectrometry (MS). In modern practice, tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) dominates: proteins are digested with a protease such as trypsin, the resulting peptides are fragmented in the instrument, and sequences are inferred from the mass differences between fragment ions — either by matching to sequence databases (peptide mass fingerprinting and database search) or de novo.

History

Frederick Sanger determined the first complete protein sequence, that of insulin (51 amino acids), in the early 1950s, proving that proteins have defined sequences and earning the 1958 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Today, sequences are usually inferred from DNA sequencing, with direct protein sequencing used for validation, antibody characterization, and the study of modifications not predictable from genes.
