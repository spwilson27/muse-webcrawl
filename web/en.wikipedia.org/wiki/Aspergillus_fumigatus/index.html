Aspergillus fumigatus is a species of fungus in the genus Aspergillus and is one of the most common Aspergillus species to cause disease in immunocompromised individuals. It is a saprotroph that lives on decaying organic matter, especially self-heating compost, where its thermotolerance — growth at temperatures up to about 50 °C and beyond — gives it an advantage.

Biology

The fungus produces huge numbers of small airborne conidia (2–3 micrometres), readily inhaled by humans and animals. Its colonies are typically blue-green to grey with a characteristic columnar conidial head. A sexual stage (teleomorph Neosartorya fumigata) was described in 2009. Secondary metabolites include the immunosuppressive toxin gliotoxin.

Clinical significance

In people with severely weakened immune systems — for example after transplantation, chemotherapy or in advanced HIV disease — inhaled conidia can cause invasive aspergillosis, a frequently fatal infection. In asthmatic and cystic fibrosis patients it can cause allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA). Azole-resistant strains, partly linked to agricultural fungicide use, are an emerging treatment problem. The genome was sequenced in 2005.
