Scientific research

Scientific research is a systematic way of gathering data and harnessing curiosity. It provides scientific information and theories for the explanation of the nature and properties of the world. It makes practical applications possible. Scientific research is funded by public authorities, by charitable organisations and by private groups, including many companies. Scientific research can be subdivided into different classifications, such as basic research and applied research.

The research process

Although research procedures vary from one field to another, researchers generally share a common cycle: observation and description of a phenomenon; formulation of a hypothesis that explains it; prediction of consequences that follow from the hypothesis; experimental or observational testing of those predictions; and analysis and communication of results. The scientific method is often represented as this ongoing process, combined with rigorous documentation, sceptical review by peers, and willingness to revise conclusions in the light of new evidence.

Basic and applied research

Basic research (or fundamental or pure research) is driven by curiosity and the desire to expand knowledge, without a specific application in view — although it frequently underlies later technologies. Applied research is designed to solve practical problems, and translational research seeks to convert basic findings into usable products or treatments, as in the pipeline from laboratory biology to clinical medicine. Development, sometimes grouped as R&D (research and development), converts research results into commercial products and processes.

Institutions and funding

Scientific research is conducted in universities, government laboratories, hospitals, independent institutes and industry. Historically funded by patrons and learned societies, modern research is financed largely through competitive grants from national agencies such as the U.S. National Science Foundation and National Institutes of Health, the European Research Council, and research councils in the UK and elsewhere — as well as substantial corporate spending, particularly in pharmaceuticals, computing and engineering. Performance is commonly measured by publication output, citation impact and patents, measures whose use is itself debated within science policy.

Ethics and integrity

Scientific research is governed by norms of honesty, openness and organised scepticism. Misconduct — fabrication, falsification and plagiarism — is investigated by institutions and funders. Research involving human subjects and animals requires ethical approval and informed consent; concerns also arise over conflicts of interest, reproducibility, and the responsible communication of uncertain results.
