The Roman Forum (Latin: Forum Romanum; Italian: Foro Romano) is a rectangular forum surrounded by the ruins of several important ancient government buildings at the centre of the city of Rome. For centuries it was the heart of Roman public life: the site of triumphal processions and elections, public speeches, criminal trials, commercial affairs and the nucleus of the city's — and eventually the empire's — political machinery. It lies in the valley between the Palatine and Capitoline Hills.

History

The valley was originally a marshy burial ground between early settlements; tradition credited the kings of Rome with draining it via the Cloaca Maxima in the 6th century BC and paving the first forum. Under the Republic the open space filled with temples, basilicas for law courts and business, the Senate house (Curia), the Rostra speakers' platform, and commemorative arches and columns. Julius Caesar and Augustus monumentalised the area and, finding it too small, began the adjacent Imperial Fora. Successive emperors restored temples and added landmarks, including the Temple of Antoninus and Faustina, the Arch of Septimius Severus (AD 203) and the vast Basilica of Maxentius; the last monument erected was the Column of Phocas in AD 608.

Decline and rediscovery

As imperial government moved elsewhere, the Forum decayed; temples were converted to churches (which preserved some buildings), marble was burned for lime, and the buried square became the Campo Vaccino, a cow pasture. Renaissance popes quarried it heavily even as antiquarians began recording it. Systematic excavation began under Napoleon in the early 19th century and continued under the Italian state; the site is now part of the Colosseum–Roman Forum–Palatine archaeological park, visited by millions each year.

Principal remains

  • Curia Julia — the Senate house begun by Julius Caesar and completed by Augustus; its bronze doors now serve the Lateran basilica, and the building survives largely intact thanks to its use as a church.
  • Temples — of Saturn (housing the state treasury), of Castor and Pollux (three towering columns survive), of Vesta and the House of the Vestals, of Venus and Roma, and of Antoninus and Faustina.
  • Arches — of Titus (c. AD 81), depicting the sack of Jerusalem; of Septimius Severus; and the vanished Arch of Augustus.
  • Basilica Julia and Basilica Aemilia — great halls flanking the square; the Basilica of Maxentius with its colossal concrete vaults.
  • Via Sacra — the Sacred Way, Rome's oldest street, climbing from the Colosseum valley to the Capitol.
  • Lapis Niger and the Rostra — among the oldest sacred and political sites in the city.
