The Hayflick limit, or Hayflick phenomenon, is the number of times a normal somatic cell population can divide before it stops dividing and enters a state of senescence. For normal human fetal fibroblasts in culture the limit is roughly 40 to 60 population doublings.

Discovery

In 1961 Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead at the Wistar Institute showed that cultured normal human fibroblasts divide only a finite number of times before entering what Hayflick called Phase III, a state of replicative senescence. This overturned the prevailing belief, based on Alexis Carrel's flawed experiments, that normal cells in culture were immortal, and established that only cancerous or otherwise transformed cells divide indefinitely.

Mechanism

The limit is caused by the progressive shortening of telomeres, the protective repetitive DNA caps at the ends of chromosomes, which erode with each round of DNA replication because DNA polymerase cannot fully copy chromosome ends (the end-replication problem, anticipated by Alexey Olovnikov in 1971). When telomeres become critically short, the cell triggers a DNA damage response and permanently arrests, via pathways involving p53 and p16. The enzyme telomerase, which extends telomeres, is active in germline and stem cells and is reactivated in most cancers.

Significance

The Hayflick limit connects cellular senescence to organismal ageing: senescent cells accumulate in tissues with age and secrete inflammatory factors (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype), contributing to age-related disease. Conversely, the limit acts as a tumour-suppression mechanism. Animals with different lifespans show correspondingly different replicative limits.
