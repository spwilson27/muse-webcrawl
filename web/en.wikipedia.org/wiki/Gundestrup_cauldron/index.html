Gundestrup cauldron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gundestrup cauldron
MaterialSilver (97.4% pure)
Created1st century BC (c. 150 BC – AD 1 debated)
Discovered1891, Rævemose bog, Gundestrup, Denmark
Present locationNational Museum of Denmark, Copenhagen

The Gundestrup cauldron is a richly decorated silver vessel, the largest known example of European Iron Age silver work, dating to the 1st century BC. It was found dismantled, with its plates stacked in a peat bog near the hamlet of Gundestrup in Himmerland, Denmark, in 1891. The cauldron is now in the collection of the National Museum of Denmark in Copenhagen, with a replica in the National Museum of Ireland.

Description

The cauldron measures 42 cm (17 inches) in height and 69 cm (27 inches) in diameter, and weighs almost 9 kg. It consists of a round base plate, five interior plates, and seven (of probably eight) exterior plates, worked in repoussé with figures of gods, goddesses, warriors, animals, and fabulous beasts. One famous interior plate shows a large antlered male figure, seated cross-legged and holding a torc and a ram-headed serpent — a figure widely identified with the Celtic god Cernunnos. Another plate depicts a procession of warriors, a line of men apparently being immersed head-first in a cauldron by a giant figure, a scene sometimes interpreted as a rite of sacrifice or rebirth.

Origin

The cauldron's place of manufacture is debated. Although found in Denmark, its workmanship and style point to the lower Danube region — Thrace or Dacia — where silversmiths combined Celtic and Thracian motifs, possibly working for a Celtic patron such as the Scordisci. It may have reached Jutland as war booty taken from the Cimbri, a Germanic tribe thought to have originated in the region. The vessel was deliberately deposited in the bog, probably as a votive offering.

Interpretation

The imagery blends figures from Celtic, Germanic, and eastern traditions, and the cauldron remains one of the most important sources for the iconography of Iron Age religion. Its scenes have been connected to Celtic mythology, to later insular literature such as the Welsh tale of Branwen, and (controversially) to motifs as far afield as the Indus Valley. Most scholars treat it as a Thracian-made work commissioned within a Celtic milieu.
