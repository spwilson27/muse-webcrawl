Vortex

In fluid dynamics, a vortex (plural vortices or vortexes) is a region in a fluid in which the flow revolves around an axis line, which may be straight or curved. Vortices form in stirred fluids and may be observed in smoke rings, whirlpools in the wake of a boat, tornadoes, hurricanes, and the winds surrounding a tropical cyclone.

Description

Once formed, vortices can move, stretch, twist and interact in complex ways. A moving vortex carries angular and linear momentum, energy, and mass with it. The vorticity — the curl of the velocity field — is concentrated near the axis of the vortex in the core region, where the fluid rotates approximately like a solid body in an irrotational vortex's inner part, while outside the core the flow is irrotational and the tangential velocity falls off with distance.

Types

  • Irrotational (free) vortex – velocity is inversely proportional to radius; fluid particles do not rotate about their own axes.
  • Rotational (forced) vortex – the fluid rotates as a solid body.
  • Vortex lines and tubes – lines everywhere tangent to the local vorticity vector.
  • Vortex rings – toroidal vortices, such as smoke rings.

Examples

Natural vortices range from dust devils and waterspouts to the Great Red Spot of Jupiter, a persistent anticyclonic storm. Engineered flows include the trailing wingtip vortices of aircraft, which pose hazards to following planes, and vortices deliberately generated in mixers and whirlpool separators.
