Mayday is an emergency procedure word used internationally as a distress signal in voice-procedure radio communications. It is used to signal a life-threatening emergency primarily by aviators and mariners, but in some countries local organisations such as firefighters, police forces, and transportation organisations also use the term. Convention requires the word to be repeated three times in a row during the initial emergency declaration ("Mayday, Mayday, Mayday") to be easily distinguishable from a discussion about mayday calls and from sounds similar in noisy conditions.

History

The "mayday" procedure word was originated in 1921 by Frederick Stanley Mockford, a senior radio officer at Croydon Airport in London. Asked to think of a word that would indicate distress and be easily understood by all pilots and ground staff, Mockford proposed "mayday" from the French m'aider ("help me"), a shortened form of venez m'aider ("come and help me"). It was adopted internationally at the 1927 International Radiotelegraph Convention in Washington.

Use

A mayday call reserves the frequency for emergency traffic and takes priority over all other transmissions. Lesser emergencies use the urgency signal "pan-pan". Making a false distress call is a criminal offence in many countries, punishable by fines and imprisonment, because it can endanger the lives of rescuers and others.
