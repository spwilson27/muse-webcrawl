Curonian colonization

Curonian colonization refers to the overseas colonies established in the seventeenth century by the Duchy of Courland and Semigallia, a vassal state of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth centred on the Baltic region of Courland (in modern Latvia). Despite being one of the smallest states ever to run an overseas empire, Courland maintained colonies in the Caribbean and West Africa between the 1630s and the 1680s.

Background

Under Duke Jacob Kettler (reigned 1642–1682), Courland built one of the largest merchant fleets in Europe and pursued an ambitious programme of trade and colonisation. Ships were built in the port of Ventspils (Windau), and the duchy sought tropical colonies to supply tobacco, sugar, and other goods.

Africa

In 1651 Courland acquired St. Andrew's Island in the Gambia River from local rulers and built Fort Jacob there, also establishing trading posts on the riverbank. The settlement exported ivory, gold, and other goods. The colony was captured by the Dutch in 1659 and later passed to England, which renamed the island James Island.

Tobago

Courland's most durable colony was on the island of Tobago in the Caribbean, first settled in 1654 and named New Courland. The colony, centred on Plymouth and Fort James, grew tobacco and sugar, but was repeatedly attacked and occupied by the Dutch, French, and indigenous Caribs. Courland claims to Tobago were revived several times before being finally abandoned in the 1680s as the duchy declined after the Second Northern War.

Legacy

The colonies disappeared quickly, but they remain a point of historical pride in Latvia, where Tobago's "Courlander Bay" and monuments in both Tobago and the Gambia recall the episode.
