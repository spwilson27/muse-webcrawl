Accelerator mass spectrometry

Accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) is a form of mass spectrometry that accelerates ions to extraordinarily high kinetic energies before mass analysis. The special strength of AMS among the mass spectrometric methods is its power to separate a rare isotope from an abundant neighbouring mass to an extraordinary degree, for example 14C from 12C. The method suppresses molecular isobars completely and in many cases can separate atomic isobars (e.g. 14N from 14C). This makes possible the detection of naturally occurring, long-lived radio-isotopes such as 10Be, 36Cl, 26Al and 14C.

The preeminent advantage of AMS compared to other mass spectrometric methods is its sensitivity, down to ratios of 10−15, and its ability to measure isotopic ratios for samples much smaller than those used in conventional mass spectrometry.

Method

Generally, negative ions are created (atoms are ionised) in an ion source. In fortunate cases, this already allows the suppression of ions of the wrong mass. The pre-accelerated ions are then usually further accelerated by an electrostatic particle accelerator, typically a tandem accelerator with a terminal voltage of the order of millions of volts. The ions then pass through a stripper — a dilute gas or a thin foil — where many of the ions become multiply positively charged and molecules are destroyed, eliminating molecular species of the same mass that would otherwise interfere with measurement.

After stripping, the ions are separated according to their mass-to-charge ratio in magnetic and electrostatic analysers, and finally counted one by one in sensitive detectors similar to those used in nuclear physics. Because the ions reach energies of many MeV, detection can rely on standard nuclear particle detection techniques, which allows unambiguous identification even of tiny numbers of rare atoms.

Applications

The most common application of AMS is radiocarbon dating, in which 14C/12C ratios are measured to determine the age of organic material. AMS made it possible to date samples containing only a few milligrams of carbon, far less than the grams required by earlier decay-counting methods. AMS facilities have dated artefacts such as the Dead Sea Scrolls and, controversially, the Shroud of Turin.

Other applications include the measurement of 10Be and 26Al for exposure dating of rocks and surfaces in geology and glaciology, 36Cl for groundwater dating, 129I in environmental studies, and 41Ca and other long-lived isotopes in biomedical tracer studies.

History

The first use of an accelerator as a mass spectrometer was reported by L. W. Alvarez and Robert Cornog in 1939, who used a cyclotron to demonstrate the stability of 3He. The modern era of AMS began in 1977, when groups at the University of Rochester, General Atomics, the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Toronto, and Simon Fraser University demonstrated radiocarbon dating with accelerators, and independent sensitivity breakthroughs followed rapidly.
