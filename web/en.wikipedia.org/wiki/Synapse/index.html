Synapse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A synapse is a structure in the nervous system that permits a neuron to pass an electrical or chemical signal to another neuron or to a target cell such as a muscle fibre. Synapses are the junctions where nervous information is transferred, modified and integrated; the human brain contains on the order of 100 trillion of them. The word was introduced in 1897 by the physiologist Charles Sherrington, from the Greek synapsis, "conjunction".

Chemical synapses

Most synapses are chemical. When an action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, it opens calcium channels; calcium influx triggers vesicles to release neurotransmitter molecules into the synaptic cleft, a gap of about 20–40 nanometres. The transmitters bind receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ion channels that excite or inhibit the receiving cell. Transmitters such as glutamate (the brain's main excitatory messenger), GABA (its main inhibitory one), dopamine, serotonin and acetylcholine are then cleared by reuptake or enzymes, ending the signal.

Electrical synapses and plasticity

Electrical synapses, joined by gap-junction channels, pass current directly and are faster but less modifiable. Chemical synapses can change strength with use — synaptic plasticity. Long-term potentiation, in which repeated activity strengthens a synapse, is a leading candidate mechanism for learning and memory ("cells that fire together wire together"). Many drugs and diseases act at synapses: antidepressants block serotonin reuptake, and in myasthenia gravis the immune system attacks acetylcholine receptors at the nerve–muscle synapse.
