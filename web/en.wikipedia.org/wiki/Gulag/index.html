The Gulag was a government agency in charge of the Soviet network of forced labour camps which ran from the 1920s until the mid-1950s, during Joseph Stalin's rule of the Soviet Union. The acronym GULAG stands for Glavnoye upravleniye lagerey (Главное управление лагерей), "Main Administration of Camps". The term came to denote the entire system of Soviet prison camps and the repression associated with it, especially after the publication of Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's The Gulag Archipelago (1973).

History

The camp system began shortly after the 1917 Revolution, drawing on the Tsarist katorga penal tradition. The Solovetsky Monastery camp (Solovki), founded in 1923, became the prototype. Under Stalin the system expanded enormously during collectivisation and the Great Terror: prisoners built canals (the White Sea–Baltic Canal), railways, mines in Kolyma and Norilsk, and much of the infrastructure of the Soviet far north and east. At its peak in the early 1950s, the camps and colonies held about 2.5 million people, with millions more in internal exile as "special settlers".

Prisoners included ordinary criminals, peasants deported as "kulaks", members of purged Communist Party and military elites, ethnic groups deported wholesale, prisoners of war, and people convicted under Article 58 of the criminal code for alleged counter-revolutionary activity — often on fabricated charges or denunciations. Sentences of 5, 10 or 25 years were common; rations were tied to fulfilment of work norms, and mortality from cold, hunger, disease and overwork was severe, spiking during the Second World War.

Decline and legacy

Within weeks of Stalin's death in March 1953, his police chief Lavrentiy Beria issued a mass amnesty; Nikita Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" of 1956 denounced Stalin's terror, and the agency was formally abolished in 1960, though camps for political prisoners continued in smaller form — such as Perm-36 — until the late 1980s. Historians estimate that some 18 million people passed through the Gulag between 1930 and 1953 and that at least 1.5 million died in custody, figures that remain debated as archival research continues. The Gulag is now commemorated by museums and memorial organisations such as Memorial, and remains a byword for state terror and forced labour.
