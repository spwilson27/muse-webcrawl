Amalgam (dentistry)

Dental amalgam
Composition: mercury (~50%) with silver, tin, copper and other metals
Use: dental restorations (fillings)

In dentistry, amalgam is a liquid mercury and metal alloy mixture used to fill cavities caused by tooth decay. Low-copper amalgam commonly consists of mercury (50%), silver (~22–32%), tin (~14%), zinc (~8%) and other trace metals. Dental amalgam is inexpensive, durable, and easy to apply, and it has been used for more than 150 years.

History

Amalgam fillings were popularised in the 19th century, though their introduction in Europe and America provoked the so-called "amalgam wars" over safety and professional standards. Modern amalgam is dispensed in pre-measured capsules and mixed mechanically before placement.

Safety and phase-down

Because amalgam contains elemental mercury, its use has been debated for decades. Major health organisations have concluded that amalgam fillings are safe for most adults and children over six, but environmental concerns about mercury waste have driven a global phase-down under the Minamata Convention on Mercury, and several countries have restricted or banned its use. Composite resin and glass ionomer materials are increasingly used as alternatives.
