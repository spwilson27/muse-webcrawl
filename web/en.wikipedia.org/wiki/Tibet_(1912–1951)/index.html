The Potala Palace, seat of the Dalai Lamas' government in Lhasa.

Between the collapse of China's Qing dynasty in 1912 and its incorporation into the People's Republic of China in 1951, Tibet functioned as a de facto independent state, governed from Lhasa by the Ganden Phodrang government of the Dalai Lamas. Tibet in this period issued its own currency, postage stamps and passports, maintained its own army, and conducted relations with neighbouring states, although no foreign government formally recognised its independence, and China never renounced its claim of sovereignty. The period ended with the People's Liberation Army's victory at Chamdo in 1950 and the Seventeen Point Agreement of May 1951.

Declaration and consolidation

After the Xinhai Revolution overthrew the Qing, Tibetan forces expelled the Chinese garrison and officials from Lhasa in 1912. The 13th Dalai Lama, Thubten Gyatso, returning from exile in India, issued a proclamation in 1913 (sometimes described as a declaration of independence) rejecting the old priest–patron relationship with the fallen imperial house and asserting that Tibet would govern itself. In 1913–1914 representatives of Tibet, Britain and China met at Simla; the resulting Simla Convention — signed by Tibet and Britain but never ratified by China — divided Tibet into "Outer" and "Inner" zones and drew the McMahon Line boundary with India. Tibet thereafter administered the territory west of the upper Yangtze, fighting border wars with Chinese warlords in 1917–18 and 1930–32 over Kham.

Society and government

The 13th Dalai Lama attempted limited modernisation: a small modern army under British-trained officers, a telegraph line, Tibet's first paper currency and stamps, and schools teaching English. His death in 1933 ended the reform drive; power passed to regents, first the conservative Reting Rinpoche (who discovered the reincarnation of the 14th Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso, born 1935 and enthroned in Lhasa in 1940). Society remained a theocratic hierarchy of monasteries and aristocratic estates, with a cash-poor economy based on wool, salt and borax trade with India and Nepal. Tibet sent a trade delegation abroad in 1948 carrying Tibetan passports, which several countries accepted for visa purposes — a document later cited as evidence of its practical statehood.

Incorporation into the PRC

Following the founding of the People's Republic of China in 1949, Beijing declared its intention to "liberate" Tibet. In October 1950 the PLA defeated the Tibetan army at Chamdo in eastern Tibet, opening the road to Lhasa. With no international support forthcoming, a Tibetan delegation signed the Seventeen Point Agreement in Beijing on 23 May 1951, accepting Chinese sovereignty in exchange for guarantees of Tibet's existing political system, religious freedom and internal autonomy. The 14th Dalai Lama, who had assumed full political power at fifteen during the crisis, initially remained in Tibet under the agreement until the Lhasa uprising of 1959.
