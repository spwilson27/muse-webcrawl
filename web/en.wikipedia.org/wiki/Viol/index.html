Viol

The viol (pronounced "vee-ol"), also called the viola da gamba, is a family of bowed, fretted string instruments that flourished in Europe from the late 15th century through the 18th century. Viols are held between the legs (hence da gamba, "of the leg") or, for the smallest sizes, on the lap, and are played with an underhand bow grip.

Characteristics

Viols typically have six strings (some five or seven), tuned in fourths with a major third in the middle (for example D–G–C–E–A–D for the bass), tied gut frets like a lute, a flat back, sloping shoulders and deep ribs. Their tone is softer and more resonant than that of the violin family, which was held da braccio (on the arm) and developed separately. Sizes form a consort — treble, tenor and bass — analogous to vocal ranges, with the smaller pardessus de viole appearing in the 18th century.

History

The viol emerged in late 15th-century Spain, influenced by the vihuela and Moorish instruments, and spread to Italy, France, Germany and England, where consorts of viols were central to Renaissance and Baroque chamber music. Composers such as William Byrd, John Dowland, Henry Purcell, Marin Marais and Johann Sebastian Bach wrote for it. The viol was displaced by the louder violin family in the later 18th century, but was revived in the 20th century as part of the historically informed performance movement; modern players such as Jordi Savall have made its repertoire widely known.
