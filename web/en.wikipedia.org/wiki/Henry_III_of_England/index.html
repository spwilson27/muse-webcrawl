Henry III of England

Henry III (1 October 1207 – 16 November 1272), also known as Henry of Winchester, was King of England, Lord of Ireland, and Duke of Aquitaine from 1216 until his death. Succeeding his father King John at the age of nine during the First Barons' War, he reigned for 56 years, one of the longest reigns in English history.

Reign

Henry's minority government, led first by William Marshal, defeated the rebel barons and the French at Lincoln and Sandwich in 1217 and reissued Magna Carta. As an adult Henry pursued costly attempts to recover English lands in France, quarrelled with his barons over patronage of his Savoyard and Lusignan relatives, and undertook the abortive "Sicilian business" for his son Edmund. Baronial opposition hardened into reform: in 1258 the Provisions of Oxford imposed a governing council, and Henry's repudiation of them led to the Second Barons' War (1264–1267), in which Simon de Montfort defeated and captured him at Lewes (1264) and summoned a pioneering parliament before being killed at Evesham by Henry's son Edward.

Legacy

A devout king devoted to the cult of Edward the Confessor, Henry rebuilt Westminster Abbey in Gothic style as the royal mausoleum. He died in 1272 and was succeeded by Edward I.
