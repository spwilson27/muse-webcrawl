Bartholomeus Jan "Bart" Bok (28 April 1906 – 5 August 1983) was a Dutch-born American astronomer, teacher, and lecturer. He is best known for his work on the structure and evolution of the Milky Way galaxy, and for the discovery of Bok globules — small, dark, dense clouds of interstellar gas and dust in which star formation sometimes takes place, which he characterised with his wife Priscilla Fairfield Bok.

Career

Bok studied at Leiden and Groningen and moved to the United States in 1929 to work at Harvard, becoming a professor there and a leading figure at Harvard College Observatory. From 1957 to 1965 he was director of Mount Stromlo Observatory in Australia and a professor at the Australian National University, where he built up research on the southern Milky Way. He then joined the University of Arizona, helping to develop Steward Observatory before retiring in 1974.

Research

Bok's doctoral and later work concerned star counts and the structure of the Milky Way, and he became a strong advocate of radio astronomy observations of the 21-cm hydrogen line for mapping the galaxy's spiral arms. In the 1940s he and Priscilla Bok studied the small dark nebulae now called Bok globules, proposing them as sites of ongoing star formation — an interpretation later confirmed by infrared observations.

Legacy

Together with Priscilla Bok he wrote The Milky Way (1941), a classic popular-science text that went through five editions. The asteroid 1983 Bok, the lunar crater Bok, and the Bok Telescope at Kitt Peak are named for him and his wife. He was remembered as an energetic teacher and populariser of astronomy.
