Shipbuilding

Shipbuilding is the construction of ships and other floating vessels, typically carried out in a specialised facility known as a shipyard. It combines naval architecture with heavy engineering: the design, fabrication, assembly, and outfitting of hulls and their machinery. Repair and conversion of ships are closely related activities, and shipbreaking is the final stage of a vessel's life.

History

Shipbuilding is one of the oldest industries. Egyptian, Phoenician, Greek, and Chinese shipwrights developed large wooden fleets in antiquity, and the Venetian Arsenal pioneered mass production with standardised parts in the Middle Ages. The Industrial Revolution brought iron and then steel hulls, steam propulsion, and riveted construction; welding replaced riveting in the 20th century. Methods such as prefabrication of large blocks, developed during the Second World War for Liberty ships, transformed productivity.

Modern process

A modern ship is designed using computer-aided design and tested by models and simulation. Steel plates and profiles are cut (increasingly by automated plasma or laser cutting), formed, and welded into panels, sub-assemblies, and finally large blocks of hundreds of tonnes, which are lifted by gantry crane into a building dock and joined. The erected hull is launched or floated out, then outfitted — machinery, piping, electrical systems, accommodation — and completed with dockside and sea trials before delivery and classification by a classification society.

Industry

Since the late 20th century the industry has been dominated by East Asia: China, South Korea, and Japan together build the great majority of the world's commercial tonnage, including container ships, tankers, bulk carriers, and LNG carriers. Europe retains a strong position in cruise ships, ferries, and naval vessels. Specialised sectors include warships, offshore structures, fishing vessels, and yachts.
