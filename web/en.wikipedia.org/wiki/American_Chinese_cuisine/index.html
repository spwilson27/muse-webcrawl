American Chinese cuisine

American Chinese cuisine
Origin: United States, 19th–20th century
Developed by: Chinese immigrants, largely Cantonese/Toishanese at first
Signature dishes: Chop suey, General Tso's chicken, fortune cookies, egg rolls, crab Rangoon

American Chinese cuisine is a cuisine derived from Chinese cuisine that was developed by Chinese Americans. The dishes served in many North American Chinese restaurants are adapted to American tastes and often differ significantly from those found in China, though over time more regionally authentic Chinese cuisines have also become widely available.

History

Chinese immigrants, mostly from Toishan (Taishan) in Guangdong, arrived during the California Gold Rush and railroad construction. Facing discrimination including the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882), many opened restaurants and laundries. "Chop suey" houses spread nationwide in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, offering inexpensive dishes adapted to local ingredients and palates. After 1965 immigration reform brought cooks from Taiwan, Hong Kong, Sichuan and Hunan, adding spicier and more varied dishes.

Characteristics and dishes

American Chinese cooking favours stir-frying with locally available vegetables (broccoli, carrots, onions), sweeter and thicker sauces, deep-fried items, and plentiful meat. Iconic dishes include chop suey, chow mein, egg foo young, General Tso's chicken, sesame and orange chicken, beef and broccoli, sweet and sour pork, crab Rangoon, egg rolls, wonton soup, fried rice — and the fortune cookie, invented in California (with Japanese antecedents). Takeout in oyster pails, combination platters and all-you-can-eat buffets are characteristic formats.

Differences from cuisine in China

Traditional Chinese regional cuisines use ingredients largely absent in classic American Chinese food, favour less sweetness and less deep-frying in many dishes, include more leafy greens, offal, whole fish and fermented flavours, and rarely serve fortune cookies.
