Columbarium

A columbarium (plural: columbaria) is a structure for the reverent and public storage of funerary urns holding cremated remains. The term comes from the Latin columba (dove) and originally referred solely to a dovecote; the resemblance of the compartmented walls to the nesting boxes of a dovecote gave the funerary structure its name.

History

Columbaria were built extensively in ancient Rome, both by wealthy families and by burial societies (collegia) that allowed slaves, freedmen and poorer citizens to provide for the dignified storage of their ashes. Notable surviving examples include the columbaria of the Vigna Codini on the Appian Way, built for the households of the imperial family. With the triumph of inhumation burial in late antiquity, the practice declined.

Modern columbaria

The revival of cremation in the late 19th and 20th centuries brought renewed construction of columbaria, which today form part of many cemeteries, crematoria, churches and temples. Individual compartments, called niches, are usually sealed by a stone, marble or glass front, often inscribed with the name and dates of the deceased. In East and Southeast Asia, large multi-storey columbaria attached to Buddhist temples are common, and in some land-scarce cities governments encourage their use over burial.

Famous modern examples include the San Francisco Columbarium in California and the columbarium of Père Lachaise Cemetery in Paris, which holds the remains of many notable figures.
