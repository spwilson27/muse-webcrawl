Expropriation

Expropriation is the act of a government claiming privately owned property against the wishes of the owners, ostensibly to be used for the benefit of the overall public. In the United States, expropriation is closely associated with the concept of eminent domain, under which property may be taken for public use provided that just compensation is paid, as required by the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution. In other jurisdictions the terms compulsory purchase, compulsory acquisition, or resumption are used.

Legal basis

Most legal systems require that expropriation serve a public purpose and that owners receive compensation, often at fair market value. International law, including bilateral investment treaties and the practice of investor–state arbitration, treats the uncompensated taking of foreign investors' property as unlawful expropriation, while permitting bona fide regulation for public welfare.

Historical examples

Major historical expropriations include the nationalizations of the Suez Canal by Egypt in 1956, the nationalization of foreign oil assets in Mexico in 1938, and the widespread confiscations carried out by revolutionary governments in the 20th century. Land reform programs in many countries have relied on expropriation, sometimes with below-market compensation, generating continuing legal and political controversy.
