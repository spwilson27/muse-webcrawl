Opioid peptide

Opioid peptides or opiate peptides are peptides that bind to opioid receptors in the brain; opiates and opioids mimic the effect of these peptides. Such peptides may be produced by the body itself, for example endorphins. The effects of these peptides vary, but they all resemble those of opiates. Brain opioid peptide systems are known to play an important role in motivation, emotion, attachment behaviour, responses to stress and pain, and the control of food intake.

Endogenous opioid peptides

The human genome contains three homologous genes known to code for endogenous opioid peptides. The proopiomelanocortin (POMC) gene encodes β-endorphin and other peptides; the proenkephalin gene encodes the enkephalins (Met-enkephalin and Leu-enkephalin); and the prodynorphin gene encodes the dynorphins. A fourth group, the endomorphins, was identified in the 1990s. These peptides act on the μ (mu), δ (delta), and κ (kappa) opioid receptors with differing selectivity.

Exogenous opioid peptides

Opioid peptides also occur in food proteins and can be released during digestion; these are called exorphins. Examples include casomorphins from casein in milk, gluten exorphins from wheat gluten, and rubiscolin from spinach Rubisco. Soymorphins are derived from soybean proteins.
