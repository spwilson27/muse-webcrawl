Jingikan

The Jingikan (神祇官, "Department of Divinities" or "Department of Worship") was one of the two principal organs of the Japanese imperial government established under the ritsuryō system in the 7th and 8th centuries, ranking alongside—and in ceremonial theory above—the Daijōkan (Council of State). It administered matters relating to the kami, the deities of Shinto, including festivals, shrines, and ritual purity at court.

Functions

The Jingikan supervised the calendar of state rituals (such as the Niiname-sai and other court festivals), the appointment and ranking of shrine priests, the maintenance of officially recognized shrines, and the ritual activities of diviners and shrine maidens attached to the imperial court. Its structure, defined in the Taihō and Yōrō codes, included officials such as the Jingi-haku (chief of the department), with subordinate bureaus responsible for divination, ritual implements, and shrine registries. The Engishiki, compiled in the 10th century, preserves detailed regulations for the department's festivals and lists of shrines receiving state offerings.

Decline and revival

As the ritsuryō system decayed in the later Heian period, the Jingikan's real authority waned, much of its work passing to hereditary priestly houses such as the Nakatomi and Urabe. The department was briefly revived in the early Meiji era (1868–1871) as the Jingi-kan, elevated in the first months of the Restoration above the Council of State in an attempt to restore ancient imperial rule and promote Shinto, before being demoted to a ministry and then folded into other agencies as the modern state consolidated.
