Boring (manufacturing)

In machining, boring is the process of enlarging a hole that has already been drilled (or cast) by means of a single-point cutting tool (or a boring head containing several such tools), as in boring a gun barrel or an engine cylinder. Boring is used to achieve greater accuracy of the diameter of a hole, and can be used to cut a tapered hole. Boring can be viewed as the internal-diameter counterpart to turning, which cuts external diameters.

Process

The workpiece is mounted on a lathe, milling machine, or dedicated boring machine, and a rotating boring bar carrying the cutting tool passes through the existing hole, removing material from its internal surface. Because the tool works inside a hole, tool overhang and vibration ("chatter") are major practical limits, and rigid tooling and moderate depths of cut are used to maintain accuracy and surface finish.

Types of machines

Horizontal boring mills handle large workpieces such as engine blocks and gear housings, with the spindle mounted horizontally. Vertical boring mills (vertical turret lathes) suit very large, heavy parts. Jig borers are high-precision machines originally developed for locating holes in jigs and dies. Line boring machines align and machine a series of coaxial bores, for example in bearing housings.

Related processes

Boring is closely related to reaming, which also refines existing holes but removes less material with a multi-edged tool, and to honing, an abrasive finishing process. Unlike drilling, boring cannot originate a hole; a pilot hole must already exist.
