Abu Ghraib torture and prisoner abuse

During the Iraq War, members of the United States Army and the Central Intelligence Agency committed a series of human rights violations and war crimes against detainees held at Abu Ghraib prison in Iraq. The abuse — including physical and sexual humiliation, torture, and the homicide of prisoner Manadel al-Jamadi — became public in April 2004 when photographs were broadcast by CBS News and reported by Seymour Hersh in The New Yorker.

Background

Abu Ghraib, a notorious prison under Saddam Hussein, was taken over by US forces in 2003. The 372nd Military Police Company guarded detainees, many held without charge, while military intelligence personnel directed interrogations in the wake of the growing insurgency and controversial legal memoranda on interrogation techniques.

Revelations and investigations

Soldier Joseph Darby alerted investigators in January 2004 by handing over photographs taken by the guards themselves. The Taguba Report documented "sadistic, blatant, and wanton criminal abuses". Subsequent inquiries, including the Fay Report and the Schlesinger Report, examined the roles of leadership and policy.

Trials and aftermath

Eleven soldiers were convicted in courts-martial, including Charles Graner and Lynndie England; Brigadier General Janis Karpinski was demoted. No senior officers were criminally charged. The scandal severely damaged the reputation of the United States and remains a frequently cited case in debates over torture, command responsibility and the laws of war.
