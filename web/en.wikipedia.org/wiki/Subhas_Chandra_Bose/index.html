Subhas Chandra Bose (23 January 1897 – 18 August 1945) was an Indian nationalist whose defiance of British authority made him a hero among Indians, though his wartime alliances with Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan left a legacy of controversy. Honored in India with the title Netaji ("Respected Leader"), he was president of the Indian National Congress in 1938 and 1939, and later led the Indian National Army (INA) in Southeast Asia during the Second World War.

Early life and Congress career

Born in Cuttack, Orissa, to a prominent Bengali lawyer, Bose studied at Presidency College, Calcutta, and Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge. He passed the Indian Civil Service examination in 1920 but resigned in 1921 to join the independence movement under Mahatma Gandhi and Chittaranjan Das. A brilliant orator and organizer, he rose quickly in the Congress, becoming mayor of Calcutta and, despite differences with Gandhi over his more militant, socialist-leaning outlook, Congress president.

Escape and the INA

Placed under house arrest in Calcutta, Bose escaped in January 1941, traveling via Kabul and Moscow to Berlin, where he sought Axis support for Indian independence and broadcast on Azad Hind Radio. In 1943 he traveled by submarine to Japanese-held Singapore, took command of the Indian National Army — composed largely of Indian prisoners of war — and proclaimed the Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind). INA troops fought alongside the Japanese in the Burma campaign and the advance on Imphal in 1944, but the offensive failed, and the INA retreated with the Japanese.

Death and legacy

Bose is officially reported to have died on 18 August 1945 from severe burns sustained when his overloaded plane crashed in Taihoku (Taipei), Taiwan, shortly after takeoff. The absence of a body and photographs has fueled persistent theories in India that he survived, prompting several official commissions of inquiry, most of which upheld the death account. Whatever the mystery, Bose remains an iconic figure: his slogans "Jai Hind" and "Delhi Chalo" entered India's national vocabulary, and the INA trials of 1945–46 helped galvanize the final push toward independence.
