Arcadia (Greek: Ἀρκαδία, Arkadía) is a mountainous region in the central Peloponnese of Greece, named after the mythological figure Arcas. Landlocked and rugged, it was in antiquity a byword for rustic simplicity; its inhabitants — regarded by other Greeks as aboriginal, "older than the moon" — spoke a distinctive dialect (Arcadocypriot) closely related to the language of Mycenaean Greek. Today Arcadia is a regional unit of the Peloponnese region, with Tripoli as its capital.

Ancient history

Arcadia's fiercely independent small cities — Tegea, Mantinea, Orchomenus, and others — resisted Spartan domination for centuries from strongholds in the hills. In 371 BCE the Theban victory at Leuctra broke Spartan power, and the Arcadians founded the great federal city of Megalopolis (c. 368 BCE) as a bulwark against Sparta, organising the Arcadian League. The region later came under Macedonian, Achaean League, and Roman control. Mount Lykaion, sacred to Zeus Lykaios, was associated in myth with King Lycaon's transformation into a wolf.

The pastoral ideal

Arcadia became, in Hellenistic and Roman poetry and again in Renaissance and later art, the symbolic home of an idealised pastoral life of shepherds and unspoiled nature — the home of the god Pan. Virgil's Eclogues set the fashion; the theme echoes in the famous phrase Et in Arcadia ego, in paintings by Guercino and Poussin, and in Tom Stoppard's play Arcadia (1993). The name has been given to places and projects worldwide, from Acadia in Canada to suburbs and ships.
