Species–area relationship

The species–area relationship or species–area curve describes the relationship between the area of a habitat, or of part of a habitat, and the number of species found within that area. Larger areas tend to contain larger numbers of species, and empirically, the relative numbers seem to follow systematic mathematical relationships. The species–area relationship is usually studied together with the species–area curve, and it is one of the oldest and most general patterns in ecology.

Mathematical form

The most commonly used description is the power-law function proposed by Arrhenius (1921):

S = cAz

where S is the number of species, A is area, c is a constant that depends on the taxon and region, and z is the slope of the relationship in log–log space. Values of z typically range from about 0.1 to 0.2 for samples within a mainland province and from about 0.25 to 0.35 for isolated islands. Alternative forms, such as the logarithmic model of Gleason, have also been fitted.

Mechanisms

Several processes generate the pattern: larger areas sample more individuals and hence more species (the sampling or "more individuals" effect); larger areas encompass greater habitat diversity; and, on islands, larger areas support larger populations with lower extinction rates, a central idea in the equilibrium theory of island biogeography of MacArthur and Wilson (1967).

Applications

Species–area relationships are used to estimate global species richness, to predict species losses from habitat destruction — often concluding that a 90% loss of area leads to the loss of roughly half the species — and to design nature reserves, including the SLOSS debate (single large or several small reserves). The pattern also appears in microbial ecology and in nested-subset analyses of habitat fragments.
