Beer–Lambert law

Beer–Lambert law
Also known asBeer–Lambert–Bouguer law
FormulaA = εlc
FieldSpectroscopy, analytical chemistry
Named afterPierre Bouguer, Johann Heinrich Lambert, August Beer

The Beer–Lambert law is a linear relationship between the attenuation of light through a substance and the properties of that substance. It states that the absorbance A of a solution is directly proportional to the concentration c of the absorbing species and the path length l of the light through the sample:

A = εlc

where ε is the molar attenuation (extinction) coefficient of the absorber at the given wavelength.

History

The law combines the observations of Pierre Bouguer (1729), who noted exponential attenuation with path length, Johann Heinrich Lambert (1760), and August Beer (1852), who related attenuation to concentration.

Applications and limits

The law underpins quantitative UV–visible spectrophotometry: measuring absorbance at a chosen wavelength allows concentrations of analytes to be determined. It can fail at high concentrations, where intermolecular interactions and scattering change the absorbing behaviour, and when the light is not monochromatic.
