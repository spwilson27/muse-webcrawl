Germain Hess

Germain Henri Hess (Russian: Герман Иванович Гесс, German Ivanovich Gess; 7 August 1802 – 30 November 1850) was a Swiss-Russian chemist and physician best known for Hess's law, the principle that the total enthalpy change of a chemical reaction is independent of the pathway taken. The law, published in 1840, was an early statement of the conservation of energy applied to chemistry and made Hess a founder of thermochemistry.

Life

Hess was born in Geneva, the son of an artist, and moved with his family to Russia as a small child. He studied medicine at the University of Dorpat (Tartu) and practiced as a doctor in Irkutsk, Siberia, before turning to chemistry. In Stockholm he visited Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who became his mentor. Settling in St Petersburg, he became a professor of chemistry and a member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences, taught at the Mining Institute, and wrote an influential Russian-language chemistry textbook that standardized Russian chemical nomenclature.

Hess's law

In 1840, after careful calorimetric measurements of the heat released in sequences of reactions — including the stepwise hydration of sulfuric acid — Hess announced his law of constant heat summation: the heat evolved or absorbed in a chemical process is the same whether the process takes place in one step or in several. Because it implies that heat behaves as a state function, the law anticipated the first law of thermodynamics and remains a standard tool for calculating reaction enthalpies from tabulated formation enthalpies.

Other work

Hess analyzed Siberian minerals and ores, including platinum-group minerals, and studied the sugar saccharic acid. The silver telluride mineral hessite (Ag2Te), which he described from a Siberian locality, is named after him. He also correctly predicted practical applications for the heat of chemical reactions, speculating about chemical heating in place of firewood.
