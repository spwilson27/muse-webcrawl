Joseph Papp

Joseph Papp (born Joseph Papirofsky; June 22, 1921 – October 31, 1991) was an American theatrical producer and director who founded the New York Shakespeare Festival and the Public Theater. He championed free Shakespeare in Central Park, developed new American plays and playwrights, and originated landmark productions including Hair and A Chorus Line.

Early life

Papp was born in Brooklyn to Jewish immigrant parents from Lithuania and Russia and grew up in poverty. After serving in the U.S. Navy in World War II, he studied acting and stage management in Los Angeles and worked in television at CBS, where he was called before the House Un-American Activities Committee in 1958 and refused to name names, effectively ending his television career.

New York Shakespeare Festival and the Public

In 1954 Papp founded the Shakespeare Workshop, presenting free Shakespeare, at first in church halls and then from a touring truck. In 1957 his free productions settled in Central Park, where, after a famous battle with parks commissioner Robert Moses — whom Papp defeated by shaming the city in the press — he won a permanent home at the open-air Delacorte Theater (opened 1962). "Shakespeare in the Park" became a beloved New York institution.

In 1967 he opened the Public Theater in the former Astor Library on Lafayette Street, devoted to new American writing. Its first production was the world premiere of the rock musical Hair (1967); the Public went on to premiere works by David Rabe, Sam Shepard, Ntozake Shange (for colored girls...), and Larry Kramer (The Normal Heart). Michael Bennett's A Chorus Line, developed in workshops at the Public in 1975, transferred to Broadway and became one of the longest-running shows in history, subsidizing the nonprofit theatre for years.

Later years and legacy

Papp also ran the Vivian Beaumont Theater at Lincoln Center in the 1970s and tirelessly advocated public funding for the arts and multicultural casting. He died of prostate cancer in 1991. The Public Theater's building was renamed the Joseph Papp Public Theater in his memory, and his model of free, populist, and adventurous theatre reshaped American nonprofit theatre.
