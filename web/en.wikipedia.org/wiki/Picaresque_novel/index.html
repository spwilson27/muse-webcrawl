Picaresque novel

The picaresque novel (Spanish: picaresca, from pícaro, for 'rogue' or 'rascal') is a genre of prose fiction that depicts the adventures of a roguish hero of low social class who lives by his or her wits in a corrupt society. Picaresque novels typically adopt a first-person narrative and are episodic in structure; they are often satirical, exposing the hypocrisies of the respectable world through the eyes of an outsider. The genre originated in 16th-century Spain and flourished in Europe in the 17th and 18th centuries, profoundly influencing the development of the realist novel.

Characteristics

Definitions vary, but common features include: a pícaro protagonist of humble or dishonest origins; autobiographical first-person narration; a loose, episodic plot structured by the hero's service to a series of masters; a realistic, colloquial style; and satire of social ranks, professions, and institutions. The pícaro rarely develops morally; instead, the world around him is revealed.

Spanish origins

The genre begins with the anonymous La vida de Lazarillo de Tormes (1554), in which the boy Lázaro serves a succession of masters—a blind beggar, a miserly priest, a starving squire—learning hunger and cunning. Mateo Alemán's Guzmán de Alfarache (1599, 1604) became an international bestseller, and Francisco de Quevedo's El Buscón (1626) pushed the mode toward bitter brilliance. Female pícaras appear in works such as La pícara Justina (1605).

Spread through Europe

The picaresque was imitated across Europe: Grimmelshausen's Simplicius Simplicissimus (1668) in Germany; in England, Nashe's The Unfortunate Traveller (1594), Defoe's Moll Flanders (1722), and Smollett's Roderick Random (1748); in France, Lesage's Gil Blas (1715–1735). Henry Fielding's Tom Jones (1749) adapted picaresque structure to the comic novel of manners.

Legacy

Later literature repeatedly revives the rogue's journey: Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, Thomas Mann's Confessions of Felix Krull, Saul Bellow's The Adventures of Augie March, and Günter Grass's The Tin Drum are often read as modern picaresques. Critics credit the genre with pioneering literary realism and the outsider's perspective on society.
