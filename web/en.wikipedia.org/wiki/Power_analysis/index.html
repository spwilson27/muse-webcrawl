Power analysis

A power analysis is a calculation used in statistical experimental design, most commonly to determine the sample size needed to detect an effect of a given size with a specified degree of confidence. The statistical power of a test is the probability that it correctly rejects a false null hypothesis — that is, the probability of avoiding a Type II error. Power equals 1 − β, where β is the probability of a false negative.

Factors affecting power

Power increases with larger sample size, larger true effect size, a higher significance threshold (α), and lower variability in the measurements. Conventionally, researchers often design studies to achieve power of 0.80 at α = 0.05, a convention popularised by Jacob Cohen, who also proposed standard small, medium, and large effect-size benchmarks.

Applications

A priori power analysis is a standard part of research planning and grant applications, and is required by many ethics boards to avoid both underpowered studies (which waste resources) and unnecessarily large ones. Post hoc or "observed" power calculations are controversial because they are a direct function of the observed p-value and add no information beyond it. Underpowered research has been identified as a major contributor to the replication crisis in psychology and other sciences.
