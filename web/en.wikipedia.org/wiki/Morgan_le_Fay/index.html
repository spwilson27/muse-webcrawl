Morgan le Fay (/ˈmɔːrɡən lə ˈfeɪ/, "Morgan the Fairy"), also known as Morgaine or Morgana, is a powerful enchantress in the Matter of Britain, the body of medieval literature concerning King Arthur and his knights. In many later works she is Arthur's half-sister and a scheming adversary, though in the earliest accounts she appears as a benevolent healer associated with the Isle of Avalon.

Origins

Morgan first appears in Geoffrey of Monmouth's Vita Merlini (c. 1150) as the chief of nine magical sisters who rule the Isle of Apples (Avalon), skilled in healing and shape-shifting; the mortally wounded Arthur is carried to her to be healed. Her figure may derive from Welsh mythology (the mother-goddess Modron, mother of Mabon) and possibly the Irish war-goddess the Morrígan.

Development in romance

In Chrétien de Troyes and later French romances, Morgan becomes Arthur's sister, a pupil of Merlin, and wife of King Urien, by whom she has the knight Ywain. In the Vulgate Cycle and Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur she evolves into a principal antagonist, repeatedly plotting Arthur's death — through the stolen sword Excalibur and its scabbard, the affair of Accolon, and her schemes against Lancelot and Guinevere. Yet in Malory's ending it is Morgan who comes in a black barge to bear the dying Arthur away to Avalon, a return to her original role as healer.

Modern interpretations

Morgan remains a popular figure in modern Arthurian retellings, notably as the sympathetic narrator-protagonist's perspective in Marion Zimmer Bradley's The Mists of Avalon (1983), in which she is "Morgaine of the Fairies", a priestess of the old religion. She also appears as a villain in T. H. White's The Once and Future King and in numerous films, television series, and games.
