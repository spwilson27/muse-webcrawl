Republic of Pisa

The Republic of Pisa (Italian: Repubblica di Pisa) was an Italian city-state — one of the maritime republics — centred on the Tuscan city of Pisa, which flourished as a major commercial and naval power in the western Mediterranean from the eleventh to the thirteenth centuries before declining before its rivals Genoa and Florence.

Rise

Pisa's fleets repelled Saracen raids and then joined them in profitable expeditions: the sack of Reggio (1005), the Mahdia campaign of 1087 with Genoa, and the Balearic Islands expedition of 1113–1115. Pisan merchants obtained trading quarters and privileges across the Mediterranean and the Levant, and Pisan crusader fleets supported the First Crusade, with Archbishop Dagobert becoming Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem. The city's wealth built the cathedral (begun 1063), baptistery and the famous Leaning Tower (begun 1173).

Decline

Rivalry with Genoa over Corsica and Sardinia ended catastrophically at the Battle of Meloria in 1284, where the Pisan fleet was destroyed. Internal factionalism, the silting of its harbour and pressure from Florence followed; in 1406 Pisa was conquered by Florence, briefly regaining independence (1494–1509) during the Italian Wars before final submission.

Legacy

The mathematician Fibonacci (Leonardo of Pisa) introduced Hindu–Arabic numerals to Europe in his Liber Abaci (1202). Pisan Romanesque architecture influenced church building across Tuscany, Sardinia and Corsica.
