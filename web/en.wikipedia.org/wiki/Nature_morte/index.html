Nature morte

Nature morte is the French term for a still life — a work of art depicting mostly inanimate subject matter, typically commonplace objects such as food, flowers, plants, rocks, shells, or man-made items like glasses, books, vases, and musical instruments. Literally "dead nature", the phrase became standard in French art criticism in the 18th century.

Terminology

The Dutch term stilleven ("still life") emerged around 1650 as the genre flowered in the Netherlands. French painters initially used terms such as vie coite ("quiet life"); nature morte was adopted by the French Academy and gained currency through the writings of critics such as Roger de Piles, though some artists disliked its funereal implication. The English "still life" is a calque of the Dutch.

History

Still life elements appear in Egyptian tomb painting, Greek and Roman art, and Byzantine and medieval manuscripts, but the independent genre arose in the late 16th and early 17th centuries in the work of artists such as Caravaggio, whose Basket of Fruit (c. 1599) is among the first pure still lifes. Dutch Golden Age painters — Willem Claesz. Heda, Pieter Claesz, Clara Peeters, and Jan Davidsz. de Heem — perfected subgenres including breakfast pieces, flower pieces, and vanitas paintings, which reflected on the brevity of life. In France, Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin elevated humble kitchen subjects in the 18th century.

Modern still life

Paul Cézanne made the still life central to modern art, using apples and tableware to explore structure and perception; the Cubists Picasso and Braque fractured still-life objects into geometry, and Morandi spent a career painting quiet arrangements of bottles. In French usage, nature morte remains the standard title for the genre across all periods.
