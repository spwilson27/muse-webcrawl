Jihadism is a neologism used by scholars and media to describe a militant Islamist ideology that advocates violence — framed as jihad — to establish states governed by its interpretation of Islamic law and to expel perceived enemies of Islam. Mainstream Muslim scholars and the vast majority of Muslims reject the jihadist interpretation, and the term is contested because it links violence to the broader Islamic concept of jihad, which classically includes non-violent spiritual struggle.

Origins

Modern jihadism draws heavily on the writings of Egyptian thinker Sayyid Qutb, whose concept of modern jahiliyya (pagan ignorance) held that nominally Muslim societies and rulers had lapsed into unbelief and could legitimately be fought. The Soviet–Afghan War (1979–89) globalised the movement: Arab volunteers, mobilised by Abdullah Azzam, gained combat experience and networks from which al-Qaeda emerged under Osama bin Laden.

Development

Al-Qaeda's strategy of attacking the "far enemy" culminated in the September 11 attacks of 2001, which triggered the War on Terror, the invasion of Afghanistan and the Iraq War. The Iraq insurgency gave rise to al-Qaeda in Iraq, which evolved into the Islamic State (ISIS/ISIL); in 2014 it proclaimed a caliphate across parts of Iraq and Syria before being territorially defeated by 2019. Affiliates and rivals such as the Taliban, al-Shabaab and Boko Haram have waged regional insurgencies.

Ideas

Scholars typically locate jihadism within Salafi Islamism, distinguished by takfir (declaring other Muslims apostates), rejection of democracy and nation-states, and the goal of restoring a caliphate. Researchers differentiate "global jihadists," focused on the West, from locally oriented groups, though the boundary is fluid.
