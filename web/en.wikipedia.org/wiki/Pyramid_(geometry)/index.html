Pyramid (geometry)

A pyramid is a polyhedron formed by connecting a polygonal base and a point, called the apex. Each base edge and the apex form a triangle, called a lateral face. A pyramid is a conic solid with a polygonal base. Pyramids are classified by the shape of their base; a pyramid with an n-sided base has n + 1 vertices, n + 1 faces, and 2n edges.

Types

A right pyramid has its apex directly above the centroid of its base. In a regular pyramid, the base is a regular polygon and the faces are congruent isosceles triangles. The most familiar example is the square pyramid, exemplified by the Egyptian pyramids. A pyramid with a triangular base is a tetrahedron; when all its faces are equilateral triangles it is a regular tetrahedron, one of the Platonic solids.

Measurements

The volume of a pyramid is one third of the product of the area of the base and the height (the perpendicular distance from the base to the apex):

V = (1/3) B h

where B is the base area and h is the height. This result, known in antiquity, generalizes the formula for a cone, which is likewise one third of the base area times the height. The surface area is the sum of the base area and the areas of the triangular lateral faces; for a regular pyramid, the slant height is the altitude of each triangular face.

Generalizations

The concept extends to higher dimensions as a hyperpyramid, and a pyramid can be generalized to have any planar region as its base, giving a cone-like solid. The frustum of a pyramid, obtained by cutting off the top with a plane parallel to the base, is a common shape in architecture and engineering.
