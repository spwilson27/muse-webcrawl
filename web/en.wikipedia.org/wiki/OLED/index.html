An organic light-emitting diode (OLED), also known as an organic electroluminescent diode, is a light-emitting diode in which the emissive layer is a film of organic compound that emits light in response to an electric current. Unlike LCDs, OLED pixels generate their own light and need no backlight, enabling displays that are thinner, lighter, and capable of true black (pixels switch completely off), extremely high contrast ratios, fast response times, and wide viewing angles. OLEDs are used in most premium smartphones, in televisions, wearable devices, and increasingly in monitors and lighting panels.

Working principle

A typical OLED stacks several organic thin films between two electrodes on a substrate. When a voltage is applied, electrons from the cathode and holes from the anode are injected into the transport layers and recombine in the emissive layer to form excitons, whose decay releases photons. The colour of the light depends on the organic molecules used; phosphorescent emitters (PHOLEDs) can in principle convert nearly all excitons into light, greatly improving efficiency over first-generation fluorescent materials.

History

Electroluminescence in organic materials was observed in the 1950s and 1960s, but the first efficient device was reported in 1987 by Ching Wan Tang and Steven Van Slyke at Eastman Kodak, using a two-layer structure of small molecules. Practical displays followed in the late 1990s (Pioneer's car audio displays; early mobile phones). Samsung commercialised AMOLED screens at scale in smartphones from about 2010, while LG Display's white-OLED (WOLED) architecture enabled large televisions. Samsung's QD-OLED, combining OLED with quantum-dot colour conversion, entered the TV market in 2022.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include perfect blacks, high contrast, thin and flexible form factors (on plastic substrates), fast response, and wide colour gamut. Disadvantages include susceptibility to water and oxygen damage (requiring encapsulation), differential ageing of the organic materials — blue emitters degrade fastest, causing colour shift — and the risk of permanent burn-in from static images. Manufacturing large panels with high yield was long dominated by a handful of suppliers in South Korea, China, and Japan.
