Time-division multiplexing


Time slots assigned to channels in TDM

Time-division multiplexing (TDM) is a method of transmitting and receiving independent signals over a common signal path by means of synchronized switches at each end of the transmission line so that each signal appears on the line only a fraction of time, in an alternating pattern. This method was first developed in telegraphy during the 1870s, and found its widest application in early telephone systems.

Overview

In time-division multiplexing, several signals share a single transmission channel by taking turns: the available time is divided into fixed-length slots, and each input channel is assigned one or more slots in a repeating frame. Because the sharing is scheduled rather than simultaneous, the signals are not transmitted at the same moment, avoiding the interference that frequency sharing must guard against. At the receiving end, a demultiplexer synchronized with the transmitter separates the interleaved slots back into their original channels.

Synchronous and statistical TDM

In synchronous TDM, every input channel receives a slot in every frame whether or not it has data to send; this is simple but can waste bandwidth when sources are idle. Statistical TDM, also called asynchronous TDM or statistical multiplexing, allocates slots only to channels with data waiting, using buffering and address information embedded in each block. This improves efficiency for bursty traffic and is the principle behind packet switching.

Applications

TDM underlies classic digital telephone systems: the North American T-carrier hierarchy (T1, carrying 24 digitized voice channels) and the European E-carrier hierarchy (E1, carrying 30). It is also used in the Synchronous Digital Hierarchy (SDH) and SONET optical networks, in mobile telephony standards such as GSM, and in digital audio and serial data links.
