Basic income

Basic income
Also calledUniversal basic income (UBI), citizen's income, basic income guarantee
Key featuresUniversal, unconditional, individual, periodic cash payment
Related conceptsNegative income tax, social dividend, guaranteed minimum income

Basic income, also called universal basic income (UBI), is a social welfare proposal in which all citizens of a given population regularly receive a minimum income in the form of an unconditional sum of money from the government, without a means test or need to work. A basic income that is sufficient to meet basic needs at a frugal standard of living is sometimes called a full basic income, while one below that level is a partial basic income.

UBI proposals would pay every citizen a regular, unconditional income.

History

Precursors appear in Thomas More's Utopia (1516) and in the writings of Thomas Paine, who proposed a citizen's dividend funded by a tax on land in Agrarian Justice (1797). In the 20th century the idea was discussed by Bertrand Russell, and in the United States by Milton Friedman (as a negative income tax) and Martin Luther King Jr. President Richard Nixon's Family Assistance Plan, a guaranteed-income scheme, passed the House of Representatives in 1969–70 but failed in the Senate. Alaska's Permanent Fund Dividend, paid annually to all residents since 1982 from oil revenues, is often cited as the closest real-world example of a basic income. In 2020, US presidential candidate Andrew Yang made a "$1,000 a month" Freedom Dividend the centrepiece of his campaign.

Arguments

Supporters argue that basic income reduces poverty and bureaucracy, improves health and education outcomes, strengthens workers' bargaining power, and provides security as automation displaces jobs. Results from pilots — including the Mincome experiment in Manitoba in the 1970s, Finland's two-year trial (2017–2018), and GiveDirectly's long-running trial in Kenya — generally show improvements in wellbeing with modest effects on employment. Critics argue that a full UBI would be prohibitively expensive, inflationary, or would weaken the incentive to work, and that universal payments are less efficient than targeted welfare.
