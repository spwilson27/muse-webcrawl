Saturnalia was an ancient Roman festival in honour of the god Saturn, held on 17 December of the Julian calendar and later extended with festivities through 23 December. It was one of the most popular festivals in the Roman calendar, characterised by feasting, gift-giving, gambling, and a carnival atmosphere in which social norms were relaxed and roles reversed.

Celebration

The festival began with a public sacrifice at the Temple of Saturn in the Roman Forum and a public banquet. During Saturnalia the woolen bonds around the feet of Saturn's cult statue were loosened, symbolising liberation. Schools and courts closed, gambling with dice — normally forbidden — was permitted in public, and slaves were temporarily freed from work, dining with or even being served by their masters.

Romans exchanged small gifts (sigillaria), often candles or clay figurines, and greeted one another with the cry "io Saturnalia!" Participants at private banquets might elect a Saturnalicius princeps, a mock "king of Saturnalia" who issued humorous commands. Instead of the toga, citizens wore the informal synthesis and the felt cap of freedmen, the pilleus.

Origins and legacy

The festival was associated with the mythical Golden Age under Saturn, a time of equality and abundance before the reign of Jupiter. It was linked to the Greek festival of Kronia. Saturnalia's season, customs of feasting and gift-giving, and its position near the winter solstice have often been compared with aspects of Christmas, and the festival is frequently cited in discussions of the origins of later winter holidays, though direct lines of influence remain debated.
