Jewish resistance in German-occupied Europe encompassed the armed, political, cultural, and spiritual opposition by Jews to Nazi Germany and its collaborators during the Second World War and the Holocaust. It ranged from ghetto uprisings and camp revolts to partisan units in the forests, clandestine newspapers, smuggling and rescue networks, and the preservation of archives and religious life under conditions of mass murder.

Resistance took place under extreme constraints: Jews were isolated, unarmed, starved, and subject to collective reprisals, without a state sponsor or a territorial base. Historians such as Yehuda Bauer distinguish "resistance" broadly defined from amidah ("standing against") — unarmed defiance including education, documentation, mutual aid, and religious observance — while emphasising the scale of armed Jewish combat, from an estimated 20,000–30,000 Jewish partisans in Eastern Europe to service in national resistance movements and Allied armies.

Background

Jewish political parties, youth movements (Zionist, Bundist, communist), and welfare organisations built underground structures in the ghettos, initially focused on survival — smuggling food, running soup kitchens and clandestine schools — and turned increasingly to armed organisation as deportations to killing centres revealed, from 1942, that ghetto liquidation meant total extermination. Underground presses produced hundreds of clandestine publications. Archivists such as Emanuel Ringelblum's Oyneg Shabes group in Warsaw documented ghetto life and Nazi crimes, burying the archive in caches recovered after the war.

Ghetto uprisings

The Warsaw Ghetto uprising (19 April – 16 May 1943), led by the Jewish Combat Organisation (ŻOB) under Mordechai Anielewicz and joined by the Jewish Military Union (ŻZW), was the largest Jewish revolt of the war: several hundred poorly armed fighters held off SS forces under Jürgen Stroop for nearly a month before the ghetto was burned block by block. Uprisings and armed resistance also occurred in the Białystok Ghetto (August 1943), Vilna (where Abba Kovner's FPO issued the famous 1942 manifesto "Let us not go like sheep to the slaughter"), Częstochowa, Będzin, and during smaller ghetto liquidations; in Kraków, ŻOB fighters attacked German targets outside the ghetto, including the Cyganeria café bombing of December 1942.

Camp revolts

Prisoner revolts erupted in the killing centres despite total surveillance: at Treblinka (2 August 1943) prisoners seized weapons and set fires — about 200 escaped, few surviving the war — and at Sobibór (14 October 1943), led by Alexander Pechersky and Leon Feldhendler, roughly 300 prisoners escaped after killing SS staff; Himmler ordered Sobibór razed. At Auschwitz-Birkenau, the Sonderkommando revolted on 7 October 1944, destroying Crematorium IV with gunpowder smuggled by Jewish women forced labourers (four of whom — Roza Robota, Ala Gertner, Estusia Wajcblum, Regina Safirsztajn — were hanged). Escapees from Auschwitz, such as Rudolf Vrba and Alfréd Wetzler (1944), produced detailed reports intended to warn Hungarian Jewry and the Allies.

Partisans

Escaped Jews formed or joined partisan units in the forests of Belarus, Lithuania, Poland, Ukraine, Slovakia, Yugoslavia, and Greece. The most famous "family camp" was that of the Bielski brothers, who rescued some 1,200 Jews in the Naliboki forest. Jewish fighters served in Soviet partisan formations (sometimes facing antisemitism within them), in the Polish Armia Ludowa and some Armia Krajowa units, in the Slovak National Uprising (1944), and in Tito's Partisans. About 1.5 million Jews served in Allied regular armies, and Palestinian Jewish units such as the Jewish Brigade fought under British command.

Resistance in Western Europe

In France, the Armée Juive and the communist-affiliated MOI groups (including the Manouchian group, executed in 1944) carried out sabotage and assassinations of German personnel; the Œuvre de secours aux enfants (OSE) and joint networks such as the Dutch Westerweel group smuggled children and young people to safety in Switzerland and Spain or into hiding. In Belgium, Jewish partisans of the Front de l'Indépendance took part in the April 1943 attack on the twentieth deportation convoy to Auschwitz — the only such convoy attack in Europe, freeing prisoners from the train. In the Netherlands, Jews participated disproportionately in the Dutch resistance and in hiding networks.

Unarmed resistance

Beyond armed struggle, Jews resisted through illegal schools, religious observance, satirical theatre, underground newspapers, and aid networks; rescuers such as the Żegota council in Poland (with Irena Sendler's children's section) and smugglers of food into ghettos risked execution. Historians note that deception, bribery, escapes to neutral countries and to the forests, and mass flight (e.g., the Danish rescue of October 1943, aided by the surrounding population) saved tens of thousands and formed part of the spectrum of resistance.
