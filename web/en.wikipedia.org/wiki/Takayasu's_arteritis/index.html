Takayasu's arteritis (TA), also known as pulseless disease or Takayasu arteritis, is a rare chronic inflammatory disease of unknown cause that primarily affects the aorta and its main branches, and sometimes the pulmonary and coronary arteries. Granulomatous inflammation of the vessel wall leads to thickening, fibrosis, stenosis, occlusion, and occasionally aneurysm formation. The disease most often strikes young women — roughly 80–90 percent of patients are female — typically between the ages of 15 and 30, and is reported most frequently in Asia, though it occurs worldwide.

History

The condition takes its name from Mikito Takayasu, a Japanese ophthalmologist who in 1908 described a young woman with characteristic wreath-like arteriovenous anastomoses around the optic disc and absent radial pulses — the ocular effects of severe carotid and subclavian disease. The systemic vascular pathology was progressively delineated in subsequent decades, and the disease came to be classified among the large-vessel vasculitides alongside giant cell arteritis.

Signs and symptoms

An early systemic phase may feature fever, fatigue, weight loss, joint pains, and elevated inflammatory markers. As stenoses develop, patients experience limb claudication (pain on exertion), diminished or absent peripheral pulses, bruits over narrowed arteries, differences in blood pressure between the arms (a difference of more than 10 mmHg is a classic criterion), dizziness or visual disturbance from carotid involvement, renovascular hypertension from renal artery narrowing, and angina if the coronary ostia are affected.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on clinical findings and vascular imaging — MR angiography, CT angiography, ultrasound, or conventional angiography — which show long, smooth, tapered narrowing or occlusion of the aorta and its primary branches. Laboratory tests (ESR, CRP) support but cannot confirm activity. Treatment centers on high-dose glucocorticoids, usually with steroid-sparing agents such as methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, or biologic therapies including TNF inhibitors and the IL-6 receptor blocker tocilizumab. Critical stenoses may require angioplasty, stenting, or surgical bypass, ideally performed while inflammation is controlled. Long-term antiplatelet therapy and cardiovascular risk management are also standard.
