Pittsburgh Agreement

Signed: May 31, 1918
Location: Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, United States
Signatories: Czech and Slovak organisations in the United States
Purpose: Support for a common Czecho-Slovak state

The Pittsburgh Agreement was a memorandum of understanding signed on May 31, 1918, in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, by representatives of Czech and Slovak expatriate organisations in the United States, in the presence of Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, the future first president of Czechoslovakia. The agreement endorsed the creation of a common independent state of Czechs and Slovaks after the First World War.

Terms

The signatories agreed that the new Czecho-Slovak republic would be a democratic state with a republican form of government. Crucially, the document promised Slovakia its own administration, its own parliament (diet), its own courts, and the use of the Slovak language in schools, offices, and public life in Slovakia. The precise details of Slovak autonomy were to be settled by the elected representatives of the two peoples after liberation.

Signatories

The agreement was signed at the Loyal Order of Moose building in downtown Pittsburgh during Masaryk's visit to the city over the Memorial Day holiday. Signatories included members of the Slovak League of America, the Czech National Alliance, and the Czech Catholic Union; Masaryk signed last and later framed the document, which is preserved in Pittsburgh.

Legacy and controversy

When Czechoslovakia was established in October 1918, its 1920 constitution created a unitary state rather than the autonomous Slovak arrangement many Slovak signatories had expected. Slovak autonomists, and later Slovak nationalists, invoked the Pittsburgh Agreement for decades as evidence of a broken promise, while Czech politicians, including Masaryk, argued that it was a local understanding among American expatriates intended to calm factional disputes, not a binding state treaty. The document remained a recurring point of contention in Czech–Slovak relations throughout the twentieth century.
