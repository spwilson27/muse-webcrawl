Shamanism

Shamanism
Term originFrom Evenki (Tungusic) šamán, via Russian
Key practicesTrance, spirit journeying, healing, divination
Classic regionsSiberia, Central Asia, the Americas, circumpolar peoples
Key scholarsMircea Eliade, Michael Harner

Shamanism is a religious practice that involves a practitioner — a shaman — interacting with the spirit world through altered states of consciousness, such as trance. The goal is usually to direct spirits or spiritual energies into the physical world for healing, divination, or aid to the community. The word "shaman" comes from the Evenki people of Siberia and was generalised by anthropologists to similar specialists worldwide, though the breadth of that generalisation is debated.

Terminology and definition

There is no single agreed definition of shamanism. Mircea Eliade, in Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy (1951), defined the shaman as a master of ecstasy who journeys to the heavens and the underworld. Other scholars emphasise the shaman's role as healer and mediator between the human community and the spirits of animals, ancestors and places. Critics caution that the label can flatten distinct traditions — from the Evenki and Yakut (Sakha) of Siberia to Korean mudang, Mongolian böö, and Amazonian healers — into a single Western category.

Practices

Common elements include:

  • Initiation, often experienced as an illness, dismemberment and rebirth in vision
  • Altered states induced by drumming, chanting, dance, fasting, or psychoactive substances such as ayahuasca or fly agaric
  • Spirit helpers, frequently animals, who guide and protect the shaman
  • Soul retrieval and extraction of illness-causing intrusions in healing rites
  • Costume and tools: drums, rattles, mirrors, feather headdresses and iron ornaments

Distribution

Shamanic traditions are historically attested across Siberia and Central Asia, among the Sámi of northern Europe (noaidi), in Korea, and throughout Indigenous North and South America. Prehistoric rock art and burials — such as the 12,000-year-old burial at Hilazon Tachtit in Israel — have been interpreted as evidence of shamanism's great antiquity, though such readings remain speculative.

Decline and revival

Shamanic practice was suppressed under colonialism, Christian mission, and Soviet anti-religious campaigns in Siberia. Since the late twentieth century it has revived both in its homelands — as in the Sakha Republic and Tuva — and in the West through "neoshamanism" and Michael Harner's "core shamanism", which abstract techniques from their cultural contexts; these modern movements are criticised by some Indigenous people as appropriation.
