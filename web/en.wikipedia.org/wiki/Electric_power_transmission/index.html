Electric power transmission is the bulk movement of electrical energy from a generating site, such as a power plant, to an electrical substation where it is stepped down for distribution to consumers. The interconnected lines and facilities that accomplish this form a transmission network, or grid. Transmission is distinct from the lower-voltage distribution wiring that delivers power the "final mile" to end users.

Why high voltage

For a given amount of power, raising the voltage lowers the current, and because resistive losses grow with the square of current, high-voltage transmission dramatically reduces losses and conductor cost. Transformers make it practical to step alternating current up for transmission and down for use. Most systems use three-phase AC at voltages from about 110 kV to 765 kV. High-voltage direct current (HVDC) is preferred for very long overhead lines, submarine cables, and links between asynchronous grids, despite costly converter stations.

History

Early systems such as Edison's 1882 Pearl Street Station distributed low-voltage DC over short distances. The "war of the currents" ended with AC's victory, demonstrated by long-distance feats such as the 1891 Lauffen–Frankfurt three-phase transmission and the 1896 Niagara-to-Buffalo line. Through the 20th century, voltages climbed and regional grids interconnected into continental systems, punctuated by lessons from major blackouts such as the Northeast blackout of 2003.

Network operation

Meshed transmission networks provide redundant paths so that power keeps flowing if a line fails (the N−1 criterion). System operators balance generation and load continuously, manage congestion, and trade electricity in wholesale markets. Lines are limited by thermal ratings, voltage stability and transient stability; conductors sag as they heat, a factor in several cascading failures. Modern grids add flexible AC transmission systems (FACTS), phasor measurement units and, increasingly, bulk energy storage.
