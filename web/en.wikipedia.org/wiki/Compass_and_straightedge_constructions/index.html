Compass and straightedge constructions

Compass and straightedge constructions (also known as ruler-and-compass constructions) are constructions of lengths, angles, and other geometric figures using only an idealised straightedge and a compass. The straightedge is assumed to be infinite in length, to have only one edge, and to have no markings on it; the compass is assumed to collapse when lifted from the page, though this restriction is immaterial, since a non-collapsing compass can be simulated. Constructions of this type were systematised in Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC) and remain a classic topic in geometry.

Basic constructions

Elementary constructions include drawing the line through two given points, drawing the circle with a given centre through a given point, bisecting a line segment and an angle, erecting and dropping perpendiculars, copying a segment or angle, and drawing a parallel through a point. Regular polygons with 3, 4, 5, and 15 sides are constructible, as are polygons obtained from these by repeated bisection.

Constructible numbers

Every construction corresponds to operations on lengths: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and taking square roots. A number is therefore constructible if and only if it can be obtained from the rational numbers by finitely many of these operations — equivalently, if it lies in a field obtained from the rationals by a tower of quadratic extensions. This algebraic characterisation explains the classical impossibility results.

Famous impossible problems

Three problems of antiquity resisted solution for two thousand years and were eventually proved impossible:

  • Squaring the circle — constructing a square with the same area as a given circle; impossible because π is transcendental (proved by Ferdinand von Lindemann in 1882).
  • Doubling the cube — requires constructing the cube root of 2, which has algebraic degree 3, not a power of 2 (proved impossible by Pierre Wantzel in 1837).
  • Trisecting the angle — the general angle cannot be trisected, by the same degree argument.

Carl Friedrich Gauss showed in 1796 that the regular 17-gon is constructible, and the full classification (Gauss–Wantzel theorem) states that a regular n-gon is constructible exactly when n is a power of two times a product of distinct Fermat primes.

Variations

Related problems consider constructions with compass alone (Mohr–Mascheroni theorem: everything constructible with both tools is constructible with compass alone) or straightedge alone given one circle with its centre (Poncelet–Steiner theorem). Origami and marked-ruler (neusis) constructions are strictly more powerful, allowing angle trisection and cube doubling.
