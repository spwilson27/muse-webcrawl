Insular script is a medieval script system originating in Ireland that spread to Anglo-Saxon England and continental Europe under the influence of Irish Christian missionaries. The term "insular" refers to the British Isles (Latin: insulae). The script was used from about the 7th century and remained in use in Ireland, and for the Irish language, into the 19th century in the form of the Gaelic script.

Origins

Insular script developed in Ireland from late Roman cursive and half-uncial scripts brought by early Christian missionaries. Irish monasteries developed a distinctive family of scripts characterised by wedge-shaped ascenders, rounded letterforms and a rich system of abbreviations. From Ireland the script spread to the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms through missions such as that of Lindisfarne, and to continental monasteries founded by Irish and English missionaries, including those of Columbanus.

Types

Palaeographers distinguish several grades of Insular script, used according to the formality of the text:

  • Insular majuscule (Insular half-uncial) — a formal book hand used for deluxe manuscripts such as the Book of Kells and the Lindisfarne Gospels.
  • Insular hybrid minuscule — a less formal hand for important texts.
  • Insular minuscule — a cursive hand used for glosses, charters and everyday writing; the Anglo-Saxon and Irish forms diverged over time.

Features

Characteristic features include tall, wedge-topped ascenders on letters such as b, d, h and l; distinctive forms of g (flat-topped), r and s; the use of the Tironian sign ⁊ for "and"; and letters borrowed or adapted for vernacular sounds, such as wynn (ƿ) and thorn (þ) in Old English. Insular scribes also developed elaborate decorated initials and carpet pages in illuminated manuscripts.

Decline and legacy

In England the script was gradually displaced by Caroline minuscule from the 10th century onwards, especially after the Norman Conquest. In Ireland the Gaelic script (cló Gaelach) continued in use for Irish until the mid-20th century. Letterforms derived from Insular script survive in modern Irish orthography and in the letters þ (thorn) in Icelandic.
