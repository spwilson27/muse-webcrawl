Sacred Harp

Sacred Harp singing is a tradition of sacred choral music that originated in New England and was later preserved and developed in the rural American South. It takes its name from The Sacred Harp, a shape-note hymn book first published in 1844 in Hamilton, Georgia, by Benjamin Franklin White and Elisha J. King — the most popular and influential of the many shape-note tunebooks.

Shape notes

Sacred Harp music is printed in shape notes, a system in which the note heads of the scale are given distinct shapes (for the four-syllable system used in The Sacred Harp: triangle, circle/oval, square, and diamond for fa, sol, la, mi) to help singers sight-read. Singers first sing the tune through using the syllables, then sing the words. The repertoire includes hymn tunes, fuging tunes, anthems, odes, psalm tunes and "plain tunes", many attributed to early American composers such as William Billings, as well as to the compilers of the tunebooks themselves.

The singing

A singing is not a performance: there is no audience. Participants sit in a "hollow square" — four sections (treble, alto, tenor/lead, bass) facing inward, with singers taking turns standing in the centre to lead a song of their choice. Singing is unaccompanied, robust and loud, and all present are welcome regardless of faith. All-day singings traditionally include a communal "dinner on the grounds" and memorial lessons honouring deceased and sick members of the community.

History and revival

The tradition grew out of the singing schools of colonial New England, migrated south and west, and survived in isolated rural communities in Georgia, Alabama, Texas and elsewhere after it died out in the North. The Sacred Harp has been revised repeatedly (notably in 1911, 1936 and 1991). Since the late 20th century, folk-music enthusiasts have revived Sacred Harp singing across the United States and internationally, with regular singings now held in the United Kingdom, continental Europe, Australia and elsewhere, while traditional Southern singings continue in their historic communities.
