An oceanarium is a large-scale public aquarium devoted to marine life, typically featuring very large saltwater tanks — often holding millions of litres — that exhibit open-ocean species such as sharks, rays, sea turtles and schooling fish, frequently viewed through underwater tunnels and panoramic acrylic panels. The term is also applied to marine mammal parks where dolphins and sea lions are kept and displayed, sometimes combined with amusement attractions.

History

The oceanarium concept is generally credited to Marineland of Florida, opened in 1938 near St. Augustine as an underwater film studio where the public could watch marine life through portholes; it pioneered dolphin shows and large-volume seawater systems. Post-war advances in acrylic glazing, filtration and water chemistry enabled far larger tanks, culminating in facilities such as Chicago's Shedd Aquarium Oceanarium (1991) and, in the 21st century, enormous exhibits at Georgia Aquarium (2005) and Chimelong Ocean Kingdom in China.

Design and life support

Oceanariums depend on complex life-support systems: mechanical and biological filtration, protein skimmers, ozone or ultraviolet sterilisation, and chilling or heating to maintain stable temperatures. Seawater may be drawn from the ocean or mixed from artificial salts. Viewing panels of acrylic can exceed 20 cm in thickness to withstand the pressure of deep tanks, and many exhibits incorporate walk-through tunnels or theatres with floor-to-ceiling windows.

Notable oceanariums

  • Georgia Aquarium (Atlanta, USA) — its Ocean Voyager exhibit, of some 24 million litres, was built to house whale sharks.
  • Lisbon Oceanarium (Portugal) — a central 5-million-litre tank representing the global ocean, surrounded by habitat exhibits.
  • Shedd Aquarium (Chicago, USA) — Abbott Oceanarium housing beluga whales and Pacific white-sided dolphins.
  • Okinawa Churaumi Aquarium (Japan) — famous for its Kuroshio Sea tank and whale sharks.

Marine mammal parks and controversy

Where oceanariums keep cetaceans, their welfare has become contentious. Critics argue that wide-ranging marine mammals cannot thrive in tanks, while institutions point to research, rescue and education roles and to improved standards under accreditation bodies such as the Association of Zoos and Aquariums. Several jurisdictions have banned or restricted the keeping or breeding of cetaceans in captivity.
