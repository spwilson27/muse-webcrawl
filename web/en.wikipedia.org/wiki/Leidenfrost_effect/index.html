Leidenfrost effect

The Leidenfrost effect is a physical phenomenon in which a liquid, close to a surface significantly hotter than the liquid's boiling point, produces an insulating vapour layer that keeps the liquid from boiling rapidly. This is most commonly seen when cooking: drops of water sprinkled onto a hot pan skitter across the surface instead of evaporating immediately.

Explanation

When a droplet touches a surface above the Leidenfrost point — for water on a metal pan, roughly 193 °C (379 °F) — the underside of the droplet vaporises almost instantly, and the vapour cushion levitates the droplet, greatly slowing further heat transfer. The droplet therefore survives far longer than it would on a merely hot surface, and can glide nearly frictionlessly; on asymmetric ratchet surfaces the vapour flow can even propel droplets uphill.

History and applications

The effect is named after the German scientist Johann Gottlob Leidenfrost, who described it in 1756, though it had been observed earlier by Herman Boerhaave. The phenomenon matters in spray cooling, metallurgy and nuclear-reactor safety, where film boiling can dangerously insulate hot surfaces.
