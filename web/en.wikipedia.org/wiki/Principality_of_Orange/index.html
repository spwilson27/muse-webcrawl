The Principality of Orange (French: Principauté d'Orange) was, from 1163 to 1713, a feudal state in Provence, in the south of modern-day France, centred on the town of Orange. It consisted of the city of Orange and the surrounding lands, lying largely within the present department of Vaucluse and surrounded by the territory of the Comtat Venaissin and the Rhône valley.

History

The county of Orange was raised to a principality in 1163 by the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick I (Barbarossa), granting its rulers the rank of princes of the Empire. The principality was ruled successively by the houses of Baux, Chalon, and — from 1544, through inheritance by René of Chalon — Nassau. René's heir was his cousin William of Nassau, known as William the Silent, who thus became Prince of Orange and carried the title into the Dutch royal house, which retains "Prince of Orange" as the title of the heir apparent to this day.

Because it was an enclave surrounded by French and papal territory, the principality was repeatedly occupied by France during the wars of the seventeenth century. It was finally ceded to France by the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713, although the Prussian king and others continued to dispute the succession to the title.

Legacy

The name survives in the Dutch province traditions, the title Prince of Orange borne by the heir to the Dutch throne, and — through the Dutch associations of the colour orange — in the flag and symbols of several countries, including the former South African flag and the "Orange" institutions of Northern Ireland founded in memory of William of Orange.
