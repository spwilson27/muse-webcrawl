Chalcogenide glass

Chalcogenide glass transmits in the infrared

Chalcogenide glass (also spelled chalchogenide glass) is a glass formed from one or more chalcogens — sulfur, selenium, and tellurium — combined with other elements such as arsenic, germanium, antimony, gallium, or phosphorus. Unlike conventional oxide glasses (such as silica glass), chalcogenide glasses are opaque in the visible spectrum but transmit in the infrared, typically from about 1 μm out to 10–15 μm depending on composition. They are covalent semiconductors with relatively low phonon energies, weak bonds, and low glass transition temperatures.

Composition

Well-known chalcogenide glass systems include:

  • As–S and As–Se (e.g. As₂S₃, As₂Se₃) — classic infrared-transmitting glasses.
  • Ge–Sb–Se and Ge–As–Se — used for moulded infrared optics.
  • Ge–Sb–Te (GST) — a phase-change material used in rewritable optical discs and phase-change memory.
  • Sulfide and selenide glasses doped with rare-earth ions — studied for mid-infrared lasers and amplifiers.

Properties

Chalcogenide glasses have high refractive indices (roughly 2.0–3.5), strong optical nonlinearity, and photosensitivity: their optical properties can change on illumination, an effect exploited in photolithography and holographic recording. Because their bonds are weaker than those of oxide glasses, they are softer, have lower melting points, and can be moulded or drawn into optical fibres at comparatively low temperatures. Many are sensitive to moisture and oxidation and can be toxic if they contain arsenic.

Applications

  • Infrared optics: lenses and windows for thermal imaging cameras, night-vision equipment, and infrared spectroscopy, where they substitute for more expensive single-crystal germanium.
  • Optical fibres: mid-infrared fibre delivery for laser surgery, sensing, and spectroscopy.
  • Phase-change memory: Ge–Sb–Te alloys switch rapidly between amorphous and crystalline states, forming the basis of rewritable DVDs and Blu-ray discs and of electronic phase-change random-access memory (PCRAM).
  • Chemical sensing: chalcogenide glass electrodes and fibre sensors for detecting pollutants and biological species.

History

Amorphous chalcogenide semiconductors were studied systematically in the 1950s, notably by Boris Kolomiets and co-workers in the Soviet Union, who noted their semiconducting and switching properties. Stanford Ovshinsky's work in the 1960s on threshold and memory switching in disordered chalcogenide materials laid the foundation for phase-change memory technology, commercialised decades later in optical discs and electronic memory.
