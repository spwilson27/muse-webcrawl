Nursery school

A nursery school is an educational establishment or learning space offering early childhood education to children, usually between the ages of two and five, before they begin compulsory primary schooling. Nursery schools are part of preschool provision, which may also include kindergartens, playgroups, daycare and childcare centres. In the United Kingdom, "nursery school" often refers to a state-maintained school for three- and four-year-olds, distinct from a private day nursery that provides childcare from infancy.

Aims and approach

Nursery education focuses on learning through play, social development, language and early literacy and numeracy, motor skills and independence, rather than formal academic instruction. Approaches vary and include Montessori, Reggio Emilia, Waldorf (Steiner) and play-based curricula, as well as national frameworks such as England's Early Years Foundation Stage (EYFS). Typical provision includes:

  • Structured and free play, indoors and outdoors
  • Stories, songs, rhymes and conversation to develop language
  • Creative activities such as painting, drawing and construction
  • Early phonics and number activities in the year before school

History

The idea of institutional education for very young children developed in the early nineteenth century. Robert Owen opened an infant school at New Lanark in Scotland in 1816, and Friedrich Fröbel founded the first kindergarten in Germany in 1837. In Britain, Margaret and Rachel McMillan pioneered open-air nursery schools in London in the early twentieth century, emphasising health, nutrition and outdoor play for children in poor urban districts. Nursery education expanded considerably in many countries after the Second World War and is now widely regarded as an important foundation for later learning and social development.
