Dieting

Dieting is the practice of eating food in a regulated way to decrease, maintain, or increase body weight, or to prevent and treat diseases such as diabetes and gout. Dieting to lose weight is recommended for people with weight-related health problems, but not otherwise healthy people. Because weight loss depends on calorie intake, different kinds of calorie-reduced diets, such as those emphasising particular macronutrients (low-fat, low-carbohydrate, etc.), have been shown to be no more effective than one another.

History

The first popular diet was "Banting", named after William Banting. In his 1863 pamphlet, Letter on Corpulence, Addressed to the Public, he outlined the details of a particular low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet plan that had worked for him. The word "diet" comes from the Greek diaita, meaning a way of life. Dieting fashions have recurred throughout the twentieth century, including the Atkins diet, the cabbage soup diet, and various commercial programmes.

Energy intake

Weight loss occurs when the body expends more energy than it consumes. Reducing dietary energy intake (calories) below total energy expenditure produces a sustained negative energy balance. A deficit of roughly 500 kcal per day is commonly recommended to produce weight loss of about 0.5 kg per week. The minimum recommended daily energy intake for dieting adults is often cited as around 1,200 kcal for women and 1,500 kcal for men, below which medical supervision is advised.

Types of diets

  • Low-fat diets reduce the proportion of energy from fat.
  • Low-carbohydrate diets restrict carbohydrate intake, sometimes to ketogenic levels.
  • Low-calorie diets limit total energy regardless of composition.
  • Intermittent fasting restricts eating to particular time windows or days.
  • Very-low-calorie diets (under 800 kcal/day) are used clinically under supervision.

Effectiveness and risks

As dietary guidelines go, diets relying primarily on portion control and whole foods tend to show the best long-term adherence. Most people who lose weight on diets regain much of it within a few years, and weight cycling ("yo-yo dieting") is common. Dieting, especially in adolescents, is also a risk factor for eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa. Sustainable lifestyle change, including physical activity, is generally recommended over short-term restrictive dieting.
