Phenakistoscope

The phenakistoscope (from the Greek phenakistikos, "to deceive", and skopein, "to view") was an early animation device that created the illusion of motion from a sequence of still images. Invented almost simultaneously in 1832–33 by the Belgian physicist Joseph Plateau and the Austrian mathematician Simon von Stampfer (who marketed his version as the stroboscope), it was the first widely distributed device to demonstrate animated pictures.

How it works

The device consists of a cardboard disc with a series of drawings around its edge showing successive phases of a movement, separated by equally spaced radial slits. The user spins the disc in front of a mirror and looks through the passing slits at the reflected images. Because each image is glimpsed only briefly and always in the same position, persistence of vision blends the sequence into a single figure that appears to move — dancers twirl, jugglers juggle, faces grimace.

History and influence

Phenakistoscopes were sold as parlour amusements across Europe and America in the 1830s; commercial discs were produced by firms such as McLean's Optical Illusions in London and Alphonse Giroux in Paris. The principle led directly to the zoetrope (which moved the images to the inside of a drum, allowing several viewers at once) and later to the praxinoscope and, ultimately, to cinematography. Plateau's related research on the effects of light and duration on the retina also informed the study of visual perception.

In the twenty-first century, phenakistoscope imagery has enjoyed a revival in animated GIFs, vinyl-record art and contemporary optical art.
