The Great Buddha of Kamakura (Japanese: 鎌倉大仏, Kamakura Daibutsu) is a large bronze statue of Amida Buddha (Amitābha) at the temple of Kōtoku-in in Kamakura, Kanagawa Prefecture, Japan. It is the most famous of Japan's daibutsu (great Buddha statues) after the earlier example at Tōdai-ji in Nara, and unusual among them in standing — in fact seated — in the open air.

History

Records suggest the project began in 1238, when a wooden statue was completed after ten years of work, funded by the lady Inada and the priest Jōkō. The wooden image was damaged by a storm in 1247, and Jōkō proposed replacing it with a bronze statue together with a great hall to house it. Casting of the bronze statue began in 1252; the sculptors are thought to have included Ōno Gorōemon and Tanji Hisatomo. The hall was destroyed by storms in 1334 and 1369, rebuilt, and finally swept away by a tsunami — often identified with the Meiō earthquake of 1498 — after which the statue has stood outdoors.

The statue survived the anti-Buddhist suppression of the early Meiji period, and repairs were undertaken in 1960–61, when the neck was strengthened against earthquakes. Rudyard Kipling visited Kamakura and commemorated the statue in the prefatory poem "Buddha at Kamakura" (1892).

Description

The hollow statue, cast in some thirty separate pieces joined by an interlocking technique visible from inside, depicts Amida seated in meditation (dhyāna) with the hands in the jōin mudra on the lap. It is 11.4 metres tall excluding its base (13.35 metres with it) and weighs about 121 tonnes. Visitors may enter the interior for a small fee. Traces of gold leaf remain near the ears. The statue is designated a National Treasure of Japan.
