Nuclear strategy

Nuclear strategy is the body of military and political doctrine concerning the role of nuclear weapons in warfare and international relations: how they are deployed, targeted, and threatened or used to deter opponents, compel concessions, or fight wars. Because of their destructive power, the central problem of nuclear strategy is usually deterrence — preventing their use at all.

Main concepts

Core ideas include the distinction between counterforce targeting (against an enemy's military and nuclear forces) and countervalue targeting (against cities and industry); assured destruction and the condition of mutual assured destruction (MAD), in which each side retains a survivable second-strike capability; the stability–instability paradox; extended deterrence, whereby a nuclear power protects allies; and escalation control and escalation dominance. Delivery systems are typically organised as a triad of land-based missiles, submarine-launched missiles and strategic bombers to guarantee retaliation. Postures differ by country: the United States, Russia, China, the United Kingdom and France maintain declared arsenals, while states such as India and China proclaim no-first-use policies.

History

Strategic thought evolved rapidly after 1945, through Bernard Brodie's early work, the doctrines of massive retaliation in the 1950s, flexible response in the 1960s, and the arms-control era that produced agreements such as SALT, the INF Treaty and the START treaties. Think tanks such as RAND and theorists like Thomas Schelling and Herman Kahn shaped debates about limited nuclear war, brinkmanship and first-strike stability.
