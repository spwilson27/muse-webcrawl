The snowy egret is a small white heron of the Americas.

The snowy egret (Egretta thula) is a small white heron native to North, Central and South America. It is the American counterpart of the Old World little egret, which it closely resembles. Adults are about 56–66 cm (22–26 in) long with a wingspan of about 100 cm (39 in). The plumage is entirely white, the bill is slender and black, the legs are black, and the feet are bright yellow — a feature often described as "golden slippers." In breeding season, adults develop long, recurved plumes on the back, and the lores turn reddish.

Distribution and habitat

Snowy egrets breed in wetlands throughout the Americas, from the United States and Mexico through Central America and the Caribbean to South America as far south as Argentina and Chile. Northern populations are migratory, wintering along coasts and in warmer southern regions. They inhabit marshes, swamps, ponds, tidal flats, and the edges of lakes and rivers.

Behaviour

The species feeds in shallow water on fish, crustaceans, insects, frogs, and other small aquatic animals. It is an active and varied hunter: snowy egrets stalk prey, stir the bottom with their feet to flush animals out, hover, and sometimes run through the shallows with wings spread. They nest colonially, often with other herons and egrets, building stick platforms in trees or shrubs over water. The female lays three to five pale bluish-green eggs, which both parents incubate.

Conservation

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, snowy egrets were hunted extensively for their breeding plumes, which were highly prized in the millinery trade; populations declined drastically. Early conservation campaigns in the United States — among the first successes of the bird-protection movement — and legal protection, including under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act of 1918, allowed the species to recover. It is now widespread and listed as Least Concern, though wetland loss remains a threat.
