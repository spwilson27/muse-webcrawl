Matcha

Matcha
TypePowdered green tea
OriginChina (origins); developed in Japan
PlantCamellia sinensis
Key componentsCaffeine, L-theanine, catechins (EGCG), chlorophyll

Matcha (Japanese: 抹茶) is a finely ground powder of specially grown and processed green tea leaves (Camellia sinensis) that is consumed whole rather than steeped and discarded. Traditionally made from shade-grown tea leaves — the same leaves used for gyokuro — matcha is whisked with hot water using a bamboo whisk (chasen) to form a frothy drink, and is the tea used in the Japanese tea ceremony (chanoyu).

Production

Tea bushes are shaded for roughly three to four weeks before harvest, increasing chlorophyll and the amino acid L-theanine and producing the powder's vivid green colour and sweet, umami-rich flavour. After picking, the leaves are steamed, dried, and de-stemmed and de-veined to produce tencha, which is then stone-ground slowly into a fine powder. Grades range from ceremonial grade, made from the youngest leaves, to culinary grades used in cooking.

History

Powdered tea whisked into water originated in Song-dynasty China and was brought to Japan by Zen Buddhist monks — the monk Eisai is credited with introducing tea seeds and the method in the 12th century. Matcha became central to the tea ceremony refined by Sen no Rikyū in the 16th century.

Modern use

Beyond the tea ceremony, matcha is used worldwide in lattes, smoothies, ice cream, wagashi sweets and baked goods, and is marketed for its caffeine, theanine and antioxidant catechin content. Major producing regions include Uji in Kyoto, Nishio in Aichi, and parts of Kyushu.
