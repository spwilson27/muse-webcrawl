United States constitutional law

United States constitutional law is the body of law governing the interpretation and implementation of the United States Constitution. It defines the structure of the federal government, divides power between the federal government and the states, and protects individual liberties. The Supreme Court of the United States is the ultimate arbiter of constitutional questions.

Overview

The Constitution establishes three branches of the federal government — legislative (Article I), executive (Article II), and judicial (Article III) — and a system of checks and balances between them. Article VI makes the Constitution the "supreme Law of the Land." The first ten amendments, the Bill of Rights (1791), guarantee fundamental freedoms including speech, religion, and due process. The Reconstruction Amendments — the Thirteenth (1865), Fourteenth (1868), and Fifteenth (1870) — abolished slavery, established birthright citizenship and equal protection, and protected voting rights regardless of race.

Key doctrines

  • Judicial review — established in Marbury v. Madison (1803), the power of courts to strike down laws inconsistent with the Constitution.
  • Federalism — the division of sovereignty between the federal government and the states, governed in part by the Tenth Amendment and the Supremacy Clause.
  • Separation of powers — the allocation of authority among the three branches.
  • Commerce Clause — Congress's power to regulate interstate commerce, a basis for much federal legislation.
  • Due process and equal protection — under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, the source of many modern rights decisions.
  • Incorporation — the application of most Bill of Rights protections against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Interpretive approaches

Scholars and judges debate how the Constitution should be interpreted. Originalism holds that the text should be read according to its original public meaning; textualism focuses on the words themselves; pragmatism and "living constitutionalism" emphasize adaptation to changing circumstances and consequences. Landmark cases such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Miranda v. Arizona (1966), and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) illustrate the evolution of constitutional doctrine.
