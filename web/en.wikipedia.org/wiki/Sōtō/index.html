The Sōtō school (曹洞宗, Sōtō-shū) is the largest of the three traditional schools of Zen in Japanese Buddhism, the others being the Rinzai and Ōbaku schools. The school stresses shikantaza, meditation with no objects, anchors, or content — "just sitting" — as the expression of one's innate Buddha nature rather than a means to attain enlightenment.

History

Sōtō derives from the Chinese Caodong school of Chan, founded by Dongshan Liangjie (807–869) and his disciple Caoshan Benji. The Japanese monk Dōgen Zenji travelled to China in 1223, studied under the Caodong master Rujing, and returned to Japan in 1227 carrying the transmission. In 1244 he founded Eihei-ji in Echizen Province (modern Fukui Prefecture), which remains one of the school's two head temples. Dōgen's masterwork, the Shōbōgenzō ("Treasury of the True Dharma Eye"), is one of the masterpieces of Japanese religious literature.

The school spread widely during the Edo period under reformers such as Manzan Dōhaku and Menzan Zuihō, who standardised the Dōgen-centred doctrine. In the twentieth century, teachers including Shunryu Suzuki, author of Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, transmitted Sōtō Zen to North America and Europe, where it became one of the most influential forms of Western Zen.

Practice

The central practice is zazen, seated meditation, performed in Sōtō temples facing a wall. Dōgen taught the unity of practice and enlightenment (shushō-ichinyo): zazen is not a technique for becoming a Buddha but the activity of a Buddha. Monastic life follows the detailed regulations Dōgen set out in the Eihei Shingi, covering everything from cooking (the office of tenzo, head cook) to the use of the washroom.
