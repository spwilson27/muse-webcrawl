Chirurgia Magna

Author: Guy de Chauliac

Completed: 1363, Avignon

Language: Latin

Subject: Surgery

The Chirurgia Magna ("Great Surgery"), also known as the Inventarium sive chirurgia magna, is a treatise on surgical practice completed in 1363 by the French physician and surgeon Guy de Chauliac (c. 1300–1368), physician to the Avignon popes. It became the most influential surgical textbook of the later Middle Ages and remained a standard reference into the 17th century.

Contents

The work is organised into seven treatises covering anatomy, abscesses and tumours, wounds, ulcers, fractures and dislocations, special diseases, and an antidotary of remedies. Guy drew on Galen, Avicenna and earlier surgeons such as Henri de Mondeville and Theodoric Borgognoni, combining their teachings with his own clinical experience, including his account of the Black Death at Avignon in 1348.

Practice and influence

Guy emphasised careful wound management, the use of sutures, and surgical treatment of hernias and cataracts; he described procedures for trepanation and for the removal of bladder stones. He is frequently quoted for the dictum that a surgeon should be "learned, skilful, ingenious and of good character". Translated into French, English, Dutch, Italian, Provençal and Hebrew, the Chirurgia Magna appeared in many manuscript copies and early printed editions, and shaped surgical education for some three hundred years.
