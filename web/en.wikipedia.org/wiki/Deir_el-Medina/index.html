Deir el-Medina (Arabic for "Monastery of the City") is an ancient Egyptian workmen's village on the west bank of the Nile opposite modern Luxor. It was home to the artisans and craftsmen — stonemasons, painters, sculptors and scribes — who built and decorated the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings and Valley of the Queens during the New Kingdom (18th to 20th Dynasties, c. 1550–1070 BC). Its ancient name was Set Maat, "The Place of Truth", and its inhabitants called themselves "Servants in the Place of Truth."

The village and its people

The walled village grew to about seventy houses along a main street. Because the workmen counted many literate scribes and draughtsmen, they left an unmatched documentary record on thousands of ostraca and papyri: work registers, letters, legal disputes, laundry lists, hymns and accounts of the first recorded labour strike in history, in year 29 of Ramesses III (c. 1152 BC), when unpaid rations drove the tomb builders to sit down at temples demanding food. Nearby are the men's own beautifully painted tombs (such as those of Sennutem and Inherkhau) and a small temple to Hathor, later a Coptic monastery, which gave the site its modern name.

Excavation

The village was excavated by Ernesto Schiaparelli's Italian mission (1905–1909) and by Bernard Bruyère for the French Institute (1917–1947), who recovered the great dump of ostraca that makes Deir el-Medina the best-documented community of the ancient world. It forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site of Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis.
