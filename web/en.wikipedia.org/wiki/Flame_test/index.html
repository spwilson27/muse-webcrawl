Flame test

A flame test is an analytical procedure used by chemists to detect the presence of certain elements, primarily metal cations, based on each element's characteristic emission spectrum. A sample — usually a salt held on a clean wire loop or wooden splint — is placed in the hot, non-luminous flame of a Bunsen burner; atoms of the element are excited by the heat and emit light at specific wavelengths as their electrons fall back to lower energy states, colouring the flame.

Characteristic colours

LithiumCrimson red
SodiumIntense yellow-orange
PotassiumLilac
CalciumOrange-red ("brick red")
StrontiumScarlet red
BariumApple green
Copper(II)Blue-green
LeadGrey-white
ZincBluish-green / whitish

Procedure and limitations

The wire, traditionally platinum or nichrome, is cleaned by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric acid and heating until the flame is colourless; the acid also converts the sample to volatile chlorides that colour the flame more strongly. Potassium's lilac is easily masked by even traces of sodium, so it is viewed through cobalt-blue glass, which absorbs the sodium yellow. Because the test is qualitative, brief, and unable to distinguish mixtures reliably, modern analysis uses atomic emission spectroscopy and flame photometry — instrumented descendants of the same principle, first systematised in the flame and spark spectra studied by Bunsen and Kirchhoff around 1860, work that led directly to the discovery of caesium and rubidium.

Applications

Flame tests remain a staple of school chemistry and a quick field check for metal salts. The same emissions give fireworks their colours: strontium for red, barium for green, sodium for gold.
