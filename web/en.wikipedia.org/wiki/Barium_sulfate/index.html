Barium sulfate (or sulphate) is the inorganic compound with the chemical formula BaSO4. It is a white crystalline solid that is odourless and insoluble in water. It occurs naturally as the mineral barite, which is the main commercial source of barium and materials prepared from it. Its high density gives it the name barite derives from the Greek barys, "heavy".

Production

Nearly all commercial barium sulfate comes from mined barite. Lower-grade ore is washed, crushed and ground; high-purity material, sold as blanc fixe ("permanent white"), is precipitated by reacting barium sulfide with sodium sulfate or sulfuric acid. Most of the world's production is consumed as a weighting agent in drilling fluids for oil and gas wells, where its density helps contain downhole pressures.

Uses

Barium sulfate is widely used as a white pigment and filler in paints, plastics, rubber and paper coatings, valued for its brightness and inertness. In medicine, a suspension of barium sulfate is swallowed as a radiocontrast agent for X-ray imaging of the digestive tract — the "barium meal" and "barium enema" — because barium's high atomic number absorbs X-rays strongly. Although soluble barium salts are toxic, barium sulfate's extreme insolubility means it passes through the gut unabsorbed and is essentially harmless.

Properties

Barium sulfate is notable for its exceptionally low solubility product (Ksp ≈ 1.1 × 10−10), which underlies both its medical safety and its use as the qualitative test product for sulfate ions in analytical chemistry. It is thermally stable up to about 1,580 °C, where it decomposes to barium oxide, sulfur dioxide and oxygen.
