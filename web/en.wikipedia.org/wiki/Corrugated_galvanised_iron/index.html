Corrugated galvanised iron

Corrugated galvanised iron (CGI), colloquially corrugated iron, "wriggly tin," or in Australia simply "iron," is a building material formed from mild steel sheets that are corrugated — pressed into parallel ridges and grooves — and coated with zinc by hot-dip galvanisation to resist corrosion. The corrugations greatly increase the sheet's bending strength relative to its weight.

History

The material was patented in England in 1829 by Henry Robinson Palmer, an engineer for the London Dock Company, who recognised that corrugating thin iron sheets made them stiff enough for self-supporting roofs. Galvanising with zinc was developed in the following decade. Light, durable, cheap and easy to transport, corrugated iron spread throughout the British Empire and beyond during the 19th century, roofing everything from Australian homesteads and woolsheds to military huts, railway buildings and colonial settlements.

Uses

CGI is used for roofing and cladding of houses, farm buildings, sheds and industrial structures, and for fencing. In parts of Africa, Asia and Latin America it is the standard roofing of informal housing, including the machimbombo-roofed shanties of South African townships. Modern coated-steel profiles (often now galvanised steel or aluminium-zinc alloy, sold pre-painted under trade names such as Colorbond) have largely replaced traditional iron in new construction in developed countries, but the classic sinusoidal profile remains ubiquitous in rural landscapes, notably in Australia and New Zealand, where the corrugated-iron water tank and roof are vernacular icons.

Properties

The material is light, strong for its weight, fire-resistant and recyclable, but can be noisy in rain and hail, hot in the sun without insulation, and is subject to corrosion once the zinc coating is breached, especially in coastal environments.
