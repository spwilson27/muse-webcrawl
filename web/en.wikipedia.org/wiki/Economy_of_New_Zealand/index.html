The economy of New Zealand is a highly developed free-market economy, ranked among the world's fifty largest by nominal GDP. Services account for roughly two-thirds of GDP, but the economy remains distinctive among developed countries for the weight of its export-oriented primary sector: New Zealand is the world's largest exporter of dairy products, and agriculture, horticulture, forestry and tourism underpin much of its trade.

History

From the 1880s, refrigerated shipping allowed New Zealand to build prosperity on exports of meat, butter, cheese and wool to Britain. Entry of Britain into the European Economic Community in 1973 forced a diversification of markets and products. Facing an economic crisis in 1984, the Fourth Labour Government under finance minister Roger Douglas undertook sweeping market reforms — floating the dollar, removing subsidies and tariffs, privatising state assets and corporatising government departments — continued under the following National government. The Reserve Bank Act 1989 made the Reserve Bank of New Zealand one of the first central banks to adopt formal inflation targeting.

Sectors

Dairy farming, dominated by the cooperative Fonterra, accounts for around a third of exports; meat, wine, kiwifruit, apples and logs are also major earners. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, international tourism rivalled dairy as a source of foreign exchange. Manufacturing is modest and concentrated in food processing. The economy is closely tied to China and Australia through trade and to global commodity prices.
