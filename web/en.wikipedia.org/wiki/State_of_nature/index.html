State of nature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In ethics, political philosophy and social contract theory, the state of nature is the hypothetical or posited condition of human life before or without political society — used as a thought experiment to identify what government is for and what could justify it. Different theorists fill the condition differently, and the conclusions track the assumptions: Hobbes's state of nature is a war of all against all, justifying near-absolute sovereignty for security; Locke's is a condition of freedom and equality governed by natural law but lacking impartial judges and enforcement ("inconveniences" that government remedies while remaining a limited trust); Rousseau's is a peaceful, solitary sufficiency corrupted by society, property and comparison.

Uses and criticism

The device shaped modern ideas of natural rights, consent and resistance, and through them constitutions and declarations of rights. Critics object historically (no such condition existed; anthropology shows humans always in social groups), theoretically (the theorists smuggle social assumptions into "nature"), and politically (colonial writers applied the label to indigenous peoples to deny their polities). Modern uses persist in international-relations theory (anarchy as the state of nature among states) and in game-theoretic reconstructions of cooperation's emergence.
