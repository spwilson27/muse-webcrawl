A peak programme meter (PPM) is an instrument used in professional audio for indicating the level of an audio signal. It displays a quasi-peak value: its pointer (or electronic bar graph) rises rapidly toward the peak level of programme material but with integration characteristics defined by standard, so that very brief transients are not fully registered.

Characteristics

Unlike a VU meter, which indicates average level with slow ballistics, the PPM was developed — principally by the BBC in the 1930s — to monitor and control signal peaks so as to avoid over-modulation of transmitters and distortion on recordings. A standard PPM has an integration time of about 10 ms (a 5 ms burst reaches within a few dB of the steady-state reading) and a slow fallback, typically 24 dB in about 3 seconds (in the British type), making peaks easy to read.

Scales

Several regional scales exist. The British PPM scale is marked 1 to 7 (with an extended 0 mark on some versions), each mark representing 4 dB, with mark 4 conventionally aligned to 0 dBu test tone (permitted maximum level is usually mark 6). Other variants include the EBU scale in dB, the DIN scale used in Germany, and the Nordic scale. Digital implementations follow IEC 60268-10.

Use today

PPMs remain in use in broadcasting, particularly in the United Kingdom and much of Europe, although loudness measurement (LUFS/LKFS) per ITU-R BS.1770 has become the primary loudness standard for programme delivery. The PPM continues to be valued for its consistent indication of peak programme level in studio monitoring and transmission chains.
