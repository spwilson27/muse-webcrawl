Protolanguage

In the tree model of historical linguistics, a protolanguage is a language that was the common ancestor of a related set of languages that form a language family. When a protolanguage is unattested in surviving texts, it is reconstructed by applying the comparative method to its descendants, and reconstructed forms are marked with an asterisk. The term "proto-" is prefixed to the family name, as in Proto-Indo-European, Proto-Germanic, or Proto-Slavic.

Reconstruction

Linguists identify systematic sound correspondences and shared grammatical features among related languages and infer the features of their ancestor. A complete reconstruction is an idealization: real protolanguages were spoken communities with dialect variation, and reconstructions are abstractions of selected features at a chosen time depth. Internal reconstruction, using alternations within a single language, can supplement the comparative method.

Attested and unattested protolanguages

Some protolanguages are partly attested: Proto-Romance is well approximated by Latin, though Vulgar Latin (the actual spoken ancestor) differed from the Classical written standard. Others, such as Proto-Indo-European (spoken perhaps 4500–2500 BC), are entirely reconstructed. Intermediate protolanguages stand between deeper ancestors and modern languages — Proto-West Germanic, for example, descends from Proto-Germanic and precedes Old English, Old High German, and their relatives.

Related but distinct concepts include the monogenesis hypothesis of a single original "Proto-World" language for all humanity, which lies beyond the reach of the comparative method and is not generally accepted as demonstrable.
