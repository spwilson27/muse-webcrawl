Tusk

Tusks are elongated, continuously growing front teeth that protrude well beyond the mouth of certain mammal species. They are most commonly canine teeth, as in pigs, hippos and walruses, or, in the case of elephants, elongated incisors. Tusks are often paired and grow throughout the animal's life. In most tusked species both males and females have tusks, although the tusks of males tend to be larger.

Structure and growth

Tusks are composed principally of dentine. In elephants, the visible tusk is largely ivory — a form of dentine — surrounding a pulp cavity that extends far into the tusk. Elephant tusks are elongated upper incisors; about a third of each tusk is embedded in the skull. Because they are exposed teeth, tusks have nerves running through them and are sensitive, and they continue growing throughout life at a rate of roughly 17 cm (7 in) per year in African elephants.

Functions

Tusks serve many purposes depending on the species: digging for water and minerals, stripping bark from trees, defence against predators, display in social interactions, and fighting between males during mating seasons. Walruses use their tusks to haul themselves onto ice and to maintain breathing holes; warthogs use theirs in combat; the male narwhal's single spiral tusk — a left canine — is thought to play a role in sensing the environment as well as display.

Humans and tusks

Ivory from elephant tusks has been prized by humans for millennia for carvings, piano keys, billiard balls and ornaments. The ivory trade drove catastrophic poaching of African and Asian elephants throughout the 19th and 20th centuries; international commercial trade in elephant ivory was banned under CITES in 1989, though illegal poaching persists. Sustained hunting of large-tusked animals has also produced evolutionary pressure: in some heavily poached populations, the proportion of naturally tuskless elephants has risen sharply.
