Stargate Project

The Stargate Project was the collective code name for a series of United States government programs investigating the potential military and intelligence applications of psychic phenomena, particularly remote viewing — the claimed ability to perceive distant or unseen targets. The research ran from the 1970s until 1995, when it was terminated and declassified.

History

Concerned that the Soviet Union was investing in psychotronic research, the CIA funded experiments at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) beginning in 1972, led by physicists Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ, with subjects including Ingo Swann and Pat Price. The work moved among several agencies and code names — Grill Flame, Center Lane, Sun Streak — and a small operational unit at Fort Meade, Maryland, employed military remote viewers such as Joseph McMoneagle on intelligence tasks. In 1991 the program was consolidated under the Defense Intelligence Agency as Stargate.

Evaluation and termination

In 1995 the CIA commissioned the American Institutes for Research to evaluate the program. The reviewers disagreed: statistician Jessica Utts found statistically significant effects, while psychologist Ray Hyman argued the results could be explained by methodological flaws. Both agreed remote viewing had not been shown to work in a way useful for intelligence. The program was shut down, and its files were largely declassified.

Legacy

The Stargate Project remains the most prominent government investigation of parapsychology. Its history has been widely recounted in books and media, including Jon Ronson's The Men Who Stare at Goats (2004), later adapted into a film. Mainstream science regards remote viewing as a pseudoscience unsupported by reliable evidence.
