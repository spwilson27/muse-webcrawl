Cultural geography


A cultural landscape: rice terraces inscribed by human practice

Cultural geography is a sub-field within human geography. Though the first traces of the study of different peoples as an element of geography can be traced back as early as classical antiquity, cultural geography as a distinct academic study first emerged in the early 20th century as an alternative to the environmental determinism of the time, which held that peoples and societies were controlled by the environment in which they developed. Instead of studying predetermined regional cultures, cultural geography focuses on cultural landscapes.

History

The American geographer Carl O. Sauer and the Berkeley School he founded were central to the field's formation. In The Morphology of Landscape (1925) Sauer proposed that the cultural landscape — the visible imprint of a culture group upon the natural landscape — should be the focus of geographical study, in contrast to the environmental determinism of earlier writers. The German tradition of Landschaftskunde and the work of Friedrich Ratzel also fed into the discipline.

New cultural geography

From the 1980s a "new cultural geography" emerged, drawing on critical theory, postmodernism, feminism and postcolonial studies. Scholars such as Peter Jackson and Denis Cosgrove expanded the field from material landscapes to questions of identity, representation, power, place, and the politics of culture, and studies of everyday landscapes, media, and consumption. Cultural geography now overlaps with anthropology, sociology, cultural studies, and political ecology.

Concepts

  • Cultural landscape — the fashioned landscape produced by a culture over time.
  • Culture region — an area sharing cultural traits, language, or religion.
  • Cultural diffusion and acculturation — the spread and blending of cultural traits.
  • Sense of place and topophilia — human attachment to places.
