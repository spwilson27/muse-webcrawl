The gauss, symbol G (sometimes written Gs), is a unit of measurement of magnetic induction, also known as magnetic flux density. It is the unit of the B-field in the Gaussian system of units, which is based on the centimetre–gram–second (CGS) system. The unit is named in honour of the German mathematician and physicist Carl Friedrich Gauss.

Definition

One gauss is defined as one maxwell per square centimetre: 1 G = 1 Mx/cm2. The corresponding SI unit, the tesla, is much larger: 1 G = 10−4 T, so 10,000 gauss equal one tesla. In Gaussian units the gauss is dimensionally equivalent to the oersted, the CGS unit of the H-field, when the medium is vacuum.

Typical magnitudes

The magnetic field of the Earth at its surface ranges from roughly 0.25 to 0.65 gauss. A small iron magnet produces a field of about 100 gauss near its surface, while a large electromagnet can exceed 15,000 gauss (1.5 T). The strongest steady fields produced in laboratories reach several hundred thousand gauss (tens of teslas), and neutron stars may have fields of 1011 gauss or more.

History and use

The name gauss for the CGS unit of magnetic induction was adopted internationally in the early 20th century. Although the tesla replaced the gauss in the SI system adopted in 1960, the gauss remains in common use in many fields, including geophysics, astrophysics and the permanent-magnet industry, where magnet strengths are frequently quoted in gauss or kilogauss. Magnetometers that measure weak fields are sometimes called gaussmeters.
