A swept wing is a wing that angles either backward or occasionally forward from its root rather than in a straight sideways direction. Wing sweep has the effect of delaying the onset of shock waves and the accompanying aerodynamic drag rise caused by fluid compressibility near the speed of sound, so it is a key enabling technology for transonic and supersonic flight. Swept wings have been used since the Second World War on nearly all jet aircraft, from fighters to airliners such as the Boeing 747.

Reasons for sweep

The main aerodynamic reason for sweeping a wing is to delay compressibility effects. When an aircraft flies at transonic speeds, the airflow over the wing can locally exceed the speed of sound, forming shock waves that sharply increase drag. Sweep reduces the component of the airflow perpendicular to the wing's leading edge, effectively making the wing "see" a lower Mach number than the aircraft is actually flying. Sweep also reduces the strength of any shocks that do form, and hence the severity of the resulting drag rise and buffet.

Sweep is conventionally measured at the quarter-chord line. Typical airliners use around 25–35 degrees of sweep, while high-performance military aircraft may use 40–60 degrees, and some designs use variable geometry ("swing wings") to combine good low-speed and high-speed performance.

History

The benefits of sweep were first seriously studied in Germany in the 1930s, notably by Adolf Busemann, who proposed swept wings for supersonic flight in 1935. The Messerschmitt Me 262, the first operational jet fighter, used a modest 18.5 degrees of sweep, mainly to position the centre of lift correctly. Captured German research after the war, combined with the work of engineers such as Robert T. Jones at NACA in the United States, led to swept wings appearing on the Boeing B-47 Stratojet and North American F-86 Sabre, and thence on virtually every jetliner since the Boeing 707.

Disadvantages

Swept wings suffer spanwise flow, which thickens the boundary layer toward the wingtips and can cause the tips to stall first — a dangerous pitch-up tendency, partly mitigated by wing fences, vortilons, and washout. They are also structurally heavier than straight wings of equivalent strength, and produce less lift at a given angle of attack, requiring higher take-off and landing speeds or extensive high-lift devices such as slats and flaps.
