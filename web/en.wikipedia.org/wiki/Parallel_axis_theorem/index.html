Parallel axis theorem

The parallel axis theorem, also known as the Huygens–Steiner theorem after Christiaan Huygens and Jakob Steiner, relates the moment of inertia of a rigid body about any axis to its moment of inertia about a parallel axis through the body's centre of mass.

Statement

If Icm is the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass, M is the total mass, and d is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel axes, then the moment of inertia about the second axis is:

I = Icm + Md²

The theorem holds in reverse as well: knowing I about an arbitrary axis, the centroidal value is Icm = I − Md². It also applies to the second moment of area (area moment of inertia) used in beam theory, in the form I = Ic + Ad², where A is the cross-sectional area.

Derivation

The result follows by expanding the integral of the squared distance from the new axis: writing each mass element's position as the sum of its position relative to the centre of mass and the offset vector, the cross-term vanishes because the first moment of mass about the centre of mass is zero, leaving the centroidal inertia plus the term Md².

Applications

The theorem makes it possible to compute moments of inertia for composite bodies — for instance, a physical pendulum, a rod swinging about one end, or an off-centre wheel — from tabulated centroidal values. Its analogue for products of inertia and its tensor generalization (the inertia tensor transformation) are used throughout rigid-body dynamics and structural engineering.
