Bell's spaceship paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bell's spaceship paradox is a thought experiment in special relativity, first described by E. Dewan and M. Beran in 1959 and made famous by John Stewart Bell. It concerns two spaceships, joined by a fragile string, which accelerate identically in an inertial frame. The question is whether the string breaks.

The paradox

Two spaceships initially at rest in an inertial frame S are connected by a taut string. Both ships then accelerate with exactly the same constant proper acceleration in the same direction, as measured in S. Because their velocity profiles in S are identical, the distance between them in S remains constant at all times. However, the string, if it were at rest in the ships' instantaneous rest frame, would undergo Lorentz contraction; since it is being forced to span a constant distance in S while its own rest length tries to contract relative to S, stresses build up in the string, and in the standard analysis it eventually breaks.

Resolution

The paradox arises from neglecting the relativity of simultaneity and the fact that the ships' shared instantaneous rest frames change during the acceleration. In the momentarily comoving inertial frame of the ships, their separation increases because the rear ship's acceleration events are not simultaneous with the front ship's. Bell used the paradox to argue that Lorentz contraction is a physically real effect with dynamical consequences, famously polling colleagues at CERN, most of whom initially (and, in Bell's view, incorrectly) answered that the string would not break.
