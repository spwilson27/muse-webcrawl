Revenge play

A revenge play is a dramatic form, principally associated with the theatre of Elizabethan and Jacobean England, in which the protagonist seeks to avenge a wrong—usually a murder—often at the urging of the victim's ghost. The genre reached its height between the 1580s and the 1640s and is closely related to, but broader in application than, the revenge tragedy, with which the term is frequently used interchangeably.

Origins

The revenge play derives chiefly from the tragedies of the Roman dramatist Seneca the Younger, whose plays—filled with ghosts, madness, rhetorical declamation, and spectacular bloodshed—were avidly read and imitated in Renaissance schools and universities. Senecan conventions were fused with the traditions of the medieval morality play and native English drama.

Conventions

Scholars such as Fredson Bowers identified a recurring set of conventions in the genre:

  • A ghost who demands revenge for his murder;
  • The revenger's hesitation, feigned or real madness, and soliloquies;
  • Secret murder, often by poisoning, and the difficulty of obtaining legal redress;
  • The revenger's use of disguise and meta-theatrical devices, including a play within the play;
  • A final catastrophe in which the revenger himself usually dies along with his enemies;
  • Abundant on-stage violence and a high body count.

Major examples

Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy (c. 1587) established the form and was one of the most popular plays of the period; its protagonist Hieronimo, driven mad by his son's murder, stages a play that becomes the instrument of his revenge. William Shakespeare's Hamlet (c. 1600) is the genre's most famous example, subverting its conventions by foregrounding the hero's doubt and delay. Other significant examples include The Revenger's Tragedy (1606, attributed to Cyril Tourneur or Thomas Middleton), John Webster's The White Devil (1612) and The Duchess of Malfi (1613–14), and Middleton and Rowley's The Changeling (1622).

Criticism and legacy

T. S. Eliot famously called Hamlet an "artistic failure" partly because Shakespeare inherited the creaking machinery of the revenge play. Modern criticism has instead emphasized the genre's engagement with early modern anxieties about justice, the ethics of private vengeance versus public law, and the tensions between Christian prohibitions on revenge and a residual honour culture. The revenge plot survives robustly in modern film, from westerns to contemporary action cinema.
