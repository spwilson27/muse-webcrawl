Caper

Capers are the pickled unopened flower buds of the caper bush, Capparis spinosa, a perennial plant native to the Mediterranean region. The buds are harvested by hand, dried, and cured in brine or salt, developing a sharp, piquant, lemony flavour through the formation of mustard oil (methyl isothiocyanate) from glucocapparin in the buds. Capers are a signature ingredient of Mediterranean cuisine, used in dishes such as chicken piccata, puttanesca sauce, and tartar sauce. The mature fruit of the plant, called caper berries, are also pickled and eaten, often served with drinks like olives.

Cultivation and harvest

The caper bush is a sprawling, scrambling shrub with fleshy leaves and large white-to-pink flowers, adapted to poor, rocky soils and dry climates; it grows wild on walls and cliffs around the Mediterranean and is cultivated in Spain, Italy (notably the islands of Pantelleria and Salina), Greece, Turkey, Morocco, and elsewhere. Buds are picked daily during the season before they open, sorted by size — the smallest grades, such as French nonpareilles, are the most prized — and then wilted and packed in salt or brine.

Culinary use

Capers are rarely eaten raw; pickling gives them their characteristic salty, sour, pungent taste. Salt-packed capers are rinsed before use. They pair classically with lemon, butter, and fish; feature in Italian dishes like vitello tonnato and spaghetti alla puttanesca; in French remoulade and sauce gribiche; in German Königsberger Klopse; and as a garnish for smoked salmon and bagels. Caper berries, picked with their stems, are larger and milder and are eaten as an antipasto.

History

Capers were known in antiquity: they are mentioned in the Sumerian Epic of Gilgamesh tradition and were used by the ancient Greeks and Romans both as food and as medicine, appearing in the writings of Dioscorides. They remain an important smallholder crop on arid Mediterranean islands, where Pantelleria capers hold EU protected geographical status.
