A safety bicycle (or simply a safety) is a type of bicycle that became the standard design in the late 1880s and remains, in essence, the architecture of the modern bicycle. In contrast to the penny-farthing, whose huge front wheel placed the rider high above the ground with a dangerous tendency to pitch forward over the handlebars ("taking a header"), the safety bicycle has two wheels of roughly equal size, pedals driving the rear wheel through a chain and sprockets, a low diamond frame, and a saddle position from which the rider can put a foot to the ground.

Development

Chain-driven, rear-wheel-drive designs appeared from the late 1860s and 1870s, but the breakthrough was John Kemp Starley's Rover safety bicycle of 1885, built in Coventry, which combined the key features in a commercially successful package. The addition of John Boyd Dunlop's pneumatic tyre in 1888 made the smaller wheels comfortable, and by the early 1890s the safety had almost completely displaced the penny-farthing.

Impact

Cheaper, safer and usable by a far wider population — including women, whose adoption of the bicycle became a symbol and instrument of social emancipation and dress reform — the safety bicycle powered the great cycling boom of the 1890s, spurred road improvement campaigns, and created manufacturing expertise and components (ball bearings, precision chain, steel tubing) that fed directly into the early motorcycle and automobile industries.
