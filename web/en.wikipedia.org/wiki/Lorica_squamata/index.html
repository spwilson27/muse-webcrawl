Lorica squamata

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The lorica squamata is a type of scale armour used by the ancient Roman military during the Roman Republic and at various periods of the Roman Empire. It consisted of small metal scales — of iron or bronze, and sometimes of horn — sewn or wired to a fabric backing of linen or leather in overlapping rows, so that the arrangement resembled the scales of a fish or the feathers of a bird.

Construction

Individual scales typically measured between about 0.5 and 2 cm in width and were pierced with pairs of holes through which wire or cord fastened them to the backing and to one another. Because the scales overlapped downward, blows tended to glance off, and the armour offered good protection against cuts and missile impacts. It was, however, vulnerable to an upward thrust, and required regular maintenance to prevent the metal scales from rusting and the backing from rotting.

Use

Scale armour was worn by standard-bearers, musicians, centurions, cavalrymen and auxiliary infantry, and appears frequently in Roman sculpture. It remained in use alongside mail (lorica hamata) and the articulated plate armour (lorica segmentata) of the legions, and similar scale defences were used by Rome's eastern neighbours and enemies, including the Parthians and Sassanid Persians, whose heavy cavalry sometimes wore scale armour for both rider and horse.
