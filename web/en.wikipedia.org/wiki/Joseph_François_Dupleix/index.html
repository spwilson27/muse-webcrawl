Joseph François Dupleix

Joseph François Dupleix, Marquis Dupleix (1 January 1697 – 10 November 1763) was a French colonial administrator, governor-general of French India from 1742 to 1754. A brilliant and ambitious strategist, he built a French sphere of influence across southern India through alliances with Indian rulers, and came close to making France the dominant European power on the subcontinent before being recalled by a government that preferred commerce to empire.

Early career in India

Dupleix was born in Landrecies, France. His father, a director of the French East India Company, obtained him a post in India, and he arrived at Pondicherry in 1715. He amassed a large private fortune in trade, served as governor of Chandernagore in Bengal — which he developed into a prosperous trading station — and in 1742 became governor-general of all French establishments in India.

Governor-general

During the War of the Austrian Succession Dupleix captured Madras from the British in 1746 and successfully defended Pondicherry against a British siege in 1748, though Madras was returned by the peace treaty. In the succession struggles that followed in the Carnatic and Hyderabad, Dupleix intervened on behalf of allied claimants — Chanda Sahib and Muzaffar Jung — installing French-backed rulers and receiving vast territories and honours, including a Mughal title and governorship south of the Krishna River. His trained Indian troops (sepoys), led by officers such as the Marquis de Bussy, repeatedly defeated larger forces, pioneering methods the British East India Company would soon copy under Robert Clive, Dupleix's great rival.

Recall and legacy

The French East India Company, alarmed at the cost of Dupleix's political ventures and under pressure in Europe, recalled him in 1754 and negotiated a settlement with the British that surrendered most of his gains. Dupleix spent his remaining years in litigation, vainly seeking reimbursement of the fortune he had spent in the Company's service, and died in Paris in relative obscurity in 1763. Historians have generally seen him as a visionary empire-builder frustrated by his own government; a Paris Métro station and several French warships have borne his name.
