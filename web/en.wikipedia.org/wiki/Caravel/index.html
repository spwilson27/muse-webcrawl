The caravel (Portuguese: caravela) is a small, highly manoeuvrable sailing ship developed in the 15th century by the Portuguese. It explored the Atlantic and West African coasts during the Age of Discovery. A light vessel with a shallow draft and triangular lateen sails, the caravel could sail much closer to the wind than earlier European ships, beat against prevailing winds, and navigate in shallow coastal waters, making it ideal for exploration. The caravels Niña and Pinta, which accompanied Christopher Columbus on his first voyage of 1492, are among the most famous examples.

History

The caravel evolved from earlier Portuguese fishing boats and small lateen-rigged vessels (the caravela latina), drawing on Mediterranean and Islamic maritime traditions. Under the patronage of Prince Henry the Navigator, Portuguese shipwrights refined the type during expeditions down the African coast, reaching the Gulf of Guinea by the 1460s and rounding the Cape of Good Hope under Bartolomeu Dias in 1488.

Later a square-rigged variant, the caravela redonda, added square sails on the foremast for greater drive in following winds on long ocean passages, and was used by Vasco da Gama's fleet. As trade with India and Brazil demanded larger cargo capacity, the caravel was progressively superseded by the larger carrack and galleon, though caravels remained in use for coastal and exploratory work into the 17th century.

Design

Early caravels displaced around 50 tons, with a length-to-beam ratio of about 3.5:1, a single deck, and a plain transom stern. Their carvel-built hulls (planks flush rather than overlapping) gave a smooth, efficient surface. The lateen rig allowed sailing points far closer to the wind than square rigs, an essential capability for returning from Guinea against the northeast trade winds — the technique known as the volta do mar. Later oceanic versions grew to 100–150 tons or more with three masts.
