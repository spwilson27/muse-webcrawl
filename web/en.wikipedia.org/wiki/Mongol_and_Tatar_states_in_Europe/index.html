Mongol and Tatar states in Europe

The Mongol and Tatar states in Europe were the successor polities of the Mongol Empire established in Eastern Europe following the Mongol invasions of the 13th century. The most important of these was the Golden Horde, which dominated the Pontic-Caspian steppe and exercised suzerainty over the principalities of Kievan Rus' for more than two centuries. After the Golden Horde fragmented in the 15th century, several Tatar khanates emerged, including the Khanates of Kazan, Crimea, Astrakhan, and Siberia, as well as the Nogai Horde and the Qasim Khanate.

The Golden Horde

The Golden Horde originated as the Ulus of Jochi, the appanage granted to the eldest son of Genghis Khan. Under Batu Khan, Mongol armies conquered the Volga Bulgars, the Rus' principalities, and campaigned into Poland and Hungary in 1241–42. The Horde's capitals at Sarai on the lower Volga became major centres of trade linking Europe with Central Asia and China. From the late 13th century the khans converted to Islam, a process completed under Özbeg Khan (r. 1313–1341).

Fragmentation and successor khanates

Defeats by Timur at the end of the 14th century and internal civil wars weakened the Horde, and by the mid-15th century it had splintered. The Khanate of Kazan (1438) controlled the middle Volga; the Crimean Khanate (1441) under the Giray dynasty ruled the Black Sea steppe and became an Ottoman vassal; the Khanate of Astrakhan (1466) held the Volga delta; and the Khanate of Siberia lay beyond the Urals. The rump Great Horde was destroyed by Crimea in 1502.

Relations with the Rus' and Russia

The Rus' princes paid tribute (the so-called "Mongol yoke") and received patents (yarliqs) from the khan. Moscow gradually consolidated power, defeated the Horde's forces symbolically at the stand on the Ugra River in 1480, and under Ivan IV conquered Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556). The Crimean Khanate, protected by the Ottomans, survived until its annexation by the Russian Empire in 1783.

Legacy

The Mongol and Tatar states shaped the political geography of Eastern Europe, influenced Russian administrative and military practices, and left lasting Tatar populations in the Volga region, Crimea, and elsewhere.
