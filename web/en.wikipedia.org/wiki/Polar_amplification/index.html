Polar amplification

Polar amplification is the phenomenon that any change in the net radiation balance of the Earth tends to produce a larger change in temperature near the poles than the planetary average. In the current era of anthropogenic global warming it appears principally as Arctic amplification: the Arctic has warmed several times faster than the global mean rate in recent decades, while Antarctic amplification has been weaker and delayed, partly because of the buffering effect of the Southern Ocean.

Causes

The leading mechanism is the ice–albedo feedback: as bright sea ice and snow retreat, darker ocean and land absorb more sunlight, causing further warming and melt. Additional contributors include the lapse-rate feedback (near-surface warming is stronger in the stable polar atmosphere), the Planck feedback's latitude dependence, increased poleward transport of heat and moisture by the atmosphere and oceans, and changes in clouds and in black carbon deposition on snow.

Consequences

Amplified Arctic warming drives the observed decline of Arctic sea ice, accelerates Greenland ice loss and permafrost thaw, and reduces the equator-to-pole temperature gradient, with debated effects on mid-latitude weather patterns such as jet stream behaviour and blocking events.
