The Szilárd petition, drafted in July 1945 by the physicist Leó Szilárd, was a document signed by 70 scientists working on the Manhattan Project in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, and the Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago. It asked President Harry S. Truman to inform Japan of the terms of surrender demanded by the Allies, and to allow Japan to either accept or refuse those terms, before the United States used the atomic bomb against it.

Background

Szilárd, who had played a central role in initiating the American atomic bomb programme — he co-drafted the 1939 Einstein–Szilárd letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt — became increasingly concerned during 1945 about the moral and political consequences of using the weapon against cities. In June 1945 a committee of Chicago scientists chaired by James Franck issued the Franck Report, which recommended a demonstration of the bomb on an uninhabited area before any use against a populated target.

After the successful Trinity test of July 16, 1945, Szilárd drafted the petition. It argued that the United States, having been the first to develop atomic weapons, bore a special responsibility: "If after this war a situation is allowed to develop in the world which permits rival powers to be in uncontrolled possession of these new means of destruction, the cities of the United States as well as the cities of other nations will be in continuous danger of sudden annihilation."

Contents

The petition did not demand that the bomb never be used. It asked that its use against Japan be conditional: Japan should first be given a full statement of the terms of surrender, and only if those terms were refused should atomic weapons be employed — and then only after "adequate warning" had been given. The signatories warned that a surprise attack on Japan would sacrifice public support for the United States throughout the world and precipitate an arms race.

Fate of the petition

The petition was sent through official channels to Washington. However, it was delayed by the military authorities in Oak Ridge, classified, and apparently never reached President Truman before the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki on August 6 and 9, 1945. General Leslie Groves, the military head of the Manhattan Project, later stated that he had not forwarded it. The document was declassified in 1961 and published in 1963.

Legacy

The petition is frequently cited in discussions of scientists' social responsibility. Szilárd went on to found, with Albert Einstein and others, the Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists in 1946, and later helped establish the Council for a Livable World, a political action committee devoted to arms control.
