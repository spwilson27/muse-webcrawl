Child development

Child development
ScopeBiological, psychological and emotional changes from birth to adolescence
DomainsPhysical, cognitive, language, social-emotional
Key theoristsJean Piaget, Lev Vygotsky, Erik Erikson, John Bowlby

Child development involves the biological, psychological and emotional changes that occur in human beings between birth and the conclusion of adolescence. Childhood is divided into three stages: early childhood (including infancy and the toddler years), middle childhood, and adolescence. Development proceeds as a continuous, if uneven, process, shaped by both genetic inheritance and environmental influence — the longstanding "nature versus nurture" interplay.

Domains and milestones

Development is commonly tracked in four domains. Physical development covers growth and motor skills, from first steps around the age of one to the refined coordination of later childhood. Cognitive development covers learning, memory, problem-solving and language comprehension. Language development moves from babbling and first words to fluent speech. Social-emotional development covers attachment, empathy, self-regulation and relationships. Developmental milestones — typical ages by which children achieve skills such as walking, talking in sentences or reading — are used by clinicians to screen for developmental delays.

Theories

Jean Piaget proposed that children pass through four stages of cognitive development — sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational and formal operational — actively constructing their understanding of the world. Lev Vygotsky emphasised the social and cultural scaffolding of learning, introducing the zone of proximal development. Erik Erikson described psychosocial stages across the lifespan, while John Bowlby's attachment theory highlighted the importance of early bonds with caregivers.

Influences

Nutrition, health, parenting, play, early education and socioeconomic conditions all affect development. Adverse experiences such as neglect or chronic stress can have lasting effects, while enriched, responsive caregiving supports resilience. Public-health and education policy increasingly focuses on the first years of life, when brain development is most rapid.
