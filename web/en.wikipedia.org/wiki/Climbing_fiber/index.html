Climbing fiber

Climbing fiber
Origin: Inferior olivary nucleus (medulla)
Target: Purkinje cells of the cerebellum
Neurotransmitter: Glutamate (excitatory)
Signature event: Complex spike

Climbing fibers are the axonal projections of neurons in the inferior olivary nucleus of the medulla that form powerful excitatory synapses on the Purkinje cells of the cerebellar cortex. Together with mossy fibers, they are one of the two main input pathways to the cerebellum.

Anatomy

Each climbing fiber ascends from the contralateral inferior olive, enters the cerebellum through the inferior cerebellar peduncle, and "climbs" — as the name describes — up the dendritic tree of a single Purkinje cell, making hundreds of synaptic contacts along its proximal dendrites. In adult mammals the relationship is largely one-to-one: each Purkinje cell receives input from just one climbing fiber, a refinement achieved by developmental synapse elimination of initially multiple inputs.

Physiology

Climbing fibers fire at a low rate (around 1 Hz), but each action potential evokes a uniquely large response in the Purkinje cell: the complex spike, a powerful depolarisation followed by a burst of smaller spikelets and a large calcium influx into the dendrites. This calcium signal is central to theories of cerebellar learning, in which co-activation of climbing fibers and parallel fibers induces long-term depression (LTD) at parallel-fiber synapses.

Function

The classic Marr–Albus–Ito theory casts climbing fibers as "teaching" or error signals that instruct the cerebellum when a movement has gone wrong, driving motor learning. They are also implicated in timing, coordination, and in the pathophysiology of tremor, since the inferior olive's rhythmic oscillations can propagate through this pathway.
