Direct-acting antiviral

Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) are a class of antiviral drugs that act directly on specific viral proteins essential to a virus's life cycle, in contrast to older, broadly acting agents such as interferon. The term is most commonly applied to the drugs developed against the hepatitis C virus (HCV) that transformed treatment of chronic hepatitis C in the 2010s.

Hepatitis C DAAs

DAAs target three viral proteins: the NS3/4A serine protease (inhibitors such as glecaprevir and grazoprevir, whose names end in -previr), the NS5A replication complex protein (inhibitors such as ledipasvir, daclatasvir and pibrentasvir, ending in -asvir), and the NS5B RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (inhibitors such as sofosbuvir and dasabuvir, ending in -buvir). The first DAAs, the protease inhibitors boceprevir and telaprevir, were approved in 2011 but had to be combined with interferon. Sofosbuvir, approved in December 2013, enabled interferon-free, all-oral regimens.

Effectiveness

Combination DAA regimens typically cure more than 95% of treated patients, measured as sustained virologic response 12 weeks after treatment (SVR12), over courses of 8–12 weeks with mild side effects. Pangenotypic combinations such as sofosbuvir/velpatasvir and glecaprevir/pibrentasvir work across all HCV genotypes. The World Health Organization has set a goal of eliminating hepatitis C as a public health problem by 2030, although the high initial list prices of DAAs — sofosbuvir launched at about $1,000 per pill in the United States — raised major access debates, partly addressed by generic licensing for low-income countries.
