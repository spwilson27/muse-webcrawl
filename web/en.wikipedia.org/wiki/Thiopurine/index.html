Thiopurine

The thiopurines are a class of purine antimetabolite immunosuppressive drugs. The three principal agents are azathioprine, mercaptopurine (6-mercaptopurine), and thioguanine (6-thioguanine). They are widely used in the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, autoimmune disorders (such as Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and rheumatoid arthritis), and to prevent rejection after organ transplantation.

Mechanism of action

Thiopurines are prodrugs or analogues of the natural purines adenine and guanine in which the hydroxyl/amine functionality is replaced by a thiol group. After metabolic activation — principally by the enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) — they are converted into thioinosine and thioguanine nucleotides, which are incorporated into DNA and RNA and inhibit de novo purine synthesis. The resulting interference with nucleic acid synthesis preferentially affects rapidly proliferating cells such as activated lymphocytes, producing immunosuppression and cytotoxicity.

Metabolism and pharmacogenetics

Thiopurine drugs are inactivated in part by the enzyme thiopurine S-methyltransferase (TPMT). A significant minority of people inherit reduced TPMT activity, and about 1 in 300 have essentially no activity; in such patients standard doses can cause life-threatening myelosuppression (bone marrow suppression). Testing for TPMT activity or genotype, and for variants in the NUDT15 gene (particularly relevant in East Asian populations), is recommended before therapy. The xanthine oxidase inhibitor allopurinol also blocks thiopurine breakdown and requires major dose reductions when used together.

Adverse effects

Important side effects include bone marrow suppression, hepatotoxicity, nausea, and an increased risk of infection and, with long-term use, of certain malignancies including lymphoma and non-melanoma skin cancer. Azathioprine can cause a hypersensitivity reaction in the first weeks of treatment.
