The Great Books are a collection of books considered a foundation of Western literary and intellectual tradition, used as the basis of discussion in the Great Books movement in higher education. The movement holds that reading and discussing these works directly — rather than reading about them — is the best liberal education, training students to engage with the "great conversation" of enduring ideas.

History

John Erskine introduced a Great Honors course at Columbia University in 1920. Mortimer J. Adler popularized the approach, and with University of Chicago president Robert Maynard Hutchins he edited Great Books of the Western World (1952), a 54-volume set published by Encyclopædia Britannica spanning Homer to Freud, accompanied by the Syntopicon, an index of 102 "Great Ideas". St. John's College rebuilt its entire curriculum around Great Books seminars in 1937 and maintains it today.

Contents and criticism

Canons vary, but typical lists include Homer, Plato, Aristotle, the Bible, Virgil, Augustine, Aquinas, Dante, Chaucer, Machiavelli, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Locke, Newton, Hume, Kant, Mill, Darwin, Marx and Tolstoy. Critics have long challenged the canon's near-exclusion of women and non-Western works, prompting expanded and competing lists, though defenders argue the point is not the list's politics but the practice of sustained, Socratic discussion of difficult primary texts.
