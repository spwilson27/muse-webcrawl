Length of day

The length of day (LOD) is the interval between two successive solar noons — effectively the period of Earth's rotation as measured relative to the Sun. Although nominally 86,400 seconds, the true length of day varies by milliseconds over days, seasons, and centuries, and it has lengthened substantially over geological time.

Variations

Precise measurements by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS), using techniques such as very-long-baseline interferometry and satellite laser ranging, show the LOD fluctuates on several timescales. Seasonal winds and ocean currents exchange angular momentum with the solid Earth, changing the LOD by up to about a millisecond over the year. The El Niño–Southern Oscillation produces further measurable variations, and large earthquakes — such as the 2004 Sumatra and 2011 Tōhoku events — slightly shortened the day by redistributing Earth's mass.

Long-term change

Tidal friction from the Moon transfers angular momentum from Earth to the Moon, lengthening the day by roughly 2.3 milliseconds per century and pushing the Moon about 3.8 cm farther away each year. Records of ancient eclipses, and growth bands in fossil corals and tidal rhythmites, show that around 620 million years ago a day lasted about 21.9 hours, and that the year then contained roughly 400 days. In the distant future the day will continue to lengthen.

Timekeeping

Because atomic time (TAI) and coordinated universal time (UTC) are uniform, while Earth's rotation is irregular and on average slowing, leap seconds have historically been inserted into UTC to keep it aligned with solar time (UT1). The accumulated difference, ΔT, is used in astronomy to reconcile the two timescales.
