Primal cut

A primal cut (also called a primal) is a piece of meat initially separated from the carcass of an animal during butchering. Examples of primal cuts include the round, loin, rib, and chuck for beef, and the ham, loin, Boston butt, and picnic for pork. Primals are the large wholesale sections into which a carcass is first divided; they are then further fabricated into subprimals and finally into retail cuts such as steaks, roasts, and chops.

Different countries and cultures make these cuts in different ways, and primal cuts also differ between types of meat. The British, American, and French systems of dividing a beef carcass, for instance, assign the same muscles to different named primals, so comparison between systems requires anatomical rather than name-by-name matching.

Pork primals

US pork primals are the Boston butt (upper shoulder), picnic shoulder (lower shoulder), loin, ham (hind leg), belly (source of bacon and spare ribs), and jowl. Lamb is commonly divided into shoulder, rack, loin, and leg, while veal follows a pattern similar to beef.

Use in the trade

Because primal and subprimal cuts in vacuum packaging keep far longer than retail cuts, modern meatpacking ships "boxed beef" and boxed pork — primals and subprimals sealed at the packing plant — to retailers, who perform only final fabrication. Specifications for primal cuts in the United States are standardized in the USDA's Institutional Meat Purchase Specifications (IMPS).
