Black power movement

The Black power movement or Black liberation movement was a political and social movement whose proponents believed in racial pride, economic empowerment and the creation of political and cultural institutions. It developed in the United States in the mid-1960s out of, and partly in reaction to, the civil rights movement, and remained influential into the 1970s.

Origins

The phrase "Black Power" was popularised by Stokely Carmichael (later Kwame Ture) and Willie Ricks in June 1966 during the March Against Fear in Mississippi, after Carmichael's arrest in Greenwood. Carmichael and Charles V. Hamilton set out the concept in their 1967 book Black Power: The Politics of Liberation. Earlier roots include the Nation of Islam, the teachings of Malcolm X, and Robert F. Williams's advocacy of armed self-defence.

Organisations and ideas

The Black Panther Party, founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale, became the movement's most visible organisation, combining community programmes such as Free Breakfast for Children with a Ten-Point Program demanding full employment, decent housing and an end to police brutality. The movement promoted Black cultural pride — the slogan "Black is beautiful", natural hairstyles, African-inspired dress — and inspired Black studies programmes in universities. The raised-fist salute of sprinters Tommie Smith and John Carlos at the 1968 Mexico City Olympics became an enduring symbol.

Decline and legacy

By the mid-1970s the movement had fragmented under internal divisions, state repression (including the FBI's COINTELPRO operations) and the assassinations and imprisonment of leaders. Its legacy includes increased Black political representation, cultural institutions, and continuing influence on later movements for racial justice.
