Codex Zouche-Nuttall

Codex Zouche-Nuttall
Created: 14th–15th century
Place of origin: Mixteca, Oaxaca, Mexico
Material: Deerskin, gesso
Pages: 47 leaves, 86 painted pages
Location: British Museum, London

The Codex Zouche-Nuttall, also known as the Codex Tonindeye, is a pre-Hispanic Mixtec pictorial manuscript, one of only a handful of Mesoamerican codices to survive the Spanish conquest. It is a screenfold manuscript of deerskin, painted on both sides, and records the dynastic histories of several Mixtec city-states in the region of modern Oaxaca.

Contents

One side of the codex presents the history of the city-states of Tilantongo, Jaltepec, and Zaachila, among others, from their mythical origins in the tenth century. The other side is dominated by the biography of the great eleventh- and twelfth-century ruler Lord Eight Deer "Jaguar Claw" (Iya Nacuaa Teyusi Ñaña), whose conquests, alliances, marriages, and eventual death in 1115 are depicted in extraordinary narrative detail.

History

The codex was reportedly found in the Dominican monastery of San Marco in Florence, though its route from Mexico to Italy is undocumented. In 1859 it was in the collection of Lord Zouche at Parham Park, Sussex. The anthropologist Zelia Nuttall published a facsimile of it in 1902 while working at Harvard's Peabody Museum; the manuscript is now named for both Zouche and Nuttall. It was acquired by the British Museum in 1917.

Interpretation

Much of the modern understanding of the codex is due to Alfonso Caso, who in the mid-twentieth century identified the historical figures and place signs in the manuscript. The Zouche-Nuttall is prized for the clarity and elegance of its drawing and for the richness of its genealogical information, which can be compared with archaeological evidence from sites such as Monte Albán and Zaachila.
