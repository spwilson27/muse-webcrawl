Poverty Point

Poverty Point is a prehistoric earthwork complex of Native American culture in northeastern Louisiana, built during the Late Archaic period, roughly 1700–1100 BC. Its concentric semicircular ridges and great mounds form one of the largest hunter-gatherer earthwork sites in North America, and it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Description

The site consists of six concentric, C-shaped earthen ridges opening toward the Bayou Maçon, formerly an old channel of the Mississippi River, together with several mounds, the largest of which (Mound A) rises about 22 metres (72 ft) and may have been built in a matter of months. The ridges are thought to have been the foundations of dwellings for a large resident or gathering population.

Culture and trade

Remarkably, this monumental construction was undertaken by hunter-fisher-gatherers rather than agriculturalists. Artefacts at Poverty Point include stone and minerals imported from distant sources — copper from the Great Lakes region, flint from the Ohio Valley, and steatite from the Appalachians — indicating a vast exchange network. The distinctive fired-clay "Poverty Point objects", probably used as heating elements in earth ovens, are a hallmark of the culture.

Recognition

The site is preserved as Poverty Point National Monument and a Louisiana state historic site. In 2014 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as the "Monumental Earthworks of Poverty Point".
