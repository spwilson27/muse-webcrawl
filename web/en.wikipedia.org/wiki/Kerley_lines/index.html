Kerley lines
Named afterPeter Kerley (1900–1979)
SpecialtyRadiology, cardiology
SignificanceThickened interlobular septa; interstitial pulmonary oedema

Kerley lines are radiographic signs seen on a chest X-ray when the interlobular septa of the lungs become thickened and visible, most commonly because of fluid accumulation in pulmonary oedema due to heart failure. They are named after the Irish radiologist Peter Kerley, who described them in the 1930s and 1940s while studying chest radiographs of patients with mitral stenosis.

Types

  • Kerley A lines are long (2–6 cm), thin, unbranching lines in the upper lobes, running obliquely from the periphery toward the hila. They represent distended interlobular septa and anastomotic lymphatic channels.
  • Kerley B lines are the best known: short (1–2 cm), thin horizontal lines at the lung periphery, especially at the costophrenic angles, perpendicular to the pleural surface. They represent thickened interlobular septa, classically from interstitial oedema in left ventricular failure or mitral stenosis.
  • Kerley C lines form a fine reticular network over the lung bases, produced by the superimposition of many thickened septa seen end-on and in cross-section.

Causes

Although most strongly associated with cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, septal lines can also appear in lymphangitis carcinomatosa, viral pneumonias, interstitial fibrosis, pneumoconiosis and other conditions that thicken the pulmonary interstitium. Their presence is used alongside signs such as cardiomegaly, pleural effusions and upper-lobe blood diversion (cephalisation) in diagnosing heart failure on plain films.
