The peanut butter and jelly sandwich, or PB&J, is a sandwich consisting of one or more layers of peanut butter and one layer of fruit jelly or preserves, served on sliced bread. It is one of the most iconic foods of the United States, especially associated with childhood and school lunches. Its rise was made possible by three early-20th-century food technologies — cheap processed peanut butter, commercial grape jelly (notably Welch's Grapelade, from 1918), and pre-sliced packaged bread (from 1928) — and its inclusion in American military rations in the Second World War spread the combination nationwide.

History

Peanut butter began as a health food: Dr John Harvey Kellogg patented a process for making peanut paste in 1895 and served it at his Battle Creek Sanitarium, and the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair popularised it. Pairings of peanut butter with pimento, cheese, or celery first appeared in society cookery columns, usually as dainty tea sandwiches. The earliest known reference to peanut butter with jelly is by Julia Davis Chandler in the Boston Cooking School Magazine in 1901, who suggested currant or crabapple jelly with peanut paste on thin bread — then still an upper-class novelty. The combination democratised during the Great Depression, when peanut butter was an inexpensive source of protein for families. During the Second World War, both peanut butter and jelly appeared in US soldiers' ration lists, and returning servicemen made the PB&J a household staple; post-war brands (Skippy, Jif, Peter Pan) and sliced white bread made it the default American child's lunch.

Preparation and variations

The classic construction spreads peanut butter on one slice of soft white bread and grape jelly on the other; spreading a thin layer of peanut butter on both slices first prevents the jelly from soaking through. Concord grape jelly is traditional, but strawberry jam is at least as popular, and the sandwich is endlessly varied: crunchy versus smooth peanut butter, crusts on or off, diagonal or rectangular cuts, and additions such as honey, sliced banana, bacon, or marshmallow fluff (the fluffernutter of New England). Grilled and deep-fried versions exist, and Elvis Presley's famed Fool's Gold Loaf — a whole loaf stuffed with peanut butter, jelly, and bacon — is an extreme descendant. Since 2000, Smucker's "Uncrustables" (sealed, crustless frozen PB&J rounds) have sold in the hundreds of millions. Typical nutrition for a sandwich on white bread is roughly 400–450 calories with 15–20 g of protein.

Cultural significance

The PB&J is a cultural shorthand for American childhood, simplicity, and comfort; it appears in the viral 2002 novelty hit "Peanut Butter Jelly Time" and in countless films and memoirs. Its very ubiquity has created a modern complication: peanut allergy, affecting around 1–2% of American children, has led many schools to adopt peanut-free tables or bans, and to the rise of sunflower-seed butter substitutes. Food writers also note the sandwich as a case study in industrialised food — a Depression-era thrift food turned mass-market icon whose three ingredients are all factory products.
