The Bibliotheca Corviniana was one of the most renowned libraries of the Renaissance world, created by King Matthias Corvinus of Hungary (r. 1458–1490). Housed in Buda Castle, it was among the largest collections of books in Europe, second only to the Vatican Library, and a central monument of early Renaissance culture north of the Alps.

Collection

At Matthias's death the library held an estimated 2,000–2,500 codices, encompassing classical Latin and Greek authors, Church Fathers, history, philosophy, science, law and contemporary humanist works. Many were sumptuous illuminated manuscripts commissioned from workshops in Florence, Naples and Milan, and from the royal workshop in Buda; they were bound in gilded leather or velvet and emblazoned with the king's arms — a raven (corvus in Latin, hence "Corviniana"). The librarian Taddeo Ugoleto oversaw acquisitions across Italy.

Dispersal and survival

After Matthias's death the library was neglected, and it was plundered when the Ottoman Turks captured Buda in 1526 and again in following decades; volumes were carried off to Constantinople or scattered as gifts and spoils across Europe. Today about 216 authentic Corvinian codices are known, held by libraries in Hungary (notably the National Széchényi Library), Austria, Italy and elsewhere. Thirty-five Hungarian-held volumes were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2005.
