Pholidota

Pholidota (pangolins)
Order: Pholidota
Family: Manidae
Genera: Manis, Phataginus, Smutsia
Species: 8 living species
Range: Sub-Saharan Africa and South/Southeast Asia
Conservation: All species threatened; listed in CITES Appendix I

Pholidota is the order of mammals comprising the pangolins (family Manidae), eight living species of scaly anteaters native to Africa and Asia. Pangolins are the only mammals wholly covered in scales, which are made of keratin — the same material as human fingernails and rhinoceros horn.

Description and behaviour

Pangolins range from the small long-tailed pangolin to the giant pangolin, which can exceed a metre in length. They have small heads, toothless jaws, powerful digging claws and extremely long, sticky tongues — anchored near the pelvis and sometimes longer than the body — with which they lap up ants and termites. Mostly nocturnal and solitary, pangolins defend themselves when threatened by rolling into a tight ball, presenting only their sharp-edged scales. The African species in Phataginus are largely arboreal; the ground pangolins (Smutsia) and Asian Manis species dig burrows or shelter in rock crevices.

Evolution and taxonomy

Molecular studies place Pholidota as the sister group of Carnivora, together forming the clade Ferae. Fossil pangolins and their stem relatives (palaeanodonts) are known from the Eocene of Europe and North America, including the well-preserved Eomanis from Messel, Germany.

Conservation

Pangolins are believed to be the most trafficked mammals in the world: their scales are used in traditional medicine and their meat is prized as a delicacy, and they are also hunted as bushmeat. All eight species are listed in CITES Appendix I, prohibiting international commercial trade, and range from Vulnerable to Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List.
