Amiens

Amiens is a city and commune in northern France, about 120 km (75 mi) north of Paris. It is the prefecture of the Somme department in the Hauts-de-France region and was the historical capital of Picardy. Situated on the Somme River, Amiens is renowned for its Gothic cathedral — the largest in France — and for the Hortillonnages, a network of floating market gardens threaded by canals.

History

The city began as Samarobriva ("bridge over the Somme"), chief town of the Gallic Ambiani tribe, and became an important Roman centre. During the Middle Ages Amiens grew rich on the woad trade and textile manufacturing. The Treaty of Amiens (1802) briefly settled peace between Britain and France during the Napoleonic Wars. The Mise of Amiens (1264) was Louis IX's arbitration in the English barons' dispute with Henry III.

In the First World War the Battle of Amiens (8 August 1918) opened the Allied Hundred Days Offensive — Ludendorff called it "the black day of the German Army". In the Second World War the city was occupied from 1940, and in February 1944 the French Resistance and RAF raid known as Operation Jericho bombed Amiens prison in an attempt to free condemned prisoners.

Amiens Cathedral

The Cathedral of Notre-Dame d'Amiens, begun in 1220, is the largest Gothic cathedral in France by internal volume and one of the purest examples of the Rayonnant style, with a nave 42.3 m high. It is famed for its sculpted portals, its labyrinth pavement, and the weeping angel of the ambulatory. It has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1981.

Other features

The Hortillonnages, some 300 hectares of market gardens interlaced with 65 km of canals, have been cultivated since Roman times and are toured by boat. Amiens is also the birthplace of writer Jules Verne, who lived in the city for many years, and of former French president Emmanuel Macron. Local specialities include macarons d'Amiens and ficelle picarde.
