Callaloo

Callaloo (also spelled calaloo or kallaloo) is a popular Caribbean vegetable dish and leaf vegetable. There are many variants across the region, but the name most often refers either to the leaves of the taro (dasheen) plant used as a cooking green, or to the thick soup made from those leaves, particularly in Trinidad and Tobago, where callaloo is regarded as a national dish.

The dish

Trinidadian callaloo is a Sunday-lunch staple made by simmering young dasheen (taro) leaves with okra, coconut milk, pumpkin, onions, chillies, herbs and seasonings, often with crab or salted meats such as pig tail added; the mixture is swizzled or blended to a smooth, dark-green consistency and served with rice, macaroni pie and stewed meat. In Jamaica, by contrast, “callaloo” usually means leaves of amaranth (often Amaranthus viridis), steamed or sautéed with onion, tomato, Scotch bonnet pepper and sometimes saltfish, and eaten at breakfast with fried dumplings or breadfruit. Guyanese, Grenadian and other island versions use various leafy greens — dasheen leaves, amaranth, or the leaves of tannia (Xanthosoma) — and proteins including crab, shrimp and salted meats.

Origins

The dish has roots in West African leafy soups and stews brought to the Caribbean by enslaved Africans, combined with Indigenous American ingredients and later South Asian influences in colonies such as Trinidad and Guyana. The word is thought to derive from African languages, though its precise etymology is disputed.

Culturally, “callaloo” is also used metaphorically in the Caribbean, notably in Trinidad, to describe a harmonious mixture of peoples and cultures.
