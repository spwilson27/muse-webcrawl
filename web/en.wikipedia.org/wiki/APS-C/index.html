Advanced Photo System type-C (APS-C) is an image sensor format approximately equivalent in size to the "Classic" frame of the defunct Advanced Photo System film format, roughly 25.1 × 16.7 mm with a 3:2 aspect ratio. In digital cameras, "APS-C" loosely denotes sensors around 23–24 mm wide used in consumer DSLRs and mirrorless cameras from Canon, Nikon, Sony, Fujifilm, and others; the exact dimensions differ slightly by manufacturer.

Crop factor

Because an APS-C sensor is smaller than a 35 mm ("full-frame") frame, the field of view of a given lens is narrowed by the crop factor — about 1.5× for Nikon, Sony, Pentax and Fujifilm sensors and 1.6× for Canon's slightly smaller variant. A 35 mm lens therefore frames like a ~52 mm lens on full frame. The smaller format allows smaller and cheaper cameras and dedicated lenses (such as Canon EF-S, Nikon DX, and Sony E-mount APS-C lines), and gives extra apparent "reach" for telephoto subjects, at the cost of somewhat shallower control over depth of field and typically more noise per pixel at equal pixel counts.

History

The format descends from the 1996 APS film system, whose C (Classic) frame was one of three selectable formats. When digital SLRs appeared, sensor production economics made APS-C-sized sensors the practical standard; early professional digital cameras such as the Nikon D1 (1999) used sensors in this class, and the format anchored consumer DSLRs for two decades before full-frame prices fell.
