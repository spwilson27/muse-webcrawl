Plateau–Rayleigh instability

The Plateau–Rayleigh instability, often just called the Rayleigh instability, explains why and how a falling stream of fluid breaks up into smaller packets with the same total volume but less surface area. It is named after Joseph Plateau and Lord Rayleigh.

Mechanism

Surface tension acts to minimise surface area, so a cylindrical jet is not the lowest-energy shape. Small perturbations of the jet's radius grow when their wavelength exceeds the jet's circumference: in the bulges the curvature — and hence the Laplace pressure — varies in such a way that liquid is driven from the necks into the bulges, amplifying the disturbance until the jet pinches off into droplets. The fastest-growing wavelength for an inviscid jet is about 9 times the jet radius, which sets the typical droplet size.

Examples and applications

The instability explains the breakup of water dripping from a tap, the beading of dew on a spider's web or a fine wire, and is exploited in inkjet printing, spray formation, and the production of uniform droplets and powders. A related effect governs the breakup of fluid films and ligaments in fuel injectors.
