The cornet is a brass instrument similar to the trumpet but distinguished by its conical bore, more compact shape, and mellower, rounder tone. It evolved from the post horn in France in the 1820s, when valves were applied to the coiled circular horn, and became one of the most popular solo and band instruments of the 19th century.

Construction and sound

The modern cornet has three piston (Périnet) valves, a predominantly conical bore that widens continuously toward the bell, and a distinctive "shepherd's crook" bend in the leadpipe on many models. Its conical bore produces a warmer, darker sound than the more cylindrical trumpet, blending well with other brass, at some cost to brilliance in the highest register. The most common cornet is in B♭; the smaller E♭ soprano cornet is a fixture of British brass bands, and C cornets were once common in orchestras.

History

Jean-Baptiste Arban, whose Grande méthode (1864) remains the standard tutor, established the cornet as a virtuoso solo instrument; soloists such as Herbert L. Clarke and the fictional "cornets" of Sousa's band were popular stars. Early jazz was led by cornetists — Buddy Bolden, King Oliver, Bix Beiderbecke and the young Louis Armstrong all played cornet before the trumpet took over in the 1930s. French orchestral parts by Berlioz, Bizet and Tchaikovsky call for cornets alongside trumpets.

Use today

The cornet's stronghold is the British-style brass band, where an entire section of B♭ cornets (with one E♭ soprano) carries the melodic role that violins hold in the orchestra. It also remains common in concert bands, traditional/Dixieland jazz, and as a first instrument for young brass students, owing to its compact size and comfortable hold.
