Judenrat

TypeAdministrative council imposed by Nazi Germany
Period1939–1945
LocationJewish ghettos in German-occupied Europe

Judenrat (plural Judenräte; German for "Jewish council") were administrative bodies that Nazi Germany forced Jewish communities to form in ghettos and occupied territories during the Second World War. Ordered by Reinhard Heydrich in September 1939, the councils were made responsible for carrying out German instructions within the ghettos.

Role and functions

The councils administered housing, food distribution, sanitation, labor conscription and policing inside the ghettos. Council members were placed in an impossible position: the Germans held them personally responsible for compliance, while the ghetto population often depended on them for survival.

Dilemmas and deportations

When deportations to extermination camps began, many councils were ordered to compile lists of deportees. Some leaders, such as Chaim Rumkowski in Łódź, pursued a strategy of cooperation in the hope of saving part of the population; others, such as Adam Czerniaków of Warsaw, took their own lives rather than comply. Historians continue to debate the councils' role, though most stress the coercion under which they operated.

Legacy

The Judenräte became a focus of postwar controversy, notably in Hannah Arendt's reporting on the Eichmann trial. Subsequent scholarship has generally emphasized the extreme constraints and lack of real choice faced by council members.
