The Porfiriato is the period in Mexican history from 1876 to 1911 during which General Porfirio Díaz dominated the country, serving as president for all but four years of that span. The era is characterised by political stability enforced by an authoritarian regime, rapid economic growth and modernisation driven by foreign investment, and widening inequality that ultimately provoked the Mexican Revolution.

Background

Díaz, a hero of the war against the French Intervention, seized power in 1876 under the Plan de Tuxtepec, proclaiming the principle of "no re-election". After a brief interval under Manuel González (1880–1884), Díaz returned to the presidency in 1884 and had the constitution amended to permit his indefinite re-election; he won seven consecutive elections.

Politics and order

Díaz governed through a combination of conciliation and repression ("pan o palo" — bread or the club). He centralised power, subordinated state governors and the press, built up the rural mounted police (the Rurales) and the army, and relied on a circle of technocratic advisers known as the Científicos, influenced by positivist ideas of order and progress.

Economy

The Porfiriato saw dramatic development: railway mileage grew from some 400 km to roughly 19,000 km, telegraphs and ports were built, mining and export agriculture boomed, public finances were stabilised under finance minister José Yves Limantour, and Mexico City was modernised with electric lighting, trams and grand avenues. Much of the growth was financed by British, American and French capital. Land laws favoured haciendas and surveying companies, dispossessing many village communities of communal lands; real wages fell for many workers, and labour unrest — suppressed at Cananea (1906) and Río Blanco (1907) — grew.

Fall

In 1908 Díaz told an American journalist that Mexico was ready for democracy and that he would retire, prompting Francisco I. Madero to organise an anti-re-electionist movement. When Díaz instead ran again in 1910 and jailed Madero, Madero called for revolt in the Plan de San Luis Potosí. The Mexican Revolution began in November 1910; Díaz resigned in May 1911 and went into exile in Paris, where he died in 1915.
