The Shahnameh (Persian: شاهنامه, romanized: Šāhnāme, lit. "The Book of Kings") is a long epic poem written by the Persian poet Ferdowsi between c. 977 and 1010 AD and is the national epic of Greater Iran. Consisting of some 50,000 couplets (bayts) — making it one of the world's longest epic poems and the longest written by a single author — the Shahnameh tells the mythical and to some extent historical past of the Persian Empire, from the creation of the world until the Muslim conquest of Persia in the seventh century AD.

Content

The poem falls into three parts: a mythical age of the first kings (Keyumars, Jamshid, Zahhak, Fereydun); a heroic age dominated by the champions of Sistan, above all Rostam, whose feats include his seven labours, his duel with Esfandiyar, and his unwitting killing of his son Sohrab — one of world literature's most famous tragedies; and a historical age covering the Arsacid and Sasanian dynasties, ending darkly with the Arab conquest and the death of Yazdegerd III. Ferdowsi drew on earlier prose and verse compilations, continuing the unfinished work of the poet Daqiqi, and on oral tradition of the kind studied by Milman Parry.

Significance

Written in a deliberately pure Persian with minimal Arabic vocabulary, the Shahnameh is central to the survival of the Persian language and to Iranian cultural identity; Ferdowsi is celebrated for reviving Persian after the Arab conquest. The poem was copied in splendid illustrated manuscripts — the Great Mongol (Demotte) Shahnameh and the Shahnameh of Shah Tahmasp are pinnacles of Persian miniature painting — and its stories are still recited by storytellers (naqqals) in Iran. Its millennium was celebrated in 2010, and Ferdowsi's tomb at Tus, near Mashhad, is a national shrine.
