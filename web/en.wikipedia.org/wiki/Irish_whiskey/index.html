Irish whiskey

Irish whiskey (Irish: fuisce or uisce beatha) is whiskey made on the island of Ireland. The word "whiskey" derives from the Irish uisce beatha, "water of life". Under Irish law and EU geographical-indication rules, Irish whiskey must be distilled and aged in wooden casks on the island of Ireland for at least three years. It is characteristically smooth, owing in part to widespread triple distillation.

Styles

  • Single pot still whiskey: made at a single distillery from a mash of malted and unmalted barley distilled in a pot still — a style unique to Ireland (e.g. Redbreast, Green Spot).
  • Single malt: from 100% malted barley at one distillery (e.g. Bushmills, Tyrconnell).
  • Grain whiskey: column-distilled, usually from maize or wheat, mainly used in blends.
  • Blended whiskey: a mixture of pot still/malt and grain whiskeys (e.g. Jameson, Powers, Tullamore D.E.W.).

History

Ireland was one of the earliest centres of whiskey distilling; the Old Bushmills Distillery claims a licence from 1608, and the Red Book of Ossory (c. 1324) contains an early reference to aqua vitae. In the nineteenth century Irish whiskey dominated world markets, but the industry collapsed in the early twentieth century through a combination of the Irish War of Independence and trade disputes with Britain, American Prohibition, and competition from blended Scotch. By the 1960s only a handful of distilleries remained, consolidated under Irish Distillers. Since the late 1980s the sector has revived dramatically, with sales growing strongly worldwide and dozens of new distilleries opening across the island.
