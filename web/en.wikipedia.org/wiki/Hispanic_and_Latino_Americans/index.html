Hispanic and Latino Americans

Hispanic and Latino Americans
Total population: about 65 million (c. 19–20% of the US population)
Largest ancestry: Mexican American
Languages: Spanish, English

Hispanic and Latino Americans are Americans of Spanish-speaking or Latin American origin and ancestry. Numbering roughly 65 million, they form the largest ethnic minority group in the United States — around a fifth of the total population — and the country's largest Spanish-speaking population makes the United States one of the biggest Spanish-speaking nations in the world.

Terminology

"Hispanic", adopted by the US federal government in the 1970s and used in the census from 1980, strictly denotes origin in a Spanish-speaking country, while "Latino" (and the feminine "Latina") denotes origin in Latin America, including Portuguese-speaking Brazil. The categories are ethnic, not racial: Hispanic Americans may be of any race. The gender-neutral alternatives "Latinx" and "Latine" have been promoted since the 2010s but are used by only a small minority of the population itself.

Composition and history

Mexican Americans are by far the largest group (about three-fifths of the total), followed by Puerto Ricans, Salvadorans, Cubans, Dominicans and Guatemalans. The community's roots run deep: Spanish settlements in Florida, New Mexico and California predate the United States, and a large Mexican population was incorporated by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848, after which successive waves of immigration — driven by labour demand, revolution, the Bracero Program, and economic and political upheaval across Latin America — built today's population, concentrated in California, Texas, Florida, New York and the Southwest.

Culture and influence

Hispanic Americans have shaped US music, cuisine, literature, sport and politics, from Tejano and salsa to reggaetón, from the farmworker movement of César Chávez to the appointment of Sonia Sotomayor to the Supreme Court in 2009. Spanish-language media, led by Univision and Telemundo, form a major sector of American broadcasting, and the Hispanic vote is a closely contested force in national elections.
