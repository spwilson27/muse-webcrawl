Concordat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Concordats are formal treaties between the Holy See and sovereign states

A concordat is a convention between the Holy See (the legal, juridical and internationally represented entity of the Pope and the Roman Curia) and a sovereign state that defines the relationship between the Catholic Church and the state in matters that concern both, i.e. the recognition and privileges of the Catholic Church in a specific country and with secular matters that impact on church interests.

History

The Concordat of Worms (1122) ended the Investiture Controversy by distinguishing spiritual and temporal authority in the appointment of bishops. Later famous concordats include the Concordat of Bologna (1516) between France and Pope Leo X, and the Concordat of 1801 between Napoleon Bonaparte and Pope Pius VII, which restored the Catholic Church in France after the Revolution while keeping it subordinate to the state.

In the 20th century, the Lateran Treaty of 1929 between Italy and the Holy See created Vatican City State, and the Reichskonkordat of 1933 with Nazi Germany remains controversial. Since the Second Vatican Council, concordats have typically emphasized religious freedom rather than privilege, and the Holy See has concluded agreements with states of many political systems, including post-communist countries in Europe.

Content

Concordats commonly regulate the legal status of the Church, appointment of bishops, religious education in public schools, chaplaincies to the armed forces, the civil effects of canonical marriage, and the Church's property and tax status. As international treaties, they are binding under international law, though their compatibility with constitutional principles of religious equality is sometimes debated.
