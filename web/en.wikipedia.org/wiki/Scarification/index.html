Scarification

Scarification involves scratching, etching, burning, branding, or superficially cutting designs, pictures, or words into the skin as a permanent body modification. In this process scars are formed deliberately by cutting or branding the skin; when the wound heals, raised keloid or regular scar tissue displays the design.

Traditional practice

Scarification has a long history in many cultures, particularly in parts of Africa, Australia, and the Pacific. Designs served as markers of identity — clan, tribe, age set, or social status — and as rites of passage into adulthood, records of achievement, or forms of beautification. In some cultures scarification was preferred over tattooing because raised light-coloured scars stand out more visibly on darker skin than ink pigment does. Ritual practice could also be connected with spirituality and with the marking of life stages from childhood to elderhood.

Modern practice

In contemporary body-modification communities, scarification is performed by cutting, skin removal, branding (strike or cautery), or abrasion. Because the results depend heavily on the individual's healing response, outcomes are less predictable than tattoos, and the practice carries significant risks of infection and excessive scarring; it is illegal for non-medical practitioners in some jurisdictions.

Terminology

The word should not be confused with scarification in horticulture (nick or treat a seed coat to aid germination) or soil scarification in forestry.
