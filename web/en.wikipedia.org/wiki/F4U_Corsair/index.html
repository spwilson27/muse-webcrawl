The Vought F4U Corsair is an American fighter aircraft that saw service primarily in World War II and the Korean War. Designed around the enormous Pratt & Whitney R-2800 Double Wasp engine and its 4-metre propeller, the Corsair used a distinctive inverted gull wing so that the landing gear could be kept short while still giving the propeller ground clearance. It was among the fastest piston-engined fighters of its era and the first American single-seat fighter to exceed 400 mph in level flight.

Design and development

The prototype XF4U-1 flew on 29 May 1940. Early carrier trials revealed poor low-speed handling and visibility over the long nose, so the US Navy initially assigned Corsairs to land-based Marine Corps squadrons; the Royal Navy's Fleet Air Arm was the first to operate them routinely from carriers, developing the curved approach technique that made deck landings practical. With a revised cockpit and other changes, the Corsair became a mainstay of US carrier air groups from 1944.

Operational history

From its combat debut at Guadalcanal in February 1943, the Corsair built an outstanding record in the Pacific, credited with an 11:1 kill ratio against Japanese aircraft. Japanese pilots reportedly nicknamed it "Whistling Death" for the sound of air over its wing-root intakes. In Korea it flew ground-attack missions in large numbers, and it remained in production until 1953 — the longest production run of any American piston-engined fighter. France used Aeronavale Corsairs in Indochina, Algeria and Suez, and the type's last combat came in the 1969 "Football War" between Honduras and El Salvador, often described as the last war fought by piston-engined fighters.
