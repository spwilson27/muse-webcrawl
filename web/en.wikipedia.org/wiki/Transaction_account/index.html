Transaction account

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transaction account (also called a checking account in the United States and Canada, or a current account in much of the rest of the English-speaking world) is a deposit account held at a bank or other financial institution that is designed for frequent, convenient access to funds on demand. Unlike a savings account, a transaction account typically allows an unlimited or large number of deposits and withdrawals, and is the primary account from which everyday payments are made.

Features

Transaction accounts are usually accessed by debit card, automated teller machines, electronic funds transfer, direct debit, standing orders, and — historically — paper cheques, from which the American name "checking account" derives. Because the balance is payable on demand and turns over quickly, such accounts traditionally pay little or no interest; some banks instead reward customers with fee waivers or ancillary services.

An overdraft facility is commonly attached, allowing the balance to fall below zero up to an agreed limit, with interest and fees charged on the borrowed amount.

Names and regional use

  • Current account — United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, India and much of the Commonwealth.
  • Checking account — United States and Canada.
  • Transaction account — a generic term used in Australia and New Zealand and in banking regulation.
  • Giro account — historically used in several European countries for payment-transfer accounts operated through banks or post offices.

History

Demand deposit accounts developed alongside early banking in 16th- and 17th-century Amsterdam and London, where goldsmiths issued transferable receipts for deposited coin. The spread of cheque clearing houses in the 19th century, salary payments by transfer in the 20th, and electronic banking from the 1970s onward progressively made the transaction account the centre of most people's finances. In many countries the great majority of adults now hold one, and governments increasingly pay wages, benefits and tax refunds directly into transaction accounts.

Consumer protection

Balances in transaction accounts are commonly covered by deposit insurance schemes up to a statutory limit (for example, $250,000 per depositor in the United States under the FDIC, and £85,000 in the United Kingdom under the FSCS). Regulation in many jurisdictions also governs overdraft fees, account switching, and the provision of basic bank accounts to customers who might otherwise be excluded.
