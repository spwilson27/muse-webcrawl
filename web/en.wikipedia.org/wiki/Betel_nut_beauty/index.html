Betel nut beauty

A betel nut beauty (Chinese: 檳榔西施, pinyin: bīnláng xīshī, "betel nut Xi Shi") is a young woman employed to sell betel nut and related goods from brightly lit glass roadside stalls in Taiwan. The phenomenon emerged in the 1960s and became conspicuous in the 1990s, when competition among stalls led some sellers to dress in revealing clothing to attract the largely male, working-class clientele — principally truck and taxi drivers — who chew betel nut as a stimulant.

The stalls

Betel nut stalls, often little more than glass booths outlined in fluorescent or LED lighting, line highways and truck routes across Taiwan. Beauties prepare and sell packets of betel nut with lime paste, as well as cigarettes and drinks, typically to customers who remain in their vehicles. The term borrows "Xi Shi", the name of one of the legendary Four Beauties of ancient China.

Controversy and regulation

The trade has drawn debate over sexual objectification, road safety (drivers distracted by stall displays and lighting), and the health harms of betel nut itself, which is a known carcinogen and is chewed by a substantial minority of Taiwanese men. In 2002 the Taoyuan County government introduced a dress code prohibiting sellers from exposing breasts, navels or buttocks, a policy other localities debated or imitated. Advocates counter that the rules stigmatise working-class women, many of whom choose the relatively well-paid work; researchers have also noted that most beauties dress unremarkably and that sensational coverage exaggerates the trade's character.

Cultural depictions

Betel nut beauties have become a distinctive icon of Taiwanese street culture, appearing in photography projects, documentaries and international reporting, and symbolising for some observers the island's mix of tradition, commerce and modern sexual politics.
