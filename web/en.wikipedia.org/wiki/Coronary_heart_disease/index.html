Coronary artery disease

Coronary artery disease
SynonymsCoronary heart disease, ischaemic heart disease
SpecialtyCardiology
SymptomsChest pain (angina), shortness of breath; sometimes none
ComplicationsHeart attack, heart failure, arrhythmia
Deaths~9 million per year (leading cause globally)

Coronary artery disease (CAD), also called coronary heart disease or ischaemic heart disease, is the narrowing of the coronary arteries that supply blood to the heart muscle, usually by atherosclerosis — the build-up of fatty plaque within the artery walls. It is the most common type of cardiovascular disease and the leading cause of death worldwide.

Atherosclerotic plaque narrowing a coronary artery.

Signs and symptoms

Reduced blood flow may cause exertional chest pain (angina pectoris), shortness of breath, or no symptoms at all. If a plaque ruptures and a clot completely blocks an artery, the result is a myocardial infarction (heart attack), which can cause permanent heart damage or death.

Risk factors

Major risk factors include high blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, high blood cholesterol (especially LDL), obesity, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, excessive alcohol, family history, and increasing age. Prevention focuses on modifying these risks: a healthy diet, regular exercise, not smoking, maintaining a healthy weight, and controlling blood pressure, lipids, and blood sugar.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis uses the history, electrocardiogram, blood tests (troponin after suspected infarction), stress testing, CT coronary angiography, and invasive coronary angiography. Treatment includes lifestyle change and medications — antiplatelets such as aspirin, statins, beta blockers, and ACE inhibitors. Severe disease may be treated with percutaneous coronary intervention (angioplasty with stenting) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).

Epidemiology

Ischaemic heart disease caused roughly 9 million deaths in 2019, around one in six of all deaths globally. Rates have fallen in many high-income countries since the mid-20th century thanks to prevention and treatment, while rising in much of the developing world.
