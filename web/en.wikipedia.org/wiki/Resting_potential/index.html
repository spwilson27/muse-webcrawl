Resting potential

The resting potential of a neuron is the membrane potential of the neuron when it is not transmitting a signal. By convention, the resting potential is taken to be about −70 mV, meaning that the inside of the cell is negative relative to the outside. The resting potential is determined by the concentration gradients of ions across the membrane and by the membrane permeability for each type of ion.

Mechanism

Potassium ions (K+) are at much higher concentration inside the cell, while sodium ions (Na+) and chloride are more concentrated outside. At rest, the membrane is far more permeable to potassium, because many potassium leak channels are open, so the resting potential lies close to the potassium equilibrium potential (about −90 mV) but is pulled somewhat positive by a small sodium leak. The sodium–potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase) maintains the gradients by expelling three sodium ions for every two potassium ions imported, consuming ATP.

Significance

The resting potential provides the electrochemical baseline from which action potentials are generated. When excitatory inputs depolarize the membrane to threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and an action potential fires. The value of the resting potential can be predicted by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation, which weights each ion's equilibrium potential by its relative permeability.
