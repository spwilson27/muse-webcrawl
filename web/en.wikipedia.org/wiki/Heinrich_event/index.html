The "Heinrich belt" of ice-rafted debris across the North Atlantic.

A Heinrich event is a natural phenomenon of the last glacial period in which large armadas of icebergs broke off from the Laurentide Ice Sheet and traversed the North Atlantic, dropping layers of coarse ice-rafted debris (IRD) onto the ocean floor. The events are recorded as distinctive sediment layers in marine cores, first described by German marine geologist Hartmut Heinrich in 1988 and linked to the northern ice sheets by Gerard Bond and colleagues.

Characteristics

Six principal events (H1–H6) occurred between roughly 60,000 and 16,000 years ago. The debris layers are rich in limestone and dolomite fragments sourced from Hudson Strait, indicating discharge from the Laurentide ice through that outlet. Freshwater and iceberg influx is thought to have weakened the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, cooling the North Atlantic region while the Southern Hemisphere warmed — the "bipolar seesaw." Heinrich events tend to occur near the end of the cold phases (stadials) of Dansgaard–Oeschger cycles.

Causes

The leading explanation is an internal oscillation of the ice sheet — the "binge–purge" model of Douglas MacAyeal — in which the ice bed warms geothermally until basal sliding triggers a surge of ice into the sea, after which the sheet rebuilds. External triggers involving ocean warming beneath ice shelves have also been proposed.
