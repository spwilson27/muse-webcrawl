Worse is better

Coined byRichard P. Gabriel
First described1989, essay "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big"
DomainSoftware design philosophy
Also calledThe New Jersey style

Worse is better, also called the New Jersey style, is a software design philosophy articulated by computer scientist Richard P. Gabriel in his 1989 essay "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big." It argues that software which is simple, even at the expense of completeness and correctness, will spread and succeed more readily than software designed to do "the right thing" in every respect.

The four design priorities

Gabriel contrasted two schools. In the "MIT/Stanford style" — the right thing — design must above all be correct, then consistent, then complete, with simplicity of implementation secondary. In the "New Jersey style" (associated with Bell Labs and the creators of Unix and C), simplicity of implementation is paramount, followed by correctness, consistency, and only lastly completeness. Simplicity of the interface is also subordinated to simplicity of implementation.

Argument

Gabriel argued that "worse" systems win because they are easier to write, easier to port, and easier for users to adopt; a system that is 50% as functional but simple and available now will spread virally, and can then be improved from a position of market success, whereas a perfect design that is hard to implement may never be finished or adopted. He cited Unix and C defeating more elegant competitors as evidence.

Criticism and legacy

Gabriel himself later complicated and partly retracted the argument, noting that "worse is better" can entrench poor designs. The essay nonetheless became a classic of hacker culture and is frequently invoked in debates over programming languages, protocols, and product design, often summarized by the slogan that a rough, working system beats an elegant, nonexistent one.
