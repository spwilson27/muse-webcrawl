United front

A united front is an alliance of groups against a common enemy or for a common goal. The term originated in communist strategy in the 1920s, when the Communist International (Comintern) encouraged communist parties to ally with socialist and other working-class organizations against capitalism and, later, fascism — while retaining their own independent organization and criticism of their allies. The slogan associated with the tactic was "march separately, strike together."

Origins

The united front policy was formally adopted by the Comintern at its Third and Fourth Congresses (1921–1922), after the failure of revolutionary uprisings in Germany and elsewhere. Leon Trotsky and other theorists developed the concept, arguing that communists should seek joint action with reformist-led unions and parties on concrete demands without dissolving into them. The tactic was contrasted with the "popular front" of the mid-1930s, which broadened alliances to include liberal and bourgeois-democratic parties against fascism.

In China

In China, the term describes two major alliances between the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and the Kuomintang (KMT): the First United Front (1924–1927), which collapsed in the Shanghai massacre of 1927, and the Second United Front (1937–1945) against Japanese invasion. In the People's Republic of China, the "United Front" also denotes the system, coordinated by the CCP's United Front Work Department, that brings together the eight minor political parties, religious bodies, business figures, and other non-party elites under CCP leadership; the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference is its institutional expression.

Other uses

Beyond communist politics, "united front" is used generically for broad coalitions — for example, national liberation movements and wartime alliances — in which groups with different long-term aims cooperate on a shared immediate objective.
