Nazism, formally National Socialism, is the ideology and set of practices associated with Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party (NSDAP) in Nazi Germany. It is a form of fascism characterized by dictatorial power, ultranationalism, racial hierarchy, and virulent antisemitism.

Core ideas

Central to Nazi ideology was the belief in a racial hierarchy headed by a so-called "Aryan master race", with Jews portrayed as an existential enemy. The doctrine of Lebensraum ("living space") held that Germany must expand eastward at the expense of Slavic peoples. The Führerprinzip demanded absolute obedience to the leader, and the Volksgemeinschaft ("people's community") was to subordinate individual and class interests to the racially defined nation. Nazism was anti-communist, anti-liberal and anti-democratic, while using the language of "socialism" to appeal to workers without challenging private property in practice.

Origins

Nazism drew on 19th-century völkisch nationalism, racial pseudoscience, Social Darwinism and the trauma of Germany's defeat in the First World War, the "stab-in-the-back" myth, and resentment of the Treaty of Versailles. Hitler codified his views in Mein Kampf (1925).

Consequences

In power from 1933 to 1945, the Nazi regime launched the Second World War in Europe and perpetrated the Holocaust, the genocide of six million Jews, alongside the mass murder of Roma, Poles and other Slavs, people with disabilities, political opponents, and other groups. Nazi ideology is banned or restricted in several countries, notably Germany, and remains the subject of extensive historical study.
