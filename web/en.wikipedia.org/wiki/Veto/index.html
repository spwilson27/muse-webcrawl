Veto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A veto is a legal power to unilaterally stop an official action — most often the enactment of legislation — held by a head of state, an executive, or a member of an international body. The word comes from Latin veto, "I forbid", recalling the Roman tribunes' power to block measures.

Legislative vetoes

Presidents in presidential systems commonly hold a qualified veto: the US President's veto can be overridden by two-thirds of both houses of Congress, and a "pocket veto" kills bills at session's end; some constitutions provide line-item or partial vetoes. In parliamentary systems, heads of state may formally refuse or delay assent — the Belgian king Baudouin's 1990 temporary "inability to reign" over an abortion law is the famous modern workaround — but the power is conventionally dormant where responsible government prevails. Legislative veto points also exist within chambers (committees, second houses, supermajority rules) and in federal second chambers.

International vetoes

The most consequential veto in international politics belongs to the five permanent members of the UN Security Council, whose negative vote blocks substantive resolutions; its repeated use over conflicts in Syria, Ukraine and Gaza has sustained decades of reform debate. Veto rights also structure the European Union's unanimity fields (foreign policy, taxation) and many corporate and constitutional arrangements.
