The Savoy Declaration is a confession of faith drawn up in 1658 by English Congregationalists meeting at the Savoy Palace in London. Its full title is A Declaration of the Faith and Order owned and practised in the Congregational Churches in England. Delegates from about 120 congregations met at the Savoy between September and October 1658, in the political uncertainty following the death of Oliver Cromwell.

History

The leading figures in drafting the Declaration were Thomas Goodwin and John Owen, two of the most prominent Independent divines of the Commonwealth period. The assembly was called partly to demonstrate the doctrinal respectability of Congregationalism at a moment when its political position was weakening.

Contents

The Declaration consists of a preface, thirty-two chapters of doctrine, and a section on church order known as the "Platform of Polity." In doctrine it follows the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) very closely, with modifications to the chapters on church government, the civil magistrate, and the sacraments. Where the Westminster Confession teaches a presbyterian ordering of the church, the Savoy Declaration teaches that each particular congregation is a complete church in itself, governed by its own officers under Christ.

Influence

The Savoy Declaration became the standard confessional document of English Congregationalism. It influenced later confessions, including the Cambridge Platform tradition in New England, and was substantially readopted in the Congregational Union's declaration of 1833 in modified form.
