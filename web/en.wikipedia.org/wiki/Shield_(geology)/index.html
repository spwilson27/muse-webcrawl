Shield (geology)

A shield is a large area of exposed Precambrian crystalline igneous and high-grade metamorphic rocks that form the ancient geological core of a continent. Shields are the exposed portions of cratons — the stable interior blocks of continental crust that have survived cycles of mountain building (orogeny) largely undeformed for hundreds of millions to billions of years. Where the craton is covered by flat-lying younger sediments it is called a platform; shield and platform together make up the craton.

Hero image: the glaciated Precambrian outcrops of the Canadian Shield.

Characteristics

Shield rocks are typically more than 570 million years old, and some are over 3.5 billion years old — among the oldest rocks on Earth. They consist largely of granite, gneiss, schist and greenstone belts, recording the fused remains of ancient volcanic arcs and microcontinents. Because shields have been tectonically quiet for so long, erosion has planed them into low-relief surfaces of lakes, bogs and thin soils. Their long stability and complex geology make them rich in mineral deposits, including gold, nickel, copper, iron ore and diamonds (from kimberlite pipes punched up through the deep cratonic roots).

Major shields

  • Canadian Shield — the largest, covering about half of Canada and parts of the northern United States, centred on Hudson Bay; famous for its lake-dotted, glacier-scoured landscape.
  • Baltic (Fennoscandian) Shield — Finland, Sweden, Norway and north-western Russia.
  • Ukrainian Shield and the Aldan and Anabar Shields of Siberia — together framing the East European and Siberian cratons.
  • Australian shields — the Yilgarn and Pilbara blocks of Western Australia, among the world's oldest crust.
  • African shields — the West African, Congo, Tanzanian and Kaapvaal cratons, the latter hosting the Witwatersrand gold fields.
  • South American shields — the Amazonian (Guiana and Central Brazil) shields.
  • Indian Shield — including the Dharwad craton of the Deccan.
  • Antarctic Shield — largely buried beneath the Antarctic ice sheet.

Significance

Shields preserve the record of Earth's earliest crust and the growth of the continents. Their deep lithospheric roots — extending 200 km or more into the mantle — are buoyant and strong, which explains why cratons resist destruction by subduction and rifting. Glaciation of shields during the Pleistocene scraped away soils and gouged the lake basins that now characterise regions such as Canada and Finland.
