Statin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Statins are a class of lipid-lowering medications that reduce cholesterol by inhibiting the enzyme HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis in the liver. The liver responds by pulling more LDL ("bad") cholesterol from the blood; statins typically lower LDL by 30–50% or more and are among the most widely prescribed drugs in the world — atorvastatin (Lipitor) became the best-selling prescription drug in history, and statins are credited with a substantial share of the decline in heart-attack and stroke deaths since the 1990s.

History and use

The first statin, mevastatin, was isolated from a Penicillium fungus by Akira Endo in Japan in 1976, following his insight that microbes might defend themselves with reductase inhibitors; Merck's lovastatin (approved 1987) opened the class, followed by simvastatin, pravastatin, atorvastatin (1996) and the fully synthetic rosuvastatin. Guidelines recommend statins for secondary prevention after cardiovascular events and for primary prevention at sufficient risk — a threshold repeatedly lowered, making hundreds of millions of people candidates. Trials show roughly a fifth to a quarter reduction in major vascular events per 1 mmol/L of LDL lowering, sustained over decades. Criticism persists around side effects — muscle pain (myalgia) is common, rhabdomyolysis rare, a small increase in diabetes risk — and over medicalising healthy people, nocebo effects (the SAMSON trial showed most reported symptoms also occur on placebo), and industry influence on guidelines. Even so, in the judgement of cardiology's mainstream, statins are second only to aspirin in cheap cardiovascular prevention, a cornerstone of modern medicine alongside the lifestyle measures their critics say they distract from.
