A neutrino detector is a physics apparatus designed to study neutrinos — electrically neutral, extremely weakly interacting elementary particles that usually pass through matter unimpeded. Because neutrino interactions are so rare, detectors must be very large, heavily shielded (typically deep underground to block cosmic rays), and use huge target masses, awaiting the tiny fraction of neutrinos that interact.

Types

  • Radiochemical detectors count atoms transmuted by neutrino capture, as in Raymond Davis's Homestake experiment (chlorine) and gallium detectors (GALLEX, SAGE), which first measured solar neutrinos.
  • Water Cherenkov detectors (Kamiokande, Super-Kamiokande, Hyper-Kamiokande) detect the cone of Cherenkov light from charged particles produced in interactions, yielding direction and energy in real time. IceCube uses a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice instrumented with photomultipliers.
  • Scintillator detectors (Borexino, KamLAND, Daya Bay, JUNO) use organic liquids yielding bright scintillation light, achieving low energy thresholds.
  • Heavy-water and tracking detectors: the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory used deuterium to distinguish neutrino flavours; liquid-argon time-projection chambers (MicroBooNE, DUNE) image particle tracks in fine detail.

Discoveries

Neutrino detectors detected the burst from supernova SN 1987A, the first (and so far only) high-statistics observation of neutrinos from beyond the solar system. By comparing solar and atmospheric neutrino flavours, detectors at Super-Kamiokande (1998) and SNO (2001–2002) established neutrino oscillation, proving neutrinos have mass — work recognized by the 2015 Nobel Prize shared by Takaaki Kajita and Arthur B. McDonald.
