Dry aging

Dry aging is the process by which large cuts of beef are aged for several weeks in a controlled, refrigerated environment before being cut into steaks and other portions. The process changes the flavour and texture of the meat through enzymatic breakdown of muscle fibres and connective tissue, and through moisture loss that concentrates flavour. Dry-aged beef is a specialty product associated with high-end steakhouses and butcher shops.

Process

Large primal or sub-primal cuts, typically rib, strip loin, or short loin sections, are stored unpackaged on racks or hung in a refrigerated room maintained at roughly 0.5–3 °C (33–38 °F) with relative humidity around 75–85% and constant gentle airflow. Aging periods commonly range from 14 to 45 days, with some speciality producers aging for 60, 90, or more days. During aging, naturally occurring enzymes such as calpains and cathepsins break down proteins, tenderising the meat and producing amino acids, including glutamate, that contribute savoury flavour.

Flavour

Dry-aged beef is often described as having concentrated, nutty, earthy, and sometimes blue-cheese-like flavours compared with fresh or wet-aged beef. Moisture loss can reach 10–30% of the original weight. The exterior crust of the cut hardens and may develop moulds; this outer layer is trimmed away before cooking, adding to the overall yield loss and high price of dry-aged meat.

Wet aging comparison

Unlike dry aging, wet aging stores meat in vacuum-sealed plastic bags, retaining moisture and preventing yield loss. Wet-aged beef is far more common in supermarkets and is generally milder and more metallic or bloody in flavour. Dry aging predates refrigeration and vacuum packaging and was the standard method of conditioning beef before the mid-20th century.
