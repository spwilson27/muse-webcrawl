Anti-communist resistance in Poland (1944–1953)

The anti-communist resistance in Poland, also known as the Polish anti-communist underground, was a guerrilla war fought between 1944 and 1953 by Polish resistance movements against the Soviet occupation of Poland and the communist puppet government installed in Warsaw. Former soldiers of the Home Army (Armia Krajowa) and other formations — later remembered as the "cursed soldiers" (żołnierze wyklęci) — continued armed struggle after the Second World War, refusing to accept Soviet domination.

Background

As the Red Army advanced into Poland in 1944, the Soviet-backed Polish Committee of National Liberation moved to disarm and suppress the Polish Underground State loyal to the government-in-exile in London. NKVD arrests, deportations, and show trials — including the Trial of the Sixteen — drove many resistance fighters back into the forests.

Course of the resistance

Organizations such as Freedom and Independence (WiN), the National Armed Forces remnants, and regional units under commanders like Hieronim Dekutowski ("Zapora") and Zygmunt Szendzielarz ("Łupaszko") attacked security posts, freed prisoners, and assassinated communist officials. Successive amnesties (1945 and 1947) drew many fighters out of hiding, but those who revealed themselves were often arrested. By the early 1950s, infiltration by the security service (UB) and exhaustion had destroyed organized resistance; the last known partisan, Józef Franczak ("Lalek"), was killed in 1963.

Legacy

Under communist rule the resistance was vilified as reactionary banditry. After 1989 the "cursed soldiers" were rehabilitated in public memory, and in 2011 Poland established a National Day of Remembrance for them on March 1.
