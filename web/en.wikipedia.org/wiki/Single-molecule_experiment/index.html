Single-molecule experiment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A single-molecule experiment is an experiment that investigates the properties of individual molecules, rather than drawing conclusions from measurements averaged over an ensemble of many molecules. The field grew out of developments in sensitive fluorescence detection and micromanipulation in the late twentieth century and is closely associated with single-molecule fluorescence spectroscopy and single-molecule biophysics.

Rationale

Ensemble measurements report population averages, which can hide important behaviour: molecules may switch between discrete states, follow different reaction pathways, or perform mechanical work in individual steps that are averaged away when billions of unsynchronised molecules are observed together. By following one molecule at a time, researchers can build up distributions of properties, observe transient intermediates, and watch dynamic processes — such as protein folding, enzyme turnover and molecular-motor stepping — unfold in real time.

Techniques

  • Single-molecule fluorescence — individual fluorophores are detected with confocal or total-internal-reflection (TIRF) microscopy. Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) measures distances of roughly 1–10 nm within a molecule.
  • Optical tweezers — a focused laser traps a microscopic bead attached to a molecule, allowing forces in the piconewton range and nanometre displacements to be measured, for example when stretching DNA or following a motor protein.
  • Magnetic tweezers — magnetic beads apply controlled force and torque to single molecules, well suited to studying DNA topology and topoisomerases.
  • Atomic force microscopy — single molecules can be imaged and mechanically unfolded by pulling with the AFM tip.
  • Single-molecule electrical measurements — nanopores and break junctions measure ionic or electronic currents through individual molecules, the principle behind nanopore DNA sequencing.
  • Super-resolution imaging — methods such as PALM and STORM localise individual fluorophores to build images below the diffraction limit.

History

Early milestones include the detection of single pentacene molecules in a crystal by Moerner and Kador (1989) and single-molecule fluorescence in solution in the early 1990s. In biology, optical-tweezer experiments in the 1990s measured the piconewton forces and 8-nanometre steps of kinesin motors and the elasticity of single DNA molecules, transforming the study of molecular machines. Work on single-molecule and super-resolution fluorescence methods contributed to the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William E. Moerner.

Applications

Single-molecule methods are used to study protein and RNA folding, enzyme kinetics, transcription and translation, chromatin dynamics, membrane-protein behaviour and amyloid aggregation, and they underpin technologies such as single-molecule real-time (SMRT) and nanopore sequencing.
