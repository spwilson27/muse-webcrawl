Tigre (ትግሬ təgre), also known in Sudan as Xasa (الخاصية, ḫāṣiya), is an Ethiopian Semitic language spoken by the Tigre people of western and northern Eritrea and parts of eastern Sudan. It is the second most widely spoken Semitic language in Eritrea after Tigrinya. Tigre is closely related to Tigrinya and to the ancient language Ge'ez, but it is a distinct language and is not mutually intelligible with Tigrinya in its spoken form.

Distribution and speakers

The Tigre people are predominantly Muslim agro-pastoralists living in the lowlands and foothills of western Eritrea, around Keren, Agordat, and Barentu, and across the border in Sudan's Kassala region. Tigre speakers number over a million. Many Tigre speakers are bilingual in Arabic, and in Sudan Arabic has largely displaced Tigre among some communities.

Linguistic features

Tigre retains the two pharyngeal consonants /ħ/ and /ʕ/ and a system of ejective consonants shared with other Ethiopian Semitic languages. Its syntax is subject–object–verb. Tigre has a particularly elaborate system of converbs and compound verbs, and its lexicon shows heavy Arabic influence owing to centuries of contact. Scholars regard Tigre, together with the nearly extinct Dahalik language of the Dahlak Archipelago, as the closest living relative of Ge'ez among the North Ethiopian languages, though it is not a direct descendant of classical Ge'ez.

Writing and literature

Tigre is written both in the Ge'ez (Ethiopic) script — used in Eritrea, where Tigre is taught in primary schools and used in newspapers and radio — and in the Arabic script among Muslim communities in Sudan. A body of oral literature, including poetry and folk tales, was recorded by European linguists such as Enno Littmann in the early 20th century.
