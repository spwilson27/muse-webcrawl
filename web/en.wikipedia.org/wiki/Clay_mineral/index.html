Clay mineral

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clay minerals are hydrous aluminium phyllosilicates (layer silicates), sometimes with variable amounts of iron, magnesium, alkali metals, and other cations. They form by the chemical weathering of silicate rocks and are the principal constituents of clay and of most soils and shales. Their tiny, plate-like crystals and layered structures give clays their characteristic plasticity when wet.

Structure and classification

Clay mineral structures combine sheets of silica tetrahedra with sheets of alumina octahedra. In 1:1 minerals such as kaolinite, one tetrahedral sheet pairs with one octahedral sheet; in 2:1 minerals such as illite, smectite (including montmorillonite and bentonite), and chlorite, an octahedral sheet is sandwiched between two tetrahedral sheets. Isomorphous substitution creates layer charge, balanced by interlayer cations and water; in smectites the interlayer swells with water, producing expanding clays.

Formation and occurrence

Clay minerals form through weathering and hydrothermal alteration of feldspars and other silicates, and they accumulate in soils, lake and marine sediments, and sedimentary rocks. Kaolinite typifies intense tropical leaching; smectites form in poorly drained, silica-rich environments; illite commonly forms during burial diagenesis of shales.

Uses

Humans have used clays since prehistory for pottery and bricks. Today kaolin is used in paper coating and ceramics, bentonite in drilling muds, cat litter, and foundry sands, and various clay minerals in pharmaceuticals, catalysts, and as barriers in landfills and for nuclear-waste isolation, owing to their sorption and swelling properties. Their high surface area and cation-exchange capacity also make them central to soil fertility.
