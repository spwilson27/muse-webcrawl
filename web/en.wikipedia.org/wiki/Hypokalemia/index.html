Hypokalemia

Hypokalemia is a low level of potassium (K+) in the blood serum. Mild low potassium usually causes no symptoms. More severe deficiency can cause fatigue, muscle weakness and cramps, constipation, abnormal heart rhythms (arrhythmias), and, in extreme cases, paralysis and respiratory failure. Normal serum potassium is 3.5 to 5.0 mmol/L; levels below 3.5 mmol/L define hypokalemia, and below 2.5 mmol/L is considered severe.

Causes

Hypokalemia results from inadequate intake (rarely the sole cause), loss from the body, or shifts of potassium into cells. Gastrointestinal losses from vomiting, diarrhoea, and nasogastric suction are common, as are urinary losses driven by diuretics, mineralocorticoid excess (such as primary aldosteronism), and certain kidney disorders. Low magnesium frequently accompanies and aggravates hypokalemia. Transcellular shifts occur with insulin, beta-adrenergic agonists, and alkalosis.

Signs and diagnosis

Electrocardiographic changes include flattening or inversion of the T wave, a prominent U wave, ST-segment depression, and prolongation of the QT interval; these predispose to arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes, particularly in patients taking digoxin or with heart disease. Diagnosis is by blood testing, with further investigation — urine potassium, acid–base status, magnesium, and blood pressure — to identify the cause.

Treatment

Treatment consists of potassium replacement, given orally for mild cases and intravenously (at carefully controlled rates) when deficiency is severe or symptomatic, alongside correction of magnesium deficiency and treatment of the underlying cause.
