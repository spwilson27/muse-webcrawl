Ricotta (Italian: [riˈkɔtta]) is an Italian whey cheese made from the whey left over from the production of other cheeses such as mozzarella and provolone. Its name means "recooked": the whey is heated again so that its remaining proteins — mainly albumin and globulin rather than casein — coagulate into fine, soft curds. Traditionally a way to waste nothing of the milk, ricotta is produced from sheep's, cow's, goat's or Italian water buffalo's whey, sometimes with a little added milk or cream.

Production

Whey is heated to near boiling (80–95 °C), often with a little acid (citric acid, vinegar or sour whey), whereupon delicate white flakes rise and are skimmed into perforated moulds to drain. The result is a fresh, perishable, mildly sweet cheese with a soft, slightly grainy texture, eaten within days. Sheep's-milk ricotta from Sicily and Sardinia is especially prized.

Varieties

Fresh ricotta is the base form; others include ricotta salata (pressed, salted and aged, firm enough to grate, from Sicily), ricotta forte (sharp, fermented, from Apulia), ricotta affumicata (smoked), and baked ricotta. Buffalo ricotta from Campania (ricotta di bufala campana) holds PDO status, as does Roman sheep's ricotta (ricotta romana).

Culinary uses

Ricotta fills pasta (ravioli, cannelloni), layers lasagne in Italian-American cooking, stuffs Sicilian cannoli and cassata, tops pizzas and crostini, and features in cheesecakes, pancakes and gnocchi di ricotta. Its role as a fresh curd cheese made by reheating whey parallels other whey cheeses worldwide; its soft white-curd character invites comparison with India's paneer, though paneer is pressed from whole milk curdled with acid rather than from whey.

Nutrition

Ricotta is relatively low in fat compared with aged cheeses (about 10–13% for whole-milk versions), provides whey protein of high biological value, and is a source of calcium.
