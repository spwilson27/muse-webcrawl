Horse racing is an equestrian performance sport, typically involving two or more horses ridden by jockeys (or sometimes driven without riders) over a specified distance for competition. It is one of the most ancient of all sports, as its basic premise — to identify which of two or more horses is the fastest over a set course or distance — has been mostly unchanged since at least classical antiquity.

History

Horse races were held in ancient Greece (including at the Olympic Games from 648 BC, with both mounted races and chariot racing), Rome, Babylon, Syria, and Arabia. Racing as an organised, wagered sport took modern shape in England: during the reign of Charles II, Newmarket became its centre, and in the 18th century the Jockey Club codified the rules. The three foundation sires of the Thoroughbred breed — the Byerley Turk, the Darley Arabian, and the Godolphin Arabian — were imported in this period. British colonists carried racing to North America, where the Kentucky Derby was first run in 1875.

Types of racing

In flat racing, horses gallop directly between two points around an oval track, at distances typically from 5 furlongs (1,000 m) to 2 miles (3,200 m). Jump racing (National Hunt racing in Britain and Ireland) is run over hurdles or the larger fences of steeplechases, at longer distances. In harness racing, horses trot or pace while pulling a driver in a two-wheeled sulky; in endurance racing, horse and rider cover cross-country distances of 50–100 miles. Pari-mutuel betting is integral to the economics of the sport in most countries, and major races — the Prix de l'Arc de Triomphe, the Melbourne Cup, the Japan Cup, the Breeders' Cup, and the Triple Crown series — are global sporting events.

Welfare and regulation

The sport is governed internationally through the International Federation of Horseracing Authorities and nationally by bodies such as the British Horseracing Authority and US state racing commissions. Recurring controversies concern the use of race-day medication (notably furosemide in the United States), whip rules, catastrophic injuries, the fate of retired racehorses, and the breeding of very large numbers of foals of whom only a minority race successfully.
