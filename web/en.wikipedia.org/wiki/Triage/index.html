Triage

Triage is the process of determining the priority of patients' treatments by the severity of their condition or likelihood of recovery with and without treatment. It is practised in emergency rooms, in the field during disasters, and on the battlefield, where scarce resources must be allocated to maximise the number of survivors. The term derives from the French word trier, meaning to separate, sort or select.

History

Modern triage is generally dated to the Napoleonic Wars, when the French military surgeon Dominique Jean Larrey introduced a system of sorting wounded soldiers by the urgency of their condition rather than by rank. Mass casualties in the two World Wars and the Korean and Vietnam Wars refined the system, and triage later became standard practice in civilian emergency medicine.

Simple triage

Simple triage divides patients into colour-coded categories. Those able to walk are typically tagged green ("minor"); those whose treatment can be delayed are tagged yellow ("delayed"); those who will survive only with prompt care are tagged red ("immediate"); and those likely to die despite care, or already dead, are tagged black. The START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) protocol used in the United States assesses respiration, perfusion and mental status using a 30-2-can-do rule.

Hospital triage

In hospital emergency departments, nurses assign an acuity level on standardised scales such as the five-level Emergency Severity Index (ESI) in the United States, the Manchester Triage System in Britain, the Australian Triage Scale, and the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale. Telephone triage is also used by health services to direct callers to appropriate care.

Ethics

Triage involves utilitarian judgements: resources are directed toward those most likely to benefit, which can conflict with the impulse to treat the most seriously injured first. During public-health emergencies such as the COVID-19 pandemic, triage protocols for ventilators and intensive-care beds attracted intense ethical debate.
