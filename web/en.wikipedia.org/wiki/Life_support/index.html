Life support

Life support comprises the treatments and techniques performed in an emergency in order to support life after the failure of one or more vital organs. Healthcare providers and emergency medical technicians are generally certified to perform basic and advanced life support procedures; however, basic life support is sometimes provided at the scene of an emergency by family members or bystanders before emergency services arrive. In the case of cardiac injuries, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is initiated by bystanders or family members 25% of the time. Basic life support techniques, such as CPR on a patient whose heart has stopped, can double or triple that patient's chance of survival. Other types of basic life support include relief from choking, first aid, and the use of an automated external defibrillator (AED).

Medical life support

In a medical setting, life support may include mechanical ventilation for respiratory failure, vasopressor drugs and intravenous fluids for shock, renal replacement therapy (dialysis) for kidney failure, artificial nutrition, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), which temporarily takes over the function of the heart and lungs. Advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) protocols guide the management of cardiac arrest with defibrillation, airway management and medications such as epinephrine.

Purpose

The purpose of basic life support is to save lives in emergencies. Breakdown of the circulatory system or the respiratory system can be fatal in a matter of minutes, so life support is needed to maintain perfusion of the brain and other vital organs until definitive treatment can restore spontaneous circulation and breathing.

Life-support systems in spaceflight

The term also describes environmental control and life-support systems (ECLSS) in spacecraft and submarines, which regenerate air and water and regulate temperature and pressure. The International Space Station's system recycles water from urine and condensate and generates oxygen by electrolysis.

Ethics

Decisions to withhold or withdraw life-sustaining treatment raise ethical and legal questions involving patient autonomy, advance directives, do-not-resuscitate orders, and the determination of brain death. Landmark cases, such as those of Karen Ann Quinlan and Terri Schiavo, shaped public debate and law in the United States.
