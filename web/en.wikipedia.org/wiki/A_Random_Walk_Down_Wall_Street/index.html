A Random Walk Down Wall Street

AuthorBurton Malkiel
Published1973 (W. W. Norton); 13th edition 2023
SubjectInvesting, efficient-market hypothesis

A Random Walk Down Wall Street is a 1973 book by Princeton economist Burton Malkiel. Popularising the random walk hypothesis and the efficient-market hypothesis for a general audience, it argues that stock prices cannot be reliably predicted and that most investors are best served by buying and holding low-cost, broadly diversified index funds rather than paying for active management or attempting market timing.

Contents

The book surveys the history of speculative bubbles (tulip mania, the South Sea Bubble, the 1929 crash, the "nifty fifty", and later the dot-com boom in updated editions), critiques technical analysis ("charting") and fundamental analysis, and presents academic evidence that professionally managed funds on average underperform their benchmarks after fees. Malkiel recommends life-cycle portfolios of index funds spanning domestic and international equities, bonds, and real-estate investment trusts, rebalanced periodically.

Reception and influence

The book has sold well over a million copies and gone through many revised editions (the thirteenth appeared in 2023, addressing cryptocurrencies, smart beta and ESG investing). It is credited with helping launch the index-fund era — the first retail index fund, Vanguard's, followed in 1976 — though critics dispute strict market efficiency and Malkiel himself allows that markets can be "a little" inefficient at times.
