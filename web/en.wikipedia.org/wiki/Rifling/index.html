Rifling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rifling is the term for helical grooves machined into the internal surface of a firearms barrel for imparting a spin to a projectile to improve its aerodynamic stability and accuracy. It contrasts with smoothbore barrels such as those of shotguns and early muskets. The raised portions of the rifling are called lands and the recessed portions grooves; collectively these grip the projectile as it travels down the barrel and rotate it around the bore axis.

Principles

A spinning projectile behaves like a gyroscope: spin stabilization keeps its axis aligned with its trajectory, preventing the tumbling that afflicts unspun elongated projectiles. The rate of spin required is expressed as a twist rate — for example one full turn in 10 inches of barrel travel (1:10). Too slow a twist under-stabilizes long, heavy bullets; too fast a twist can over-stress light bullets and increase fouling.

Manufacture

Modern rifling is produced by several methods: cut rifling, in which a single-point cutter or broach cuts each groove; button rifling, in which a hardened button is pushed or pulled through the barrel to displace metal; and hammer forging, in which the barrel is forged around a mandrel bearing the rifling profile. Polygonal rifling, used by some makers, replaces sharp lands and grooves with a smooth polygonal bore profile.

History

Spiral grooving appeared in German gunmaking around the late 15th and early 16th centuries. Rifled arms were long hampered by slow loading with tight-fitting balls, a problem largely solved in the 19th century by the Minié ball, whose hollow base expanded into the grooves. Rifling then became standard in military small arms and artillery.
