Duchy of Warsaw

The Duchy of Warsaw after its enlargement in 1809

The Duchy of Warsaw (Polish: Księstwo Warszawskie; French: Duché de Varsovie; German: Herzogtum Warschau) was a Polish state established by Napoleon Bonaparte in 1807 from Polish lands ceded by the Kingdom of Prussia under the Treaties of Tilsit. It was a client state of the French Empire, ruled in personal union by Frederick Augustus I of Saxony, and it endured until 1815, when the Congress of Vienna divided its territory between Prussia, Austria and the new Russian-dominated Congress Poland.

Creation and government

Napoleon deliberately avoided the name "Poland" to avoid provoking the partitioning powers, but the duchy restored a measure of Polish statehood twelve years after the partitions had erased the Commonwealth. Its constitution, dictated by Napoleon in Dresden in July 1807, introduced the Napoleonic Code, legal equality, and the formal abolition of serfdom (though peasants remained bound to the land in practice). The duchy was administered by a Council of State and Council of Ministers; its army, commanded by Prince Józef Poniatowski, was a significant contingent of Napoleon's forces.

Expansion and war

In 1809, after defeating Austria in the War of the Fifth Coalition, the duchy was enlarged with Polish lands from the Austrian partition, including Kraków and Lublin, reaching roughly 155,000 km² with about 4.3 million inhabitants. The duchy contributed some 100,000 troops to the French invasion of Russia in 1812 — the largest single contingent after France itself. After Napoleon's defeat, Russian troops occupied the duchy in 1813, and Poniatowski died at the Battle of Leipzig.

Legacy

Although short-lived and economically burdened by its military obligations, the Duchy of Warsaw restored Polish state institutions, a Polish army and the Napoleonic legal order in central Poland, and it remains a potent symbol of Polish national aspirations during the partitions era.
