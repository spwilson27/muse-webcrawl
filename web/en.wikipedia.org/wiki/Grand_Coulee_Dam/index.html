Grand Coulee Dam

Grand Coulee Dam and its spillway.

Grand Coulee Dam is a concrete gravity dam on the Columbia River in the U.S. state of Washington, built to produce hydroelectric power and provide irrigation water. It is the largest power station in the United States by generating capacity, with an installed capacity of about 6,800 megawatts, and forms Franklin D. Roosevelt Lake behind it.

Construction

The dam was constructed between 1933 and 1942 by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation as part of the Columbia Basin Project, initially under a plan dating to the 1920s to irrigate the arid Grand Coulee and Columbia Basin of central Washington. The original dam was completed during the Second World War primarily for power, supplying electricity to aluminum plants and the Hanford Site. A third powerplant, added between 1967 and 1974, greatly increased capacity. The dam is 550 feet (168 m) high and 5,223 feet (1,592 m) long, and contains nearly 12 million cubic yards of concrete.

Irrigation and effects

Pumping plants lift water from Lake Roosevelt into Banks Lake, from which it is distributed to irrigate roughly 670,000 acres (about 2,700 km²) of farmland. The dam has no fish ladder, and together with the Chief Joseph Dam downstream it blocks salmon migration to the upper Columbia; its reservoir also flooded lands of the Colville Confederated Tribes and Spokane Tribe, including burial grounds, leading to long-running compensation disputes.
