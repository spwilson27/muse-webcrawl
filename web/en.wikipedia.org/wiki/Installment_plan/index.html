Installment plan
A system of credit in which a purchase is paid for in a series of payments.

An installment plan, also called installment credit or hire purchase in some jurisdictions, is a method of buying goods in which the buyer takes possession of the item immediately but pays for it in a series of scheduled partial payments (installments) over time, usually with interest or a finance charge added. If the buyer defaults, the seller may repossess the goods under the terms of the contract and applicable law.

History

Installment selling expanded in the nineteenth century with furniture, pianos, and sewing machines — Singer was a notable pioneer in the 1850s. In the 1920s the system was applied on a mass scale to automobiles; General Motors Acceptance Corporation (founded 1919) made installment financing a central part of car retailing, and consumer durable purchases on credit underpinned the decade's consumer culture. Critics warned about over-indebtedness, and installment debt contracted sharply during the Great Depression. Post-war consumer credit, credit cards, and modern "buy now, pay later" services are descendants of the installment plan.

Mechanics

A typical plan requires a down payment followed by equal periodic payments covering principal and interest, with the title or a security interest retained by the seller or lender until completion. Consumer-protection laws in many countries require disclosure of the total cost of credit — for example, truth-in-lending rules in the United States and consumer credit legislation in the United Kingdom — and regulate repossession and cooling-off periods.
