Prose Edda

The Prose Edda, also known as the Younger Edda or Snorri's Edda, is an Old Norse textbook of poetry and mythology written in Iceland in the early thirteenth century (c. 1220) by the historian and poet Snorri Sturluson. It is the fullest and most systematic surviving source for Norse mythology, and it was composed as a handbook to teach young poets the myths and diction of traditional skaldic verse two centuries after Iceland had converted to Christianity.

Contents

The work consists of a prologue and three books:

  • Prologue: a euhemeristic account explaining the pagan gods as deified human heroes from Troy and Asia, adapted to Christian sensibilities.
  • Gylfaginning ("The Beguiling of Gylfi"): a dialogue in which the disguised Swedish king Gylfi questions three enthroned figures of Odin and learns the cosmology of the Norse world — its creation, the gods and goddesses, and its foretold destruction and rebirth at Ragnarök.
  • Skáldskaparmál ("The Language of Poetry"): a dialogue between the sea-god Ægir and Bragi on poetic diction, explaining the kennings of skaldic verse through the myths behind them.
  • Háttatal ("List of Verse Forms"): a clavis metrica of 102 stanzas composed by Snorri himself, demonstrating the metres and devices available to poets.

Manuscripts and influence

The Prose Edda survives in several medieval manuscripts, of which the principal ones are the Codex Regius, Codex Wormianus, Codex Trajectinus, and the Codex Upsaliensis. Because Snorri quotes extensively from older poems — many preserved only in his work — the Prose Edda is indispensable alongside the Poetic Edda for reconstructing Norse myth. It has shaped nearly all modern retellings of the Norse gods, from children's adaptations to contemporary novels and films.
