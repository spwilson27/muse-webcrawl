Lurianic Kabbalah

Lurianic Kabbalah is a school of Kabbalah named after its founder, Rabbi Isaac Luria (1534–1572), known as the Ari ("the Lion"). Developed in 16th-century Safed in Ottoman Galilee, it revolutionised Jewish mystical thought and, through its popular dissemination, reshaped Jewish spirituality, prayer and messianic expectation. Luria himself wrote almost nothing; his teachings were recorded by his chief disciple, Chaim Vital, principally in Etz Hayim ("Tree of Life").

Background

Luria's circle arose in Safed among exiles and descendants of exiles from the 1492 expulsion from Spain. The trauma of exile supplied the experiential ground for Luria's mythic system, in which cosmic catastrophe and repair mirror the historical exile and hoped-for redemption of Israel.

Primary concepts

Tzimtzum ("contraction") describes how the infinite God (Ein Sof) contracted His light to make a vacated space in which the finite world could come into being. Into this space a ray of light, the kav, entered, and the primordial man, Adam Kadmon, emanated the lights of the sefirot.

Shevirat ha-Kelim ("Breaking of the Vessels") recounts that the vessels meant to contain the divine light of the lower sefirot were overwhelmed and shattered. Sparks of holiness (netzotzot) fell and became trapped within husks or shells (kelipot), the realm of impurity and evil.

Tikkun ("repair" or "rectification") is the process of restoring the fallen sparks. Through prayer, mitzvot (commandments) performed with mystical intention (kavvanot), and ethical conduct, human beings liberate the sparks, repair the divine structure of the partzufim (divine "faces" or configurations), and hasten redemption. Unlike earlier Kabbalah, Lurianism made the fate of the cosmos depend on human action in a distinctly messianic key.

Influence

Lurianic ideas spread rapidly through the Jewish world in manuscript and print, transformed the prayer book (including the Kabbalat Shabbat service composed by Luria's contemporary Solomon Alkabetz), and prepared the ground for the messianic movement of Sabbatai Zevi in 1665–66, whose prophet, Nathan of Gaza, framed Zevi's mission in Lurianic terms. A century later, Hasidism internalised and democratised Lurianic themes. Lurianic Kabbalah remains the dominant framework of later Jewish mysticism and a major subject of modern scholarship, notably through the work of Gershom Scholem.
