Large for gestational age

Large for gestational age (LGA) is a term used to describe infants whose birth weight is above the 90th percentile for their gestational age and sex. An LGA newborn weighs more than about 90 percent of babies of the same gestational age. The related term fetal macrosomia usually refers to an absolute birth weight above 4,000–4,500 grams regardless of gestational age.

Causes

The most common cause of LGA is maternal diabetes mellitus, including gestational diabetes: elevated maternal glucose crosses the placenta, stimulating fetal insulin production, and insulin acts as a growth factor, increasing fat deposition and organ size. Other risk factors include maternal obesity, excessive weight gain during pregnancy, post-term gestation, multiparity, a previous LGA infant, and certain genetic overgrowth syndromes such as Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome. Some infants are constitutionally large because of tall, heavy parents without any pathology.

Risks and complications

LGA increases the risk of labour complications including shoulder dystocia, prolonged labour, caesarean delivery, and birth injury such as brachial plexus injury or clavicle fracture. After birth, infants of diabetic mothers are prone to hypoglycaemia as their high insulin levels persist while the maternal glucose supply is cut off. Other neonatal issues can include polycythaemia, hyperbilirubinaemia (jaundice), and respiratory problems. In later life, LGA infants — especially those of diabetic pregnancies — have increased risks of obesity and type 2 diabetes.

Diagnosis and management

LGA may be suspected from fundal height measurements and confirmed by ultrasound estimation of fetal weight, though ultrasound estimates near term carry a significant margin of error. Management centres on controlling maternal blood glucose, monitoring fetal growth, and planning delivery; induction of labour or planned caesarean may be discussed when estimated weight is very high. After delivery the infant's blood sugar is monitored closely.
