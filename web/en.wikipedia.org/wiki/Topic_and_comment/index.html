Topic and comment

In linguistics, topic and comment (also theme and rheme, or topic and focus) describe the information structure of a clause. The topic is what the sentence is about — typically given, familiar information — while the comment is what is said about the topic, typically new information.

Properties

The distinction, developed by the Prague School and by scholars such as Charles Li and Sandra Thompson, is pragmatic rather than purely syntactic: a topic need not be the grammatical subject, though in many languages they coincide. Topics tend to be definite, appear early in the clause, and set the frame within which the comment is interpreted. Li and Thompson's typology divides languages into subject-prominent (e.g., English), topic-prominent (e.g., Mandarin Chinese, Japanese, Korean), both, or neither; topic-prominent languages mark topics with particles such as Japanese wa and allow "double subject" constructions.

Examples

In "As for the soup, I haven't tried it yet", the soup is the topic and the rest the comment. In Mandarin, Zhèi kē shù, yèzi hěn dà ("this tree, [its] leaves are big") shows a topic distinct from any subject of the comment clause.
