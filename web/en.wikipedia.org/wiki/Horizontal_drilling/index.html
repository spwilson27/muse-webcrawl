Horizontal drilling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A horizontal wellbore profile

Horizontal drilling is a form of directional drilling in which a wellbore is drilled vertically to a kick-off point above the target formation and then curved so that it enters and runs within the target layer roughly horizontally. Any well inclined more than about 80 degrees from vertical is generally considered horizontal; such wells may run horizontally for one to several kilometres.

Technique

The well is steered using a downhole mud motor with a bent housing, or a rotary steerable system, together with measurement-while-drilling (MWD) tools that transmit the drill bit's inclination and azimuth to the surface in real time. The curve from vertical to horizontal is described by its radius — long, medium, or short — and modern wells typically use a build rate of roughly 8 to 12 degrees per 30 metres. Horizontal sections greatly increase the wellbore's contact with a thin reservoir compared with a vertical well through the same layer.

Applications

Horizontal drilling, combined with multi-stage hydraulic fracturing, underpinned the shale gas and tight oil boom in North America beginning in the 2000s, making formations such as the Bakken, Eagle Ford, and Marcellus economically productive. It is also used for offshore fields developed from a single platform, for wells beneath cities and environmentally sensitive areas, for geothermal energy, and in the trenchless construction sector, where horizontal directional drilling (HDD) installs pipelines and cables beneath rivers and roads.

History

Early horizontal wells were drilled in the Soviet Union and China in the mid-twentieth century, and short-radius techniques were used in the Austin Chalk in the 1980s. Advances in steerable motors, polycrystalline diamond compact bits, and MWD telemetry during the 1990s made long horizontal laterals routine.
