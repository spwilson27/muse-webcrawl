Allegory of the cave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The allegory of the cave, or Plato's cave, is an allegory presented by the Greek philosopher Plato in his work Republic (514a–520a) to compare "the effect of education (παιδεία) and the lack of it on our nature". It is written as a dialogue between Plato's brother Glaucon and his mentor Socrates, narrated by the latter.

The allegory

Socrates describes a group of people who have lived chained in a cave all their lives, facing a blank wall. The people watch shadows projected on the wall from objects passing in front of a fire behind them, and give names to these shadows. The shadows are the prisoners' reality, but are not accurate representations of the real world.

A philosopher, Socrates explains, is like a prisoner freed from the cave who comes to understand that the shadows on the wall are not reality. One prisoner is freed, and — at first dazzled and pained by the firelight and then by the sun — gradually perceives the true world: reflections in water, then objects themselves, then the heavens, and finally the sun, which he identifies as the source of the seasons and of all that is visible. When he returns to the cave to free the others, he is blinded by the darkness, and the prisoners, thinking his journey has harmed him, resist and would kill anyone who tried to drag them out.

Interpretation

The allegory relates to Plato's theory of Forms: the cave represents the world of appearances, the shadows the mere images of reality, and the sun the Form of the Good, which makes truth and knowledge possible. Together with the analogy of the sun and the divided line, it sets out Plato's epistemology and his account of the philosopher's difficult ascent to knowledge, as well as the philosopher's obligation — central to the Republic — to return and rule the city despite reluctance. The image has remained one of the most influential in Western philosophy, repeatedly invoked in discussions of perception, ideology, education and media.
