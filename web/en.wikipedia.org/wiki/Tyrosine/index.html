Tyrosine

Tyrosine
IUPAC name(S)-2-Amino-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propanoic acid
Other namesL-Tyrosine; Tyr; Y
Chemical formulaC9H11NO3
Molar mass181.19 g/mol
AppearanceWhite solid
Solubility in water0.45 g/L

Tyrosine (symbol Tyr or Y) is an α-amino acid that is used by cells to synthesize proteins. It is encoded by the codons UAC and UAU. It is a non-essential amino acid with a polar side group, found within almost all proteins in the human body and vital to many biological processes. The word "tyrosine" is from the Greek tyros, meaning cheese, as it was first discovered in 1846 by German chemist Justus von Liebig in the protein casein from cheese.

Tyrosine is a non-essential amino acid, meaning that it can be synthesized by the body from phenylalanine, another amino acid. It is therefore not strictly required in the diet, provided sufficient phenylalanine is available.

Functions

Apart from being a proteinogenic amino acid, tyrosine has a special role thanks to its phenol functional group. It occurs in proteins that are part of signal transduction processes, functioning as a receiver of phosphate groups that are transferred by way of protein kinases. Phosphorylation of the hydroxyl group can change the activity of the target protein, or form part of a signaling cascade via SH2 domain binding.

Tyrosine is also the precursor to several important biologically active compounds, including the catecholamine neurotransmitters dopamine, norepinephrine (noradrenaline), and epinephrine (adrenaline), as well as the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), and the pigment melanin.

Dietary sources

Tyrosine is found in many high-protein food products such as chicken, turkey, fish, milk, yogurt, cottage cheese, cheese, peanuts, almonds, pumpkin seeds, sesame seeds, soy products, and lima beans.

Biosynthesis

In plants and most microorganisms, tyrosine is synthesized via prephenate, an intermediate on the shikimate pathway. In mammals, tyrosine is derived from the essential amino acid phenylalanine, which comes from food; this conversion is catalyzed by the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase. A deficiency of this enzyme causes the disorder phenylketonuria (PKU).

Metabolism

Tyrosine is catabolized through a series of steps ultimately yielding fumarate and acetoacetate, making it both glucogenic and ketogenic. Genetic defects in tyrosine catabolism cause the hereditary tyrosinemias, of which type I is the most severe.
