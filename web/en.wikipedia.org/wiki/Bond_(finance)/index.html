Bond (finance)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A 19th-century bond certificate

In finance, a bond is a type of security under which the issuer (debtor) owes the holder (creditor) a debt, and is obliged to repay the principal (the amount borrowed) at a later date, termed the maturity date, along with interest (called the coupon). Bonds are used by companies, municipalities, states, and sovereign governments to finance projects and operations. Owners of bonds are debtholders, or creditors, of the issuer.

Features

A bond's key terms are set out in its indenture: the principal (face or par value), the coupon rate (fixed, floating, or zero), the maturity, and any options such as call provisions allowing early redemption, put provisions, or convertibility into shares (a convertible bond). Interest is usually paid semi-annually or annually. Because coupons are fixed while market prices move, a bond's yield — the return implied by its price — fluctuates inversely with price: bond prices fall when market interest rates rise, and vice versa; longer maturities and lower coupons amplify this interest-rate risk (duration).

Types

  • Government bonds — issued by national governments (e.g. U.S. Treasuries: bills, notes and bonds; British gilts; German Bunds); municipal bonds ("munis") are issued by regional and local authorities.
  • Corporate bonds — graded from investment grade down to high-yield ("junk") bonds according to credit ratings from agencies such as Moody's, S&P and Fitch.
  • Zero-coupon bonds — sold at a discount and redeemed at par, paying no periodic interest.
  • Inflation-indexed bonds — principal adjusts with inflation (e.g. TIPS, index-linked gilts).

Valuation and risks

The price of a bond equals the present value of its future coupon and principal payments, discounted at the market yield for comparable risk. Holders face credit risk (default), interest-rate risk, inflation risk and liquidity risk. Bondholders rank ahead of shareholders in a liquidation but behind secured creditors.

History

Transferable government debt emerged in medieval Italian city-states (Venice's prestiti) and developed in Amsterdam and London; bonds financed railroads, wars and industrial expansion in the 19th century. The global bond market today exceeds $100 trillion in outstanding debt — larger than the world's stock markets combined.
