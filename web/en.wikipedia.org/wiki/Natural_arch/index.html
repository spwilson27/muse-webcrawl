Natural arch

A natural arch, natural bridge, or (less commonly) rock arch is a natural landform in which an arch has formed with an opening underneath it. Natural arches commonly form where inland cliffs, coastal cliffs, fins or stacks are subject to erosion from the sea, rivers or weathering (subaerial processes).

Formation

Most natural arches are formed from narrow fins and sea stacks composed of sandstone or limestone with a steep, often vertical, cliff face. The formations become narrower due to erosion over geological time scales. The softer rock strata erode away creating rock shelters at the base of the formations on either side with the harder rock layer above forming a natural bridge between the two. Over time the opening enlarges until the arch eventually collapses, leaving a stack.

On coasts, arches form when headlands are eroded by wave action: waves compress air into cracks in the rock, widening them into caves, and when a cave breaks through the headland an arch results. On land, arches typically form by the weathering of exposed rock layers, often where a stream has undercut a wall of sandstone, as in the case of Natural Bridges National Monument in Utah.

Notable examples

  • Delicate Arch, in Arches National Park, Utah — perhaps the most famous natural arch in the world, depicted on Utah license plates.
  • Rainbow Bridge, Utah — one of the largest known natural bridges, spanning about 84 metres (275 feet).
  • Durdle Door, Dorset, England — a limestone arch on the Jurassic Coast.
  • Landscape Arch, Utah — among the longest natural rock spans in the world at roughly 88 metres (290 feet).
  • Azure Window, Malta — a limestone arch that collapsed into the sea in March 2017.

Terminology

Some sources draw a distinction between a natural bridge, which spans a watercourse or valley and is typically formed by water erosion, and a natural arch, formed by other weathering processes. In practice the terms are often used interchangeably.
