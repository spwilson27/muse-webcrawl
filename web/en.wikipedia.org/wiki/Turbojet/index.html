A turbojet is an airbreathing jet engine, typically used in aircraft. It consists of a gas turbine with a propelling nozzle. The gas turbine has an air inlet, a compressor, a combustion chamber, and a turbine (that drives the compressor). The compressed air from the compressor is heated by burning fuel in the combustion chamber and then allowed to expand through the turbine. The turbine exhaust is then expanded in the propelling nozzle, where it is accelerated to high speed to provide thrust.

The turbojet was the first type of practical jet engine. It powered the first jet aircraft, the Heinkel He 178, in 1939, and was developed independently by Frank Whittle in Britain and Hans von Ohain in Germany. Turbojets were widely used in early military and commercial jet aircraft but have largely been superseded by the more efficient turbofan, except in some missiles and high-speed applications.

History

The first patent for using a gas turbine to power an aircraft was filed in 1921 by Maxime Guillaume. In 1930, Frank Whittle filed his patent for a turbojet engine in England. Hans von Ohain began work on a similar design in Germany in 1935, and his engine powered the Heinkel He 178 on its first flight on 27 August 1939, the world's first jet-powered flight. Whittle's engine powered the Gloster E.28/39 in May 1941.

Design

Air is drawn into the rotating compressor via the intake and is compressed to a higher pressure before entering the combustion chamber. Fuel is mixed with the compressed air and ignited; the combustion increases the temperature and volume of the gas. The hot gases then pass through the turbine, which extracts energy to drive the compressor, before expanding through the exhaust nozzle to produce a high-velocity jet.

Key design parameters include the overall pressure ratio of the compressor, the turbine entry temperature, and the use of afterburners, which inject additional fuel into the exhaust stream to increase thrust for short periods.

Applications

Turbojets powered pioneering aircraft such as the Messerschmitt Me 262, the Gloster Meteor, the Lockheed P-80 Shooting Star, and early airliners including the de Havilland Comet. The Olympus turbojet, fitted with afterburners, powered the supersonic airliner Concorde. Because of their poor fuel efficiency at low speeds and high noise, pure turbojets are now rare in new aircraft designs.
