Self-consolidating concrete

Self-consolidating concrete (SCC), also called self-compacting concrete, is a highly flowable type of concrete that spreads into place under its own weight, fills formwork, and encapsulates reinforcement without mechanical vibration and without segregating. It was developed in Japan in the 1980s, notably through the work of Hajime Okamura at the University of Tokyo, in response to durability problems caused by poorly compacted concrete and a shortage of skilled labor.

SCC is characterized by a slump-flow spread of roughly 500–750 mm rather than a conventional slump.

Composition

SCC achieves its combination of fluidity and stability through mix design rather than extra water: a high content of fine materials (cement and fillers such as limestone powder or fly ash), polycarboxylate-based superplasticizers to disperse the cement particles, carefully graded aggregates with limited coarse content, and sometimes viscosity-modifying admixtures to prevent segregation and bleeding. The result is a low yield stress but adequately viscous mixture that can pass between closely spaced reinforcing bars.

Testing and properties

Workability is assessed by the slump-flow test (spread and T50 time), the J-ring and L-box tests for passing ability through reinforcement, and the V-funnel test for viscosity. Hardened SCC has mechanical properties and durability comparable to well-vibrated conventional concrete, with excellent surface finishes because no vibration-induced defects occur.

Applications

SCC is widely used in precast elements, heavily reinforced or architecturally exposed sections, tunnels, bridges, and repairs where vibration is impractical. Its disadvantages include higher material cost, greater sensitivity to mix variations, and increased formwork pressure because the fluid concrete exerts nearly hydrostatic pressure until it stiffens.
