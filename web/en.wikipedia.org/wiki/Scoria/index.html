Scoria

Scoria is a highly vesicular, dark-coloured volcanic rock formed from lava with many trapped gas bubbles (vesicles). It is produced during eruptions of basaltic to andesitic magma — most characteristically from cinder cones and Strombolian eruptions — when gas-rich lava is thrown into the air and solidifies quickly enough that the bubbles cannot escape before the rock hardens.

Properties

Scoria is typically black, dark brown, or reddish (oxidised) in colour, with a rough, clinkery surface. Its vesicles are larger and its walls thicker than those of pumice, and unlike pumice — which is felsic and light enough to float — scoria is mafic and usually sinks in water. Its bulk density is low for a rock because of the void space, often around 1 g/cm³ for loose fragments. Size ranges from lapilli (2–64 mm) to volcanic bombs and blocks greater than 64 mm.

Formation

As magma rises, dissolved gases such as water vapour and carbon dioxide exsolve into bubbles. In low-viscosity basaltic lava the fragments ejected from a vent cool in flight, preserving the frothy texture. Accumulations of scoria build cinder cones; extensive flows can form rough ʻaʻā lava surfaces whose clinkery top is sometimes called scoriaceous.

Uses

Scoria is quarried as lightweight aggregate for concrete and cinder blocks, as drainage material, for landscaping and decorative ground cover, in horticulture as a soil amendment and hydroponic medium, and for traction grit on icy roads. In ancient Rome, scoria and other lightweight volcanic materials were used to reduce the weight of concrete vaulting, notably in the dome of the Pantheon.
