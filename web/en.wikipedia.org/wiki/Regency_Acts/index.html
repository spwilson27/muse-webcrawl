Regency Acts

The Regency Acts are the Acts of the Parliament of the United Kingdom, a series dating from 1811 to the present day, that provide for a regent to exercise the royal functions of the British monarch when the monarch is under 18 years of age, incapacitated, or absent. Prior to 1937, regency arrangements were made by individual ad hoc Acts for each occasion; the Regency Act 1937 created the standing framework still in force, amended in 1943 and 1953.

The Regency Act 1937

The 1937 Act provides that if the sovereign is under 18, the next person in the line of succession aged 21 or over (18 since 1953) becomes regent until the sovereign comes of age. If the sovereign is incapacitated by infirmity of mind or body, a regency is established upon a declaration by at least three of specified persons (including the sovereign's spouse, the Lord Chancellor, the Speaker of the House of Commons, the Lord Chief Justice, and the Master of the Rolls), supported by medical evidence. The regent exercises the royal functions, subject to restrictions — notably, a regent cannot assent to bills altering the succession or the established church settlement in Scotland.

Earlier and later Acts

Earlier Regency Acts included that of 1811, which formalised the Regency of the Prince of Wales (later George IV) during George III's illness, giving the period its name, and Acts of 1751, 1765, 1830, and 1840 concerning potential minorities. The Regency Act 1943 lowered the age at which the sovereign's heir could act as counsellor of state or regent, and the Regency Act 1953 made special provision allowing Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, to act as regent for the children of Elizabeth II in preference to Princess Margaret.

Counsellors of State

The same Acts regulate the appointment of Counsellors of State, who deputise for the sovereign during temporary absence or illness, a function used more frequently than a full regency — which has never been declared under the 1937 Act.
