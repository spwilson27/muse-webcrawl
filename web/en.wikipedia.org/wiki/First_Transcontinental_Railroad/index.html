First transcontinental railroad

North America's first transcontinental railroad (known originally as the "Pacific Railroad" and later as the "Overland Route") was a 1,911-mile (3,075 km) continuous railroad line built between 1863 and 1869 that linked the existing eastern U.S. rail network at Council Bluffs, Iowa, with the Pacific coast at the Oakland Long Wharf on San Francisco Bay. The line was built by three private companies — the Western Pacific Railroad, the Central Pacific Railroad of California, and the Union Pacific Railroad — with federal support under the Pacific Railroad Acts of 1862 and 1864. Its completion revolutionised the settlement and economy of the American West, reducing coast-to-coast travel from months to about a week.

Construction

The Central Pacific, directed by the "Big Four" (Leland Stanford, Collis Huntington, Mark Hopkins, and Charles Crocker), built eastward from Sacramento across the Sierra Nevada, employing more than 10,000 Chinese immigrant labourers at its peak — workers who performed much of the most dangerous tunnelling and snow-shed work, including the Summit Tunnel at Donner Pass, in severe Sierra winters. The Union Pacific built westward from Omaha across the Great Plains and Rockies, employing largely Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans, and suffering attacks on the exposed plains.

Because federal subsidies were paid per mile, the two companies raced and even graded parallel roadbeds past each other before Congress fixed the meeting point. Record-setting feats included the Central Pacific's "Ten Mile Day" of 28 April 1869, when crews laid over ten miles of track in a single day.

Completion and impact

The line was joined at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on 10 May 1869, where Central Pacific president Leland Stanford drove the ceremonial golden spike (the "Last Spike"), completing the first transcontinental railroad. A telegraphed "DONE" signalled celebrations nationwide. Passenger and freight traffic transformed the West: cross-country travel fell from several months (by wagon or via Panama) to roughly a week, freight rates collapsed, and the railway accelerated the settlement of the plains, the dispossession of Native American nations, and the near-extermination of the bison. A through route to San Francisco proper was completed later in 1869 via connecting lines and ferry.

Financing and scandal

The project was financed by federal loans and vast land grants, and marred by the Crédit Mobilier scandal, in which Union Pacific insiders awarded themselves inflated construction contracts — a scandal that broke in 1872 and implicated members of Congress. The golden spike itself is now held by Stanford University, and the site is preserved as Golden Spike National Historical Park.
