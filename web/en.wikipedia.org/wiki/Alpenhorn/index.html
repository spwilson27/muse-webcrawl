The alpenhorn (German: Alphorn) is a traditional long wooden wind instrument of the European Alps, consisting of a straight, conical tube several metres long — commonly around 3.5 metres (11 ft) — ending in an upturned bell. It is made, traditionally, from spruce or pine wood hollowed and bound with bark or rattan, and is played with a cup-shaped wooden mouthpiece. Being a natural horn without valves or finger holes, it can produce only the pitches of the harmonic series.

History and use

Long wooden horns served Alpine herdsmen for centuries as signalling instruments: to call cattle, communicate across valleys, and mark the hours of the working day and evening prayer. The instrument's sound carries great distances in mountain terrain. With the decline of the traditional dairy economy, the alpenhorn became emblematic of Swiss and Alpine folk culture, performed at festivals, in compositions for alphorn ensembles, and as a symbol of Switzerland itself.

Classical composers have occasionally written for or imitated the instrument: the "Ranz des vaches" herding melodies associated with the alphorn are quoted by Rossini in William Tell and by Wagner and Beethoven, whose Pastoral Symphony evokes the alphorn's calls. Brahms wrote a famous alphorn theme in the finale of his First Symphony after hearing the instrument in the mountains.

Construction

Traditional makers select a young conifer growing bent at the base — the bend becoming the upturned bell — split and hollow the wood, glue the halves, and wrap the tube in bark or cane. Modern instruments are made in sections for transport, with metal ferrules and sometimes a tuning mechanism. Most Swiss alphorns are pitched in F♯/G♭, giving a tube length of about 3.47 m.
