Overview

Claudius Ptolemy (c. AD 100 – c. 170) was a Greek mathematician, astronomer and geographer who worked in Alexandria, Egypt, then part of the Roman Empire. His two great surviving works, the Almagest and the Geography, defined their subjects for well over a thousand years in Europe and the Islamic world.

Astronomy and geography

The Almagest set out the geocentric model of the universe — Earth stationary at the centre, the planets carried on epicycles — with mathematical rigour and tables accurate enough to dominate astronomy until Copernicus. It preserves and extends the work of Hipparchus, including his star catalogue, and computes throughout in sexagesimal.

The Geography listed some 8,000 places with latitude and longitude, explained how to project the spherical Earth onto a flat map, and discussed the errors of his predecessor Marinus of Tyre. Rediscovered in Latin Europe around 1400, it launched Renaissance map-making — though its coordinates, its too-small Earth and its enclosed Indian Ocean also misled later explorers, Columbus among them.
