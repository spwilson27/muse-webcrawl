Waaqeffanna

Waaqeffanna is the traditional monotheistic religion of the Oromo people of Ethiopia and northern Kenya, centred on belief in Waaqa (also spelled Waqa or Waaq), the supreme creator God, referred to as Waaqa Tokkicha, "the One God". Followers of the religion are called Waaqeffataa (or Waaqeffattoota in the plural). It is estimated that about 3% of the Oromo population practices Waaqeffanna today, though historically it was the predominant faith of the Oromo before the spread of Christianity and Islam from the 19th century onward.

Beliefs

Waaqa is understood as the sole creator of heaven and earth, omnipotent, omniscient, and benevolent. Waaqa is neither male nor female and cannot be depicted in images. The religion does not recognize Satan as an independent power; evil is attributed to human failure to follow Waaqa's order. Intermediary spirits called ayana are believed to connect humans with Waaqa and are associated with natural features such as rivers, mountains, and trees.

Central moral concepts include safu, respect for the sacred and for the balance of creation, and seera, the divinely given law governing society and nature. The qaalluu serve as the religious leaders and custodians of the faith, and historically played a role in the Gadaa system of Oromo governance.

Ceremonies

The best-known Waaqeffanna ceremony is Irreechaa, the annual thanksgiving festival held at the end of the rainy season, when worshippers gather at lakes and riverbanks — most famously at Lake Hora Arsadi near Bishoftu — carrying bundles of fresh grass and flowers to give thanks to Waaqa for the transition from the dark rainy season to the bright harvest season. Other ceremonies include Irreechaa Tulluu (mountain thanksgiving) and rites of passage marking birth, marriage, and death.

History

Waaqeffanna is among the indigenous religions of the Cushitic peoples of the Horn of Africa; related beliefs in a sky god named Waaq are found among the Somali, Borana, and other Cushitic groups. During the 19th and 20th centuries the faith declined under pressure from Ethiopian imperial expansion and the growth of Orthodox Christianity and Islam, but it has experienced a revival since the 1990s, alongside a broader Oromo cultural renaissance.
