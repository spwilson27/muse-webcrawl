Guinea (coin)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The guinea was a coin minted in Great Britain between 1663 and 1814 that contained approximately one-quarter of an ounce of gold. The name came from the Guinea region of West Africa, from which much of the gold used to make the coins was sourced by the Royal African Company, whose elephant-and-castle badge some issues bear.

History

Originally worth twenty shillings, the guinea's value floated with the price of gold, rising as high as thirty shillings before being fixed at twenty-one shillings in 1717 — an overvaluation of gold that, as Isaac Newton intended as Master of the Mint, helped drive Britain onto a de facto gold standard. Guineas were struck under every monarch from Charles II to George III, in denominations including the celebrated five-guinea piece and the "spade guinea" of 1787–1799.

The guinea as a unit of account

Long after the coin itself vanished, prices in Britain were quoted in guineas for professional fees, luxury goods, racehorses and bloodstock — a convention that persisted into decimalisation in 1971 and survives vestigially in horse racing (the 1,000 Guineas and 2,000 Guineas Stakes take their names from the original prize money) and in some auction sales.
