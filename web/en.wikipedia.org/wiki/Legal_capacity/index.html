Legal capacity

Legal capacity is a quality denoting either the legal competence of a person to hold rights and duties (the capacity to have rights) or the legal competence to exercise them (the capacity to act). As a concept it is central to private law: a natural person acquires the capacity to hold rights at birth, while legal persons such as companies acquire it on incorporation. The capacity to act — to enter contracts, marry, make a will or bring legal proceedings — is generally attained at the age of majority and may be limited by mental incapacity or legal order.

Natural persons

Children and minors have limited capacity: contracts entered by minors are generally voidable, with exceptions for necessaries and beneficial contracts of service. Adults who lack mental capacity, for example because of mental illness, brain injury or dementia, may have their affairs managed under regimes such as England and Wales's Mental Capacity Act 2005, which presumes capacity and requires that a person be assumed able to make a decision unless it is established that they lack capacity in relation to it.

Legal persons

For companies and other juristic persons, capacity is governed by their constitutional documents and the relevant companies legislation; the doctrine of ultra vires historically limited companies to acts within their stated objects, though modern statutes have greatly reduced its effect for outsiders dealing with the company in good faith.

International law

Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities requires states to recognise that persons with disabilities enjoy legal capacity on an equal basis with others in all aspects of life, and to provide access to supported decision-making rather than substituted decision-making wherever possible.
