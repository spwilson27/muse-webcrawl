The Cour de cassation (French: [kuʁ də kasɑsjɔ̃]; "Court of Cassation") is the supreme court of the French judicial (ordinary) court system, sitting in the Palais de Justice on the Île de la Cité in Paris. It is the court of final appeal for civil and criminal cases, standing at the head of the ordre judiciaire; the separate administrative court system is headed by the Conseil d'État. The court's task is not to retry cases but to ensure that lower courts have applied the law correctly — from casser, "to break" or quash, because it annuls judgments that rest on an error of law and remits the case for retrial.

History

The court descends from the Tribunal de cassation created in 1790 during the French Revolution, itself heir to the royal council's power to annul judgments contrary to law. It took the name Cour de cassation in 1804 under the Consulate. Its jurisprudence over two centuries has shaped French private law, and since the introduction of the question prioritaire de constitutionnalité (QPC) in 2010 it also filters challenges to the constitutionality of statutes, referring qualifying questions to the Constitutional Council.

Organisation and procedure

The court comprises six chambers — three civil chambers, a commercial chamber, a social chamber and a criminal chamber — together with a mixed chamber and a plenary assembly (assemblée plénière) for cases of particular importance or conflicting lines of authority. It is headed by the First President, the most senior judicial officer in France, assisted by the Prosecutor General, who heads the parquet général and delivers advisory opinions in the public interest. Most appeals (pourvois en cassation) are first screened by non-admission formations; full hearings are reserved for cases raising significant points of law. If the court quashes a judgment, the case is usually sent to a different court of appeal of equal rank (juridiction de renvoi); if that court defies the cassation ruling, the plenary assembly's second decision binds it on the point of law.

Role and output

The court receives roughly 15,000–20,000 appeals a year and publishes its leading decisions in its Bulletin and annual report, which frequently proposes legislative reforms. Its advisory procedure allows courts below to request its opinion on novel legal questions. Advocates before the court are a specialised corps, the avocats aux Conseils.
