Gaeltacht (plural Gaeltachtaí) is the Irish term for a district where the Irish language (Gaeilge) is, or was until recently, the principal spoken language of the majority of the local population. The Gaeltacht districts are legally defined areas, mostly scattered along Ireland's western seaboard in counties Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork and Waterford, with a small inland Gaeltacht at Ráth Chairn and Baile Ghib in County Meath. The largest and most strongly Irish-speaking area is Connemara in County Galway, including the Aran Islands.

Connemara, County Galway — the largest Gaeltacht region, where signs are in Irish.

Status and decline

Official Gaeltacht boundaries were first drawn in 1926, redefined more narrowly in 1956. Within them, Irish remains the community language to widely varying degrees: census figures show tens of thousands of daily speakers outside the education system, but sociolinguistic studies warn that in even the strongest districts English dominates among the young, and that without intervention Irish could cease to be a majority community language within a generation. Nineteenth-century emigration and the Famine devastated the Irish-speaking west, and the language boundary has been retreating for two centuries.

Support and culture

The state supports the Gaeltacht through Údarás na Gaeltachta, an elected development authority promoting employment that can sustain Irish-speaking communities; through TG4, the Irish-language television channel headquartered in Baile na hAbhann, County Galway (founded 1996); and Raidió na Gaeltachta (1972). Each summer thousands of schoolchildren attend Irish colleges (coláistí samhraidh) in Gaeltacht areas, a rite of passage that provides crucial income. The Gaeltacht Act 2012 introduced language planning areas, and the Gaeltacht is central to Irish cultural identity, preserving oral traditions, sean-nós singing and distinctive dialects in Ulster, Connacht and Munster Irish.
