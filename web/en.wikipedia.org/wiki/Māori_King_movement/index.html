Māori King movement

The Māori King movement, called the Kīngitanga in the Māori language, is a movement that arose among some Māori iwi (tribes) in the central North Island of New Zealand in the 1850s. It established a monarchy intended to stand as a counterpart to the British sovereign, halt the alienation of Māori land, and unite Māori under a single figure. The movement continues today, centred on Tūrangawaewae marae at Ngāruawāhia in the Waikato.

Origins

By the 1850s many Māori leaders feared that land sales to European settlers were fragmenting tribal holdings and eroding chiefly authority. Inspired partly by the Old Testament and by the example of the British monarchy, supporters canvassed several senior chiefs before the Waikato leader Pōtatau Te Wherowhero accepted election. He was crowned as the first Māori king in 1858 at Ngāruawāhia.

New Zealand Wars

The colonial government regarded the movement as a challenge to the Queen's sovereignty. In 1863 Governor George Grey invaded the Waikato, beginning the Waikato War; after defeat, Kīngitanga lands were confiscated (raupatu) and the second king, Tāwhiao, withdrew into the Ngāti Maniapoto territory that became known as the King Country, remaining there in exile until 1881. The confiscations were later acknowledged as unjust, and the Waikato-Tainui settlement of 1995 included a Crown apology.

Monarchs

The succession has passed through Pōtatau (1858–1860), Tāwhiao (1860–1894), Mahuta (1894–1912), Te Rata (1912–1933), Korokī (1933–1966), Te Atairangikaahu (1966–2006) — the movement's first queen and its longest-serving monarch — and Tūheitia (2006–2024). Following Tūheitia's death in August 2024, his daughter Ngā Wai Hono i te Pō was anointed as the eighth monarch.

Role today

The Kīngitanga holds no constitutional authority under New Zealand law, but the monarch serves as a unifying figurehead and advocate for Māori interests, hosting the annual Koroneihana celebrations at Tūrangawaewae and maintaining relationships with Pacific monarchies, including Tonga and the Cook Islands. The movement's motto is drawn from Tāwhiao's sayings, and its supporters — principally the Tainui confederation — regard the king or queen as the embodiment of Māori mana motuhake (self-determination).
