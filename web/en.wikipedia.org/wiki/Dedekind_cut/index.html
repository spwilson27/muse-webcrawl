Dedekind cut

Dedekind cut
Introduced byRichard Dedekind (1872)
PurposeConstruction of the real numbers from the rationals
WorkStetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen ("Continuity and Irrational Numbers")

In mathematics, Dedekind cuts, named after the German mathematician Richard Dedekind, are a method of constructing the real numbers from the rational numbers. A Dedekind cut is a partition of the rationals into two non-empty sets A and B such that every element of A is less than every element of B, and A contains no greatest element. The cut itself is then taken to be a real number: if B has a smallest element, the cut corresponds to that rational; otherwise it defines a new, irrational number.

Example: the square root of 2

Let A be the set of rationals whose square is less than 2, together with all non-positive rationals, and B the remaining positive rationals. No rational lies exactly on the boundary, because no rational squares to 2. The cut (A, B) therefore represents a "gap" in the rationals, which the construction fills with the irrational number √2.

Properties

  • The set of all cuts, ordered by inclusion of their lower sets, forms a complete ordered field isomorphic to the real numbers.
  • Arithmetic operations can be defined set-theoretically; for example, the sum of two cuts is obtained by adding their lower sets elementwise.
  • The construction guarantees the least-upper-bound property: every bounded set of reals has a supremum, which is precisely what the rationals lack.

Generalisations

The same idea underlies the Dedekind–MacNeille completion of a partially ordered set, and related constructions appear in the theory of surreal numbers and in locale theory. Dedekind published the construction in 1872, having conceived it in 1858 while teaching calculus in Zürich and seeking a rigorous foundation for the intuitive notion of continuity.
