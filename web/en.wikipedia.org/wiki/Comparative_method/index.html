Comparative method (linguistics)

In linguistics, the comparative method is a technique for studying the development of languages by performing a feature-by-feature comparison of two or more languages with common descent from a shared ancestor and then extrapolating backwards to infer the properties of that ancestor. Together with the method of internal reconstruction, the comparative method is the most important technique for establishing how languages were related in prehistory.

Procedure

The comparative method proceeds by assembling sets of cognates — words in different languages descended from the same ancestral word — and identifying regular sound correspondences between them. From these correspondences, linguists reconstruct the phonology, and parts of the morphology and vocabulary, of the unattested proto-language, marking reconstructed forms with an asterisk. Famous applications include the reconstruction of Proto-Indo-European from its daughter languages, and the formulation of sound laws such as Grimm's law, which describes the systematic consonant shifts separating Germanic from other Indo-European languages.

Criteria for relatedness

Genetic relatedness is demonstrated by shared innovations and systematic correspondences that cannot plausibly be explained by borrowing or chance. The method contrasts with lexicostatistics and glottochronology, which rely on statistical counts of basic vocabulary and are considered less rigorous.
