The caribou (known as the reindeer in Eurasia) is a species of deer, Rangifer tarandus, native to Arctic, subarctic, tundra, boreal and mountainous regions of North America, Europe and Asia. In North America, the name caribou — from the Mi'kmaq qalipu, "snow shoveller" — is used for wild populations, while reindeer is often reserved for domesticated herds.

Biology

Uniquely among deer, females as well as males grow antlers, which are shed annually. Caribou are adapted to cold climates with a thick double coat, broad hooves for walking on snow and swimming, and a nose that warms cold air. Many populations undertake some of the longest land migrations of any mammal, travelling thousands of kilometres between calving grounds and winter ranges.

Subspecies and conservation

Recognised forms include barren-ground, woodland, Peary and Grant's caribou. Several populations, notably boreal woodland caribou in Canada, have declined sharply due to habitat fragmentation, industrial development and increased predation, and are listed as threatened.

Relationship with humans

Reindeer herding is central to the cultures of Sámi, Nenets and other Indigenous peoples of the north. In North America caribou are an important subsistence resource for Inuit and First Nations communities.
