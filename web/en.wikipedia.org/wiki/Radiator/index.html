Radiator

Radiators are heat exchangers used to transfer thermal energy from one medium to another for the purpose of cooling or heating. Despite the name, most radiators transfer the bulk of their heat by convection rather than by thermal radiation. The term is applied to several quite different devices, including the cooling radiator of an internal combustion engine, the heating radiator of a building's central heating system, and radiator panels that reject heat from spacecraft.

Engine cooling

In automobiles and aircraft, a radiator cools the liquid coolant circulating through an internal combustion engine. Hot coolant passes through a core of flattened tubes fitted with thin fins; air flowing through the core — driven by the vehicle's motion or by a fan — carries the heat away. The cooled liquid then returns to the engine. A pressure cap raises the coolant's boiling point, and a thermostat regulates flow to keep the engine at its optimal operating temperature.

Building heating

In hydronic and steam central heating, radiators distribute heat from a boiler around a building. Hot water or steam circulates through panels, columns, or cast-iron sections, warming the air of the room by natural convection and radiation. The modern heating radiator is generally credited to Franz San Galli, who developed it in St. Petersburg in the 1850s. Modern panel radiators are usually pressed steel; thermostatic radiator valves allow room-by-room temperature control.

Electronics and spacecraft

Heat sinks in electronics are sometimes called radiators, as are the large deployable panels that reject waste heat from spacecraft — in the vacuum of space, radiation is the only available means of heat disposal, so these devices genuinely work by radiation.
