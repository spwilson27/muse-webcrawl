Pierre Clastres (17 May 1934 – 29 July 1977) was a French anthropologist and ethnographer. He is best known for his work on the political systems of the indigenous peoples of South America, and in particular for his thesis that many Amazonian societies were not stateless for want of development but actively organised themselves against the emergence of state power.

Life and fieldwork

Clastres studied philosophy at the Sorbonne and anthropology under Claude Lévi-Strauss. He conducted fieldwork in Paraguay among the Guayaki (Aché), and later among the Guaraní and the Yanomami of Venezuela, experiences recorded in his vivid ethnographic memoir Chronicle of the Guayaki Indians (1972). He became director of studies at the École pratique des hautes études. Clastres died in a car accident in 1977, aged 43.

Society Against the State

In a series of essays collected as La Société contre l'État (1974; English: Society Against the State, 1977), Clastres argued that the chiefs of many Amerindian societies wielded prestige without coercive power: obliged to be generous, to speak well and to give away goods, the chief was kept deliberately powerless by the group. Such societies, he claimed, possessed mechanisms — from warfare patterns to ritual — that warded off the concentration of authority, reversing the evolutionist assumption that the state is a universal destiny.

Influence

Clastres's work reshaped political anthropology and was taken up by thinkers including Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, and later by anthropologists such as David Graeber. Debate continues over how far his model generalises beyond the societies he studied.
