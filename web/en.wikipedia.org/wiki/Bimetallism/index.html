Bimetallism is a monetary standard in which the value of the monetary unit is defined as equivalent to fixed quantities of two metals, typically gold and silver, with an exchange rate between them fixed by law. Both metals are coined and circulate as legal tender, and either may be brought to the mint for coinage ("free coinage"). Bimetallic systems were common in the nineteenth century, including in the United States, France, and the Latin Monetary Union.

Problems of the fixed ratio

The central difficulty of bimetallism is that the legal ratio between the two metals rarely matches their market ratio. When it diverges, Gresham's law operates: the metal undervalued at the mint is hoarded or exported ("bad money drives out good"), and the system tends in practice to become monometallic in the overvalued metal. Large silver discoveries and Germany's adoption of the gold standard after 1871 flooded markets with silver, destabilising bimetallic systems; France and the Latin Monetary Union suspended free silver coinage in the 1870s, leaving a "limping standard".

The American free silver debate

In the United States, bimetallism became a major political cause. The Coinage Act of 1873 — denounced by silver supporters as the "Crime of '73" — ended free coinage of silver. Debt-burdened farmers and western silver miners demanded its restoration at a ratio of 16:1, arguing it would raise prices and ease debts; the Bland–Allison (1878) and Sherman Silver Purchase (1890) Acts were partial concessions. The issue climaxed in the 1896 presidential campaign, when Democrat William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech championed free silver; his defeat by William McKinley, followed by new gold discoveries, paved the way for the Gold Standard Act of 1900.
