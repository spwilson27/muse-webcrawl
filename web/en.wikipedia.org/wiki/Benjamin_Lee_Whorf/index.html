Benjamin Lee Whorf

Benjamin Lee Whorf (24 April 1897 – 26 July 1941) was an American linguist and fire prevention engineer. He is best known for the principle of linguistic relativity — the idea that the structure of a language affects its speakers' cognition and worldview — developed in association with his teacher Edward Sapir and commonly known as the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis.

Life

Whorf trained as a chemical engineer at MIT and spent his career with the Hartford Fire Insurance Company, where his investigations of fires led him to reflect on how language shapes behaviour — he observed, for example, that workers treated "empty" gasoline drums carelessly even though the drums' vapour made them more dangerous than full ones. Pursuing linguistics at Yale under Edward Sapir, he studied Hebrew, Nahuatl and the Hopi language of Arizona, conducting fieldwork with Hopi speaker Ernest Naquayouma.

Linguistic relativity

Whorf argued that grammatical categories — such as Hopi's treatment of time, which he contrasted with "Standard Average European" — embody different metaphysics and channel habitual thought. His writings, published posthumously in Language, Thought, and Reality (1956), became highly influential and controversial: strong readings of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis have been criticised and partially tested in psycholinguistics, while weaker versions (language influences, rather than determines, cognition) remain an active research topic.

Other work

Whorf contributed an influential decipherment-oriented study of Aztec (Nahuatl) writing, worked on the historical linguistics of Uto-Aztecan languages, and wrote speculatively on the connections between language, thought and his theosophical interests.
