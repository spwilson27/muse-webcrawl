Sex-determination system

Sex determination

A sex-determination system is a biological system that determines the development of sexual characteristics in an organism. Most organisms that reproduce sexually have two sexes; the mechanism by which sex is set may be genetic — governed by sex chromosomes or other genes — or environmental, depending on conditions during development.

Chromosomal systems

In the XY system, found in most mammals including humans, females carry two X chromosomes and males one X and one Y; the SRY gene on the Y chromosome triggers male development. Birds, some reptiles, and some insects use the ZW system, in which females are the heterogametic sex (ZW) and males are ZZ. Many insects use the XO system, where males have a single X and no second sex chromosome. Hymenopterans (ants, bees, wasps) use haplodiploidy: fertilised eggs become females, unfertilised eggs become haploid males.

Environmental systems

In temperature-dependent sex determination, found in many reptiles such as turtles and crocodilians, the incubation temperature of the eggs determines the sex of the offspring. Other environmental triggers include social environment (in some fish, sex changes in response to the social group), photoperiod, and nutrition.

Evolution

Sex-determination mechanisms evolve rapidly; closely related species may use different systems, and transitions between genetic and environmental determination are documented, for example in skinks. The diversity of systems is a major topic in evolutionary biology.
