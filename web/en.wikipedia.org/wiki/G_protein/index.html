G proteins, also known as guanine nucleotide-binding proteins, are a family of proteins that act as molecular switches inside cells, and are involved in transmitting signals from a variety of stimuli outside a cell to its interior. Their activity is regulated by their ability to bind and hydrolyse guanosine triphosphate (GTP) to guanosine diphosphate (GDP). When they are bound to GTP, they are "on", and when they are bound to GDP, they are "off". G proteins belong to the larger group of enzymes called GTPases.

Types

There are two classes of G proteins. The first function as monomeric small GTPases (such as Ras, Rho and Rab), while the second function as heterotrimeric G protein complexes, composed of alpha (Gα), beta (Gβ) and gamma (Gγ) subunits. The heterotrimeric proteins couple to G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), the largest family of cell-surface receptors and a major class of drug targets.

Mechanism

When a ligand activates a GPCR, the receptor acts as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor, prompting the Gα subunit to release GDP and bind GTP. The GTP-bound Gα separates from the Gβγ dimer, and both parts can regulate effector proteins such as adenylyl cyclase or phospholipase C, producing second messengers like cyclic AMP. The Gα subunit's intrinsic GTPase activity hydrolyses GTP back to GDP, switching the signal off; this hydrolysis is accelerated by RGS proteins.

Families and disease

Human Gα subunits fall into four families — Gs, Gi/o, Gq/11 and G12/13 — distinguished by the signals they relay. Cholera toxin permanently activates Gs by ADP-ribosylation, while pertussis toxin inactivates Gi; mutations in G proteins are implicated in cancers and endocrine disorders. The central role of G proteins was recognised by the 1994 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine awarded to Alfred G. Gilman and Martin Rodbell.
