Guler School

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Guler School is one of the principal schools of Pahari painting, the Rajput miniature painting of the Himalayan foothill kingdoms of northern India. Guler, a small state in the Kangra valley in present-day Himachal Pradesh, is often regarded as the birthplace of the celebrated Kangra style: the art historians who first identified it described Guler as the training ground from which painters carried a refined, naturalistic manner to the other hill courts in the later 18th century.

Development

Painting at Guler flourished under Raja Goverdhan Chand (1743–1773), who sheltered artists fleeing the Mughal capital after the invasions of Nadir Shah (1739) and Ahmad Shah Abdali. Trained in the late Mughal idiom, these artists — most notably the family workshop of Pandit Seu (c. 1680–1740) and his sons Manaku and Nainsukh — blended Mughal naturalism with local sentiment. Nainsukh, working for Raja Balwant Singh of Jasrota, is considered one of the greatest of all Indian miniaturists.

Style and subjects

Early Guler painting (c. 1720–1750) is lyrical and somewhat tentative; the mature phase (from about 1750) achieves the delicate line, cool palettes, atmospheric landscapes and tender treatment of love themes — especially Krishna subjects and illustrations to the Gita Govinda, Ramayana and Baramasa series — that define Kangra painting proper. Portraits of the Guler rajas and refined depictions of court life are also characteristic.

Legacy

By the end of the 18th century the centre of patronage had shifted to Kangra under Sansar Chand, whose court drew Guler-trained painters; the Guler idiom thus became the foundation of the most famous school of Rajasthani and Pahari art.
