William Eggleston (born July 27, 1939) is an American photographer. He is widely credited with increasing the recognition of colour photography as a legitimate artistic medium. His 1976 exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, curated by John Szarkowski, and the accompanying book William Eggleston's Guide, marked a turning point in the acceptance of colour work by the art world.

Life and work

Eggleston was raised in Memphis, Tennessee, and in the Mississippi Delta. He began photographing in his late teens, working first in black and white under the influence of Henri Cartier-Bresson and Robert Frank, before turning to colour in the mid-1960s. He became known for images of ordinary American Southern subjects — diners, petrol stations, supermarkets, interiors, and portraits — rendered with intense, saturated colour using the dye-transfer printing process.

His best-known photograph is often cited as The Red Ceiling (1973), shot in a friend's home in Greenwood, Mississippi. Later bodies of work include the portfolios Los Alamos (1965–74, published 2003), The Democratic Forest (1989), and large bodies of work made in Memphis and the surrounding South. In 1998 he received the Hasselblad Award.

Style

Eggleston has described his approach as "democratic": treating all subjects with equal attention rather than arranging them into hierarchies. His compositions frequently use central, frontal framing and oblique vantage points — famously including a tricycle photographed from ground level — that critics initially dismissed as snapshots before re-evaluating them as a radical new visual language.
