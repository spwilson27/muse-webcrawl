Metabolic network modelling

Metabolic network modelling is the computational reconstruction and simulation of the metabolic networks of cells and organisms, a central activity of systems biology. Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) encode the stoichiometry of every known metabolic reaction in an organism together with the gene–protein–reaction associations that link reactions to the genome, allowing the cell's metabolic capabilities to be predicted in silico.

From genome annotation to a predictive metabolic model

Reconstruction process

Building a model proceeds in stages: a draft reconstruction is generated automatically from the annotated genome and reaction databases; it is then manually curated against the biochemical literature; the network is converted into a mathematical (stoichiometric matrix) representation; and the model is tested by comparing its predictions — growth on defined media, gene essentiality, secretion products — with experimental data. Standard formats such as SBML and community resources such as the BiGG database promote reuse. Draft reconstructions are now also generated semi-automatically by tools like ModelSEED and CarveMe.

Constraint-based analysis

Because kinetic parameters are rarely known at genome scale, most modelling uses constraint-based methods that assume a steady state: the stoichiometric matrix S multiplied by the flux vector v equals zero, with bounds on each flux. Flux balance analysis (FBA) then uses linear programming to find flux distributions optimising an objective such as growth rate. Variants include flux variability analysis, parsimonious FBA, and methods integrating transcriptomic or other omics data. Applications include designing microbial strains for chemical production, predicting drug targets, modelling the human metabolism in health and disease (for example with the Recon and Human1 reconstructions), and studying microbial communities.
