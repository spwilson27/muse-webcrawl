Ganesha is one of the most beloved and widely worshipped deities in Hinduism, easily recognised by his elephant head and pot-bellied human body. Son of Shiva and Parvati, he is invoked at the beginning of undertakings, journeys, books and businesses as Vighnaharta, remover of obstacles, and honoured as patron of wisdom, learning and letters — tradition says he served as scribe of the Mahābhārata, writing it down at Vyāsa's dictation with his own broken tusk.

Myths

The best-known story of his birth tells how Parvati fashioned a boy from the paste of her bath and set him to guard her door; Shiva, returning and finding his way barred by a stranger, cut off the boy's head in anger, and to console Parvati replaced it with the head of the first creature found — an elephant. Other stories explain his mount, the tiny mouse, his love of the sweet modaka, and his victory over his brother Kartikeya in a race around the world — Ganesha simply circumambulated his parents, declaring them his world.

Worship

Largely non-sectarian, Ganesha is worshipped across Hindu traditions and beyond (in Jainism and some Buddhist contexts) before all other gods. His great public festival, Ganesh Chaturthi, was transformed in the 1890s by the nationalist leader Bal Gangadhar Tilak into a mass public celebration in Maharashtra, where vast clay images are processed and immersed in water — the pattern now repeated in cities across India and the diaspora. He is shown with one tusk, a snake for a belt, and in his four hands an axe, a lasso or goad, a lotus and his bowl of sweets.
