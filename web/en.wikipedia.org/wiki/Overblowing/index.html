Overblowing

Overblowing is a technique used in playing wind instruments in which the player, by increasing the speed of the airstream (and usually altering the embouchure), makes the instrument sound a pitch higher than the fundamental of the note fingered — jumping to a higher member of the harmonic series instead. It is how most woodwind instruments reach their upper registers.

Acoustics

A stopped or open air column supports standing waves at its resonance frequencies. In the lowest register the whole column vibrates at the fundamental; by overblowing, the player suppresses the fundamental and excites the second or a higher resonance. Instruments with a conical bore or an open cylindrical bore (flute, oboe, bassoon, saxophone) overblow at the octave, because their air columns support all harmonics. The clarinet, a cylindrical bore effectively closed at the reed end, supports only odd harmonics and therefore overblows at the twelfth (octave plus fifth), which is why its registers are separated by a large gap and why it needs a register key rather than an octave key. Flutes can be overblown through several harmonics, and brass players obtain entire series of notes by analogous changes of embouchure and air speed.

On the harmonica

In blues and jazz harmonica playing, "overblowing" has a specialised meaning: a technique, popularised by players such as Howard Levy, in which a draw reed in a blow hole is made to sound by a precisely shaped overblown airstream, producing a note a semitone above the draw note. Its counterpart, the overdraw, works on the upper holes. Together with ordinary note bending, overblows give the diatonic harmonica a fully chromatic range — at the cost of demanding breath control and usually some adjustment of the instrument's reed gaps.
