Lactase

Lactase (EC 3.2.1.108) is an enzyme produced by many organisms, best known in humans as the brush-border enzyme of the small intestine that is essential for digesting whole milk. It breaks down the disaccharide lactose into its constituent sugars, glucose and galactose, which can then be absorbed into the bloodstream. In humans lactase is encoded by the LCT gene on chromosome 2 and is identical to the enzyme lactase-phlorizin hydrolase, which also cleaves certain plant glycosides.

Biology

Lactase activity is highest in infants, for whom milk is the sole food. In most human populations — and in mammals generally — expression falls sharply after weaning, a trait called lactase non-persistence; undigested lactose then reaches the colon, where bacterial fermentation produces the symptoms of lactose intolerance. In several populations with a long history of dairying, mutations in an enhancer region within the neighbouring MCM6 gene keep the LCT gene switched on into adulthood (lactase persistence), one of the strongest known examples of recent positive selection and gene–culture coevolution in humans.

Deficiency

Congenital lactase deficiency, caused by disabling mutations in LCT, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder producing severe diarrhoea in newborns fed milk. Secondary deficiency can follow damage to the intestinal lining, for example in coeliac disease or gastroenteritis.

Industrial use

Commercial lactase, usually obtained from yeasts such as Kluyveromyces species or the fungus Aspergillus oryzae, is used to produce lactose-free milk, to make ice cream and yoghurt sweeter and less gritty by pre-hydrolysing lactose, and as an over-the-counter digestive supplement taken with dairy meals by lactose-intolerant consumers.
