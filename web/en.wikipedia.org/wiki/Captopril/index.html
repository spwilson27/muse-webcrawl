Captopril, sold under the brand name Capoten among others, is an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor used to treat high blood pressure, congestive heart failure, and kidney problems caused by diabetes. It was the first ACE inhibitor developed and is regarded as a landmark in rational drug design.

History

Captopril was developed in the 1970s by researchers at Squibb (David Cushman, Miguel Ondetti and colleagues), inspired by peptides in the venom of the Brazilian pit viper Bothrops jararaca that inhibit ACE. By modelling the enzyme's active site — by analogy with carboxypeptidase A — they designed a small orally active molecule, approved by the US FDA in 1980. Its success opened the way to later ACE inhibitors such as enalapril and lisinopril.

Medical use and side effects

Captopril lowers blood pressure by blocking conversion of angiotensin I to the vasoconstrictor angiotensin II and by reducing aldosterone secretion. Unlike its longer-acting successors it is taken two to three times daily. Common adverse effects include a persistent dry cough, taste disturbance, rash, and — class-wide — the risk of angioedema; it must not be used in pregnancy because of fetal toxicity. The SAVE trial (1992) showed captopril improved survival after myocardial infarction with left ventricular dysfunction.
