Monetary economics

[Image placeholder: money and central banking]

Monetary economics is the branch of economics that studies the different theories of money: it provides a framework for analyzing money and its functions as a medium of exchange, store of value, and unit of account, and it examines how money, for example in the form of a fiat currency, can gain acceptance purely because of its convenience as a public good. The discipline historically prefigured operational central banking and provides the theoretical underpinning for monetary policy.

Core concepts

Central questions in monetary economics include what determines the demand for money, how the supply of money is created by central banks and the banking system, and how changes in money affect prices, output, interest rates and employment in the short and long run. The quantity theory of money, expressed in the equation of exchange MV = PY, links the money stock to the price level; monetarists such as Milton Friedman argued that sustained inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."

Monetary policy

Modern central banks conduct monetary policy mainly by setting short-term interest rates and, since the 2008 financial crisis, by quantitative easing and other balance-sheet operations. Most now operate inflation-targeting regimes, aiming — in many countries — at consumer price inflation of around 2 percent per year. Monetary economics studies the transmission mechanism by which policy rates affect credit, asset prices, exchange rates and aggregate demand.

Schools of thought

Keynesian economics holds that monetary policy works through interest rates but may be ineffective in liquidity traps; monetarism emphasised control of money supply growth; new classical and real business cycle theories questioned the real effects of anticipated policy, while the monetary theory of fiscal policy and modern monetary theory dispute the primacy of central banking altogether. Whether money is neutral in the long run — affecting only nominal variables — remains a foundational question.
