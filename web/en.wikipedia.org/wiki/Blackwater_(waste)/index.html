Blackwater (waste)

Blackwater in a sanitation context is wastewater contaminated with faeces, urine and flushwater from toilets — that is, water from water closets and urinals, together with associated cleaning water. It is distinguished from greywater, which comes from sinks, showers, baths and washing machines and carries a much lower pathogen load.

Characteristics

Blackwater contains high concentrations of organic matter, nitrogen and phosphorus, and potentially pathogenic bacteria, viruses and parasites such as E. coli, Salmonella, norovirus and helminth eggs. Because of this risk it requires treatment or safe disposal before discharge or reuse, whereas greywater can often be reused after much simpler treatment.

Management and treatment

In sewered areas blackwater is mixed with other wastewater and conveyed to a sewage treatment plant, where screening, sedimentation, biological treatment and disinfection reduce pollution. Off-grid it is handled by septic tanks, pit latrines or composting toilets. Treated blackwater can yield biogas through anaerobic digestion, and its nutrients can be recovered as fertiliser; source-separation systems that keep blackwater apart from greywater make such reuse more efficient. Vacuum toilets and low-flush systems reduce the volume of blackwater produced.

Regulation and health

Discharge of untreated blackwater is prohibited in most jurisdictions because of the risk of waterborne disease and eutrophication. International maritime rules (MARPOL Annex IV) restrict sewage discharge from ships. Sanitation planning in developing regions often focuses on safely containing blackwater to protect groundwater and drinking-water sources.
