Meet-in-the-middle attack

A meet-in-the-middle attack is a cryptographic attack that trades increased storage for reduced time, typically against block ciphers built from two independent transformations. It is a form of time–memory trade-off first described by Diffie and Hellman in 1977.

Method

Against double encryption with two independent keys, a naive brute-force search costs 22n for n-bit keys. The attacker instead encrypts the known plaintext under all 2n first keys and stores the results, then decrypts the ciphertext under all second keys and looks for a match in the middle — reducing the work to about 2n+1 at the cost of 2n storage.

Consequences

The attack is why Double DES offers far less security than its key length suggests, and why Triple DES was standardised instead. Variants, including multidimensional meet-in-the-middle attacks, have been applied to other ciphers and hash functions.
