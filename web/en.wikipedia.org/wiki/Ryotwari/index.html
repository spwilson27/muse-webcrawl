Ryotwari

RegionMadras and Bombay presidencies, British India
Introduced1820s (Thomas Munro, Mountstuart Elphinstone)
TaxpayerThe individual cultivator (ryot)

The Ryotwari system was a land revenue system in British India in which the government settled revenue directly with the individual peasant cultivator, the ryot (from Arabic/Hindi ra'iyyat, "subject/peasant"), rather than with landlords or village communities. It was the principal system in the Madras and Bombay presidencies and parts of Assam and Berar.

Origins

First tried by Alexander Read in the Baramahal in the 1790s, the system was systematised by Thomas Munro, Governor of Madras (1820–1827), and extended to Bombay under Mountstuart Elphinstone and George Wingate. Unlike the Permanent Settlement of Bengal, which fixed revenue in perpetuity through zamindars, ryotwari assessments were periodically revised — typically every 20–30 years — after detailed field surveys.

Operation and criticism

The ryot was recognised as proprietor of his holding so long as he paid the assessment, which in the early decades often approached half the gross produce and could be demanded in cash regardless of harvest failure. Critics charged that excessive assessments, rigidity of collection and the costs of measurement drove peasants into debt to moneylenders; defenders argued the system avoided an exploiting intermediary class and encouraged improvement. The settlement operations' cadastral surveys and records of rights became a lasting legacy for land administration in India.
