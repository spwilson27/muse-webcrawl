Ethnocentrism

Ethnocentrism is the tendency to view one's own culture or ethnic group as central and superior, and to judge other cultures by its standards and values. The term was coined by the American sociologist William Graham Sumner in his 1906 book Folkways, where he defined it as "the view of things in which one's own group is the center of everything, and all others are scaled and rated with reference to it".

Concepts and theory

Sumner linked ethnocentrism to in-group loyalty and out-group hostility: a group that regards itself as superior tends to regard outsiders with contempt, a dynamic also studied in social identity theory and realistic conflict theory. In anthropology, ethnocentrism is seen as a key obstacle to understanding other societies, and Franz Boas and his students promoted cultural relativism — evaluating a culture by its own terms — as the methodological corrective. Consumer ethnocentrism, the preference for domestic over foreign products, is studied in marketing.

Consequences and criticism

Ethnocentrism is associated with prejudice, stereotyping, nationalism and, in extreme forms, xenophobia and ethnic conflict; colonial ideologies often rested on ethnocentric assumptions of civilisational superiority. At the same time, some scholars distinguish benign in-group pride from hostile ethnocentrism, and note that a degree of attachment to one's own group may support social cohesion. The opposite extreme — judging one's own culture exclusively negatively — is sometimes termed xeno-centrism. See also cultural relativism, nationalism and cognitive psychology.
