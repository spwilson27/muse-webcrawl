Jay Gould

Portrait of Jay Gould, railroad magnate of the Gilded Age

Jason "Jay" Gould (May 27, 1836 – December 2, 1892) was a leading American railroad developer and speculator, and one of the most notorious "robber barons" of the Gilded Age. Ruthless in business, he built a fortune through stock manipulation, control of railroads, and the Western Union telegraph empire.

Early career

Born in Roxbury, New York, Gould began as a surveyor and tanner before entering railroad speculation. In 1868–69 he and his associates James Fisk and Daniel Drew battled Cornelius Vanderbilt for control of the Erie Railroad, issuing fraudulent stock and bribing legislators. In 1869 Gould and Fisk attempted to corner the gold market, triggering the Black Friday panic of September 24, 1869, when the price of gold collapsed after President Ulysses S. Grant released federal gold.

Railroad empire

In the 1870s and 1880s Gould gained control of the Union Pacific and built a southwestern system including the Missouri Pacific and Texas and Pacific lines; at its peak he controlled some 16,000 km of track, a tenth of the nation's mileage. He also dominated telegraphy through Western Union and owned elevated railways in New York City. His use of strikebreakers made him a symbol of anti-labor capital; he allegedly boasted he could "hire one half of the working class to kill the other half."

Legacy

Gould died of tuberculosis in 1892 leaving an estate estimated at $72 million to his six children, including railroad executive George Jay Gould and socialite Anna Gould. Though reviled in his lifetime as the archetypal unscrupulous financier, later historians have noted his genuine operational skill in reorganizing and integrating railroad systems.
