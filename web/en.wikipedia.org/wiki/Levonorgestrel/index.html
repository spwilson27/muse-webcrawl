Levonorgestrel is a hormonal medication used in a number of birth control methods. It is a synthetic progestogen (a progestin) with effects similar to the hormone progesterone. It is used in combined and progestogen-only oral contraceptives, in hormonal intrauterine devices (IUDs), in contraceptive implants, and as an emergency contraceptive pill sold under brand names such as Plan B.

Medical uses

Emergency contraception

Levonorgestrel taken orally within 72 hours after unprotected intercourse significantly reduces the risk of pregnancy; its effectiveness is greatest the sooner it is taken. It works primarily by preventing or delaying ovulation and does not interrupt an established pregnancy. In the United States, one-dose levonorgestrel emergency contraception is available without prescription and without age restriction since 2013.

Long-acting contraception

Levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine systems (such as Mirena and Kyleena) release small amounts of the hormone into the uterus over periods of three to eight years depending on the product, and are among the most effective reversible contraceptives. Subdermal implants containing levonorgestrel or its successor etonogestrel provide several years of protection.

Side effects

Common side effects of the emergency contraceptive pill include nausea, changes in the timing of the next menstrual period, fatigue, and headache. Long-acting methods may cause irregular bleeding, especially in the first months of use, and IUD insertion carries a small risk of expulsion or uterine perforation.

History

Levonorgestrel was first synthesised in the 1960s and introduced for medical use in the 1970s. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
