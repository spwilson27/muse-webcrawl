Apophenia
Coined byKlaus Conrad (1958)
DefinitionPerceiving meaningful patterns in random or meaningless data
RelatedPareidolia; clustering illusion; synchronicity

Apophenia is the tendency to perceive meaningful connections or patterns between unrelated things. The term was coined in 1958 by the German psychiatrist Klaus Conrad, who used it to describe an early stage of psychosis in which patients experience the world as charged with abnormal significance directed at themselves.

Psychology

In modern usage apophenia covers a general feature of human cognition: pattern-seeking that errs toward false positives. Examples include seeing faces in clouds or objects (pareidolia), perceiving streaks or "hot hands" in random sequences, finding hidden messages in texts, and inferring conspiracies from coincidences. Researchers link heightened apophenia to schizotypy and to dopamine function, while regarding moderate pattern detection as essential to learning, science and creativity.

In culture and statistics

The concept is invoked in discussions of superstition, gambling fallacies, synchronicity and conspiracy theories. In statistics, its formal counterpart is the Type I error — rejecting a true null hypothesis — which procedures such as significance testing and correction for multiple comparisons are designed to control.
