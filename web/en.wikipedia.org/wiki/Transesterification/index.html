Transesterification

Transesterification is the process of exchanging the alkoxy group of an ester with that of an alcohol. The reaction is commonly catalysed by an acid or a base and is widely used in the chemical industry, including in the production of biodiesel and in the degradation or synthesis of polyesters.

Mechanism

In base-catalysed transesterification, the alcohol is deprotonated to give a strongly nucleophilic alkoxide, which attacks the carbonyl carbon of the ester. The resulting tetrahedral intermediate collapses, expelling the original alkoxide group of the ester and forming the new ester. Because the reaction is an equilibrium, it is usually driven to completion by using an excess of the alcohol or by removing one of the products.

Applications

The most important industrial application is the production of biodiesel: triglycerides from vegetable oils or animal fats react with methanol (methanolysis) to give fatty acid methyl esters and glycerol. Transesterification is also used to synthesise monomers, to produce certain polymers, and in the recycling of PET plastic, where glycolysis breaks the polymer down into recoverable monomers.

Biochemistry

In biochemistry, the term is also applied to reactions in which an ester bond is transferred without hydrolysis, such as the splicing of RNA by the spliceosome, in which two transesterification reactions excise an intron and join the flanking exons.
