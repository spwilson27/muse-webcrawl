Expert

An expert is a person with extensive knowledge or ability in a particular subject or field, typically gained through training, study, and prolonged practical experience. Experts are recognised by their peers and by laypeople as reliable sources of judgment within their domain, and they are frequently called upon to give opinions in courts of law, in the media, in government, and in professional practice. The corresponding state or quality is called expertise.

The concept of the expert is closely connected to ideas of skill, tacit knowledge, and the social processes by which communities decide whose knowledge counts. Scholars of expertise distinguish between contributory expertise — the ability to actually do the things a domain requires — and interactional expertise, the ability to talk fluently about a domain without necessarily being able to practise it.

Definitions and characteristics

An expert is commonly defined as a person whose performance in a domain is consistently superior to that of most others. Psychologists who study expertise, notably K. Anders Ericsson and colleagues, have emphasised the role of deliberate practice: sustained, effortful training with feedback, usually over many years, often summarised in popular accounts as the "ten thousand hours" rule. Deliberate practice differs from mere repetition in that it targets specific weaknesses just beyond the learner's current ability.

Experts typically possess highly organised knowledge structures that allow them to recognise patterns quickly. Chess masters, for example, perceive positions in terms of meaningful chunks rather than individual pieces. Experts also tend to monitor their own performance and to know the limits of their knowledge, although confidence and competence do not always coincide — a mismatch explored by the Dunning–Kruger effect.

Expertise in society

Modern societies depend heavily on experts: physicians, engineers, scientists, lawyers, and financial specialists all make decisions that laypeople cannot easily evaluate. This dependence creates problems of trust: citizens must decide which experts to believe, a problem that has become politically charged in debates over climate change, vaccines, and economic policy. Sociologists such as Harry Collins and Robert Evans have proposed a "Third Wave" of science studies that treats expertise as a real and analysable property rather than dissolving it into pure social negotiation.

Expert witnesses

In legal systems, an expert witness is permitted to give opinion evidence, unlike ordinary witnesses who may testify only to facts. Courts assess expert testimony under standards such as the Frye standard and, in the United States, the Daubert standard, which require that methods be testable, peer-reviewed, and generally accepted.

Criticism

Expertise is not infallible. Studies of clinical and political judgment have repeatedly found that simple statistical models can outperform experienced practitioners in some predictive tasks, and experts are subject to overconfidence and bias like everyone else. Philosophers have also asked whether artificial intelligence systems — expert systems in the twentieth century and large language models in the twenty-first — can themselves be experts, blurring the line between tool and authority.
