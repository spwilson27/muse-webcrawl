Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914

Clayton Antitrust Act
Enacted: 15 October 1914
Signed by: President Woodrow Wilson
Sponsor: Henry De Lamar Clayton Jr.
Codified: 15 U.S.C. §§ 12–27; 29 U.S.C. §§ 52–53
Amended: Sherman Antitrust Act (1890)

The Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 (Pub. L. 63–212, 38 Stat. 730) is a part of the federal law of the United States on competition, passed during the Wilson administration to supplement and clarify the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890. It was named after its principal author, Congressman Henry De Lamar Clayton Jr. of Alabama.

Provisions

Where the Sherman Act broadly prohibited restraints of trade, the Clayton Act specified particular practices. Among its key provisions:

  • Section 2 — prohibits price discrimination between purchasers where the effect may substantially lessen competition (later strengthened by the Robinson–Patman Act of 1936).
  • Section 3 — prohibits tying and exclusive-dealing arrangements that lessen competition.
  • Section 7 — prohibits mergers and acquisitions, including acquisitions of stock, whose effect may be substantially to lessen competition — the foundation of modern US merger control.
  • Section 8 — prohibits interlocking directorates among competing corporations.

Labour provisions

Section 6 declared that "the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce", exempting labour unions from antitrust prosecution, while Section 20 limited the use of injunctions in labour disputes. Samuel Gompers hailed the Act as labour's "Magna Carta", though later court decisions narrowed these protections.

Enforcement

The Act authorises the Federal Trade Commission (created the same year) and the Department of Justice to enforce its provisions, and Section 4 gives private parties injured by violations the right to sue for treble damages.
