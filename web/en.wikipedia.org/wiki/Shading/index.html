Shading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shading is the process of altering the colour of a surface in a 3D model or illustration according to the angle at which the surface meets a light source, its distance from the light, and the properties of its material, in order to produce a photorealistic or stylised effect. In drawing, shading refers to the depiction of depth perception through varying levels of darkness.

In computer graphics

Shading algorithms determine the colour of each pixel by evaluating a lighting model at points on a surface. The simplest approach, flat shading, computes one colour per polygon. Gouraud shading interpolates colours computed at vertices, while Phong shading interpolates surface normals and evaluates the lighting model per pixel, giving smoother specular highlights. Modern renderers generalise these ideas with programmable shaders and physically based shading models that simulate the interaction of light with materials such as metal, plastic and skin.

In drawing and painting

Artists use techniques including hatching, cross-hatching, stippling and tonal blending to suggest volume and shadow on a two-dimensional surface. The gradual transition from light to shadow across a curved form, with a distinct band of reflected light near the terminator, is a cornerstone of traditional life drawing.
