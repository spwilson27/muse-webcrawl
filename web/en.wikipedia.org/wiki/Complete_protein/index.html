Complete protein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A complete protein or whole protein is a source of protein that contains an adequate proportion of each of the nine essential amino acids necessary in the human diet. By contrast, an incomplete protein is deficient in one or more of the essential amino acids, with the deficient one called the limiting amino acid.

Essential amino acids

The nine amino acids humans cannot synthesise and must obtain from food are histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Animal-derived foods — meat, fish, eggs, milk and other dairy products — are generally complete proteins. A few plant foods are also complete or nearly so, including soy products such as tofu and edamame, quinoa, buckwheat, hempseed, chia, and the algae spirulina.

Protein combining

Most individual plant proteins are low in one or more essential amino acids: grains tend to be low in lysine, and legumes low in methionine. Eating a variety of plant foods over the course of a day supplies complementary amino acid profiles — the traditional combinations include rice and beans, and wheat bread with peanut butter. The idea that complementary proteins must be eaten at the same meal, popularised by Frances Moore Lappé's 1971 book Diet for a Small Planet, was later revised by its author and by dietetic associations: a varied diet over the day is sufficient.

Protein quality measures

Protein quality is assessed by measures such as the Protein Digestibility Corrected Amino Acid Score (PDCAAS) and its successor, the Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score (DIAAS), which combine amino acid composition with digestibility. Animal proteins and soy generally score highest; many other plant proteins score lower mainly because of a single limiting amino acid.

Daily requirements

For adults, the recommended dietary allowance for protein is commonly set at about 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, with higher intakes recommended for athletes, older adults, and during pregnancy. Total intake and overall dietary variety matter more in practice than the complete/incomplete status of any single food.
