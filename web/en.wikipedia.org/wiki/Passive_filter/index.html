Passive filter

A passive filter is an electronic filter built entirely from passive components — resistors, capacitors, and inductors — without any amplifying element such as a transistor or operational amplifier. Passive filters are used in audio systems, power supplies, radio equipment, and signal processing to allow signals in certain frequency ranges to pass while attenuating others.

Types

Common configurations include low-pass filters (passing low frequencies and attenuating high ones), high-pass filters, band-pass filters, and band-stop (notch) filters. The simplest passive filters are RC (resistor–capacitor) and RL (resistor–inductor) networks, which are first-order and roll off at 6 dB per octave. LC filters use inductors and capacitors and can achieve sharper roll-offs and resonant behaviour; they are classified by their response prototypes, such as Butterworth, Chebyshev, and elliptic (Cauer) filters.

Advantages and limitations

Passive filters are simple, reliable, handle large signal voltages and currents, and introduce little noise, which makes them standard in power and radio-frequency applications. Their disadvantages include insertion loss (they cannot provide gain), sensitivity to load impedance, and the bulk and non-ideal behaviour of inductors at low frequencies. For many low-frequency signal applications, active filters are preferred, while passive LC filters remain dominant in crossover networks for loudspeakers and in RF front ends.
