Free fall

Free fall
Definition (Newtonian)Motion under the force of gravity only
Acceleration near Earthg ≈ 9.81 m/s²
Related conceptsWeightlessness, orbit, equivalence principle

In Newtonian physics, free fall is any motion of a body where gravity is the only force acting upon it. In the context of general relativity, where gravitation is reduced to a space-time curvature, a body in free fall has no force acting on it and moves along a geodesic.

An object in free fall near the surface of the Earth accelerates downward at approximately 9.81 m/s², independent of its mass — neglecting air resistance. The distance fallen from rest grows with the square of the elapsed time:

d = ½ · g · t²

History

Aristotle taught that heavy objects fall faster than light ones, in proportion to their weight. Galileo Galilei, in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, showed through inclined-plane experiments and thought experiments that, absent resistance, all bodies fall with the same acceleration. Astronaut David Scott famously repeated the demonstration on the Moon during Apollo 15 in 1971, dropping a hammer and a feather that struck the dust simultaneously.

Free fall and weightlessness

A person in a freely falling elevator experiences apparent weightlessness because they and their surroundings accelerate together. Orbiting spacecraft are in continuous free fall around the Earth: they have enough tangential velocity that they perpetually "miss" the surface as they fall, so astronauts aboard the International Space Station float in a state of microgravity. Einstein used the freely falling observer as a key thought experiment — his "happiest thought" of 1907 — leading to the equivalence principle and general relativity.

Terminal velocity

In a fluid such as air, drag opposes gravity. When drag equals weight, acceleration ceases and the object falls at a constant terminal velocity; for a skydiver in a belly-to-earth posture this is roughly 53 m/s (about 190 km/h). Strictly, motion at terminal velocity is no longer free fall, since a force other than gravity balances it.
