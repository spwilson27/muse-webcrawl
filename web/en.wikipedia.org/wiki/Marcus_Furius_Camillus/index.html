Marcus Furius Camillus

Marcus Furius Camillus (c. 446 – 365 BC) was a Roman soldier and statesman of the patrician gens Furia. According to Livy and other later sources, he was appointed dictator five times, was honoured with four triumphs, and was revered in later tradition as the second founder of Rome after its sack by the Gauls.

Career

Camillus served as censor in 403 BC and as military tribune with consular power several times. His most famous victory was the capture of the Etruscan city of Veii in 396 BC after a siege said to have lasted ten years, following his vow to dedicate a tenth of the spoils to Apollo and a golden bowl to Delphi.

Exile and the Gallic sack

Accused of mishandling the spoils of Veii, Camillus withdrew into voluntary exile at Ardea. When the Gauls under Brennus defeated the Romans at the Battle of the Allia and sacked Rome in 390 BC, Camillus was recalled and appointed dictator. In the traditional account, he arrived as the ransom gold was being weighed, annulled the agreement with his famous words — that Rome would be ransomed with iron, not gold — and defeated the Gauls.

Later years

Camillus went on to campaign against the Aequi, Volsci and Etruscans, and played a leading part in Roman politics during the struggle over the Licinian-Sextian laws. He is said to have died of plague in 365 BC. Modern historians regard many details of his life as legendary accretions, though a historical core is generally accepted.
