Qualitative inorganic analysis

Flame tests are a classic preliminary in qualitative inorganic analysis.

Qualitative inorganic analysis is a method of analytical chemistry which seeks to find the elemental composition of inorganic compounds. It is mainly focused on detecting ions in solution (or analysing the composition of a solid) by a systematic sequence of characteristic chemical reactions — precipitation, colour change, gas evolution, and flame coloration — rather than by measuring quantities, which is the province of quantitative analysis.

Scheme of analysis

The classical scheme for cations separates the common metal ions into groups using group reagents:

  • Group I — silver, lead(II), and mercury(I), precipitated as chlorides by dilute hydrochloric acid.
  • Group II — copper, cadmium, bismuth, arsenic, antimony, and tin, precipitated as sulfides from acidic solution.
  • Group III — iron, aluminium, chromium, zinc, manganese, nickel, and cobalt, precipitated as hydroxides or sulfides from alkaline solution.
  • Group IV — calcium, strontium, and barium, precipitated as carbonates.
  • Group V — magnesium, sodium, potassium, and ammonium, identified by specific tests.

Anions are identified by separate tests: carbonates effervesce with acid, sulfates give a white precipitate with barium chloride, halides give characteristic silver halide precipitates, and nitrates are detected by the brown ring test. Colours of precipitates and solutions, together with flame tests (e.g. crimson for lithium, brick-red for calcium, green for copper), provide confirmatory evidence.

Modern practice

Although instrumental methods such as spectroscopy and mass spectrometry have largely replaced wet-chemical schemes in professional laboratories, qualitative analysis remains a standard part of chemical education, teaching equilibrium, solubility, and redox principles through direct observation.
