Space age pop

Space age pop (also called space age bachelor pad music) is a loosely defined retro genre of easy-listening and exotica music produced mainly in the United States in the 1950s and early 1960s. Associated with the optimism of the Space Age, the dawn of stereo recording and hi-fi culture, it combined lush orchestration, Latin and Polynesian exoticism, unusual instrumentation and early electronic sounds to evoke futuristic, otherworldly moods.

Characteristics

Space age pop records were often showcase pieces for new stereophonic technology: instruments ping-ponged between channels, and arrangements featured vibraphones, marimbas, bongos, gongs, wordless vocals, and the electronic tones of instruments such as the ondes Martenot and the theremin. Album covers favoured stylised images of rockets, satellites, hi-fi equipment and glamorous "bachelor pad" interiors.

Artists

Key figures include Juan García Esquivel, whose "zooming" orchestral arrangements became the genre's signature; Les Baxter, a founder of exotica; Martin Denny, whose "Quiet Village" (1959) was a hit; Dick Hyman and his electronic Moog experiments; Ferrante & Teicher; Enoch Light, whose Command Records "Persuasive Percussion" series sold stereo demonstration discs in volume; and the venturesome composing team of Attilio Mineo, whose Man in Space with Sounds accompanied the 1962 Seattle World's Fair.

Revival

The genre was largely forgotten after the 1960s until the 1990s, when reissue labels such as Rhino (with the Cocktail Mix series) and Bar/None, along with compilations like Music for a Bachelor's Den, sparked a lounge revival. The British-French group Stereolab and others drew openly on the style, and space age pop aesthetics entered retro-futurist design generally.
