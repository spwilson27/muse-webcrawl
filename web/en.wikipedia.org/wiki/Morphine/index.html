Morphine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Morphine is a strong opioid painkiller, the principal active alkaloid of opium from the poppy Papaver somniferum. Isolated in 1804–1806 by Friedrich Sertürner — the first alkaloid extracted from a plant — it remains the standard against which other strong analgesics are measured and is on the WHO list of essential medicines.

Action and use

Morphine activates opioid (mainly mu) receptors in the brain and spinal cord, reducing pain perception and the emotional response to pain. It is used for severe pain — after major surgery, in serious injury, advanced cancer and at the end of life — and for breathlessness in palliative care. It can be given by mouth, injection or infusion; related opioids include codeine (a weaker precursor), oxycodone, fentanyl (far more potent) and heroin (diamorphine).

Risks and regulation

Effects include drowsiness, constipation, nausea and — the chief danger — slowed or stopped breathing, especially in overdose; the antagonist naloxone reverses it. Tolerance and physical dependence develop with use, and morphine has significant misuse potential, so it is tightly regulated under international drug conventions; access for medical pain relief remains very unequal worldwide.
