William Blackstone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sir William Blackstone (10 July 1723 – 14 February 1780) was an English jurist, judge and Tory politician, most noted for writing the Commentaries on the Laws of England (1765–1769), the first comprehensive, readable synthesis of the common law. The four volumes — on the rights of persons, the rights of things, private wrongs, and public wrongs — systematised a mass of case law and statute into an elegant structure that shaped legal education in England and became the foundational legal text of the early United States.

Blackstone lectured at Oxford as the first Vinerian Professor of English Law (from 1758), sat as an MP, and became a judge of the Court of King's Bench and then Common Pleas (1770). The Commentaries' influence was immense: Abraham Lincoln and generations of American lawyers learned law from them; their account of rights and of Parliament's sovereign authority was quoted by courts into the modern era. Critics, notably Jeremy Bentham (whose Fragment on Government, 1776, began as an attack on Blackstone), charged him with complacent defence of every existing rule — “everything is as it should be” — and with smoothing over the law's irrationalities.
