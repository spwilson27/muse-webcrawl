Hetaira

In ancient Greek society, hetaerae (singular hetaira, Ancient Greek: ἑταίρα, "companion") were highly educated, independent women who, in addition to providing sexual services, engaged in conversation and intellectual discourse with their clients. They were distinguished from the common pornai (brothel prostitutes) and from citizen wives, whose lives were largely confined to the household.

Social role

Hetairai were often former slaves or foreign women (metics) who had been trained in music, dance, literature and rhetoric. They attended the symposium, the all-male drinking party from which respectable citizen women were excluded, as companions and entertainers. Some, such as Aspasia, the companion of Pericles, became famous for their intellect and influence, while others, such as Phryne, were celebrated for their beauty and wealth.

In art and literature

Hetairai appear frequently in Attic vase painting, New Comedy, and in the orations of the Attic orators; the speech Against Neaera famously divides women into hetairai "for pleasure", concubines for the care of the body, and wives for the production of legitimate children.
