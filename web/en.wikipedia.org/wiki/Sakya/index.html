Sakya (Tibetan: ས་སྐྱ་, "pale earth") is one of the four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism, named after Sakya Monastery in south-western Tibet. Founded in 1073 by Khön Könchok Gyalpo, the school is headed by the Sakya Trizin, an office held by members of the Khön family by hereditary succession. Its central teaching is the Lamdre ("Path and Result"), centred on the Hevajra Tantra.

History

The school takes its name from the greyish-white soil of the Ponpo hills near Shigatse, where Könchok Gyalpo built the first Sakya monastery in 1073. His successors, the "Five Venerable Supreme Masters", including Sachen Kunga Nyingpo and Sakya Pandita, developed the school's philosophy and learning. In the 13th century, under Sakya Pandita and his nephew Drogön Chögyal Phagpa, the Sakya hierarchs became the effective rulers of Tibet under Mongol patronage; Phagpa became Imperial Preceptor to Kublai Khan and devised the 'Phags-pa script.

Sakya political supremacy waned in the 14th century, but the school remained influential. Its principal sub-schools are Ngor (founded 1429) and Tsar. Today the Sakya tradition is headed from exile in India, with centres worldwide.

Teachings

The Sakya school emphasises the inseparability of samsara and nirvana and tantric practice based on the Hevajra cycle, transmitted in the Lamdre instructions. Scholarly study of the Indian Buddhist classics — logic, Abhidharma, Madhyamaka — has long been a hallmark of Sakya monasteries.
