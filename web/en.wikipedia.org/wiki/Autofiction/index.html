Autofiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Autofiction is a form of fictionalised autobiography. The term was coined by French writer Serge Doubrovsky in 1977 with respect to his novel Fils. Autofiction combines two paradoxically contradictory styles: that of autobiography and of fiction. It usually presents the author as the protagonist in experiences that may be based in reality but are shaped, embellished or reinvented by narrative invention.

Autofiction blurs the boundary between memoir and novel.

Definition and history

Doubrovsky defined autofiction as "fiction of strictly real events and facts" — a response to Philippe Lejeune's "autobiographical pact", in which author, narrator and protagonist share an identity. Later critics broadened the term to cover any work in which the author appears as a character while openly using novelistic technique. Precursors are often traced to writers such as Colette and to earlier autobiographical novels.

The genre flourished in France (Doubrovsky, Annie Ernaux, Hervé Guibert) and spread internationally. In the twenty-first century, widely discussed autofictional works include Karl Ove Knausgård's My Struggle series, Ben Lerner's 10:04, and works by Rachel Cusk, Maggie Nelson and Ocean Vuong.

Criticism

Critics debate whether autofiction represents a genuine genre, a marketing label, or a symptom of literary culture's turn toward the self. Some praise its honesty and formal innovation; others see it as narcissistic or overly reliant on the reader's knowledge of the author's life.
