America was a 19th-century racing yacht built in 1851 for a syndicate of members of the New York Yacht Club. On 22 August 1851 she won the Royal Yacht Squadron's 100 Guinea Cup, a race around the Isle of Wight off the south coast of England. The trophy she won was later renamed the America's Cup in her honour, and it became the oldest international trophy in sport.

Design and construction

America was designed by James Rich Steers and built at the shipyard of William H. Brown on the East River in New York City. She was a two-masted schooner of about 170 tons, roughly 101 feet (31 m) on deck, with a sharp, hollow bow that departed from the bluff-bowed "cod's head and mackerel tail" tradition of earlier British and American yachts. Her sails were of tightly woven cotton duck, and her spars were raked strongly aft.

The 1851 race

The syndicate, led by Commodore John Cox Stevens, sailed America across the Atlantic to challenge British yachts. In the race around the Isle of Wight on 22 August 1851, America competed against 15 British yachts and finished first, beating the cutter Aurora by eight minutes under the rules then in force. According to legend, when Queen Victoria asked who was second, she was told, "Ah, Your Majesty, there is no second."

Later career

America was sold to British owners shortly after her victory and renamed Camilla. She later returned to American hands, served the Confederate States Navy as a blockade runner during the American Civil War under the name Memphis, and was scuttled and then raised by Union forces, serving the United States Navy as USS America. She competed in the first America's Cup defence in 1870, finishing fourth. After decades of deterioration, she was broken up in 1945.
