Potlatch

A potlatch is a gift-giving feast practised by Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast of Canada and the United States, among them the Haida, Heiltsuk, Nuxalk, Tlingit, Makah, Tsimshian, Nuu-chah-nulth and Kwakwaka'wakw. The word comes from the Chinook Jargon patshatl, ultimately from a Nuu-chah-nulth word meaning "to give". Potlatches are held to mark births, marriages, deaths, the raising of totem poles, and the assumption of names, titles and crests.

Function

The potlatch is the central institution of Northwest Coast governance and social organisation. A host chief invites guests — who act as witnesses — and validates a claim to a name, crest, fishing ground or chiefly title by publicly giving away large quantities of goods: blankets, canoes, food, carved objects and prized shield-shaped "coppers". Status is measured by generosity: a rival chief must match or exceed a potlatch or lose face. In the competitively escalating potlatches of the late 19th century, goods were sometimes ostentatiously destroyed — coppers broken, oil poured on fires — a practice analysed by anthropologists such as Franz Boas, Marcel Mauss (in The Gift) and Ruth Benedict.

Prohibition

Canadian officials and missionaries viewed the potlatch as wasteful and an obstacle to assimilation. An 1885 amendment to the Indian Act banned it, and enforcement culminated in 1921, when dozens of Kwakwaka'wakw were arrested after Dan Cranmer's potlatch at Village Island and hundreds of pieces of regalia were confiscated and sold to museums. The ban was dropped in the 1951 revision of the Indian Act, and confiscated regalia has since been repatriated to the U'mista Cultural Centre at Alert Bay and the Nuyumbalees Cultural Centre. Potlatches are again held openly and remain living institutions.
