Ground lease

A ground lease is a long-term lease of land (the "ground") that permits the tenant to develop the property during the lease term, with the land and usually the improvements reverting to the landowner at the end of the lease. Ground leases typically run for periods of 50 to 99 years.

Structure

The landowner retains ownership of the land and collects ground rent, while the tenant finances, builds and operates the improvements — offices, hotels, retail buildings or apartments — and bears taxes, insurance and maintenance. In a subordinated ground lease the landowner's interest is pledged behind the tenant's construction financing, making funding easier but putting the land at risk; in an unsubordinated lease the landowner's interest takes priority. Ground rent is often reset periodically by appraisal or indexed formulas.

Advantages and risks

For developers, a ground lease avoids the up-front cost of buying land and can make prime sites available from owners — universities, churches, municipalities and estates — unwilling to sell. For landowners it produces a steady income while preserving long-term ownership. The tenant's major risk is the reversion of the building at expiry, which also makes financing harder late in the term. Ground leases are common in places such as Hawaii, Baltimore (with its historic ground rent system), London estates and Battery Park City in New York.
