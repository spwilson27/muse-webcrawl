Stagecoach

A stagecoach (or stage coach) is a horse-drawn public coach used to carry passengers and mail along a route in scheduled "stages", changing horses at relay stations along the way. Stagecoaches were the principal means of long-distance land travel in Britain, Europe and North America from the 17th century until they were displaced by railways in the mid-19th century, surviving longer in frontier regions such as the American West and Australia.

A Concord stagecoach of the American West

History

Scheduled coach services began in Britain in the mid-1600s, and improved roads and coach design in the 18th century cut journey times dramatically; by the 1830s mail coaches could average around 10 miles per hour including stops. In the United States, firms such as Wells Fargo and the Butterfield Overland Mail operated long routes across the continent, using the sturdy Concord coach, whose body was slung on leather thoroughbraces that acted as suspension. Fresh horse teams were kept at stations roughly every 10–15 miles, and a change took only a few minutes.

Operation

Coaches were driven by a coachman (or "whip"), often accompanied by a guard who carried a blunderbuss and sounded a horn or bugle to announce arrivals. Passengers travelled inside the coach or on cheaper outside seats; luggage and mail were carried in boots at the rear and under the seats. Travel was slow, uncomfortable and exposed to highway robbery, and the word "stage" in the vehicle's name refers to the stages into which the journey was divided.

Legacy

The stagecoach became an enduring symbol of the American Old West and of pre-railway travel generally. Surviving coaches are preserved in museums, and the term survives in the names of businesses such as the Stagecoach Group, a British bus operator, and Wells Fargo, which uses a stagecoach as its emblem.
