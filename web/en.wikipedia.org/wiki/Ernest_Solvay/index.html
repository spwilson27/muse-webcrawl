Ernest Solvay (16 April 1838 – 26 May 1922) was a Belgian chemist, industrialist and philanthropist. He invented an industrial process for producing soda ash, founded the chemical company that still bears his name, and endowed the famous Solvay Conferences in physics and chemistry.

The Solvay process

Working in his uncle's gas works, Solvay developed an ammonia-soda process in which brine is treated with ammonia and carbon dioxide to precipitate sodium bicarbonate, which is then heated to produce sodium carbonate (soda ash). Patented in 1861 and industrialised from 1863 at Couillet, the process was far cheaper and cleaner than the older Leblanc process and made the Solvay company a world leader in soda production.

Science patronage

Solvay used his fortune to found institutes of physiology and sociology in Brussels and, from 1911, the Solvay Conferences, invitation-only meetings that brought together figures such as Einstein, Bohr, Curie and Heisenberg and profoundly shaped twentieth-century physics. He also served in the Belgian Senate and promoted social reform, including the eight-hour working day.
