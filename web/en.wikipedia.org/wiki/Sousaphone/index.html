The sousaphone is a large brass instrument in the tuba family, designed to be easy to play while standing or marching. Its tubing coils around the player's body, and its very large, flaring bell points forward and above the head. It is named after the American bandmaster John Philip Sousa, who suggested its design.

History

Sousa wanted a bass instrument whose sound projected over the band rather than at the audience from the side, as with the helicon then used in marching bands. The first sousaphone was built in 1893 by J. W. Pepper of Philadelphia to Sousa's specifications, with an upright bell; C. G. Conn produced the better-known forward-facing "raincatcher"-to-umbrella design from 1898, and Sousa's band used the instrument extensively.

Design and use

Sousaphones are usually pitched in BB♭ and have three piston or rotary valves operated by the right hand while the left hand supports the instrument on the shoulder. Modern marching sousaphones are often made of fibreglass (sometimes called "sousacans" in lightweight form) to reduce weight. The sousaphone is a fixture of American marching bands, military bands, and New Orleans brass bands, where it supplies the bass line and often doubles as a visual emblem of the ensemble.
