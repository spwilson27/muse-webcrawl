Ajanta Caves

The Ajanta Caves are 29 rock-cut Buddhist cave monuments dating from the 2nd century BCE to about 480 CE in the Aurangabad district of Maharashtra, India. The caves include paintings and rock-cut sculptures described as among the finest surviving examples of ancient Indian art, particularly expressive paintings that present emotions through gesture, pose and form. The caves were designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.

History

The caves were excavated in two phases. The earliest group, mainly austere chaitya halls and vihara monasteries of the Hinayana (Theravada) tradition, dates to the 2nd and 1st centuries BCE under Satavahana patronage. After a long interval, a second, far more elaborate phase began around 460 CE under the Vakataka emperor Harishena, producing richly painted Mahayana monasteries and some of the masterpieces of Indian painting, including the famous depictions of the Bodhisattva Padmapani and scenes from the Jataka tales. The caves were abandoned after the 5th century and forgotten until 1819, when a British officer, John Smith, stumbled upon them while hunting.

Art and architecture

The caves are cut into the steep basalt cliff of a horseshoe-shaped gorge above the Waghora River. Paintings cover walls and ceilings with narrative cycles of the Buddha's previous lives and contemporary courtly life, giving an unparalleled picture of dress, jewellery and customs. Cave 26 contains a monumental reclining Buddha representing his parinirvana, and Cave 19 is noted for its ornate chaitya façade.
