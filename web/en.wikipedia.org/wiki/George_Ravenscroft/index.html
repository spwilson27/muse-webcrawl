George Ravenscroft

George Ravenscroft
Born: 1632, England
Died: June 7, 1683, Barnet, England
Occupation: Glassmaker, merchant
Known for: Lead crystal glass

George Ravenscroft (1632 – June 7, 1683) was an English glassmaker and merchant credited with the invention of lead crystal glass in the 1670s, an innovation that established England's dominance of fine glassmaking.

Life and career

Ravenscroft came from a Catholic merchant family with interests in the Venetian trade; he spent years in Venice importing glass and other goods and familiarised himself with Murano glassmaking. After returning to London he managed glasshouses at the Savoy and at Henley-on-Thames for the Glass Sellers' Company, which sought an English alternative to Venetian cristallo that could also solve the problem of crizzling — a fine network of cracks that afflicted contemporary glass.

Lead crystal

In 1676 Ravenscroft obtained a patent for glass made with lead oxide ("flint glass" from the calcined flints he first used as a silica source). Adding lead oxide raised the refractive index and brilliance, lengthened the working time for the glassblower and produced a heavy, sonorous glass suited to deep cutting and engraving. Early pieces suffered from crizzling until the lead content was raised; surviving Ravenscroft vessels, marked with his raven's-head seal, are rare and prized by collectors. The recipe laid the foundations of the English and Irish cut-glass industries of the following centuries.
