Ecolabels (also eco-labels) are marks of environmental performance placed on products or services to show that they meet criteria for reduced environmental impact, assessed over some or all of their life cycle, by an independent body. They are a form of sustainability measurement directed at consumers, intended to make it easier to identify environmentally preferable goods and thereby to encourage markets toward lower impact patterns of production and consumption. The Ecolabel Index tracks several hundred schemes worldwide, in some 200 countries and 25 industry sectors.

History

The world's first national ecolabelling scheme was Germany's Blue Angel (Blauer Engel), launched in 1978. The Nordic Swan (1989), the EU Ecolabel (1992), Canada's Environmental Choice and Japan's Eco Mark (1989) followed. In 1994 the leading schemes formed the Global Ecolabelling Network to share criteria and promote mutual recognition. Sector-specific labels proliferated in the 1990s and 2000s: the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC, 1993) for timber, the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC, 1997) for seafood, Energy Star (1992) for energy efficiency, and numerous organic, fair-trade and carbon labels.

Types (ISO 14020 series)

  • Type I (ISO 14024) — multi-criteria, third-party certified labels based on life-cycle considerations, awarded by an independent programme: e.g. Blue Angel, EU Ecolabel, Nordic Swan.
  • Type II (ISO 14021) — self-declared environmental claims by manufacturers ("recyclable", "compostable"), which must be accurate and verifiable.
  • Type III (ISO 14025) — quantified environmental product declarations (EPDs) based on full life-cycle assessment data, verified by a third party, presenting data rather than a pass/fail judgement.

Criticism and effectiveness

Research on ecolabel effectiveness is mixed. Labels can shift market share and drive producers to improve, particularly in business-to-business and public procurement, but consumer recognition is low for many schemes; the sheer number of labels causes confusion, and weak self-declared claims shade into greenwashing. Critics also note that labels typically certify relative improvement within a product category rather than absolute sustainability, and that certification costs can burden small producers in developing countries. The WTO treats mandatory labels as potential technical barriers to trade, though voluntary schemes are generally accepted.
