Toll-like receptor

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are a class of proteins that play a key role in the innate immune system. They are pattern recognition receptors that recognize structurally conserved molecules derived from microbes—pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)—as well as damage-associated molecular patterns from injured tissue.

Types and ligands

Ten functional TLRs are known in humans (TLR1–TLR10). Cell-surface TLRs mainly detect bacterial and fungal components: TLR4 recognizes lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of Gram-negative bacteria, TLR2 (with TLR1 or TLR6) detects lipoproteins and peptidoglycan, and TLR5 detects flagellin. Endosomal TLRs detect nucleic acids: TLR3 binds double-stranded RNA, TLR7 and TLR8 single-stranded RNA, and TLR9 unmethylated CpG DNA.

Signalling

Upon ligand binding, TLRs recruit adaptor proteins such as MyD88 or TRIF to their cytoplasmic TIR domains, activating cascades that culminate in NF-κB and interferon regulatory factors, driving production of inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons.

History

The receptors are named after the Drosophila Toll gene, identified by Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard in developmental studies and shown in 1996 to mediate antifungal defence in flies. Bruce Beutler and Jules Hoffmann shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning the activation of innate immunity, including TLR4's role as the LPS receptor.
