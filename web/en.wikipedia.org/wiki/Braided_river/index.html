Braided river

A braided river is a river whose flow is divided into a network of small, interweaving channels separated by temporary islands or bars. Braiding occurs when a river carries more sediment than it can transport: the excess load is deposited as mid-channel bars, around which the current splits, erodes the banks and rejoins, producing a pattern that changes constantly with discharge.

Conditions for braiding

Braided channels develop where sediment supply is abundant and coarse, discharge is highly variable, and banks are easily eroded because they lack cohesive silt or vegetation. They are therefore characteristic of glacial meltwater streams — called sandur plains in Iceland — of mountain-front alluvial fans, and of rivers draining easily eroded terrain. The balance between stream power and sediment load is summarised by Lane's relation (1955): when load exceeds transport capacity, deposition and braiding follow.

Examples

Famous braided rivers include the Brahmaputra in Bangladesh and Assam, one of the widest rivers on Earth; the Platte River in Nebraska, celebrated habitat of migratory sandhill cranes; and the gravel-bed rivers of New Zealand's South Island, such as the Waimakariri and Rakaia, which cross the Canterbury Plains from the Southern Alps.

Behaviour and management

Because bars migrate and channels shift unpredictably, braided rivers are difficult to bridge, embank or navigate, and engineering works often narrow them into single channels, with consequences for flood capacity and ecology. Their shifting mosaics of gravel bars, shallow channels and islands nevertheless support distinctive wildlife, including nesting shorebirds that exploit bare, predator-isolated shingle.

Comparison with other patterns

Braided rivers contrast with single-thread meandering rivers, which carry finer sediment at gentler gradients, and with anastomosing rivers, whose multiple channels are separated by stable, vegetated floodplain rather than mobile bars.
