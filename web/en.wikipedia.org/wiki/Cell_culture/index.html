Cell culture

FieldBiotechnology, cell biology
PioneersRoss Harrison (1907); Alexis Carrel
Famous linesHeLa, CHO, HEK 293, Vero
Typical conditions37 °C, 5% CO2, sterile medium

Cell culture is the process by which cells are grown under controlled conditions, generally outside their natural environment. After cells of interest have been isolated from living tissue, they can be maintained under carefully controlled conditions: typically a temperature of 37 °C, a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2, and a nutrient growth medium, often supplemented with serum. Cell culture is one of the major tools of cell and molecular biology.

History

The technique began in 1907 when Ross Granville Harrison cultured frog nerve tissue in lymph, demonstrating that nerve fibres grow from individual cells. Alexis Carrel developed methods for long-term culture, and his laboratory maintained chick heart cells for many years. A landmark came in 1951 with HeLa, the first immortal human cell line, derived from cervical cancer cells taken from Henrietta Lacks; it enabled mass production of the polio vaccine and remains widely used.

Types of culture

  • Primary culture — cells taken directly from tissue, with limited lifespan.
  • Cell lines — subcultured populations; "immortalised" lines (e.g. HeLa, CHO, HEK 293) divide indefinitely.
  • Adherent culture — cells grow attached to a surface, typically treated plastic.
  • Suspension culture — cells grow free-floating in medium, common for blood cells and industrial production.
  • Three-dimensional culture — spheroids and organoids that better mimic tissue organisation.

Techniques

Cultures are maintained aseptically, usually handled in a laminar-flow biosafety cabinet. Cells are periodically "passaged" (subcultured) using enzymes such as trypsin to detach them, counted — often with a haemocytometer — and assessed for contamination, especially by Mycoplasma. Growth media such as DMEM and RPMI 1640 provide amino acids, vitamins, salts and a carbon source; antibiotics may be added to limit bacterial growth.

Applications

Cell culture underpins vaccine and therapeutic protein production, cancer research, drug screening and toxicity testing, stem cell biology, tissue engineering, and basic studies of cell physiology and gene function. Cultured meat production adapts the same methods at industrial scale.
