Local class field theory

In mathematics, local class field theory, introduced by Helmut Hasse, is the study of abelian extensions of local fields; here, a "local field" is a field which is complete with respect to an absolute value or a discrete valuation with a finite residue field — principally the p-adic fields Qp and function fields Fq((t)). It is the local counterpart of global class field theory, which describes abelian extensions of number fields, and the two are linked through the idèle class group and the Brauer group.

Abelian extensions of a local field

Main theorem

The central result is the existence of the local reciprocity (Artin) map: for a local field K there is a canonical isomorphism between the profinite completion of the multiplicative group K× and the absolute abelian Galois group of K. Finite abelian extensions of K correspond, via the norm groups, to open subgroups of finite index in K×, and the Frobenius element of an unramified extension corresponds under reciprocity to a uniformiser of K.

Structure results

The multiplicative group decomposes as K× ≅ Z × O×, where Z corresponds to the valuation and O× is the group of units; the unramified extensions correspond to extensions of the residue field and to the Z factor. Unit groups have a filtration by higher unit groups, whose graded pieces are understood through ramification theory and the theory of formal groups.

Lubin–Tate theory

John Tate and Jonathan Lubin gave an explicit construction of the maximal abelian extension of a local field using formal groups (Lubin–Tate formal groups): adjoining the torsion points of a formal group law associated to a uniformiser generates the totally ramified part of the abelian closure, analogous to the way roots of unity and torsion points of elliptic curves generate abelian extensions of Q and of imaginary quadratic fields in the Kronecker Jugendtraum.

Approaches

Modern proofs proceed either through Galois cohomology — the vanishing and computation of Brauer groups of local fields, H2(K, Gm) ≅ Q/Z, giving the Hasse invariant — following the cohomological reformulation of Tate and Serre, or through explicit methods such as Lubin–Tate theory, or through the theory of higher local fields. Neukirch's axiomatic approach derives local (and global) class field theory from a small set of axioms on a "class field theory datum".
