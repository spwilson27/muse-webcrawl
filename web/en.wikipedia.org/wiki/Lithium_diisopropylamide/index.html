Lithium diisopropylamide (commonly abbreviated LDA) is a chemical compound with the molecular formula [(CH3)2CH]2NLi. It is a strong base and has become a workhorse reagent in organic synthesis since its popularization in the 1970s, principally for generating enolates from carbonyl compounds.

Preparation and structure

LDA is commonly prepared in situ by treating diisopropylamine with n-butyllithium in tetrahydrofuran (THF) at low temperature, typically −78 °C. It is also commercially available as a solution, often complexed with THF or in hydrocarbon solvents. In solution LDA exists in aggregated forms whose composition depends on solvent and temperature; in THF it is largely dimeric.

Use as a base

LDA is the conjugate base of diisopropylamine, whose pKa in THF is about 36. Because the two bulky isopropyl groups shield the nitrogen, LDA is strongly basic but poorly nucleophilic: it deprotonates even weakly acidic C–H bonds without attacking electrophilic functional groups such as carbonyls. Its most common application is the quantitative, regioselective formation of enolates from ketones, esters, and amides under kinetic control at low temperature. These enolates are then trapped with alkyl halides, carbonyl compounds, silyl chlorides, and other electrophiles. LDA is also used to generate carbenes, in ortho-lithiation chemistry, and to deprotonate terminal alkynes and other substrates.

Handling

LDA is moisture- and air-sensitive and is handled under inert atmosphere. It reacts violently with water and protic solvents, liberating diisopropylamine.
