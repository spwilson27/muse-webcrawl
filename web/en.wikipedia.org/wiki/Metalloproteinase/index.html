Metalloproteinase

A zinc metalloproteinase active site

A metalloproteinase, or metalloprotease, is any protease enzyme whose catalytic mechanism involves a metal. Most metalloproteases are zinc-dependent, though some use other metals such as cobalt. The metal ion is coordinated by amino acid residues—typically three histidines—and activates a water molecule that performs nucleophilic attack on the peptide bond of the substrate.

Classification

Metalloproteinases are divided into two broad groups based on where they cleave the substrate: exopeptidases, which remove terminal amino acids (such as the carboxypeptidases and aminopeptidases), and endopeptidases, which cleave internal peptide bonds. In the MEROPS classification system, metallopeptidases are grouped into clans and families by the identity and arrangement of their metal-binding ligands; the metzincins, characterized by a conserved Met-turn motif, include the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), ADAMs, and astacins.

Functions

The matrix metalloproteinases are a major family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases that degrade components of the extracellular matrix and are central to tissue remodeling, wound healing, angiogenesis, and development. Their activity is regulated by endogenous tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). Dysregulated metalloproteinase activity contributes to arthritis, cancer invasion and metastasis, cardiovascular disease, and neurodegeneration. Bacterial metalloproteinases, such as thermolysin, and the metalloproteases in snake venoms also have significant physiological and medical importance.
