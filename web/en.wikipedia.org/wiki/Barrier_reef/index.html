Barrier reef

A barrier reef is a type of coral reef that runs parallel to the shoreline but is separated from it by a deep, wide lagoon. It is one of the three principal reef types identified by Charles Darwin, alongside fringing reefs and atolls. Barrier reefs may extend for hundreds of kilometres and protect the coast and lagoon behind them from ocean waves.

Formation

Darwin proposed that barrier reefs form as a volcanic island slowly subsides: a fringing reef attached to the island grows upward to keep pace with sea level, while the land sinks away, leaving a lagoon between the reef and the shore. Continued subsidence can eventually submerge the island entirely, leaving an atoll. Modern explanations also emphasize sea-level rise following glaciation over pre-existing reef platforms.

Examples

The Great Barrier Reef off Queensland, Australia, is the world's largest coral reef system and the best-known example, stretching over 2,300 kilometres. Other major barrier reefs include the Belize Barrier Reef, the second largest in the world, the New Caledonian barrier reef, and the barrier reef surrounding Mayotte. Barrier reefs support diverse ecosystems of corals, fish, and invertebrates in their lagoons and outer slopes.
