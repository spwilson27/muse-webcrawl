Pace Egging

Pace Egging is a traditional Easter custom of northern England, centred on the performance of a folk play—the Pace Egg Play—and the giving or collecting of decorated eggs. The name derives from "Pasch," from the Hebrew Pesach (Passover), via the Latin and Old English names for Easter. Pace Egging was once widespread across Lancashire, Yorkshire, Cumbria, and neighbouring counties.

The Pace Egg Play

The play is a form of mummers' play, typically performed by groups of local men or boys (the Pace Eggers) in pubs, houses, and public spaces during the Easter season. Its plot is of the hero-combat type: Saint George fights a succession of opponents—such as Bold Slasher, the Black Prince of Paradise, and the King of Egypt—one of whom is killed and then revived by a comic Doctor. Other stock characters include Tosspot, who collects money or eggs from the audience, and characters such as Old Pecker and the Hobby Horse in some local variants.

Customs

Traditionally, children also went from house to house singing Pace Egging songs and asking for eggs, which were dyed or decorated—pace eggs—and rolled down hills in egg-rolling contests on Easter Monday or used in "jarping," a game in which eggs are knocked together until one cracks. The custom declined in the early twentieth century but was revived in several communities; notable revivals include the plays at Heptonstall and Midgley in West Yorkshire.

Texts

Chapbook versions of the play, such as "The Pace-Eggers' Play" printed in the early nineteenth century, preserve the texts of local variants. Folklorists have compared the death-and-resurrection theme to other seasonal folk dramas in the British Isles.
