Metamaterial

A metamaterial (from the Greek meta, meaning "beyond") is a material engineered to have a property that is not found in naturally occurring materials. Metamaterials are made from assemblies of multiple elements fashioned from composite materials such as metals and plastics, usually arranged in repeating patterns at scales smaller than the wavelength of the phenomena they influence.

Properties and research

Metamaterials derive their properties not from the properties of their base materials, but from their precisely designed structures. Electromagnetic metamaterials can exhibit a negative index of refraction, enabling proposed "superlenses" and invisibility-cloak experiments at microwave frequencies. Acoustic and seismic metamaterials aim to control sound and vibration, while mechanical metamaterials display unusual properties such as negative Poisson's ratio.

History

Theoretical foundations were laid by Victor Veselago in 1967, who predicted materials with simultaneously negative permittivity and permeability. Experimental demonstrations followed around 2000 with the work of John Pendry and David R. Smith's group, sparking rapid growth in the field.
