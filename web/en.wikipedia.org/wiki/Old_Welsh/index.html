Old Welsh (Welsh: Hen Gymraeg) is the stage of the Welsh language spoken from roughly the 9th century until the 12th century, when it developed into Middle Welsh. It succeeded the Primitive Welsh period and is attested mainly in glosses, marginalia, short memoranda and a handful of longer texts written in the margins of Latin manuscripts.

Among the best-known witnesses are the glosses in the Cambridge Juvencus manuscript, the Computus fragment, the Lichfield Gospels (Book of St Chad) memoranda — including the famous Surexit memorandum — and glosses on Martianus Capella and on Ovid's Ars Amatoria.

Attestation

No complete books in Old Welsh survive; the language is known from several hundred glosses and a few continuous passages, most importantly the Surexit memorandum, a legal note recording the settlement of a land dispute, written in the margin of the Lichfield Gospels in the 9th century.

Phonology

Old Welsh retained final syllables that were later lost in Middle Welsh, and shows the accent shift from the final to the penultimate syllable. The diphthongisation of stressed vowels and the affection (umlaut) of vowels by a following high vowel, begun in Primitive Welsh, are fully visible in Old Welsh forms.

Orthography

Old Welsh spelling was heavily influenced by Latin conventions; the letter u often represents the vowel later written u and the consonant /w/, while doubled consonants and the use of k before front vowels appear sporadically.
