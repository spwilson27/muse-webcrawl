Roosevelt Island is an ice-covered island lying within the eastern part of the Ross Ice Shelf in Antarctica. It is about 130 km long in a northwest–southeast direction and 65 km wide, with an area of roughly 7,500 km². Its ice surface rises to about 550 m above sea level, but the bedrock beneath lies largely below sea level: the "island" is a grounded ice rise, where the floating ice shelf thickens and grounds on a submarine ridge.

Discovery and naming

The island was discovered in 1934 by the Byrd Antarctic Expedition (1933–1935), whose members recognised from the air that what had appeared to be part of the mainland coast was in fact a separate feature. Rear Admiral Richard E. Byrd named it for Franklin D. Roosevelt, President of the United States. Its outline was delineated more precisely by later airborne and satellite surveys.

Scientific research

Roosevelt Island is significant for glaciology because its ice-flow pattern — a "Raymond effect" dome produced by ice flowing over grounded bedrock — preserves a well-ordered climate record. Between 2011 and 2013, the Roosevelt Island Climate Evolution (RICE) project, led by New Zealand with international partners, drilled a 764 m ice core through the island to bedrock. The core provides a climate history spanning more than 60,000 years and is used to study the past stability of the Ross Ice Shelf and the West Antarctic Ice Sheet, with implications for future sea-level rise.
