Compassion fatigue

[Image: Caregiver fatigue]

Compassion fatigue is a condition characterised by emotional and physical exhaustion leading to a diminished ability to empathise or feel compassion for others, often described as the negative cost of caring. It is sometimes considered a form of secondary traumatic stress experienced by those who care for people who are suffering, including nurses, physicians, therapists, first responders, journalists, aid workers and animal-care staff.

Concept and symptoms

The term was popularised in the 1990s through the work of Charles Figley, who described compassion fatigue as the "cost of caring" and developed the Professional Quality of Life scale (ProQOL) to measure its components: compassion satisfaction, burnout and secondary traumatic stress. Reported symptoms include emotional numbness, intrusive thoughts, avoidance, irritability, difficulty sleeping, reduced job satisfaction and a declining sense of personal accomplishment.

Related concepts

Compassion fatigue overlaps with, but is distinguished from, burnout (a gradual response to chronic workplace stress not necessarily tied to trauma exposure), vicarious traumatisation (changes in a helper's worldview after empathic engagement with trauma survivors), and moral injury (distress from acting against one's values). A parallel use of the term in media studies describes the desensitisation of audiences to suffering through repeated exposure to images of crises.

Prevention and treatment

Approaches to prevention and recovery emphasise manageable caseloads and workload boundaries, regular supervision and peer support, self-care (sleep, exercise and time away from caring roles), mindfulness and resilience training, and organisational changes that reduce exposure to traumatic material. Research interest in the topic increased markedly during the COVID-19 pandemic, when healthcare workers faced sustained exposure to patient suffering and death.
