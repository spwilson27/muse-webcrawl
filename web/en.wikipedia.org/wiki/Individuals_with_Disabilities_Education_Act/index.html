Individuals with Disabilities Education Act

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) is a United States federal law that governs how states and public agencies provide early intervention, special education and related services to children with disabilities. First enacted in 1975 as the Education for All Handicapped Children Act, it was renamed IDEA in 1990 and most recently reauthorized in 2004. It serves more than 7.5 million children and youth.

Core principles

The Act is built around six principles: a free appropriate public education (FAPE) for every eligible child at public expense; appropriate, nondiscriminatory evaluation; an Individualized Education Program (IEP)—a written plan developed with the child's parents that sets goals and specifies services; education in the least restrictive environment (LRE), meaning children are educated with non-disabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate; procedural safeguards, including parental consent, notice, and due-process hearings; and parent and student participation in decision-making.

Structure

Part B, the largest part, covers school-age children (ages 3–21) in 13 disability categories, including autism, specific learning disabilities, speech or language impairments, emotional disturbance and intellectual disability. Part C covers early intervention for infants and toddlers (birth to age 2) through Individualized Family Service Plans. Federal funding has historically fallen well below the 40 per cent of excess costs Congress contemplated in 1975, a persistent point of contention with states and districts.

Landmark cases

Supreme Court decisions have shaped the Act's meaning: Board of Education v. Rowley (1982) held that FAPE requires an education reasonably calculated to enable progress; Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District (2017) raised that standard, holding an IEP must be "reasonably calculated to enable a child to make progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances".
