William Henry "Chick" Webb (10 February 1905 – 16 June 1939) was an American jazz and swing music drummer and bandleader. Despite a spinal deformity that left him short in stature and in frail health, Webb became one of the most celebrated drummers of the big band era, renowned for his powerful, virtuosic playing and for the tight orchestra he led at Harlem's Savoy Ballroom.

Webb's orchestra launched the career of singer Ella Fitzgerald, whom he adopted as his protégée after she won an amateur contest at the Apollo Theater in 1934. Together they recorded the hit "A-Tisket, A-Tasket" in 1938, one of the best-selling songs of the swing era.

Early life

Webb was born in Baltimore, Maryland. As a child he suffered a fall that damaged his spine and led to tuberculosis of the spine; he took up drumming partly as therapy, selling newspapers to buy his first drum kit. He moved to New York City in 1925 and was soon leading his own bands in Harlem clubs.

The Savoy Ballroom

In 1931 Webb's orchestra became the house band at the Savoy Ballroom, the center of Harlem's dance culture. The band became famous for winning "battles of the bands" against rival orchestras, including famous contests against the bands of Benny Goodman and Count Basie in 1937 and 1938. Webb's precision drumming and the band's arrangements by Edgar Sampson made it one of the most formidable ensembles of the era.

Death and legacy

Webb's health deteriorated in 1938–39; he died in Baltimore on 16 June 1939 following spinal surgery. Ella Fitzgerald fronted the band for several years after his death. Drummers such as Gene Krupa and Buddy Rich cited Webb as a major influence, and he is often ranked among the greatest jazz drummers of all time.
