Persian literature

Persian literature (Persian: ادبیات فارسی) comprises oral compositions and written texts in the Persian language and is one of the world's oldest literatures. It spans two and a half millennia and has been produced across a wide cultural sphere — modern Iran, Afghanistan, Tajikistan, and historically the Persianate world from the Balkans to Central and South Asia. Its best-known works include Ferdowsi's epic Shahnameh, the poetry of Rumi, Hafez, Saadi, and Omar Khayyam, and the prose of the Gulistan.

Pre-Islamic literature

The earliest surviving Persian texts are the Old Persian royal inscriptions of the Achaemenid Empire, such as the Behistun Inscription of Darius I, and the Zoroastrian scriptures of the Avesta. Middle Persian (Pahlavi) literature of the Sasanian era included religious, legal, and heroic material, much of it later translated into Arabic and New Persian.

Classical New Persian

New Persian, written in the Arabic script, emerged in the 9th–10th centuries in Khorasan and Transoxiana. Rudaki is honoured as the father of Persian poetry. Around 1010 Ferdowsi completed the Shahnameh ("Book of Kings"), a 50,000-couplet epic of Iran's mythical and historical past that became central to Iranian identity. The following centuries produced the quatrains of Omar Khayyam, the romantic epics of Nizami Ganjavi, the didactic and ethical works of Saadi of Shiraz (Gulistan and Bustan), the mystical Masnavi of Jalal al-Din Rumi, and the lyric ghazals of Hafez, whose Divan remains a household book in Iran.

Persianate spread

Persian served as a literary and administrative language across Ottoman Anatolia, Mughal India, and Central Asia, shaping Urdu, Turkish, and other literatures. Genres such as the ghazal, qasida, masnavi, and ruba'i were cultivated far beyond Iran's borders.

Modern literature

The 20th century brought modernist poetry (Nima Yooshij's "new poetry", Forough Farrokhzad, Ahmad Shamlou) and landmark fiction, including Sadegh Hedayat's novel The Blind Owl (1936). Contemporary Iranian, Afghan (Dari), and Tajik literatures continue the tradition in divergent political and linguistic settings, alongside a large diaspora literature.
