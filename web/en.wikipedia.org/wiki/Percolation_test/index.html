Percolation test

A percolation test (colloquially called a perc test) is a test to determine the water absorption rate of soil (that is, its capacity for percolation) in preparation for the building of a septic drain field (leach field) or infiltration basin. The results of a percolation test are required to properly design a septic system. In its broadest terms, percolation testing observes how quickly a known volume of water dissipates into the subsoil of a drilled hole of known surface area. In most jurisdictions a satisfactory perc test — or a suitable alternative site evaluation — is a precondition for a permit to install an on-site sewage treatment system.

Procedure

Testing typically involves digging one or more holes in the proposed drain field area to a specified depth (or at least to the depth at which effluent will be distributed), pre-soaking the holes to simulate wet conditions, then filling them with water and timing the drop of the water level. The rate is expressed in minutes per inch (MPI) or the inverse, inches per hour. Soil texture, structure, the presence of a seasonal high water table, and depth to bedrock are recorded at the same time, often by means of a deeper soil profile pit.

Exact methods vary by jurisdiction. In the United Kingdom, the test described in British Standard BS 6297 uses a trial hole and a measurement of the time taken for the water level to fall between fixed fractions of its depth, repeated three times, to compute a percolation value used to size drainage fields. In the United States, county health departments usually prescribe hole dimensions, pre-soak periods, and acceptable rate ranges.

Interpretation

Soil that drains too slowly cannot treat and disperse effluent adequately, leading to ponding and system failure; soil that drains too quickly may allow effluent to reach groundwater insufficiently treated. Very sandy soils with rapid percolation may still be suitable if additional treatment or distribution measures are used, while heavy clays with very slow rates may require an engineered system such as a mound system, or may render a lot unbuildable for conventional septic purposes. Acceptable ranges are commonly on the order of a few minutes per inch to 60 minutes per inch, but limits are set locally.

Alternatives

Because percolation rates measured in a test hole depend strongly on the method used and on soil moisture, some regulators have shifted toward soil morphological evaluation — describing soil texture, structure, and redoximorphic features in a profile pit — in place of, or in addition to, the traditional perc test. Falling-head and constant-head permeameter tests are also used in geotechnical practice for related purposes.
