Herbig–Haro object

Herbig–Haro (HH) objects are bright patches of nebulosity associated with newborn stars. They are formed when narrow jets of partially ionised gas ejected by stars collide with nearby clouds of gas and dust at several hundred kilometres per second. Herbig–Haro objects are commonly found in star-forming regions, and several may be seen around a single star, aligned with its rotational axis.

Discovery and history

The objects were first observed in the late 19th century by Sherburne Wesley Burnham, but were not recognised as a distinct class of emission nebula until the 1940s. The first astronomers to study them in detail were George Herbig and Guillermo Haro, after whom they are named. Herbig and Haro were working independently on studies of star formation when they catalogued these small nebulosities in the Orion Nebula.

Physical characteristics

HH objects are transient phenomena lasting only a few tens of thousands of years. They evolve visibly over timescales of a few years as they move rapidly away from their parent star into the interstellar medium. The gas ejected in the jets travels at speeds of 100–1,000 km/s; shock waves heat the material to thousands of degrees, producing characteristic emission lines of hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and sulphur. Hubble Space Telescope observations have revealed complex bow-shock structures in many HH objects. Well-known examples include HH 1/2 near Orion, HH 34, and HH 46/47 in the Gum Nebula.

Numbers and distribution

More than a thousand individual HH objects are known. They are present in large numbers in nearby star-forming complexes such as Orion, Perseus and Chamaeleon, and trace the earliest, most deeply embedded phase of stellar evolution, when the young star is still accreting from a circumstellar disc while expelling material along its poles.
