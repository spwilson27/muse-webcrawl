The Great Kantō earthquake (関東大地震, Kantō Dai-jishin) struck the Kantō Plain on Japan's main island of Honshu at 11:58 on Saturday, 1 September 1923. The magnitude 7.9 earthquake, centred beneath Sagami Bay, devastated Tokyo and the port city of Yokohama, and caused widespread damage across the prefectures of Chiba, Kanagawa, and Shizuoka. It remains one of the deadliest natural disasters in Japanese history.

Damage and fire

The quake struck just as midday cooking fires were lit across the region. Broken stoves and chimneys ignited fires that, fanned by strong winds from a typhoon offshore, merged into firestorms. In the single greatest loss of life, some 38,000 people who had taken refuge in an open space at the Honjo Army Clothing Depot in Tokyo were killed by a fire whirl. Roughly half of Tokyo and most of Yokohama were destroyed; a tsunami of up to about 12 metres struck parts of the coast, and landslides buried villages and a train in the mountains west of the bay. The confirmed death toll was about 105,000, including thousands of people never identified.

Aftermath

In the chaos, false rumours that Koreans were poisoning wells and setting fires led to massacres by vigilantes, police, and soldiers in which historians estimate several thousand Korean residents, along with Chinese and Japanese mistaken for Koreans, were murdered — the Kantō Massacre. Martial law was declared, and the government of Yamamoto Gonnohyōe undertook an ambitious reconstruction of Tokyo under Home Minister Gotō Shinpei, widening streets, creating parks as firebreaks, and land readjustment across the burnt districts, though the final plan was scaled back on cost grounds. The disaster shaped Japanese building codes, disaster-prevention culture — 1 September is now Disaster Prevention Day — and, through its economic aftershocks, contributed to the financial crisis of 1927.
