Saint Lucy's Day

Saint Lucy's Day, also called the Feast of Saint Lucy, is a Christian feast day observed on 13 December commemorating Saint Lucy of Syracuse, a 3rd-century virgin martyr. The feast is celebrated most famously in Scandinavia — especially Sweden, Norway and Swedish-speaking Finland — as well as in Italy and other countries with Catholic or Lutheran traditions.

Origins

Lucy (Lucia) was martyred during the Diocletianic persecution around 304 AD. Her name derives from the Latin lux, "light," and her feast falls near the darkest time of the northern year; under the old Julian calendar it coincided closely with the winter solstice, and in Swedish tradition the night before, Lusse night, was regarded as the longest night of the year.

Scandinavian celebrations

In Sweden, the day is marked by processions (luciatåg) led by a girl chosen as the Lucia, wearing a white dress, a red sash and a crown of candles (now usually battery-powered), accompanied by handmaidens (tärnor), star boys (stjärngossar) and sometimes attendants dressed as Saint Stephen. They sing traditional songs, including "Sankta Lucia," adapted from a Neapolitan melody. Processions take place in schools, churches, hospitals and workplaces, and a national Lucia is selected annually. Saffron buns called lussekatter and gingersnaps are served with coffee or mulled wine (glögg).

Elsewhere

In Syracuse, Sicily, the saint's birthplace, a silver statue of Lucy is carried in procession. Celebrations also occur in Denmark, Iceland, Estonia, Croatia's Dalmatian coast (where wheat is planted on the day), Malta, and among Scandinavian diaspora communities.
