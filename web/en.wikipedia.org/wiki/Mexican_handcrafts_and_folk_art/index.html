Mexican handcrafts and folk art

Mexican handcrafts and folk art is a complex collection of objects made for utilitarian, decorative or other purposes, based on the traditions of the many indigenous and European peoples of Mexico. It is one of the country's major cultural exports and an important part of Mexican national identity, blending pre-Hispanic techniques and motifs with those introduced from Europe and, later, Asia.

Handcraft production in Mexico has its origins in pre-Hispanic times, when Mesoamerican peoples produced pottery, textiles, featherwork, stone carving, and metalwork of gold and silver. The Spanish conquest introduced new techniques such as the potter's wheel, glazes, wrought iron, and European loom weaving, while suppressing some indigenous crafts, especially featherwork and certain ritual objects.

History

During the colonial period, crafts were organized in guilds and oriented both to local consumption and to export through the Manila galleon trade, which brought Asian influences such as lacquerware (maque) and certain ceramic styles. After independence, handcraft production declined with industrialization, but in the 20th century the Mexican government and intellectuals such as Dr. Atl (Gerardo Murillo) promoted folk art as a symbol of national identity. Institutions such as FONART (Fondo Nacional para el Fomento de las Artesanías), created in 1974, support artisans and market their work.

Major traditions

Prominent craft traditions include: the black clay (barro negro) pottery of San Bartolo Coyotepec, Oaxaca; Talavera pottery of Puebla; the green-glazed pottery of Santa María Atzompa; copper work of Santa Clara del Cobre, Michoacán; alebrijes, the brightly painted wooden carvings of Oaxaca; trees of life (árboles de la vida) from Metepec; textiles including rebozos, sarapes from Saltillo, and the embroidered clothing of indigenous communities in Chiapas, Oaxaca, and Yucatán; lacquered boxes from Olinalá, Guerrero; silver jewelry from Taxco; blown glass; amate bark paper from San Pablito, Puebla; and papel picado, the perforated tissue paper banners used in festivals.

Cultural significance

Handcrafts are closely tied to Mexico's festival calendar, above all Day of the Dead (Día de Muertos), for which artisans produce sugar skulls, marigold decorations, candles, and the famous catrina figures popularized by José Guadalupe Posada's engraving La Calavera Catrina. Museums dedicated to folk art include the Museo de Arte Popular in Mexico City, opened in 2006. Despite competition from cheap industrial and imported goods, Mexican folk art remains a vital economic activity for rural and indigenous communities and a major draw for tourism.
