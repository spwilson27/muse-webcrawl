Gothic plate armour

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gothic armour, c. 1480, characterised by fluting and pointed forms.

Gothic plate armour (German: Gotischer Plattenharnisch) was the style of full plate armour worn in the Holy Roman Empire and elsewhere in Europe during the late 15th century. It is distinguished from the contemporary Italian (Milanese) style by its slender, elongated proportions, sharp points, and the decorative fluting worked into the steel surfaces.

Characteristics

Gothic armour favoured graceful, attenuated forms echoing the lines of late Gothic architecture. Its plates are frequently reinforced and decorated with radiating flutes that also served to deflect weapon points. Typical components include the sallet (a one-piece helmet with an elongated tail), the pointed sabatons protecting the feet, sharply cusped pauldrons, and gauntlets with articulated, pointed cuffs. Surfaces were often left bright and undecorated apart from the fluting, sometimes with brass borders.

Development

The style flourished roughly between 1440 and 1500, centred on armour-making cities such as Nuremberg, Augsburg, and Landshut. Around 1500 it gave way to the rounded, embossed forms of Maximilian armour, whose dense parallel fluting drew partly on Gothic precedent. Surviving Gothic harnesses are prized museum pieces; a famous near-complete example associated with Archduke Sigismund of Tyrol is preserved in Vienna.
