Danube Swabians

The Danube Swabians (German: Donauschwaben) is a collective term for the ethnic German-speaking population that lived in the south-eastern Danube basin of Central and Eastern Europe — in present-day Hungary, Serbia, Croatia and Romania — having settled there largely in the 18th century. Although many settlers came from Swabia, they in fact originated from many parts of the Holy Roman Empire, including Lorraine, Alsace, the Palatinate and Hesse.

Their communities flourished for two centuries in regions such as the Banat, Bačka, Syrmia, Slavonia and southwestern Hungary ("Turkish Swabia"), until the Second World War, after which most Danube Swabians were expelled, expropriated or emigrated.

History

After the Habsburg reconquest of Hungary from the Ottomans, the imperial court recruited settlers to repopulate the devastated Pannonian plain. In three great waves (the Carolingian, Theresian and Josephinian settlements, c. 1718–1787) tens of thousands of families travelled down the Danube from Ulm on flatboats known as Ulmer Schachteln. They founded hundreds of villages, drained marshes, and became noted farmers; the Banat developed into a granary of the empire. Settlers were granted land, tax exemptions and religious toleration under Joseph II.

19th and early 20th centuries

The Danube Swabian population reached roughly 1.5 million by 1910. After the First World War, their homeland was divided among Hungary, Romania and Yugoslavia by the Treaty of Trianon, making them minorities in three states and subject to varying degrees of Magyarisation and assimilation pressure.

Second World War and expulsion

During the Second World War some Danube Swabians were conscripted into or volunteered for German forces, including the Waffen-SS division "Prinz Eugen". After 1944, communities suffered flight, internment camps in Yugoslavia (where tens of thousands died), collective expropriation and expulsion from Yugoslavia and Romania, while Hungary expelled about half of its ethnic Germans to the American occupation zone of Germany. By the early 1950s most Danube Swabians lived in West Germany, Austria and overseas countries including the United States, Canada, Brazil and Australia.

Culture

Danube Swabian culture is preserved by homeland associations (Landsmannschaften), museums such as the Donauschwäbisches Zentralmuseum in Ulm, traditional costume (Tracht), cuisine blending German, Hungarian and Balkan influences, and annual gatherings. Nobel laureate Herta Müller and writer Adam Müller-Guttenbrunn are among the best-known figures of Danube Swabian origin.
