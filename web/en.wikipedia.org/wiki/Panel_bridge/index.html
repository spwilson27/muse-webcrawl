Panel bridge

A panel bridge is a type of modular, prefabricated truss bridge whose structure is built up from interchangeable factory-made panels that are pinned or bolted together on site. The concept allows bridges of varying span and load capacity to be assembled rapidly from a stock of standard components, without heavy lifting equipment or specialised erection plant, and to be dismantled and reused elsewhere.

Design

The load-bearing elements are rectangular truss panels, typically about 3 metres (10 ft) long, which form the two side girders of the bridge. Strength is increased by adding extra panels side by side ("double" or "triple" configurations) or by stacking a second storey of panels on top of the first. Transverse beams (transoms) span between the girders to support the deck, and the whole structure is usually launched across the gap from one bank by building it out over rollers with a lightweight launching nose attached, so no temporary supports in the gap are needed.

History

The panel bridge is epitomised by the Bailey bridge, designed by Sir Donald Bailey and adopted by British and Allied forces in the Second World War. Its panels could be carried by truck, handled by a small party of soldiers, and assembled into bridges strong enough for tanks — qualities that revolutionised military bridging. Later standardised systems, such as the British Medium Girder Bridge and the Mabey logistic support bridge, refined the concept, and civilian derivatives are widely used for temporary road bridges, construction access and emergency replacement of flood- or earthquake-damaged crossings.

Applications

Panel bridges serve in military engineering, disaster relief, and civil works wherever a crossing must be provided quickly and later removed. Because every component is standard and reusable, the same stock of panels can serve as a short pedestrian footbridge one month and a long, high-capacity vehicle bridge the next.
