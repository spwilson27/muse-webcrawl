Friedrich Albert Moritz Schlick (April 14, 1882 – June 22, 1936) was a German philosopher, physicist, and the founding father of logical positivism and the Vienna Circle. His work on the philosophy of science and the theory of knowledge profoundly influenced 20th-century analytic philosophy.

Early life and education

Schlick was born in Berlin into a wealthy family. He studied physics at the University of Heidelberg, the University of Lausanne, and finally the University of Berlin, where he completed his doctorate in 1904 under Max Planck with a dissertation on the reflection of light in an inhomogeneous medium. In 1907 he married Blanche Hardy, an American, and published his first philosophical book, Lebensweisheit (1908), on the wisdom of life and eudaemonism. His habilitation followed at the University of Rostock in 1910 with a work on truth in modern logic.

Career

Schlick taught at Rostock and Kiel before accepting, in 1922, the chair in the philosophy of the inductive sciences at the University of Vienna — a chair previously held by Ernst Mach and Ludwig Boltzmann. His early masterwork, Allgemeine Erkenntnislehre (General Theory of Knowledge, 1918), criticized neo-Kantian synthetic a priori knowledge and anticipates themes of his later philosophy. Schlick also wrote influential interpretive works on Einstein's theory of relativity, which Einstein himself praised.

The Vienna Circle

In Vienna, Schlick gathered around him a discussion group of scientists and philosophers — including Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath, Herbert Feigl, Friedrich Waismann, and, as an influential visitor, Ludwig Wittgenstein — which became famous as the Vienna Circle. In 1929 the Circle published its manifesto, Wissenschaftliche Weltauffassung: Der Wiener Kreis (The Scientific Conception of the World: The Vienna Circle), dedicated to Schlick. Central to the movement was the verification principle: the meaning of a statement is its method of verification, a criterion under which much traditional metaphysics was judged cognitively meaningless. Schlick's essays, such as "Positivismus und Realismus" (1932) and "Über das Fundament der Erkenntnis" (1934), set out his mature views. He also wrote on ethics in Fragen der Ethik (1930), arguing that moral judgments express desires rather than facts.

Death

On June 22, 1936, Schlick was shot and killed on the steps of the University of Vienna by a former student, Johann Nelböck, who had repeatedly threatened him. The murder, and the sympathetic press coverage of the killer in the climate of rising Austrofascism and antisemitism, effectively marked the end of the Vienna Circle in Austria; its members soon dispersed abroad, carrying logical positivism to Britain and the United States.
