Menstrual pad

A menstrual pad, also known as a sanitary pad, sanitary towel, sanitary napkin or feminine napkin, is an absorbent item worn in the underwear by women who are menstruating, bleeding after giving birth, recovering from gynecologic surgery, experiencing a miscarriage or abortion, or in any other situation where it is necessary to absorb a flow of blood from the vagina. A menstrual pad is a type of menstrual hygiene product that is worn externally, unlike tampons and menstrual cups, which are worn inside the vagina.

Design

Modern disposable pads consist of an absorbent core of wood pulp and superabsorbent polymers placed between a fluid-permeable top sheet and a waterproof back sheet, usually with adhesive to fix the pad to underwear and optionally "wings" that fold over the sides. Pads come in many sizes and absorbencies, including panty liners for light flow and overnight pads. Cloth menstrual pads are washable and reusable alternatives, typically made of cotton flannel or similar fabrics.

History

Historically, women used various materials — rags, moss, grass, animal skins — to manage menstruation. The first commercial disposable pads were developed in the late 19th century, drawing on nurses' wartime improvisation with wood-pulp bandages during World War I; Johnson & Johnson's Modess and Kimberly-Clark's Kotex (launched in 1920) were among the earliest major brands. Self-adhesive pads replaced belted designs in the 1970s.

Environmental and health considerations

Disposable pads contribute to landfill waste, prompting interest in reusable cloth pads and period underwear. Access to menstrual products remains a public-health and equity issue worldwide, sometimes termed "period poverty".
