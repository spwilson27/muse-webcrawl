Hiwi (volunteer)

Hiwi is a German abbreviation of Hilfswilliger, meaning "auxiliary volunteer". The term is best known for the Soviet citizens — prisoners of war and civilians from occupied territories — who served the German Wehrmacht and SS as auxiliaries during the Second World War, in roles ranging from cooks, drivers and labourers to armed units and participation in mass shootings. Estimates of their total number range from several hundred thousand to over one million by 1944. After the war, many Hiwis who fell into Soviet hands were imprisoned or executed as traitors; some were forcibly repatriated from Western Allied custody.

A Hiwi distributing ammunition during the Battle of Stalingrad, 1942.

Origins

From the first weeks of Operation Barbarossa (June 1941), German units facing manpower shortages recruited local helpers informally from among the millions of captured Red Army soldiers — for whom service offered escape from starvation in POW camps, where over three million died — and from anti-communist or nationalist civilians, including Balts, Ukrainians, Cossacks and Central Asians. Hitler initially opposed arming Slavs, but necessity expanded their use; by 1943 many German divisions on the Eastern Front had 10–15% Hiwi complements in supply, construction and support roles.

Roles and crimes

Most Hiwis served unarmed in logistics, but some were armed as guards and in anti-partisan warfare. "Trawniki men", recruited from POW camps and trained at the Trawniki camp, staffed the Operation Reinhard extermination camps and participated in mass shootings such as Operation Harvest Festival (November 1943). Hiwi service thus spanned coerced labour, collaboration, and direct participation in the Holocaust — distinctions historians stress when assessing individual responsibility.

Aftermath

Under the Yalta agreements, Western Allies repatriated Soviet citizens, including Hiwis, many to execution or the Gulag. Some Hiwis emigrated west, occasionally concealing wartime roles; a few were prosecuted decades later. In modern German usage, Hiwi survives colloquially — stripped of its wartime meaning — for research assistants (wissenschaftliche Hilfskraft, "SHK"/"Hiwi") at universities.
