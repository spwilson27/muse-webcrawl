Wayfinding

Wayfinding encompasses the ways in which people orient themselves in physical space and navigate from one place to another. It involves cognitive processes — building mental maps, recognising landmarks, and following routes — as well as the design of signage, maps, and architectural cues that support those processes.

Cognitive aspects

Psychologists distinguish route knowledge (a sequence of turns and landmarks), survey knowledge (an overall map-like understanding), and landmark knowledge (recognisable reference points). People differ in wayfinding ability, and factors such as attention, spatial working memory, and familiarity with the environment all play a role.

Design

Wayfinding design in buildings, campuses, hospitals, and cities uses consistent signage hierarchies, colour coding, sightlines, and distinctive landmarks to reduce confusion and stress. Digital wayfinding — indoor positioning, mobile maps, and augmented reality overlays — increasingly supplements physical signage.

Navigation traditions

Traditional wayfinding includes Polynesian navigation by stars, currents, and bird flight, as well as the use of compasses, charts, and, in the modern era, satellite navigation systems.
