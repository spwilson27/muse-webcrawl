Cash crop

Cash crops are grown for sale and profit rather than the grower’s own use.

A cash crop is an agricultural crop grown primarily for sale and profit rather than for direct consumption by the farmer’s household. The term contrasts with subsistence crops, which are grown mainly to feed the producer. Cash crops may be foods, such as coffee and cocoa, or non-food commodities, such as cotton and tobacco.

Economics

Cash cropping links farmers to regional and global markets. It can raise incomes and finance purchased inputs, but it also exposes producers to price volatility, transport costs, and dependence on buyers or credit. Decisions about cash crops depend on climate, soil, labour, infrastructure, and access to processing and export channels.

History and debate

Plantation systems in the colonial era were organized around export cash crops including sugar, tobacco, cotton, and indigo, often using enslaved or coerced labour. Modern development debates consider whether cash cropping improves rural livelihoods or undermines food security when land and labour shift away from staple foods; outcomes vary with local institutions and markets.

Examples

Widely traded cash crops include coffee, tea, cocoa, cotton, rubber, sugar cane, tobacco, oil palm, and many fruits and vegetables grown for urban or export markets.
