Alexander Pope (21 May 1688 – 30 May 1744) was an English poet, translator, and satirist of the Enlightenment era who is considered one of the most prominent English poets of the early 18th century. An exponent of Augustan literature, Pope is best known for his satirical and discursive poetry, including The Rape of the Lock, The Dunciad, and An Essay on Criticism, and for his translation of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey.

Pope is famous for his use of the heroic couplet, and he is the second-most frequently quoted writer in The Oxford Dictionary of Quotations, after William Shakespeare. Lines such as "To err is human; to forgive, divine", "A little learning is a dang'rous thing", and "Fools rush in where angels fear to tread" have entered common usage.

Early life

Pope was born in Lombard Street, London, into a Catholic family at a time when anti-Catholic laws severely restricted his education; he was largely self-educated, reading the classics in Latin, Greek, French, and Italian. At the age of twelve he contracted Pott's disease (tuberculosis of the spine), which stunted his growth — he stood only 4 feet 6 inches (1.37 m) tall — and left him with a lifelong hunchback and poor health.

Career

Pope's first major work, An Essay on Criticism (1711), was published when he was 23 and brought him wide attention. The Rape of the Lock (1712, expanded 1714), a mock-heroic poem satirising a real quarrel between two aristocratic families, is considered one of the finest mock-epics in English. Between 1715 and 1720 he published his subscription translation of the Iliad, which made him financially independent; his Odyssey translation followed in 1725–26, completed with the help of William Broome and Elijah Fenton.

His later work grew more satirical and political. The Dunciad (1728–43) mocked the cultural decline he saw around him, while his Essay on Man (1733–34) explored philosophical themes in verse, including the famous couplet "Hope springs eternal in the human breast".

Death and legacy

Pope died at his villa in Twickenham on 30 May 1744. His mastery of the heroic couplet influenced English poetry for a century, and his wit and aphorisms remain embedded in the language.
