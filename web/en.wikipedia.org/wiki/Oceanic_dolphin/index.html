Oceanic dolphin

Oceanic dolphins are the members of the family Delphinidae, the largest family of cetaceans, comprising roughly 40 species in about 17 genera. They range from the familiar bottlenose dolphin to the largest member, the orca (killer whale), and the pilot whales. Oceanic dolphins are distinguished from the river dolphins and porpoises, and are found in all the world's oceans, chiefly in shallower seas over continental shelves.

Characteristics

Oceanic dolphins are small to medium-sized toothed whales, from about 1.2 m (Hector's dolphin) to the orca at up to 9.8 m. Most have a distinct beak, a curved dorsal fin and conical teeth for seizing fish and squid. Their streamlined bodies and powerful flukes make them fast, agile swimmers; common dolphins can exceed 50 km/h in bursts.

Behaviour and social structure

Delphinids are highly social, living in pods that range from a few individuals to "superpods" of thousands. Many species display complex social alliances, cooperative herding of prey, babysitting of calves by other pod members, and cultural transmission of foraging techniques — such as sponge-carrying in Shark Bay, Australia. Diet is dominated by fish and squid, though orcas also hunt marine mammals and sharks.

Echolocation and communication

Like other toothed whales, oceanic dolphins navigate and hunt using echolocation, generating clicks in the nasal passages and interpreting echoes with the fatty melon of the forehead. They also communicate with whistles, including individually distinctive "signature whistles" in bottlenose dolphins, and burst-pulse sounds.

Genera and species

Well-known genera include Tursiops (bottlenose dolphins), Delphinus (common dolphins), Stenella (spinner and spotted dolphins), Lagenorhynchus, Grampus (Risso's dolphin), Globicephala (pilot whales) and Orcinus (orca). Taxonomy is actively revised as genetic studies reveal cryptic species and reorganise relationships.

Threats and conservation

Oceanic dolphins face bycatch in fishing gear, drive hunts in a few countries, prey depletion, noise and chemical pollution, and habitat change. Most species are of Least Concern globally, but some populations, such as Maui dolphin and the orcas of the Salish Sea, are critically endangered.
