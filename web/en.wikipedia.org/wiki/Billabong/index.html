A billabong (UK: /ˈbɪləbɒŋ/, US: /ˈbɪləbɔːŋ/) is an Australian term for a seasonal oxbow lake, an isolated pond left behind after a river changes course. Billabongs are usually formed when the path of a creek or river changes, leaving the former branch with a dead end. They are of cultural and ecological significance to Aboriginal Australians, and serve as refuges for wildlife during dry seasons.

Etymology

The word derives from the Wiradjuri language of south-western New South Wales: bila means "river" and bong or bung means "dead". One definition describes a billabong as "a waterhole that dries up in the dry season".

Ecology

Billabongs support distinct ecosystems. Famous examples occur in Kakadu National Park in the Northern Territory, such as Yellow Water Billabong, home to saltwater crocodiles, barramundi, magpie geese and a wealth of bird life. In the Murray–Darling Basin, river regulation has reduced the flooding that once refreshed billabongs, and environmental water allocations are used to maintain their health.

In culture

The billabong is immortalised in Australia's most famous folk song, "Waltzing Matilda" by Banjo Paterson, whose swagman camps "by a billabong under the shade of a coolibah tree". The word also gave its name to the Australian surf and sportswear company Billabong, founded in 1973.
