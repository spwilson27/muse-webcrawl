macOS

macOS, previously styled Mac OS X and later OS X, is a proprietary graphical operating system developed and marketed by Apple Inc. since 2001. It is the primary operating system for Apple's Mac computers and the second most widely used desktop operating system after Microsoft Windows. macOS is built on the Darwin foundation, combining the Mach kernel and components derived from FreeBSD, and it is certified as a UNIX 03 implementation.

History

macOS descends from NeXTSTEP, the operating system developed by NeXT, the company Steve Jobs founded after leaving Apple. Apple acquired NeXT in 1997, bringing Jobs back and making NeXTSTEP the basis of its next-generation system. The first end-user release, Mac OS X 10.0 "Cheetah", shipped in March 2001 with the Aqua user interface. Subsequent versions were named after big cats (Puma, Jaguar, Panther, Tiger, Leopard, Snow Leopard, Lion, Mountain Lion) and then, from OS X 10.9 Mavericks in 2013, after places in California, including Yosemite, El Capitan, Sierra, Mojave, Catalina, Big Sur, Monterey, Ventura, Sonoma and Sequoia.

Architecture

At its core, macOS is built on Darwin, an open-source POSIX-compatible environment whose kernel, XNU, combines the Mach microkernel with elements of the BSD subsystem. Above the kernel sit the Core Services and Application Services layers, and frameworks such as Cocoa, Carbon (now deprecated), Metal for graphics, and Core Animation. The Aqua interface provides the Finder, the Dock, Mission Control and Spotlight search.

Platform transition

Mac OS X originally ran on PowerPC processors. In 2005 Apple announced a transition to Intel processors, completed in 2006, with the Rosetta emulator allowing PowerPC binaries to run. In 2020 Apple began a second transition to its own ARM-based Apple silicon chips (the M series), with Rosetta 2 providing binary translation for Intel applications; the transition of the Mac product line was completed by 2023.
