Baton (conducting)

A typical conductor's baton

A baton is a stick used by conductors primarily to enlarge and enhance the manual and bodily movements associated with directing an ensemble of musicians. The modern baton typically consists of a slim shaft of wood, fiberglass, or carbon fiber, balanced by a handle (grip) of cork or wood, and measures roughly 25 to 45 centimetres (10 to 18 inches) in length.

History

Before the baton, ensembles were directed with rolls of paper, violin bows, or by beating time audibly — the French composer Jean-Baptiste Lully died in 1687 from gangrene after striking his foot with the long staff he used to beat time. During the early 19th century, conductors such as Louis Spohr and Carl Maria von Weber adopted the slim baton, which became standard as orchestras grew larger and conducting became a distinct profession.

Use

The baton is held in one hand (usually the right) and traces beat patterns in the air; its tip defines the ictus, the precise point of each beat. By making gestures larger and more legible at a distance, the baton helps unify an ensemble, especially in large orchestras and choirs. Some conductors, notably many choral conductors and a few orchestral conductors such as Leopold Stokowski, choose to conduct without one, preferring the expressive freedom of bare hands.

Construction

Batons are individually balanced; conductors select the length, weight, and grip shape to suit their technique. The shaft is usually white or light-coloured for visibility against dark clothing and backgrounds.
