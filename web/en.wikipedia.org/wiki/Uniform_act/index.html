Uniform act

In the United States, a uniform act is a proposed state law drafted by the Uniform Law Commission (ULC), formally the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws, and offered to state legislatures for enactment without change. The purpose of uniform acts is to harmonize the law of the states in areas where uniformity is practical and desirable — for example, commercial transactions that routinely cross state lines.

The Uniform Law Commission

The ULC, founded in 1892, consists of commissioners appointed by each state, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin Islands. Commissioners — practising lawyers, judges, and law professors serving without pay — study areas of state law and draft acts, which are debated over multiple annual meetings before approval. A draft becomes a uniform act only when the commission intends it to be adopted identically in every state.

Notable uniform acts

The most successful is the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), first published in 1952 jointly with the American Law Institute and adopted (with local variations) in every state except Louisiana, which adopted most of it. Other widely adopted acts include the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act, the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act, the Uniform Probate Code, the Uniform Trade Secrets Act, and the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act. The Commission distinguishes uniform acts from model acts, which are intended as templates that states may adopt with modifications.

Enactment

No state is obliged to enact a uniform act, and legislatures sometimes amend the text; the ULC tracks enactments and may promulgate revised versions to restore uniformity or respond to developments in the law.
