Historical fiction

Historical fiction is a literary genre in which the plot takes place in a setting related to the past, typically at least a generation — often defined as fifty years or more — before the time of writing. Historical fiction works may involve fictional characters in documented historical settings, fictional accounts of real people, or mixtures of both, and commonly involve extensive research into the period's manners, language and material culture.

Development

Storytelling about the past is ancient, but the historical novel as a genre is usually dated to Walter Scott's Waverley (1814), which set the pattern of fictional protagonists moving through real historical upheavals. Nineteenth-century masters include Alexandre Dumas (The Three Musketeers), Victor Hugo, Leo Tolstoy (War and Peace) and Charles Dickens (A Tale of Two Cities). The Hungarian theorist Georg Lukács analysed the form in The Historical Novel (1937).

Subgenres and notable examples

Subgenres include historical romance, historical mystery, military fiction, the saga, and alternate history, which departs from documented events. Celebrated modern examples include Robert Graves's I, Claudius, Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose, Hilary Mantel's Wolf Hall trilogy about Thomas Cromwell, and Ken Follett's The Pillars of the Earth. The genre is also prominent in film, television drama and video games.
