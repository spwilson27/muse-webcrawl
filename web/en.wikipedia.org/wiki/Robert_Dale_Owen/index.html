Robert Dale Owen


Born: November 7, 1801, Glasgow, Scotland
Died: June 24, 1877, Lake George, New York, U.S.
Occupation: Politician, social reformer, writer

Robert Dale Owen (November 7, 1801 – June 24, 1877) was a Scottish-born American social reformer, politician and writer. The son of the utopian socialist Robert Owen, he emigrated to the United States and worked at his father's experimental community of New Harmony, Indiana.

Career

With Frances Wright, Owen edited the freethought newspaper New-Harmony Gazette (later the Free Enquirer) in New York, advocating public education, women's rights, birth control and the abolition of slavery. His 1831 book Moral Physiology was one of the first American works to discuss contraception openly.

Entering politics in Indiana, Owen served in the state legislature, where he championed publicly funded schools, and in the U.S. House of Representatives (1843–1847), where he helped draft the bill establishing the Smithsonian Institution. He later served as U.S. chargé d'affaires and minister to Naples (1853–1858). During the Civil War he advised President Lincoln on emancipation policy; his 1864 book The Wrong of Slavery, the Right of Emancipation argued for abolition and citizenship for freedmen. In his later years he became an advocate of spiritualism, writing Footfalls on the Boundary of Another World (1860).
