Wuxing (Chinese: 五行; pinyin: wǔxíng), usually translated as the Five Phases or Five Agents, is a fivefold conceptual scheme used in many traditional Chinese fields to explain a wide array of phenomena, from cosmic cycles and dynastic succession to internal organs, flavours and emotions. The five phases are Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water. Rather than static substances, they are better understood as processes or tendencies that generate and restrain one another in ordered cycles.

The cycles

In the generating cycle (sheng), Wood feeds Fire, Fire produces Earth (ash), Earth bears Metal, Metal enriches Water, and Water nourishes Wood. In the overcoming cycle (ke), Wood parts Earth, Earth dams Water, Water extinguishes Fire, Fire melts Metal, and Metal chops Wood. These two cycles together model dynamic balance.

History

The scheme was systematised during the Warring States period, notably by the cosmologist Zou Yan, and was integrated into Han dynasty correlative cosmology by thinkers such as Dong Zhongshu. Dynasties were said to succeed one another according to the overcoming cycle, each ruling under the virtue of a phase.

Applications

Wuxing correlations underpin traditional Chinese medicine (the five zang organs), feng shui, martial arts such as Xingyiquan, music theory, astrology and the calendar. The system is sometimes loosely compared to the Western four elements, though the Chinese scheme emphasises change and relation over material composition.
