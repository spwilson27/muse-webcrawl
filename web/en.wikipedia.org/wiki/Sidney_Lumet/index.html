Sidney Lumet

Sidney Arthur Lumet (June 25, 1924 – April 9, 2011) was an American film director, producer, and screenwriter. One of the most prolific directors of his era, he made more than forty feature films in a career spanning over fifty years, and is regarded as a master of the socially conscious urban drama. His films — including 12 Angry Men (1957), Serpico (1973), Dog Day Afternoon (1975), Network (1976), and The Verdict (1982) — frequently examined moral conflict, corruption, and the institutions of American life, particularly those of his native New York City.

Early life and television

Lumet was born in Philadelphia to Jewish immigrant parents and grew up in New York, where he acted on stage as a child, including on Broadway and in Yiddish theatre. After serving in the Second World War he became a director in the booming live-television industry, directing hundreds of episodes of series such as Studio One and Playhouse 90. The discipline of live TV — meticulous rehearsal, minimal takes — shaped his filmmaking for life.

Film career

His feature debut, 12 Angry Men (1957), a jury-room drama shot largely in a single room, won the Golden Bear at Berlin and established his reputation. Through the 1960s he ranged across genres with films such as The Pawnbroker (1964) and Fail Safe (1964). His greatest run came in the 1970s: Serpico and Dog Day Afternoon, both starring Al Pacino, and Network, Paddy Chayefsky's satire of television news, which won four Academy Awards. The Verdict (1982), with Paul Newman as a washed-up lawyer, is often ranked among his finest. His final film, the crime drama Before the Devil Knows You're Dead (2007), was warmly received.

Lumet was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Director four times and received an Honorary Academy Award in 2005. His 1995 book Making Movies is a widely read practical account of filmmaking craft.
