Arpad Emrick Elo (born Élő Árpád Imre; 25 August 1903 – 5 November 1992) was a Hungarian-American physics professor and chess player. He is best known as the creator of the Elo rating system for two-player games such as chess, which has since been adapted for many other sports and competitive activities.

Early life and career

Élő was born in Egyházaskesző, Austria-Hungary, and emigrated with his parents to the United States in 1913, settling in Cleveland, Ohio. He earned bachelor's and master's degrees from the University of Chicago and taught physics at Marquette University in Milwaukee from 1926 until his retirement in 1969. By the 1930s he was the strongest chess player in Wisconsin.

Chess

Elo won the Wisconsin State Championship eight times. He served as president of the United States Chess Federation's predecessor organisation and worked on its rating committee, seeking a statistically sounder replacement for the existing Harkness rating system.

The Elo rating system

Elo proposed a system in which a player's rating changes after each game according to the expected score, computed from the difference between the two players' ratings, compared with the actual result. The United States Chess Federation adopted the system in 1960, and FIDE adopted it in 1970. Elo published his work in the book The Rating of Chessplayers, Past and Present (1978). Analogous systems are now used in association football, tennis, baseball, esports matchmaking and many other fields.
