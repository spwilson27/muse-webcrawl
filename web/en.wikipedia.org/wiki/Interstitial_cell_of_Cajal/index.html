Interstitial cell of Cajal

Interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC) are specialized cells found within the muscle layers of the gastrointestinal tract. They act as electrical pacemakers, generating the rhythmic slow waves of depolarization that coordinate peristaltic contractions of gut smooth muscle, and they also mediate neurotransmission from enteric motor neurons and transduce mechanical stimuli.

History and characteristics

The cells were described by the Spanish neuroanatomist Santiago Ramón y Cajal in the late 19th century, who considered them primitive neurons. They are now known to be mesenchymal in origin and characteristically express the receptor tyrosine kinase c-Kit (CD117), a marker also relevant to gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs), which are thought to arise from ICC or their precursors.

Function

Subpopulations of ICC located between and within muscle layers generate slow wave potentials via calcium-activated chloride channels (ANO1/TMEM16A); these waves set the maximum frequency of contraction in each region of the gut — about three per minute in the stomach and around twelve per minute in the duodenum. Loss or dysfunction of ICC is implicated in disorders of gastrointestinal motility such as gastroparesis and chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction.
