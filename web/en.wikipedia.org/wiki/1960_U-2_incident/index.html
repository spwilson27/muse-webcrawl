The 1960 U-2 incident occurred on 1 May 1960, when an American Lockheed U-2 spy plane was shot down by Soviet air defences while conducting photographic aerial reconnaissance deep inside Soviet territory. The single-seat aircraft, flown by CIA pilot Francis Gary Powers, had taken off from Peshawar in Pakistan and was brought down near Sverdlovsk. Powers parachuted to safety and was captured.

Background

Since 1956 the CIA had flown U-2 reconnaissance missions over the USSR at altitudes above 70,000 feet, believed to be beyond the reach of Soviet fighters and missiles, photographing bomber and missile bases to assess the supposed "missile gap." The 1 May flight was intended to photograph intercontinental ballistic missile sites at Sverdlovsk and Plesetsk before a four-power summit scheduled in Paris.

Shootdown and cover story

Soviet air defences tracked the aircraft and a salvo of S-75 (SA-2) missiles was fired near Sverdlovsk; one exploded close enough to disable the U-2. Assuming the pilot had not survived and that little evidence would be recoverable, the United States issued a cover story that a NASA weather research aircraft had gone missing near Turkey. Premier Nikita Khrushchev then sprang a trap: on 5 May he announced the shootdown, and on 7 May produced Powers alive and much of the aircraft and its film largely intact.

Consequences

President Dwight D. Eisenhower was forced to admit that the US had been conducting spy flights over the Soviet Union for four years. The incident poisoned US–Soviet relations just before the Paris Summit of 16 May 1960: Khrushchev demanded an apology, and when Eisenhower refused, the summit collapsed. Powers was convicted of espionage in a Moscow show trial and sentenced to ten years, but was exchanged on 10 February 1962 at the Glienicke Bridge in Berlin for Soviet spy Rudolf Abel. The affair accelerated American reliance on reconnaissance satellites, whose first successful film recovery (CORONA) came months later in August 1960.
