History of Yemen

The history of Yemen reaches back to the ancient South Arabian kingdoms that grew rich on the frankincense and myrrh trade, through the coming of Islam, centuries of imamates and sultanates, Ottoman and British partition, and the unification of North and South Yemen in 1990.

Ancient South Arabia

From the late second millennium BC, kingdoms such as Saba (Sheba), Ma'in, Qataban, Hadhramawt, and Himyar flourished in the highlands and wadis of Yemen, building irrigation works like the Marib Dam and dominating the incense routes to the Mediterranean. The Aksumite kingdom of Ethiopia invaded in the sixth century AD, followed briefly by Sasanian Persian rule before Islam arrived in the 620s.

Medieval and early modern period

Yemen was governed by a succession of dynasties, including the Ziyadids, the Rasulids of Taiz — under whom Yemen prospered as a trading entrepôt — and the Tahirids. In the northern highlands, Zaydi imams established a state centred on Saada and later Sanaa that endured, in varying forms, for a thousand years. Coffee, cultivated in the mountains from the fifteenth century, was exported worldwide through the port of Mocha.

Partition and unification

The Ottoman Empire occupied the north while Britain seized Aden in 1839 as a coaling station, creating the Aden Protectorate in the south. North Yemen became independent under the Mutawakkilite Kingdom in 1918; the imamate fell in the 1962 revolution, producing the Yemen Arab Republic and an eight-year civil war. South Yemen gained independence in 1967 as the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen, the Arab world's only Marxist state. The two states unified on 22 May 1990 as the Republic of Yemen under President Ali Abdullah Saleh, whose rule ended amid the 2011 uprising; the country subsequently descended into a devastating civil war beginning in 2014.
