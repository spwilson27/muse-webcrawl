Lassa fever

Specialty: Infectious disease
Cause: Lassa virus
Transmission: Contact with multimammate rat excreta; person-to-person via body fluids

Lassa fever, also known as Lassa haemorrhagic fever, is a viral haemorrhagic fever caused by the Lassa virus, a member of the arenavirus family. It is endemic in parts of West Africa, including Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia and Guinea. The disease was first described in 1969 from the town of Lassa in Borno State, Nigeria.

Transmission

The natural reservoir is the multimammate rat (Mastomys natalensis). Humans are infected through contact with food or household items contaminated with rat urine or faeces, or by catching and preparing the rats as food. Person-to-person spread occurs through contact with infected body fluids, especially in hospital settings without adequate infection control.

Signs and symptoms

About 80% of infections are mild or asymptomatic. After an incubation of 6–21 days, symptoms begin with fever, weakness and headache, progressing in severe cases to sore throat, vomiting, facial swelling, bleeding, shock and multi-organ failure. Sensorineural hearing loss occurs in about a third of cases and is often permanent. Overall mortality is around 1%, but about 15–20% among hospitalised patients; it is particularly severe in late pregnancy.

Treatment and prevention

The antiviral ribavirin is effective if given early. Prevention centres on rodent-proof food storage, hygiene and barrier nursing of patients. Tens of thousands of infections and several thousand deaths are estimated annually in West Africa.
