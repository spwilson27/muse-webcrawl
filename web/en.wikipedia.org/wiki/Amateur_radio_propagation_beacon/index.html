Amateur radio propagation beacon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An amateur radio propagation beacon is an amateur radio station, operated unattended under an automatic control authorisation, that transmits a repeated standard signal so that listeners can assess radio propagation conditions in real time: if you can hear the beacon from a given place at a known power, the band is open to that place. Beacons operate on allocated sub-bands from LF through microwaves and are coordinated internationally to avoid mutual interference.

Operation

A typical beacon transmits its callsign (in Morse code/CW, or a digital mode), a location identifier or Maidenhead grid locator, and a carrier that steps down through calibrated power levels — for example 100 W, 10 W, 1 W, and 0.1 W — so a receiving operator can judge path quality by the lowest power level still copyable. Transmissions are short and cyclic, with beacons in a network transmitting in assigned time slots.

The IBP/NCDXF network

The best-known system is the International Beacon Project (IBP), run by the Northern California DX Foundation (NCDXF) with the IARU. Eighteen beacons worldwide transmit in turn on five HF frequencies (14.100, 18.110, 21.150, 24.930, and 28.200 MHz); each transmission lasts 10 seconds and consists of the callsign at 22 words per minute followed by four carrier dashes at descending power levels of 100, 10, 1, and 0.1 watts. Software and apps display which beacon should be transmitting at any moment, turning a single receiver into a worldwide propagation monitor.

Other beacon systems

  • VHF/UHF beacons in the 6 m, 2 m, and higher bands indicate tropospheric ducting, sporadic E, and auroral openings.
  • 10-metre beacons in the 28.2–28.3 MHz segment are numerous and individually operated.
  • WSPR and similar weak-signal digital modes (part of the WSJT-X suite) function as a crowdsourced beacon network, with automated reception reports uploaded to central databases.
  • Reverse Beacon Network — automated skimmer receivers that decode CW signals and report them online, effectively making every CQ call a propagation probe.

Regulation

Beacon operation is regulated by national amateur licensing rules and IARU band plans: sub-band allocations, maximum unattended operation conditions, and identification requirements vary by country and by frequency, with the HF IBP frequencies protected as beacon-only segments in band plans.
