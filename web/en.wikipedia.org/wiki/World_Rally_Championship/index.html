The FIA World Rally Championship (WRC) is the premier international rallying series organised by the Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA). First held in 1973, the championship is contested over a series of multi-day rallies on surfaces ranging from gravel and tarmac to snow and ice, with drivers and co-drivers racing production-based cars against the clock on closed-road "special stages".

Format

Each rally typically consists of 15–25 special stages run over three or four days, linked by public-road "liaison" sections. Crews make reconnaissance runs before the event to write or check pacenotes, which the co-driver reads aloud during the stages. Points are awarded to the top finishers of each event and for the closing "Power Stage". Separate championships are awarded for drivers, co-drivers and manufacturers.

History

The WRC began in 1973 as a manufacturers' championship, with a drivers' title added in 1979, won by Björn Waldegård. The early 1980s brought the ferocious Group B era, when turbocharged, mid-engined cars of over 500 horsepower — such as the Audi Quattro, Peugeot 205 T16 and Lancia Delta S4 — attracted huge crowds before a series of fatal accidents led to Group B's abolition at the end of 1986. Group A and then the World Rally Car formula (1997) followed. Sébastien Loeb dominated the 2000s and early 2010s, winning nine consecutive drivers' titles with Citroën from 2004 to 2012, before Sébastien Ogier won eight titles. Since 2022 the top class has been Rally1, using hybrid powertrains, with Toyota, Hyundai and M-Sport Ford as the leading teams.

Classic events

Long-running rallies include the Monte Carlo Rally, the Safari Rally in Kenya, the Acropolis Rally in Greece, Rally Finland (famed for its high-speed jumps), and Wales Rally GB.

Support categories

WRC2 and WRC3 championships run alongside the main series for Rally2 and Rally3 cars, and the Junior WRC provides a single-make entry level for young drivers.
