Plastic surgery

Reconstructive techniques have roots in ancient Indian rhinoplasty.

Plastic surgery is a surgical specialty involving the restoration, reconstruction, or alteration of the human body. It can be divided into two main categories: reconstructive surgery and cosmetic (aesthetic) surgery. Reconstructive surgery includes craniofacial surgery, hand surgery, microsurgery, and the treatment of burns, and aims to restore form and function after trauma, disease, or congenital conditions. Cosmetic surgery, by contrast, is performed to improve appearance and is usually elective.

The word "plastic" derives from the Greek plastikos, meaning "to mold or give form," and does not refer to the synthetic material. Treatments for the plastic repair of a broken nose are mentioned in ancient texts including the Edwin Smith Papyrus and the Sushruta Samhita, in which the Indian physician Sushruta described forehead-flap rhinoplasty around the 6th century BCE.

Modern development

The specialty advanced rapidly during the First and Second World Wars, when surgeons such as Harold Gillies and Archibald McIndoe developed techniques for treating severe facial injuries and burns, including skin grafting and pedicle flaps. Modern techniques include tissue expansion, free-flap microsurgical transfer, endoscopic surgery, and laser treatments.

Common procedures

Frequently performed cosmetic procedures include breast augmentation, liposuction, rhinoplasty, blepharoplasty (eyelid surgery), abdominoplasty ("tummy tuck"), and facelifts. Reconstructive procedures include breast reconstruction after mastectomy, cleft lip and palate repair, contracture release for burn survivors, and hand surgery. Like all surgery, plastic surgery carries risks including infection, scarring, hematoma, and anesthesia complications.
