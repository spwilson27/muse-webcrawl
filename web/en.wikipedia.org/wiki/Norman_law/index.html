Norman law

Norman law refers to the customary law of the Duchy of Normandy in medieval France, a system rooted in Frankish law adapted by the settled Norse (Norman) rulers after 911 and influenced by Scandinavian practice. It was recorded in custumals such as the Très ancien coutumier (c. 1200) and the Grand coutumier de Normandie (13th century).

Features

Norman custom featured strong ducal courts (the Exchequer of Normandy), distinctive rules of inheritance favouring primogeniture for knightly fiefs, the clameur de haro — a cry for immediate justice still ceremonially recognised in the Channel Islands — and characteristic procedures including trial by battle, which survived longer in Normandy than in many regions.

Influence

After 1066 the Norman Conquest carried Norman legal ideas to England, where they merged with Anglo-Saxon custom to form the common law; features such as the inquest (ancestor of the jury) and feudal land tenure owe much to Norman practice. Norman law also took root in Sicily and the Crusader states. Surviving customary law in Jersey and Guernsey still rests partly on Norman foundations, including the clameur de haro and rules on land conveyancing.
