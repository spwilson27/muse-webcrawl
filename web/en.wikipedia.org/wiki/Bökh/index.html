Bökh (Mongolian: Бөх, lit. "wrestling") is the traditional style of wrestling of the Mongols, practised for centuries in Mongolia, Inner Mongolia and Buryatia. It is one of the "Three Games of Men" at the annual Naadam festival, alongside horse racing and archery, and is inscribed with Naadam on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The object of bökh is to force any part of the opponent's body other than the feet — classically, the back, knee or elbow — to touch the ground, using throws, trips, lifts and sweeps; there are no weight classes.

History

Wrestling is depicted in Bronze Age petroglyphs in Mongolia, and wrestlers were honoured figures at the courts of the khans; the Secret History of the Mongols recounts wrestling episodes among Chinggis Khan's companions. Under the Qing dynasty Naadam gatherings were formalised, and the sport survived the socialist period as a centrepiece of national identity. Legendary champions include the 20th-century avarga ("titan/champion") Khorloogiin Bayanmönkh and Jigjidiin Mönkhbat, father of the sumo grand champion Hakuhō.

Rules and ritual

Bouts take place outdoors on grass or earth and have no time limit in traditional Naadam competition, though modern tournaments apply limits in later rounds. Wrestlers are attended by a zasuul (second/holder), who holds the wrestler's hat, sings praise songs, and leads the winner in the "eagle dance" (devekh) — a ritualised flapping movement recalling the mythical Garuda bird, performed on entering the arena and after victory. A wrestler who wins five rounds earns the title nachin (falcon); successive ranks include hartsaga (hawk), arslan (lion) and, for the overall Naadam champion, avarga (titan).

Costume

Wrestlers wear the zodog, a short open-chested jacket — legend holds it commemorates a woman wrestler's victory, or exposes the chest to prove no woman is competing — together with the shuudag, tight briefs, tall leather boots (gutul), and a pointed hat that is ceremonially held by the zasuul during the bout. The costume is often richly decorated and traditionally made of silk.

Variants

Regional styles differ: Inner Mongolian bökh historically allowed only leg-based techniques in some areas and used a jacket-grip style, while Buryat wrestling (bükhe barildaan) has its own customs. Women's participation, long excluded at major Naadam events by tradition, has been debated in modern Mongolia.
