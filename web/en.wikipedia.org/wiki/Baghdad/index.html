Baghdad is the capital and largest city of Iraq, located on the banks of the Tigris in the centre of the country, within the ancient region of Mesopotamia. Founded in 762 CE by the second Abbasid caliph, al-Mansur, as a perfectly circular planned city — the Round City of Baghdad — it grew within decades into possibly the largest city in the world, a centre of trade on routes linking Persia, the Mediterranean and the Silk Road.

Golden age and catastrophe

Under the Abbasids, Baghdad's House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma) sponsored the translation of Greek, Persian and Indian learning into Arabic and original work in mathematics (al-Khwarizmi, from whose name comes "algorithm"), astronomy, optics (Ibn al-Haytham) and medicine. The city's name became a byword for splendour in the tales of the Thousand and One Nights, set in the Baghdad of Harun al-Rashid. The golden age ended in 1258, when Mongol armies under Hulagu sacked Baghdad, destroyed its libraries and irrigation systems and killed a vast share of its people — estimates range from hundreds of thousands to a million. The city declined for centuries under successive empires.

Modern Baghdad

Baghdad revived as capital of the British Mandate and then the independent Kingdom and Republic of Iraq; in the 1970s oil boom it was a modernising metropolis. The Iran–Iraq War, the 1991 Gulf War, sanctions, and the 2003 US-led invasion — followed by years of sectarian civil war and bombings — devastated it; the population, infrastructure and famed book market of al-Mutanabbi Street have slowly recovered amid continuing political instability and electricity and water shortages. Iraq's politics still turn on Baghdad, about 1,000 km upriver-by-road from the marshes described under the Tigris's lower course.
