Reversible computing

Reversible computing is a model of computation in which the computational process is, to some extent, time-reversible. In a model where transitions between states of the machine are deterministic, a necessary and sufficient condition for reversibility is that the mapping from states to their successors is a one-to-one function: the logic is reversible. Reversible computing is a form of unconventional computing.

The concept is closely tied to physics. Because information is a physical quantity, erasing it has thermodynamic consequences: Rolf Landauer showed in 1961 that erasing one bit of information necessarily dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat (Landauer's principle). A computation that never erases information could, in principle, avoid this dissipation, which motivated interest in logically reversible circuits.

History

In 1973, Charles H. Bennett of IBM Research showed that any computation performed by a Turing machine can be embedded in a reversible Turing machine, establishing that reversibility does not reduce computational power. In the early 1980s, Edward Fredkin and Tommaso Toffoli developed models of reversible logic gates, including the billiard-ball model of conservative logic. The Toffoli gate and the Fredkin gate are universal reversible gates: any Boolean function can be computed with them while preserving information.

Reversible circuits

A reversible logic circuit maps its input vector bijectively onto its output vector, so the number of inputs equals the number of outputs and no information is lost. Common gates include the NOT gate, the controlled NOT (CNOT) gate, the Toffoli (controlled-controlled NOT) gate, and the Fredkin (controlled swap) gate. Because ordinary AND and OR gates are many-to-one, they are not reversible and must be embedded in larger reversible structures, often producing auxiliary "garbage" outputs that must be managed or uncomputed.

Relation to quantum computing

Quantum evolution is unitary and therefore reversible (except for measurement), so quantum circuits are necessarily built from reversible gates. Classical reversible computing techniques consequently play a central role in quantum circuit design, and work by Bennett on uncomputing garbage bits is routinely used in quantum algorithms.
