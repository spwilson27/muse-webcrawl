The Lesser Key of Solomon, also known as the Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis or simply Lemegeton, is an anonymous grimoire on demonology compiled in the mid-17th century, largely from materials several centuries older. It is traditionally attributed to King Solomon. The text is divided into five books: the Ars Goetia, Ars Theurgia-Goetia, Ars Paulina, Ars Almadel, and Ars Notoria.

Contents

Ars Goetia

The first and most famous section catalogues the seventy-two demons that Solomon is said to have confined in a bronze vessel, listing each spirit's rank, appearance, powers, legions, and seal. It draws heavily on Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1563) and other sources. Famous entries include Bael, Paimon, Beleth, Asmodeus, and Astaroth. Aleister Crowley published an edition of the Goetia in 1904, translated by Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers, which brought the text to wide attention in modern occultism.

Other books

The Ars Theurgia-Goetia describes thirty-one aerial spirits and their subordinate dukes, derived in part from Johannes Trithemius's Steganographia. The Ars Paulina, attributed to the Apostle Paul, deals with angels of the hours and of the zodiac. The Ars Almadel gives instructions for constructing a wax tablet through which angels may be contacted. The Ars Notoria, the oldest section, consists of prayers and orations intended to grant memory and intellectual mastery; it is often omitted from editions of the Lemegeton proper.

Sources and history

Although the compilation dates to the seventeenth century, its materials descend from medieval and Renaissance magical traditions, including the Key of Solomon manuscripts, Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa's writings, and the works of Trithemius and Weyer. No single definitive manuscript exists; surviving copies differ in arrangement and contents, and modern scholarly editions compare the principal manuscripts in the British Library and elsewhere.

Influence

The seventy-two demons of the Ars Goetia have had an outsized influence on modern occult practice and on popular culture, appearing in literature, games, comics, and film. The hierarchy and seals in the text continue to be used by ceremonial magicians and are a staple reference in works on Western esotericism.
