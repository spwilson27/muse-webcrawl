Electronic design automation


Automated placement and routing of an integrated circuit

Electronic design automation (EDA), also referred to as electronic computer-aided design (ECAD), is a category of software tools for designing electronic systems such as integrated circuits and printed circuit boards. The tools work together in a design flow that chip designers use to design and analyze entire semiconductor chips. Since a modern semiconductor chip can have billions of components, EDA tools are essential for their design; this article in particular describes EDA specifically with respect to integrated circuits (ICs).

History

Before EDA, integrated circuits were designed by hand and laid out manually with opaque tape on mylar film. The earliest commercial EDA tools emerged in the 1970s and early 1980s from companies such as Calma, Daisy Systems, Mentor Graphics, Cadence, and Synopsys. The publication of Mead and Conway's Introduction to VLSI Systems in 1980, and the development of hardware description languages such as Verilog and VHDL, enabled logic synthesis, which transformed chip design in the late 1980s.

Design flow

A typical digital design flow proceeds through system-level design, register-transfer level (RTL) description, functional verification, logic synthesis to a gate-level netlist, design for test insertion, floorplanning, placement, clock tree synthesis, routing, and physical verification (design rule checking and layout-versus-schematic). Analog and mixed-signal flows add schematic capture, simulation with SPICE-class simulators, and custom layout.

Market

The EDA industry is dominated by three large vendors — Synopsys, Cadence Design Systems, and Siemens EDA (formerly Mentor Graphics) — alongside many smaller firms specializing in particular niches such as emulation, sign-off analysis, and printed-circuit-board design.
