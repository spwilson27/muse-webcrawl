Heir presumptive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An heir presumptive is the person entitled to inherit a throne, peerage, or other hereditary honour, but whose position can be displaced by the birth of an heir apparent or of a new heir presumptive with a better claim to the position in question. This is in contrast to an heir apparent, whose claim on the position cannot be displaced in this way, except by a change in the law.

When a monarch dies, a living heir presumptive succeeds to the throne. Unlike an heir apparent, an heir presumptive could be displaced from the line of succession by the birth of children with a closer claim. For example, under male-preference primogeniture, a younger brother of a king is heir presumptive only until the king has a child, while a daughter of the king would be heir presumptive only until the birth of a son.

Definition

The position of an heir presumptive is often guaranteed by the law of the land and the position is clearly recognised; however, the position can be lost merely by the birth of another potential heir having a claim superior under the relevant law of succession. The crown of a lady who is heiress presumptive may be displaced by the birth of a male heir to her father, and the crown of a male heir presumptive may be displaced by the birth of a child to the current monarch.

In English law, the distinction goes back centuries. An heir apparent was so called because his right of inheritance was fixed by the laws of succession and could not be defeated (except by statute); an heir presumptive could be defeated by the birth of someone nearer in line.

Examples

  • Elizabeth II was heir presumptive to her father George VI from 1936, when he became king, until her own accession in 1952, because at any time a son born to the king would have taken precedence over her.
  • Victoria of Sweden was heir presumptive from her birth in 1977 until the Swedish Act of Succession was amended in 1980 to adopt absolute primogeniture, making her heir apparent.
  • Felipe, Prince of Asturias, was heir presumptive until his father's accession as Felipe VI made him heir apparent, as Spain retains male-preference primogeniture.

Heir presumptive and heir apparent compared

An heir apparent's position is secure: he or she will inherit unless the line is abolished or the person dies or is disqualified. An heir presumptive may be superseded at any time by the birth of a person with a stronger claim. Many monarchies have had long periods where the heir was merely presumptive. Under absolute primogeniture, the eldest child of the monarch is generally heir apparent, and heirs presumptive occur mainly among collateral relatives.
