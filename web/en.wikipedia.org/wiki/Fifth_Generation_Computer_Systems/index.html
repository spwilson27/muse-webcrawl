Fifth Generation Computer Systems

The Fifth Generation Computer Systems (FGCS) was a Japanese government initiative begun in 1982 to create computers based on massively parallel computing and logic programming. Run by the Institute for New Generation Computer Technology (ICOT) under the Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI), the project aimed to leapfrog Western computing by building machines that could reason, process knowledge and communicate in natural language.

Programme

The project's core technology was parallel inference machines running a concurrent logic language, KL1, derived from Prolog. Research proceeded in stages from feasibility studies to multi-processor prototypes (the PIM machines) with hundreds of processing elements. The announcement of FGCS alarmed Western governments and industry, prompting responses such as the Alvey programme in the UK, ESPRIT in Europe, and the MCC consortium in the United States.

Outcome

The project concluded in 1992 without achieving commercial success: by then, rapidly improving commodity workstations and microprocessors had overtaken its specialised hardware, and the envisioned knowledge-based computing paradigm had not materialised. It is nonetheless credited with advancing parallel logic programming and training a generation of researchers.
