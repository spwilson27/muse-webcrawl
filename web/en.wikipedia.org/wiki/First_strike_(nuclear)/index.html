First strike (nuclear)

In nuclear strategy, a first strike or preemptive strike is a nuclear attack on an adversary's military infrastructure with the aim of destroying most or all of that adversary's nuclear weapons and ability to respond, minimizing the scale and scope of a retaliatory counterstrike. The closer a nuclear strike comes to destroying the other side's arsenal, the more likely it is that the first striker can successfully respond to a retaliation and devastate the defender.

Overview

The concept of a first strike was central to Cold War strategic planning. Theorists distinguished between a strike intended as a counterforce attack — against military targets such as missile silos, bomber bases, and submarine ports — and a countervalue attack against cities and industrial targets. A successful disarming first strike would, in theory, leave the victim unable to retaliate in kind.

The feasibility of a first strike has generally been considered low since the development of survivable second-strike forces, particularly ballistic missile submarines, which can remain hidden at sea and guarantee retaliation. This gave rise to the doctrine of mutual assured destruction (MAD).

History

During the late 1940s and early 1950s, when the United States held a nuclear monopoly and then overwhelming superiority, preventive war was occasionally debated in American strategic circles but never adopted as policy. As the Soviet Union built its own arsenal, attention shifted to the danger of a surprise attack, exemplified by fears of a "nuclear Pearl Harbor". The development of hardened silos, airborne alert, and submarine-launched missiles progressively undermined the plausibility of a disarming first strike.
