Nuclease

A nuclease is an enzyme capable of cleaving the phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides of nucleic acids. Nucleases are essential to DNA replication, repair and recombination, RNA processing, and the defence of cells against foreign nucleic acids, and they are indispensable tools in molecular biology.

Classification

Nucleases are divided according to the position at which they cut:

  • Endonucleases cleave bonds within a polynucleotide chain. Restriction endonucleases, which cut DNA at specific recognition sequences, underpin recombinant DNA technology.
  • Exonucleases remove nucleotides one at a time from the 3′ or 5′ end of a chain, and are important in DNA proofreading and degradation.

They are further classified by substrate: DNases act on DNA, RNases on RNA, and some nucleases act on both. Many require divalent metal ions such as Mg2+ or Ca2+ as cofactors.

Biological roles and applications

In cells, nucleases trim primers during replication, excise damaged bases and mismatches, resolve recombination intermediates, and mature RNA molecules; in the immune system, nucleases degrade the genomes of invading viruses. Laboratory applications include restriction digestion and cloning, footprinting, nuclease protection assays, and genome editing — zinc-finger nucleases, TALENs and the Cas9 nuclease of CRISPR systems all introduce targeted double-strand breaks. Micrococcal nuclease is used to map chromatin structure. Some bacterial nucleases, such as staphylococcal nuclease, are also toxins.
