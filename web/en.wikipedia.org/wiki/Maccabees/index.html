Maccabees

The Maccabees (Hebrew: מַכַּבִּים, Makabim) were a group of Jewish rebel warriors who took control of Judea, which at the time was part of the Seleucid Empire. They founded the Hasmonean dynasty, which ruled from 167 BCE to 37 BCE, initially as a semi-autonomous region and later as an independent kingdom. The Maccabees are celebrated for their role in the rededication of the Second Temple in Jerusalem, an event commemorated by the festival of Hanukkah.

The revolt

In 167 BCE the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes issued decrees forbidding Jewish religious practice and desecrated the Temple in Jerusalem, provoking revolt. The priest Mattathias of Modein refused to sacrifice to the Greek gods, killed a Hellenistic official and a compliant Jew, and fled to the Judean hills with his five sons — John, Simon, Judas, Eleazar and Jonathan. After Mattathias died (c. 166 BCE), his son Judas, surnamed Maccabeus ("the Hammer"), took command.

Using guerrilla tactics against far larger Seleucid armies, Judas won a series of victories at Nahal el-Haramiah, Beth Horon, Emmaus and Beth Zur. In 164 BCE he liberated Jerusalem and rededicated the Temple, an event associated in tradition with the miracle of the oil that burned for eight days.

The Hasmonean dynasty

After Judas fell at the Battle of Elasa in 160 BCE, leadership passed to his brothers Jonathan and then Simon, who secured formal independence: in 142 BCE the Seleucid king Demetrius II recognised Judea's freedom from tribute. Simon's descendants ruled as high priests and then kings until Herod the Great, backed by Rome, ended the dynasty in 37 BCE.

Sources and legacy

The principal sources are the books of 1 Maccabees and 2 Maccabees — included in the Catholic and Orthodox biblical canons but not the Hebrew Bible — and the writings of the historian Josephus. The Maccabees became enduring symbols of religious liberty and national resistance in Jewish tradition.
