Rare Earth hypothesis

Rare Earth hypothesis
FieldAstrobiology, planetary science
Proposed byPeter Ward and Donald E. Brownlee, Rare Earth (2000)
ThesisComplex multicellular life is exceedingly rare in the universe
RelationProposed solution to the Fermi paradox

The Rare Earth hypothesis argues that the emergence of complex life on Earth required an improbable, perhaps unique, combination of astrophysical, geological, and biological circumstances, so that planets harbouring complex multicellular life — as opposed to simple microbes — are exceptionally rare throughout the universe.

The argument

The hypothesis was set out by palaeontologist Peter Ward and astronomer Donald Brownlee in Rare Earth: Why Complex Life Is Uncommon in the Universe (2000). They contend that microbial life is probably common, but that the chain of contingencies leading to animals — a stable star in a galactic habitable zone, a terrestrial planet with plate tectonics, a large moon stabilising axial tilt, a magnetic field, oxygenation, and avoidance of sterilising impacts — is so restrictive that extraterrestrial intelligence may be essentially absent from our galaxy.

Requirements for complex life

Proposed requirements include position in the habitable zone, a Jupiter-like planet screening comets, plate tectonics regulating climate and a carbon cycle, a large Moon damping obliquity swings, long-term climate stability, and the rare evolutionary steps of eukaryotes, sexual reproduction, and the Cambrian explosion.

Criticism

Critics argue the hypothesis is anthropic and Earth-chauvinist: many of its "requirements" merely describe Earth's particular path, while life elsewhere might follow different routes; some cited factors (such as large moons or plate tectonics) may not be as rare or as necessary as assumed.
