Trompe-l'œil

Trompe-l'œil (French for "deceive the eye", pronounced [tʁɔ̃p lœj]) is an artistic technique that uses realistic imagery to create the optical illusion that the depicted objects exist in three dimensions. Forced perspective is a comparable illusion in architecture.

History

The idea dates to antiquity: Pliny the Elder recounts the contest between the Greek painters Zeuxis and Parrhasius, in which Zeuxis painted grapes so convincing that birds pecked at them, while Parrhasius fooled Zeuxis himself with a painted curtain. The technique flourished in Renaissance perspective painting, Baroque ceiling frescoes (such as Andrea Pozzo's at Sant'Ignazio), and Dutch still life. Pere Borrell del Caso's Escaping Criticism (1874) is a celebrated example, and American painters William Harnett and John F. Peto specialised in the genre in the 19th century.

Modern uses

Trompe-l'œil survives in architectural murals that simulate windows, stonework, or entire facades; in pavement art; and in cinema and theme-park design. Oblique anamorphic works, revived by artists such as Felice Varini, extend the principle so that the illusion resolves only from a specific viewpoint.
