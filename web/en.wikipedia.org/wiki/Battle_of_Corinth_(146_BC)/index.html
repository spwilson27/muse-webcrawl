The Battle of Corinth of 146 BC, sometimes called the Battle of Leucopetra, was the final engagement of the Achaean War between the Roman Republic and the Achaean League. Fought outside Corinth, it ended in the rout of the Greek army under Diaeus and the sack and destruction of the city by the consul Lucius Mummius.

Background

Relations between Rome and the Achaean League deteriorated over Sparta and Roman demands that several cities be detached from the League. After Roman victories in Boeotia and at Scarphea, the Achaeans under their strategos Diaeus assembled their remaining forces at the Isthmus to defend Corinth.

Battle

Mummius advanced with roughly 23,000 infantry and 3,500 cavalry. After waiting several days for the Greeks to attack his camp — they did not — he advanced on Corinth. The Achaean force, around 14,000 infantry with few cavalry, was swiftly driven from the field; Diaeus fled, and the city was left undefended.

Aftermath

Corinth's male population was killed and the women and children enslaved; the city was systematically looted and burned, its artworks shipped to Rome. Greece passed under direct Roman rule, and the year 146 BC also saw the destruction of Carthage, marking Rome's unchallenged dominance of the Mediterranean.
