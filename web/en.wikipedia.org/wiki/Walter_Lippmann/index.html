Walter Lippmann

Born: 23 September 1889, New York City
Died: 14 December 1974 (aged 85), New York City
Occupation: Journalist, author, political commentator
Known for: Public Opinion (1922); coining "stereotype" in its modern sense and popularising "Cold War"

Walter Lippmann (23 September 1889 – 14 December 1974) was an American writer, reporter, and political commentator, among the most influential American journalists of the twentieth century. His syndicated column "Today and Tomorrow" ran for decades, and he won two Pulitzer Prizes. He was a co-founder of The New Republic magazine and the author of the landmark study Public Opinion (1922).

Early life and career

Born in New York City to a prosperous German-Jewish family, Lippmann attended Harvard, where he studied philosophy and was influenced by George Santayana and William James. He helped found The New Republic in 1914. During the First World War he served in US Army intelligence and in the propaganda effort, and he assisted with the drafting of President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points. Disillusioned by wartime propaganda and the Paris peace settlement, he wrote Liberty and the News (1920) and then Public Opinion.

Public Opinion

Public Opinion argued that citizens inhabit a "pseudo-environment" of simplified images — "the pictures in our heads" — mediated by a press that cannot reliably transmit the complexity of the world. It introduced the word "stereotype" in its modern social sense and remains a foundational text in media studies and democratic theory, famously debated by the philosopher John Dewey. Lippmann's scepticism about the competence of mass publics influenced later thinking about elites, experts, and the limits of democracy.

Later work

Lippmann wrote influential books on foreign policy, including U.S. Foreign Policy: Shield of the Republic (1943), and in 1947 published The Cold War, which helped popularise that term while criticising the containment doctrine. The "Lippmann gap" — the divergence between a country's commitments and its power — remains a concept in foreign-policy analysis. He advised presidents from Wilson to Lyndon Johnson, breaking with Johnson over the Vietnam War. He won Pulitzer Prizes in 1958 and 1962.
