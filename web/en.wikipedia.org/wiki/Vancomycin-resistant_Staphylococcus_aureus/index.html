Vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus

Vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (VRSA) is a strain of Staphylococcus aureus that has become resistant to the glycopeptide antibiotic vancomycin, long regarded as a drug of last resort for methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). VRSA is distinguished from vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA), in which resistance arises from a thickened cell wall; true VRSA carries the vanA gene cluster, typically acquired by horizontal gene transfer from vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE).

Mechanism

The vanA operon, carried on a plasmid-borne transposon (Tn1546), changes the terminal amino acid of the peptidoglycan precursor from D-alanyl-D-alanine to D-alanyl-D-lactate. Vancomycin binds this altered target with roughly a thousand-fold lower affinity, so cell-wall synthesis proceeds despite the drug. Transfer of the plasmid from Enterococcus faecalis to MRSA in a co-colonised patient was implicated in the first reported cases.

Epidemiology and treatment

Confirmed cases remain rare: the first clinical isolate was reported in the United States in 2002, and only a small number of cases have been documented worldwide, mostly in patients with chronic wounds, prior vancomycin exposure and co-colonisation with VRE. Infections are treated with alternative agents such as linezolid, daptomycin or ceftaroline, guided by susceptibility testing, and strict infection-control measures are used to prevent spread. Public-health authorities classify VRSA as a serious antimicrobial-resistance threat and require laboratory confirmation and notification of cases.
