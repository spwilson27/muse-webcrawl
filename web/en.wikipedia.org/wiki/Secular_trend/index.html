Secular trend
FieldTime series analysis, economics, epidemiology
Related conceptsSeasonality, business cycle, random fluctuation

A secular trend is the long-term, persistent direction of movement in a time series — upward, downward, or steady — after short-term fluctuations, cycles, and seasonal effects have been accounted for. The word secular here derives from the Latin saeculum (an age or generation), reflecting that such trends unfold over years or decades rather than days or seasons.

In economics and statistics

In classical time series decomposition, a series is modelled as the product or sum of four components: the secular trend, seasonal variation, cyclical variation (such as the business cycle), and irregular or random movements. Secular trends in economics include long-run growth of real GDP, the centuries-long decline in fertility, urbanization, and long-wave phenomena such as Kondratiev cycles. Estimating the trend — by moving averages, regression on time, or filters such as the Hodrick–Prescott filter — is a first step in forecasting and in separating structural change from the business cycle.

In epidemiology and biology

Epidemiologists use "secular trend" for gradual changes in disease incidence or health characteristics across generations. The best-known example is the secular trend in growth and maturation: the increase in average height and earlier onset of puberty observed in industrialized countries from the nineteenth into the late twentieth century, attributed to improved nutrition, sanitation, and reduced childhood disease burden. Secular increases in obesity and in conditions such as myopia are also studied as secular trends.

Distinguishing features

Unlike a business cycle, a secular trend has no fixed period and need not reverse; unlike a seasonal pattern, it does not repeat within the year. Analysts caution that an apparent secular trend may reflect changes in measurement or in the composition of the population studied, and that extrapolating a trend indefinitely is hazardous because structural breaks can reverse it.
