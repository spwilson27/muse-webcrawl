Intercultural communication

Intercultural communication is a discipline that studies communication across different cultures and social groups, or how culture affects communication. It describes the wide range of communication processes and problems that naturally appear within an organization or social context made up of individuals from different religious, social, ethnic, and educational backgrounds. In this sense it seeks to understand how people from different countries and cultures act, communicate and perceive the world around them.

Many people in intercultural business communication argue that culture determines how individuals encode messages, what medium they choose for transmitting them, and the way messages are interpreted. With regard to intercultural communication proper, it studies situations where people from different cultural backgrounds interact. Aside from language, intercultural communication focuses on social attributes, thought patterns, and the cultures of different groups of people. It also involves understanding the different cultures, languages and customs of people from other countries.

History

The discipline is commonly associated with the work of anthropologist Edward T. Hall, whose 1959 book The Silent Language is considered a founding text. Hall, who worked at the United States Foreign Service Institute in the early 1950s, developed concepts such as high-context and low-context cultures and the study of proxemics — the use of space in communication. The field grew out of post-war American efforts to train diplomats and businesspeople for overseas assignments.

Key concepts

Important concepts in intercultural communication include:

High- and low-context cultures. In high-context cultures, much of the meaning of a message is carried by context, shared understanding and nonverbal cues; in low-context cultures, meaning is conveyed explicitly through words.

Cultural dimensions. Geert Hofstede's model describes cultures along dimensions such as individualism versus collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity versus femininity.

Nonverbal communication. Gestures, eye contact, facial expressions, posture and personal space vary significantly across cultures and are frequent sources of misunderstanding.

Ethnocentrism and cultural relativism. Ethnocentrism is the tendency to evaluate other cultures by the standards of one's own; cultural relativism is the practice of understanding a culture on its own terms.

Theories

Several theories attempt to explain intercultural interaction, including communication accommodation theory, which describes how speakers adjust their speech to converge with or diverge from their interlocutors; face-negotiation theory, which links cultural values to conflict styles through the concept of "face"; and anxiety/uncertainty management theory, which holds that effective communication depends on managing the anxiety and uncertainty that arise in encounters with strangers.

Applications

Intercultural communication is applied in international business, diplomacy, education, healthcare and tourism. Training programs aim to develop intercultural competence — the ability to communicate effectively and appropriately with people of other cultures — which scholars such as Milton Bennett have modelled as a developmental progression from ethnocentric to ethnorelative stages.
