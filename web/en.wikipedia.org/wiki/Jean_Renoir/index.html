Jean Renoir

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Jean Renoir directing La Grande Illusion in 1937: the son of the painter at work behind the camera, cap on, smiling]

Jean Renoir (15 September 1894 – 12 February 1979) was a French film director, screenwriter and actor, son of the painter Pierre-Auguste Renoir and himself a giant of world cinema. His films of the 1930s — Boudu Saved from Drowning (1932), The Crime of Monsieur Lange (1936), La Grande Illusion (1937) and The Rules of the Game (1939) — invented a humane, mobile, deep-focus cinema of social sympathy that post-war realism and the French New Wave claimed as their source.

The Rules of the Game, his comedy of manners among aristocrats and servants at a country house, was booed and cut on release and banned as demoralising; restored after the war, it rose to permanent residence near the top of all-time polls. Orson Welles and Satyajit Ray both named Renoir their master in spirit.

Career

Wounded in the First World War (he limped for life), Renoir came to film through ceramics and his wife's acting ambitions. The thirties cycle, several written with Jacques Prévert's circle, mixed Popular Front hopes with clear eyes: La Grande Illusion, his First World War prison-camp film, pleaded European fraternity on the eve of the second war. In 1940 he fled to Hollywood (The Southerner, 1945), then made The River (1951) in India in Technicolor, influencing Ray directly.

Style and standing

Renoir's credo — that everyone has their reasons, as his character Octave says in The Rules of the Game (in the famous short formulation) — shaped a style of long takes, deep staging and moving camera that lets characters share frames rather than be cut apart. Bazin's Cahiers criticism built its realism on him. He received an honorary Academy Award in 1975, four years before his death in Beverly Hills.
