Oral Torah

According to rabbinic Judaism, the Oral Torah or Oral Law (Hebrew: Torah she-be-ʿal peh) is the body of interpretations, statutes and legal rulings that, together with the Written Torah, were given to Moses on Mount Sinai and transmitted orally through the generations. It forms the basis of rabbinic interpretation of the Torah and of Jewish law (halakha).

Contents

The Oral Torah includes interpretations and elaborations of the written law, legal rulings derived by hermeneutical rules, decrees of the rabbis, and traditions said to have been learned by Moses at Sinai (halakha le-Moshe mi-Sinai). It explains, for example, how commandments phrased generally in the Written Torah — such as the laws of slaughter, fringes (tzitzit), and tefillin — are to be carried out in practice.

Codification

Although by definition originally oral, the teachings were eventually committed to writing, especially after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE raised fears they would be forgotten. The Mishnah, redacted by Rabbi Judah the Prince around 200 CE, became the foundational text; subsequent discussion produced the Jerusalem and Babylonian Talmuds, the latter becoming the central text of rabbinic Judaism.

Views and disputes

The concept was historically contested. The Sadducees of the Second Temple period are generally described as rejecting an authoritative oral tradition, and Karaite Judaism likewise rejects the rabbinic Oral Torah, relying on the written Hebrew Bible. Rabbinic Judaism regards the two Torahs as a single, indivisible revelation.
