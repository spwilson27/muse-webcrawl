Congenital disorder

A congenital disorder, also known as a birth defect, is a condition present at birth regardless of its cause. Birth defects may result in disabilities that can be physical, intellectual or developmental, ranging from mild to severe. They are broadly divided into structural defects, affecting the shape of a body part, and functional or metabolic defects, affecting how the body works.

Newborn screening programmes test infants for treatable congenital disorders shortly after birth.

Causes

Causes include genetic abnormalities — chromosome disorders such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21), single-gene disorders such as cystic fibrosis, and multifactorial conditions such as neural tube defects; environmental exposures during pregnancy, including teratogenic drugs (notoriously thalidomide), alcohol (fetal alcohol spectrum disorders), and infections such as rubella and Zika virus; and nutritional deficiencies, notably of folic acid. In many cases the cause is unknown.

Prevalence and prevention

The World Health Organization estimates that congenital disorders affect a significant share of newborns worldwide and contribute substantially to infant and childhood mortality, especially in low-income settings. Preventive measures include folic acid supplementation before and during early pregnancy, rubella vaccination, avoidance of alcohol and certain medications in pregnancy, and control of maternal diabetes. Prenatal screening and newborn screening enable early diagnosis and treatment of many conditions.
