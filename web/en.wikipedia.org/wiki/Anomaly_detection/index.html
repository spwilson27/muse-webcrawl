Anomaly detection

Anomaly detection
FieldData mining, machine learning, statistics
Also known asOutlier detection
Typical tasksFraud detection, intrusion detection, fault monitoring

Anomaly detection (also known as outlier detection) is the identification of rare items, events or observations which deviate significantly from the majority of the data. Such anomalous items are often the most informative ones in an application: a fraudulent bank transaction, a failing machine component, or an intrusion into a computer network may all manifest as data points that do not conform to an expected pattern.

Anomaly detection is used in a wide variety of applications, including fraud detection for credit cards and insurance, intrusion detection in cybersecurity, fault detection in safety-critical systems, military surveillance, and predictive maintenance of industrial equipment. It is closely related to novelty detection and to noise removal in statistics.

Definition and types of anomalies

An influential definition comes from the statistician Douglas M. Hawkins, who in 1980 described an outlier as an observation generated by a different mechanism from the rest of the data. Anomalies are commonly divided into three categories:

  • Point anomalies — a single instance that is anomalous with respect to the rest of the data, such as an unusually large credit card purchase.
  • Contextual anomalies — an instance that is anomalous only in a specific context, such as a high temperature reading that is normal in summer but abnormal in winter.
  • Collective anomalies — a collection of related instances that is anomalous as a whole, although each individual instance may appear normal, as in a sequence of network packets forming an attack.

Approaches

Statistical methods

Classical approaches model the data with a probability distribution and flag points in low-probability regions. Methods include z-score based tests, Grubbs' test for outliers, and Gaussian mixture models. Density-based techniques such as the Local Outlier Factor (LOF) compare the local density of a point with that of its neighbours.

Machine learning methods

Supervised methods require labelled examples of both normal and anomalous data, which are often unavailable. Semi-supervised methods, such as one-class support vector machines, learn a model of normality from normal examples only. Unsupervised methods, including k-means clustering, isolation forests, and autoencoders, score points by how poorly they fit the structure learned from unlabelled data. Deep learning approaches extend these ideas to images, text and time series.

Information-theoretic and spectral methods

Other families of methods detect anomalies by measuring the increase in complexity of a dataset when a suspect point is included, or by projecting data onto principal components and inspecting the residual subspace, an approach rooted in principal component analysis.

Challenges

Anomaly detection is difficult because anomalies are by definition rare, so labelled training data are scarce and class imbalance is extreme. The boundary between normal and anomalous behaviour is often fuzzy, normal behaviour evolves over time (concept drift), and noise in real data can resemble true anomalies. Evaluation is also problematic, since a detector that flags too many points is useless in practice — a phenomenon related to the base rate fallacy.

Applications

Major application areas include payment fraud detection, network intrusion detection, medical diagnosis and monitoring, industrial sensor monitoring, insurance claim analysis, and detection of faults in spacecraft telemetry. In many of these settings, anomaly detection systems operate in real time and raise alerts for human analysts.
