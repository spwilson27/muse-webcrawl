Stargazing

Stargazing is the observation of the night sky as a hobby or for pleasure, typically with the naked eye, binoculars, or a telescope. It is a central activity of amateur astronomy and has been practiced by humans since prehistory, when the stars served as calendars, navigation aids, and the raw material of mythology.

Getting started

Naked-eye stargazing requires little more than a dark location and patience: the observer's eyes need twenty to thirty minutes to fully adapt to darkness, and a red flashlight preserves night vision while reading charts. With the unaided eye one can see several thousand stars, the Milky Way, the planets, meteors, satellites, and bright deep-sky objects such as the Andromeda Galaxy and the Orion Nebula.

Equipment

Binoculars are often recommended as the first instrument, revealing star clusters, lunar detail, and the moons of Jupiter. Telescopes extend the range to planets' surface features, double stars, nebulae, and galaxies. Star atlases, planispheres, and mobile apps help observers locate objects, frequently by the star hopping technique — navigating from bright reference stars to faint targets.

Conditions and light pollution

Seeing quality depends on darkness, atmospheric transparency, and "seeing" (steadiness of the air). Light pollution from cities can reduce the visible stars from thousands to a few dozen; the Bortle scale grades sky darkness from 1 (pristine) to 9 (inner city). Dark-sky parks and reserves, designated by organizations such as the International Dark-Sky Association, protect exceptional observing sites. Popular targets vary with the seasons, and public "star parties" organized by astronomy clubs introduce the hobby to newcomers.
