Grímsvötn is a volcano with a fissure system in south-eastern Iceland, lying largely beneath the Vatnajökull ice cap. It is Iceland's most frequently erupting volcano, with dozens of recorded eruptions. The name means "Grímur's lakes", referring to the subglacial caldera lakes formed by geothermal melting of the ice above the magma chamber.

Eruptions and floods

Heat from the volcano continuously melts the overlying ice, filling a subglacial lake within the caldera. When the water level rises high enough, the ice dam is lifted and the lake drains catastrophically in a glacial outburst flood called a jökulhlaup, which sweeps down the Skeiðarársandur flood plain. The 1996 eruption melted through 200–300 metres of ice and produced one of the largest floods of the 20th century, with peak discharge rivalling the Amazon River for a few hours and destroying bridges on Iceland's Ring Road.

The volcano's largest historic eruption occurred in 1783 as part of the Laki fissure eruption, which released enormous quantities of sulphur dioxide, causing crop failures and famine in Iceland and a "dry fog" across the Northern Hemisphere. More recently, the eruption of November 2004 and the powerful eruption of 21 May 2011 — Grímsvötn's largest in over a century — sent ash plumes more than 20 km into the atmosphere, causing the closure of Icelandic airspace and flight cancellations in Scotland and Scandinavia, though disruption was far smaller than that caused by Eyjafjallajökull in 2010.

Monitoring

Because eruptions are frequent and the subglacial lake provides visible warning signs — rising water, ice cauldrons, and earthquake swarms — Grímsvötn is one of the most closely monitored volcanoes in Iceland. Its 2011 eruption ended within about a week, but scientists expect further eruptions on a timescale of years to decades.
