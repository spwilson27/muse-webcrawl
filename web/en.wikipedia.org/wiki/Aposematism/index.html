Aposematism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aposematism is the advertising by an animal, or in some cases a plant, of its unprofitability to potential predators. The word derives from the Greek apo ("away") and sema ("sign"), and was coined by Edward Bagnall Poulton in 1890. Aposematic signals are typically bright warning coloration — reds, yellows, oranges and contrasting black patterns — but may also involve sound, odour, or other conspicuous cues. The strategy is the opposite of crypsis (camouflage): instead of hiding, the organism advertises itself so that predators learn, or innately know, to avoid it.

Mechanism

Aposematism works because defended prey — animals that are poisonous, venomous, foul-tasting, spiny, or otherwise costly to attack — benefit when predators can identify and avoid them. Predators may learn from a single unpleasant experience, may inherit an innate aversion to certain colour patterns, or may show neophobia (avoidance of novel foods) and dietary conservatism that give conspicuous prey an advantage. Bright coloration also makes the prey easier for predators to remember, strengthening learned avoidance.

Examples

Classic aposematic animals include the skunks (black-and-white coloration combined with a noxious spray), monarch butterflies (which sequester cardiac glycosides from milkweed), poison dart frogs of Central and South America, coral snakes, ladybird beetles, and many wasps, bees, and stinging or distasteful insects. Warning signals are not limited to sight: rattlesnakes advertise with sound, and some moths emit ultrasonic clicks that warn bats of their chemical defences.

Mimicry

Aposematism underlies two major forms of mimicry. In Müllerian mimicry, two or more genuinely defended species evolve similar warning signals, sharing the cost of educating predators. In Batesian mimicry, a harmless species (such as a hoverfly) imitates the warning signal of a defended model, gaining protection without paying the cost of a real defence; if mimics become too common relative to models, the signal's credibility — and its protective value — declines.

Evolution

The origin of aposematism poses an evolutionary puzzle: the first conspicuous mutants should be detected and eaten more often before predators have learned to avoid them. Proposed solutions include kin selection (predators learn by attacking relatives, while the conspicuous individual's family benefits), gregariousness of defended prey, gradual evolution from slight to strong conspicuousness, and predators' innate biases or neophobia. Once established, warning coloration tends to be subject to positive frequency-dependent selection, favouring uniformity of the signal within a locality.
