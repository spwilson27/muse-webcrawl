St Giles, London

St Giles in the Fields, the parish church of the district.

St Giles is a district of London, in the London Borough of Camden, lying at the southern end of the West End between Bloomsbury, Holborn, Soho, and Covent Garden. It takes its name from the parish church of St Giles in the Fields and the medieval hospital for lepers founded there around 1101 by Queen Matilda. Today the area is known for the busy junction at St Giles Circus, where Tottenham Court Road meets Oxford Street, New Oxford Street, and Charing Cross Road.

History

For centuries St Giles had a notorious reputation. The area gave its name to the first cases of the Great Plague of 1665, and in the 18th and 19th centuries the St Giles "rookery" — a maze of slum courts around Church Lane and the Seven Dials — became the archetypal Victorian slum, immortalised in Hogarth's Gin Lane (1751), set in St Giles, and in the writings of Charles Dickens. Irish immigration after the Great Famine deepened the district's poverty and its association with lodging houses and gin shops. Victorian road building, including New Oxford Street and Shaftesbury Avenue, cut through and cleared much of the rookery.

Modern St Giles

The 20th century brought office and commercial development, including the Centre Point tower at St Giles Circus, completed in 1966. The district now contains Denmark Street, famous for music shops and the British music industry ("Tin Pan Alley"), part of London's theatreland edges, and large modern developments around the rebuilt Tottenham Court Road station, an interchange on the Elizabeth line. The parish church remains an active centre of the neighbourhood.
