Long short-term memory (LSTM) is a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) architecture used in the field of deep learning. Unlike standard feedforward neural networks, LSTM has feedback connections. Such a recurrent neural network can process not only single data points (such as images), but also entire sequences of data (such as speech or video).

History

LSTM was proposed by Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber in 1997 to address the vanishing gradient problem that prevented conventional RNNs from learning long-range dependencies. A key refinement, the forget gate, was introduced by Felix Gers and colleagues in 1999/2000, allowing the network to reset its internal state. LSTM achieved record results in handwriting recognition (winning ICDAR competitions in 2009) and became a core technology in speech recognition and machine translation systems in the 2010s.

Architecture

An LSTM unit consists of a cell and three gates — an input gate, an output gate and a forget gate — that regulate the flow of information into and out of the cell state, which acts as a kind of conveyor belt running through the sequence. The gates are composed of sigmoid neural net layers and pointwise multiplication operations, allowing the network to learn what to store, what to erase and what to output. Variants include peephole connections and the gated recurrent unit (GRU), a simpler architecture introduced in 2014.

Applications

LSTM networks powered major production systems including Google's voice search and Translate, Apple's Siri improvements, and Amazon Alexa. Although transformer architectures have superseded LSTMs in most large-scale language tasks since 2017, LSTMs remain widely used in smaller-scale sequence modelling and time-series forecasting.
