Madrigal

A madrigal is a form of secular vocal music most typical of the Renaissance and early Baroque eras. Traditionally unaccompanied, it is written for two to eight voices — most commonly five — and sets a short poem, usually on a theme of love. The Italian madrigal of the 16th century, cultivated by composers such as Claudio Monteverdi, Carlo Gesualdo, Luca Marenzio and Cipriano de Rore, was the most important secular musical form of its age and strongly influenced the development of Baroque music.

History

The madrigal emerged in Italy in the 1520s–30s, partly from the lighter frottola and partly from the influence of Franco-Flemish polyphonists working in Italy. Early madrigals were largely homophonic settings for four voices; by mid-century, composers such as Adrian Willaert and Cipriano de Rore developed a more expressive, through-composed style for five voices, closely matching the text's meaning — a practice known as word painting or "madrigalisms".

Late madrigal and England

In the late 16th century the Italian madrigal grew increasingly chromatic and dramatic, reaching extremes in Gesualdo's intensely expressive settings, while Monteverdi's nine books of madrigals trace the transition to the Baroque concerto style with basso continuo. Nicholas Yonge's publication Musica Transalpina (1588) sparked an English madrigal school, whose composers — Thomas Morley, John Wilbye, Thomas Weelkes and Orlando Gibbons — produced lighter madrigals and balletts in English.

Legacy

By about 1640 the madrigal had been absorbed into newer forms such as the cantata, duet and aria. The term was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries for unaccompanied choral settings in a broadly Renaissance spirit, and madrigal singing remains a staple of collegiate and amateur choral societies.
