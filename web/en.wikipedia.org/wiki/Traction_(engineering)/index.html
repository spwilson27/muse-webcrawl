Traction (engineering)

Traction, or tractive force, is the force used to generate motion between a body and a tangential surface, through the use of dry friction or shear stress of the surface. In engineering, traction commonly refers to the grip of a wheel or track on a surface — for example, between a train's steel wheels and the rail, or a vehicle's tires and the road — and to the maximum pulling force that can be transmitted before slipping occurs.

Physics

The maximum tractive force is limited by the coefficient of friction between the surfaces and the normal force pressing them together: Fmax = μ × N. For a locomotive this gives the concept of adhesive weight — only the weight carried on driven wheels contributes to traction. Steel-on-steel contact has a low coefficient of friction (around 0.2 dry, much less when rails are wet or leaf-contaminated), which is why railways use sanding gear and why steep gradients are difficult for adhesion railways.

Applications

  • Railways — tractive effort ratings for locomotives; adhesion versus rack-and-pinion or cable systems on steep lines
  • Road vehicles — tire grip determines acceleration, braking and cornering limits; traction control systems prevent wheelspin
  • Agriculture and earthmoving — tractor drawbar pull; tracks spread weight and increase grip on soft ground
  • Traction engines and elevators — the word also names machines: a "traction engine" is a steam-powered road locomotive, and "traction" elevators use ropes and counterweights

Traction control

Modern vehicles employ electronic traction control, which detects driven-wheel slip and intervenes by braking the spinning wheel or reducing engine torque, effectively raising usable traction on slippery surfaces.
