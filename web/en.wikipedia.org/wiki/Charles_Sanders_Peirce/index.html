Charles Sanders Peirce (pronounced "purse"; 10 September 1839 – 19 April 1914) was an American scientist, mathematician, logician and philosopher who is sometimes known as "the father of pragmatism". Educated as a chemist and employed as a scientist for thirty years, he is now largely appreciated for his contributions to logic, mathematics, philosophy, and semiotics (his own theory of signs).

Life

The son of Harvard mathematician Benjamin Peirce, Charles graduated from Harvard in 1859 and worked for the United States Coast Survey from 1861 to 1891, conducting gravimetric and astronomical research. He lectured in logic at Johns Hopkins University (1879–1884), where his students included John Dewey and Christine Ladd-Franklin, but academic scandal and personal difficulties ended his university career. He spent his final decades in Milford, Pennsylvania, writing voluminously in poverty; his friend William James helped raise support for him.

Philosophy

In papers of 1877–78, including "The Fixation of Belief" and "How to Make Our Ideas Clear", Peirce formulated the pragmatic maxim: the meaning of a concept lies in its conceivable practical consequences. He distinguished three modes of inference — deduction, induction, and abduction (hypothesis formation) — and built an elaborate metaphysics on the categories of Firstness, Secondness, and Thirdness. His semiotics classifies signs as icons, indices, and symbols, analyses sign-action (semiosis) as irreducibly triadic, and has influenced communication theory ever since.

Logic and mathematics

Peirce made foundational contributions to the logic of relations, quantifier theory (independently of Frege), Boolean algebra, and the theory of graphs; he anticipated truth tables and designed an electrical switching circuit for logic decades before its practical use. His writings fill some 12,000 published pages and an estimated 80,000 pages of manuscripts, edited posthumously as the Collected Papers (1931–58).
