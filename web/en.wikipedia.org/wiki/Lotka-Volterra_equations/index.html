Lotka–Volterra equations

The Lotka–Volterra equations, also known as the predator–prey equations, are a pair of first-order nonlinear differential equations proposed independently by Alfred J. Lotka (1925) and Vito Volterra (1926) to describe the dynamics of two interacting populations: prey, which grow in number when unchecked, and predators, which depend on the prey for food.

The model

In standard form, with x the prey population and y the predator population: dx/dt = αx − βxy and dy/dt = δxy − γy, where α and γ are the prey growth and predator death rates and β and δ measure the effect of encounters between the species. In the absence of predators the prey grow exponentially; in the absence of prey the predators decline exponentially.

Behaviour and applications

Rather than settling to equilibrium, solutions of the basic model oscillate: predator peaks lag behind prey peaks, a pattern qualitatively seen in data such as the lynx–snowshoe hare cycles recorded by the Hudson's Bay Company. The equations founded mathematical ecology and have been extended with logistic prey growth, carrying capacities, functional responses, refuges and additional species, and applied in epidemiology, economics and chemical kinetics.
