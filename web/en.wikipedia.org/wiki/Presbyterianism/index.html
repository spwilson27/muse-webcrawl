Presbyterianism


Illustration placeholder: a Reformed church

Presbyterianism is a part of the Reformed tradition within Protestantism that traces its institutional roots to the Church of Scotland and the theology of John Calvin, particularly as developed by John Knox and others in the 16th century. Presbyterian churches are distinguished by their presbyterian polity: government by elected elders (presbyters) gathered in ascending courts — the session, the presbytery, the synod, and the general assembly.

The word "Presbyterian" derives from the Greek presbyteros ("elder"). Historically, Presbyterians placed great value on education and democratic church governance, and played a significant role in the development of parliamentary democracy.

Theology

Presbyterian theology is thoroughly Reformed and Calvinist in character. Most historic confessions — above all the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) with its Shorter and Larger Catechisms — teach the sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture, salvation by grace through faith, and the doctrines often summarised by the acronym TULIP. Presbyterians generally practice infant baptism and celebrate two sacraments: baptism and the Lord's Supper. In the 20th century, many Presbyterian bodies revised or qualified their confessional standards, notably with the Presbyterian Church (USA)'s Book of Confessions (1967, expanded 1983).

Church government

The typical Presbyterian structure runs upward from the local congregation: the session (teaching and ruling elders of one church), the presbytery (elders from a region), the synod (several presbyteries), and the general assembly (the national body). Pastors are called "teaching elders" or "ministers of word and sacrament", while lay "ruling elders" share in governance.

History

John Knox brought Reformed ideas to Scotland, where the Church of Scotland was established by the Parliament of 1560. Presbyterianism spread through Ulster to America, where Francis Makemie organised the first presbytery in 1706, and throughout the British Empire. In the 18th century, Presbyterians were central to the First Great Awakening in the colonies. In the 20th century the tradition divided over modernism and biblical inerrancy, producing both mainline bodies (such as the Presbyterian Church (USA) and the Church of Scotland) and conservative ones (such as the Presbyterian Church in America, the Orthodox Presbyterian Church, and the Free Church of Scotland).
