Buckwheat

Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum), or common buckwheat, is a flowering plant in the knotweed family Polygonaceae cultivated for its grain-like seeds and as a cover crop. Despite its name, buckwheat is unrelated to wheat, as it is not a grass. Instead, buckwheat is related to sorrel, knotweed, and rhubarb. Buckwheat is referred to as a pseudocereal because its seeds' culinary use is the same as cereals, owing to their composition of complex carbohydrates.

The domestication and cultivation of buckwheat has spread from its point of origin in China across Asia. Buckwheat dough, either from its own flour or from a wheat-based flour, is widely used in East Asian and European cuisines. Its most common form is kasha in Eastern Europe, while it is also used to make soba noodles in Japan and crêpes or galettes in Brittany, France.

Botany

Buckwheat is a short-season crop that grows well in low-fertility or acidic soils; too much fertilizer, especially nitrogen, reduces yields, and the soil must be well drained. The plant has a branching root system with a primary taproot that reaches deeply into moist soil. It grows 75 to 125 centimetres (30 to 50 inches) tall. The inflorescences, borne along the stem, are clusters of small pinkish-white flowers. The seeds are triangular, brown or black, and usually have a hull that must be removed before human consumption.

History

Domesticated and first cultivated in inland Southeast Asia, possibly around 6000 BCE, buckwheat spread from there to Central Asia and Tibet, and then to the Middle East and Europe. The wild ancestor of common buckwheat, Fagopyrum esculentum ssp. ancestrale, is thought to have originated in western Yunnan province, China. The earliest remains of buckwheat in Europe date to the Netherlands around 5000 BCE, suggesting the crop spread along two routes: one through Russia into Europe and one along the Silk Road into the Middle East.

Cultivation

Buckwheat production requires a short growing season — it matures in 10 to 12 weeks — and is often sown as a catch crop after the failure of a main crop. It is frequently grown as a honey crop because its flowers attract bees; buckwheat honey is dark and strongly flavoured. The plant is also used as green manure, since it grows rapidly and suppresses weeds while improving soil phosphorus availability.

Culinary uses

Buckwheat groats are commonly used in Eastern European porridges such as kasha. Buckwheat flour is used for Japanese soba noodles, Korean memil-muk (a jelly), Breton galettes, Italian pizzoccheri, and Russian blini. Because it contains no gluten, buckwheat flour is used in gluten-free breads and other products. The grain is rich in protein, with a well-balanced amino acid composition, and contains rutin, a flavonoid with antioxidant properties.
