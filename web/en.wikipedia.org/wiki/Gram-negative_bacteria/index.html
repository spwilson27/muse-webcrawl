Gram-negative bacteria

Cell envelopeThin peptidoglycan layer plus outer membrane with lipopolysaccharide
ExamplesEscherichia coli, Salmonella, Pseudomonas, Neisseria

Gram-negative bacteria are bacteria that do not retain the crystal violet stain used in the Gram staining method of bacterial differentiation, appearing pink or red after counterstaining. Their cell envelope comprises a thin peptidoglycan layer sandwiched between an inner cytoplasmic membrane and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS), in contrast to Gram-positive bacteria, which have a thick peptidoglycan layer and no outer membrane.

Characteristics

The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria acts as a barrier to many antibiotics, detergents, and dyes, contributing to the intrinsic resistance of these organisms. Porin proteins in the outer membrane allow the passage of small hydrophilic molecules. The LPS in the outer leaflet acts as an endotoxin: when released into the bloodstream it can trigger fever and septic shock. Gram-negative bacteria also possess a periplasmic space between the two membranes.

Medical significance

Many important pathogens are Gram-negative, including Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Helicobacter pylori, and Vibrio cholerae. Antibiotic resistance — including carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales — is a major public-health concern, and Gram-negative infections are a leading cause of hospital-acquired sepsis.

Classification

Most Gram-negative bacteria belong to the phylum Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria), but the trait occurs in several other phyla, including Cyanobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Spirochaetota; the Gram reaction is a cell-envelope property rather than a single clade.
