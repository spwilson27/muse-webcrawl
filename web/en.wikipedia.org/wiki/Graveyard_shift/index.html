Graveyard shift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The graveyard shift (also called the night shift or third shift) is a work shift running through the late night and early morning hours, typically from around midnight to 8 a.m. The term is most common in North America. In three-shift systems it follows the day shift (first shift) and the swing or evening shift (second shift).

Etymology

The phrase is recorded in the United States from the late nineteenth century, originally among printers and railway workers. Popular folk etymologies link it to watchmen employed in graveyards — either to guard against grave robbers or, in a frequently repeated but unverified story, to listen for bells attached to coffins of people buried alive. Lexicographers generally treat the term as a figurative reference to the quiet, "dead" hours of the night and to the shift's resemblance to the hours when churchyards were watched.

Industries

Continuous-operation industries require night staffing, including:

  • Healthcare — hospitals, emergency services, and care homes
  • Emergency services — police, fire, and ambulance dispatch
  • Transport and logistics — freight, aviation, warehousing
  • Manufacturing — process industries such as steel, chemicals, and refining
  • Hospitality, media, and information technology operations

Health effects

Working against the circadian rhythm is associated with shift work sleep disorder, fatigue, and elevated long-term risks of cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and gastrointestinal complaints. In 2007 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified shift work involving circadian disruption as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A). Many labour codes and collective agreements provide a night-shift premium (shift differential) in pay, limits on consecutive night shifts, and mandated rest periods.
