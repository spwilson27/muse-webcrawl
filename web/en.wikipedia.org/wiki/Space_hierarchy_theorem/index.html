Space hierarchy theorem

Space hierarchy theorem
FieldComputational complexity theory
Statement (informal)Given asymptotically more space, a Turing machine can solve strictly more problems
Key requirementSpace-constructibility of the bounding functions
MethodDiagonalisation

In computational complexity theory, the space hierarchy theorems state that a Turing machine with more memory can recognise strictly more languages than one with less memory, provided the bounds involved are reasonably well-behaved (space-constructible). Formally, for space-constructible functions f and g with f(n) = o(g(n)) and g(n) ≥ log n:

SPACE(f(n)) ⊊ SPACE(g(n))

The proof is by diagonalisation: one constructs a machine that simulates all machines using at most f(n) space and does the opposite on some input, ensuring the resulting language lies in SPACE(g) but not SPACE(f). A small technical overhead — asymptotically negligible — is needed to mark off the allowed space and to run a clock guarding against infinite loops, which is why a little slack between the bounds is required.

Consequences

The theorem yields a strict hierarchy of space classes. Combined with Savitch's theorem, it implies that PSPACE strictly contains, for example, L and NL-bounded subclasses at lower levels; in particular EXPSPACE ≠ PSPACE, and the corresponding separations separate classes such as SPACE(n) ⊊ SPACE(n²). It is the space analogue of the time hierarchy theorem, though with stronger conclusions because space, unlike time, can be reused — an ingredient in the proof that PSPACE strictly contains P (via padding and related arguments in standard treatments).
