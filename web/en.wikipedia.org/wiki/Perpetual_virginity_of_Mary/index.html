The perpetual virginity of Mary is a Christian doctrine that Mary, the mother of Jesus, was a virgin ante partum, in partu, et post partum — before, during, and after the birth of Christ. It is one of the four Marian dogmas of the Catholic Church, held also by the Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches, and affirmed in the early creeds of Christianity.

Doctrine

The doctrine holds that Mary conceived Jesus virginally by the power of the Holy Spirit, that her physical virginity remained intact in the act of giving birth, and that she remained a virgin for the rest of her life, having no other children. The "brothers" and "sisters" of Jesus mentioned in the New Testament are therefore understood, in traditions affirming the doctrine, as cousins or other close kin, or as children of Joseph from a prior marriage.

History

Belief in Mary's lifelong virginity appears early. The second-century Protoevangelium of James describes Mary as a consecrated virgin and Joseph as an elderly widower. The title Aeiparthenos ("ever-virgin") was applied to Mary by the fourth century and was affirmed at the Second Council of Constantinople in 553. The Lateran Council of 649, under Pope Martin I, gave the doctrine a precise dogmatic formulation.

Church Fathers including Athanasius, Epiphanius, Jerome, and Augustine defended the doctrine; Jerome's treatise Against Helvidius (c. 383) is the classic patristic defence. The early Protestant reformers Martin Luther, John Calvin, and Huldrych Zwingli also affirmed Mary's perpetual virginity, though many later Protestant traditions came to reject it on the basis of the biblical references to Jesus's siblings.

In Islam

Mary (Maryam) is honoured in Islam as a virgin who miraculously conceived Jesus (Isa); the Quran does not, however, teach a dogma of her perpetual virginity after the birth.
