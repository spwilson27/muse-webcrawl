Củ Chi is a rural district (huyện) of Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, located northwest of the city centre along the Saigon River. It is best known internationally for the Củ Chi tunnels, the extensive underground network used by Viet Cong fighters during the Vietnam War.

History

During the First Indochina War and the Vietnam War, Củ Chi's laterite clay soil made it ideal for tunnelling. Local guerrillas dug a network eventually exceeding 200 km of passages with kitchens, hospitals, meeting rooms and booby-trapped entrances. Despite heavy American and South Vietnamese operations, including Operation Crimp in 1966 and the use of "tunnel rats", the tunnels were never fully destroyed, and the district became a symbol of resistance. Củ Chi was among the most heavily bombed and defoliated areas of the war.

The tunnels today

Two sections of the tunnel system, at Bến Dình and Bến Dược, are preserved as war memorials and popular tourist sites, where visitors can crawl through widened sections of tunnel and view displays of wartime life and weaponry.

Geography and economy

The district consists of one town, Củ Chi, and 20 communes. Agriculture — rice, rubber, vegetables and dairy cattle — remains important, alongside growing industrial parks as Ho Chi Minh City expands outward.
