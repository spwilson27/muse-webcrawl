Closure (wine bottle)

A closure is the device used to seal a wine bottle and prevent oxidation and spoilage. For centuries the dominant closure was the natural cork, but since the late twentieth century winemakers have increasingly used screw caps, synthetic corks, and glass stoppers, prompting an ongoing debate about ageing, faults, and tradition.

Natural cork

Corks are cut from the bark of the cork oak, chiefly in Portugal and Spain. They allow a very slow ingress of oxygen regarded by many as essential for the maturation of fine wine, but a small percentage of bottles are spoiled by "cork taint", caused by the compound 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA), and corks vary in performance.

Alternative closures

Screw caps (such as the Stelvin closure) provide a consistent, near-hermetic seal and eliminate cork taint; they were adopted overwhelmingly by Australian and New Zealand producers from the early 2000s, though some critics associate them with reductive aromas and doubted their suitability for long ageing. Synthetic corks, moulded or extruded from plastic, imitate cork's appearance but can allow more oxygen over time. Glass stoppers such as Vino-Lok seal with an inert gasket and are resealable. Technical corks — agglomerated or "1+1" corks such as Diam — are engineered to reduce taint and variability.

Debate

Research on oxygen transmission and long-term cellaring trials has narrowed but not ended the controversy, which is also influenced by consumer perception: in some markets screw caps still carry an image of cheapness, while in others they signal modern quality.
