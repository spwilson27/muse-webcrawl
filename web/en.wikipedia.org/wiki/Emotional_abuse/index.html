Psychological abuse

Psychological abuse, also known as emotional abuse and mental abuse, is a form of abuse characterized by a person subjecting or exposing another person to behaviour that may result in psychological trauma, including anxiety, chronic depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder. It is often associated with situations of power imbalance in abusive relationships, including bullying, gaslighting, and abuse in the workplace. It may also be perpetrated by persons who repeatedly use verbal assaults, threats, and controlling behaviour.

Psychological abuse often occurs within relationships of unequal power.

Definition and behaviours

Definitions vary and the concept lacks a single legal standard, but commonly described tactics include:

  • verbal aggression: insults, name-calling, yelling, threats;
  • gaslighting — manipulating someone into doubting their own perceptions and memory;
  • coercive control: monitoring, isolation from friends and family, restricting money, movement, or communication;
  • intimidation, silent treatment, and unpredictable "hot and cold" behaviour;
  • humiliation, blame-shifting, and minimization or denial of the abuse itself.

Researchers emphasize that harm comes from patterns of behaviour within a relationship of coercion rather than isolated angry words. Coercive control, a concept developed by sociologist Evan Stark, frames much psychological abuse in intimate relationships as a liberty crime — an ongoing pattern that entraps the victim. Psychologist Lenore Walker described the cycle of abuse (tension-building, acute incident, reconciliation/honeymoon) in battering relationships.

Effects

Studies associate psychological abuse with depression, anxiety disorders, PTSD and complex PTSD, low self-esteem, substance misuse, and physical health problems. In children, emotional abuse and neglect are linked to attachment disorders, developmental and behavioural problems, and long-term mental health difficulties. Survivors and clinicians frequently report that psychological abuse can be as damaging as, or harder to recover from than, physical abuse, partly because it erodes the victim's sense of reality and self-worth.

Contexts and prevalence

Psychological abuse occurs in intimate partner relationships, families (including child and elder abuse), schools and workplaces (bullying/mobbing), institutions, and online. Survey data in many countries find lifetime exposure to psychological aggression by an intimate partner reported by substantial minorities of adults — often more common than physical violence — though measurement differences make comparisons difficult. Certain jurisdictions have begun criminalizing coercive control specifically (e.g. England and Wales in 2015, France, Ireland, and parts of Australia), creating evidentiary debates about proving patterns of non-physical abuse.
