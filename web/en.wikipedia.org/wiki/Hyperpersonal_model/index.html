Hyperpersonal model

The hyperpersonal model is a model of interpersonal communication proposed by communication scholar Joseph Walther in 1996. It suggests that computer-mediated communication (CMC) can under some conditions become hyperpersonal — that is, more intimate and socially desirable than face-to-face interaction.

Components

The model has four components. Senders engage in selective self-presentation, carefully composing and editing messages to present themselves favourably. Receivers, having limited cues, tend to idealise senders by over-attributing similarities and positive qualities. The asynchronous channel lets users plan, edit and time their messages. Finally, a feedback loop of behavioural confirmation can intensify the exchange, as partners act in ways that confirm each other's idealised expectations.

Context and criticism

The model contrasted with earlier "cues-filtered-out" views that treated CMC as inherently impersonal, and it built on Walther's social information processing theory. It has been applied to online dating, social networking and workplace communication. Critics note that hyperpersonal effects depend on context, motivation and the affordances of particular platforms, and that idealisation can collapse when online partners finally meet face to face.
