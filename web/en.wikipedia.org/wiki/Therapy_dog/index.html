Therapy dog

A therapy dog is a dog that is trained to provide affection, comfort and support to people, often in settings such as hospitals, retirement homes, nursing homes, schools, libraries, hospices and disaster areas. Unlike assistance or service dogs, which are trained to perform specific tasks for a person with a disability, therapy dogs work with their handlers to benefit many different people and enjoy interaction with strangers.

History

The therapeutic value of animals was noted as early as Florence Nightingale, and Sigmund Freud observed his dog's calming effect on patients. Modern organised animal-assisted therapy developed in the later twentieth century; Elaine Smith, an American nurse working in England, founded Therapy Dogs International in 1976 after seeing how well patients responded to a visiting chaplain's dog.

Training and certification

Therapy dogs must be calm, friendly, well-socialised and comfortable with crowds, sudden noises and medical equipment. Organisations such as Pet Partners and Therapy Dogs International register handler–dog teams after temperament and obedience evaluations. Therapy dogs have no special legal right of public access: they go only where they are invited, in contrast to service animals.
