Service dog

A guide dog wearing its working harness.

A service dog is a dog individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. Tasks may include guiding people who are blind, alerting people who are deaf, pulling a wheelchair, alerting and protecting a person having a seizure, reminding a person with mental illness to take prescribed medication, or calming a person with post-traumatic stress disorder during an anxiety attack. In the United States, service animals are protected under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA).

Types

Well-known categories include guide dogs for the blind, hearing dogs for the deaf, mobility assistance dogs, medical alert and response dogs (for example diabetic alert and seizure response), autism service dogs, and psychiatric service dogs. Emotional support animals, which provide comfort by presence alone, are distinguished in US law from service dogs because they are not trained to perform specific tasks.

Training and access

Service dogs are trained to a high standard of public behaviour, typically over one to two years, by organisations or by their handlers. Under the ADA, businesses may ask only whether the dog is a service animal required because of a disability and what task it has been trained to perform; no certification or identification is required by federal law. Rules differ in other countries; in the UK, assistance dogs are generally trained by accredited charities.
