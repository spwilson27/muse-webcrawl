Cryptovirology

Cryptovirology
FieldComputer security, cryptography, malware research
Originated1996, Adam L. Young and Moti Yung
Key conceptThe cryptovirus: malware using public-key cryptography against the victim
Best-known realizationRansomware (e.g., CryptoLocker, 2013)
Related fieldKleptography

Cryptovirology is the study of the use of cryptography in malicious software — in particular, of how strong public-key cryptography can be turned against users to build malware that resists analysis and enables extortion. The field was founded by Adam L. Young and Moti Yung of Columbia University, whose 1996 paper "Cryptovirology: Extortion-Based Security Threats and Countermeasures" (presented at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy) defined the central concept, the cryptovirus.

The cryptovirus

A cryptovirus is a computer virus or worm that carries the public key of its author and uses hybrid encryption in its attack: upon infecting a machine, it generates a fresh symmetric session key, encrypts the victim's files with it, then encrypts the session key under the author's embedded public key and destroys the plaintext. The victim's data can then be recovered only with the author's private key — for which the author demands ransom. The key insight is asymmetry: because the decryption key never appears on the victim's machine, victims and antivirus researchers cannot simply extract a key from the code (as they could when malware used a single hardcoded symmetric key), and tampering cannot turn the attacker's cryptography to the defender's advantage. Young and Yung also described kleptographic attacks, in which cryptographic systems themselves are subverted so that their outputs leak secrets to the designer — an idea later echoed in debates over backdoored random number generators and standards.

History and realizations

The earliest real-world precursor, the AIDS Trojan (PC Cyborg) of 1989, mailed by biologist Joseph Popp, used only weak symmetric encryption and was quickly defeated. The cryptovirology framework predicted genuinely robust successors, which arrived with the ransomware era: early samples such as GPCode (2004–2008) progressively fixed implementation errors — weak keys, recoverable temporary files — until families such as CryptoLocker (2013) implemented the 1996 design essentially correctly, combining RSA-2048 key protection, anonymous payment in Bitcoin, and botnet distribution to extort tens of millions of dollars before an international law-enforcement operation dismantled its infrastructure in 2014. Later ransomware-as-a-service operations, including the double-extortion groups that also exfiltrate and threaten to publish data, descend directly from this model.

Defenses

Countermeasures studied in the literature include antivirus monitoring of cryptographic API calls and entropy analysis to catch mass encryption in progress, OS-level restrictions on bulk file modification, and — decisive in practice — maintained, offline, tested backups that remove the victim's need to pay. Young and Yung themselves argued that the field was dual-use and necessary: the same analysis underlies defensive design (for example, cryptography that malware cannot abuse, and secure deletion), and publishing the attack model was essential to preparing defenses against adversaries who would discover it independently.
