Anomeric effect

FieldOrganic chemistry, stereochemistry
ObservedIn pyranoses and other acetals
Related termsAnomer, gauche effect, rabbit-ear effect

The anomeric effect is a stereoelectronic effect observed in certain heterocyclic and acetal systems, in which an electronegative substituent at the anomeric carbon (adjacent to a ring oxygen or other heteroatom) prefers the axial orientation over the equatorial one, contrary to what steric considerations alone would predict.

Origin of the term

The term was introduced by Lemieux and Chü in 1958, building on observations by J. T. Edward (1955) that axial anomers of glycosides were unexpectedly favoured. Classic evidence comes from tetrahydropyranyl acetals and from halopyranoses, where the axial preference increases with the electronegativity of the substituent and in less polar solvents.

Explanations

Two main explanations have been debated. The hyperconjugation model attributes the effect to donation from a lone pair on the ring oxygen (nO) into the antibonding σ* orbital of the exocyclic C–X bond, which is geometrically possible only in the axial conformer. The electrostatic or "rabbit-ear" model, advanced by Lemieux, emphasizes minimization of dipole repulsion between the oxygen lone pairs and the polar C–X bond. Modern computational work generally supports a dominant hyperconjugative contribution with additional electrostatic effects.

Consequences

The anomeric effect influences the stereochemical outcome of glycosylation reactions, the conformation of carbohydrates and acetals, and the stability of anomers in solution. A related phenomenon, the exo-anomeric effect, governs rotation about the exocyclic C–O bond of glycosides.
