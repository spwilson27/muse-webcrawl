The Ocean Cleanup is a Dutch non-profit environmental engineering organisation, founded in 2013 by inventor and entrepreneur Boyan Slat, that develops technology to extract plastic pollution from the oceans and to intercept it in rivers before it reaches the sea. Based in Rotterdam, the organisation's stated goal is to remove 90% of floating ocean plastic pollution. It became internationally known after a 2013 TEDx talk by Slat, then 18 years old, presenting a concept for passively concentrating ocean plastic with long floating barriers, went viral.

Ocean systems

The organisation's ocean technology consists of a long, U-shaped floating barrier towed slowly by two vessels, which concentrates floating debris in a retention zone for periodic removal by support ships. After a feasibility study and crowdfunding campaign that raised over US$2 million in 2014, prototypes were tested in the North Sea. The first full-scale system, System 001 ("Wilson"), was deployed in the Great Pacific Garbage Patch in 2018; it initially failed to retain plastic and suffered a structural fracture, and was towed to Hawaii for repair. A redesigned System 001/B in 2019 successfully captured plastic. Subsequent, larger iterations — System 002 ("Jenny") in 2021 and the much larger System 03 in 2023 — made repeated extraction trips to the patch, removing quantities of debris ranging from kilograms in early trials to multi-tonne hauls.

River Interceptors

Arguing that a large share of ocean plastic arrives via rivers, the organisation unveiled the Interceptor in 2019: a solar-powered, barge-like device moored in a river that uses a barrier and conveyor to divert floating waste into dumpsters. Interceptors have been deployed in rivers in Malaysia, Indonesia, the Dominican Republic, Vietnam, Jamaica, Thailand, Guatemala and the United States, among others.

Research and criticism

The Ocean Cleanup has funded and published scientific research on ocean plastic, including aerial and trawl surveys of the Great Pacific Garbage Patch and a 2018 study in Scientific Reports estimating the patch at some 1.8 trillion pieces of plastic weighing about 80,000 tonnes, much of it — by mass — abandoned fishing gear. Marine scientists have voiced criticisms and concerns, including the risk of harming surface-dwelling organisms (the neuston), carbon emissions from towing vessels, and whether cleanup diverts attention from reducing plastic production; the organisation has responded with environmental impact assessments and design changes, and has emphasised recycling collected ocean plastic into verified products to fund operations.
