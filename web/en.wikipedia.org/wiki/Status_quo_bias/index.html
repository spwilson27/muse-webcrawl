Status quo bias

Status quo bias is an emotional bias in decision-making: a preference for the maintenance of the current state of affairs, such that the current baseline (the status quo) is taken as a reference point and any change from it is perceived as a loss. It is a cognitive bias studied in behavioural economics and psychology, closely related to loss aversion, the endowment effect, and omission bias.

Evidence

The term was coined by William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser in their 1988 paper "Status Quo Bias in Decision Making", which demonstrated the bias in experiments on consumer choice and policy preferences: subjects disproportionately chose options framed as the existing state. Status quo bias helps explain phenomena such as the power of defaults (for example in organ donation opt-in versus opt-out regimes and retirement savings auto-enrolment), consumer brand loyalty, and resistance to policy reform.

Explanations

Proposed explanations include loss aversion (losses from change loom larger than gains), anticipated regret, cognitive dissonance and rationalisation of prior choices, the mere-exposure effect, and the real costs of transition. Some degree of status quo preference can be rational where switching is costly or information is imperfect, so researchers distinguish irrational bias from legitimate inertia.

Applications

Policymakers exploit or counteract the bias through default rules — Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's "nudge" approach, for instance, relies on setting beneficial options as the default so that status quo bias works in people's favour.
