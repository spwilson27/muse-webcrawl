Homestead strike

DateJune–November 1892
LocationHomestead, Pennsylvania, United States
PartiesAmalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers; Carnegie Steel Company
DeathsAt least 9 strikers/supporters and 7 Pinkerton agents (estimates vary)

The Homestead strike, also known as the Homestead steel strike, was an industrial lockout and strike at the Homestead steel mill in Pennsylvania in 1892. The dispute between the Carnegie Steel Company and the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers (AA) culminated in a battle between strikers and Pinkerton private security agents on 6 July 1892, and became one of the most serious labour disputes in United States history.

Background

Homestead was one of Andrew Carnegie's largest steel works. Under operations manager Henry Clay Frick, known for his hostility to unions, the company sought to cut wages as the AA's contract came up for renewal in 1892, and Frick moved to break the union's power at the mill. Negotiations deadlocked; on 30 June the company locked out the workforce, fenced the plant, and prepared to operate with strikebreakers guarded by some 300 Pinkerton agents.

The battle of 6 July

Before dawn on 6 July, two barges carrying the Pinkertons attempted to land at the mill's riverfront on the Monongahela River. Thousands of strikers and townspeople confronted them, and a gun battle lasting many hours ensued. Several men on both sides were killed and many wounded. The Pinkertons eventually surrendered and were beaten as they were run through a gauntlet of townspeople, an ordeal widely reported in the press.

State intervention and defeat

On 12 July, Pennsylvania Governor Robert Pattison sent the state militia — some 8,000 troops — to occupy Homestead, and the plant reopened under military protection with strikebreakers. Public sympathy for the strikers eroded further after the anarchist Alexander Berkman attempted to assassinate Frick on 23 July. The strike committee's funds ran out, strike leaders were prosecuted, and on 20 November 1892 the strike collapsed. The AA was effectively destroyed at Homestead and across Carnegie Steel; the steel industry remained largely unorganized for decades afterwards.

Legacy

The strike became a defining episode of the Gilded Age labour movement, illustrating the willingness of employers to use private armed force and of governments to deploy troops in industrial disputes, and it set back unionization in American steel until the 1930s.
