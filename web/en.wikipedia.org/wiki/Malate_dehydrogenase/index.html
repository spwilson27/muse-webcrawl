Malate dehydrogenase

Malate dehydrogenase
EC number1.1.1.37
ReactionMalate + NAD⁺ ⇌ oxaloacetate + NADH + H⁺
LocationMitochondria and cytosol

Malate dehydrogenase (MDH) is an enzyme that reversibly catalyses the oxidation of malate to oxaloacetate using the reduction of NAD+ to NADH. This reaction is the final step of the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle), regenerating oxaloacetate so that the cycle can continue. Malate dehydrogenase occurs in all aerobic organisms and exists in two principal isoforms in eukaryotes: a mitochondrial form that functions in the citric acid cycle, and a cytosolic form that participates in the malate–aspartate shuttle, which transfers reducing equivalents across the mitochondrial membrane.

Reaction and mechanism

The enzyme is a dimer of identical subunits, each with an NAD-binding domain of the Rossmann fold type and a substrate-binding domain. Catalysis proceeds by an ordered mechanism: NAD+ binds first, then malate. A histidine residue in the active site acts as a general base, accepting the proton from malate's hydroxyl group while the hydride is transferred to NAD+, forming oxaloacetate and NADH. Although the equilibrium strongly favours malate formation, the reaction runs toward oxaloacetate in the mitochondrion because oxaloacetate is rapidly consumed by citrate synthase and NADH by the respiratory chain.

Isoenzymes

In humans, mitochondrial MDH is encoded by the gene MDH2 and cytosolic MDH by MDH1. Plants possess additional isoforms in chloroplasts (an NADP-dependent form important in C4 photosynthesis and redox regulation) and in peroxisomes and glyoxysomes. Some bacteria and archaea have further specialised forms, including enzymes using quinones rather than NAD.

Role in metabolism

  • Citric acid cycle: oxidises malate to oxaloacetate in the mitochondrial matrix.
  • Malate–aspartate shuttle: cytosolic MDH reduces oxaloacetate to malate using NADH generated in glycolysis; malate crosses into the mitochondrion, where mitochondrial MDH re-oxidises it, delivering NADH to the electron transport chain.
  • Gluconeogenesis and C4 photosynthesis: malate serves as a transportable store of carbon and reducing power.
