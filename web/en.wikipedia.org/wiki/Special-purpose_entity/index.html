Special-purpose entity
Also known asSpecial-purpose vehicle (SPV); special-purpose company
TypeSubsidiary company with restricted purpose
Common usesSecuritization, project finance, risk isolation, asset holding

A special-purpose entity (SPE), also called a special-purpose vehicle (SPV) or special-purpose company, is a legal entity (usually a limited company or limited partnership) created to fulfil narrow, specific, or temporary objectives. SPEs are typically used to isolate financial risk: their assets and liabilities are legally separated ("ring-fenced") from those of the sponsoring company, so that the SPE's obligations are secured only by its own assets and the sponsor's creditworthiness is not directly affected if the SPE fails, and vice versa (bankruptcy remoteness).

Uses

  • Securitization — A sponsor transfers receivables, mortgages, or loans to an SPE, which issues securities backed by those assets to investors (asset-backed and mortgage-backed securities; collateralized debt obligations).
  • Project finance — Infrastructure and energy projects are held in SPEs so lenders have recourse only to project cash flows.
  • Asset holding and leasing — Aircraft, ships, and property are often held by SPEs for legal, tax, or regulatory reasons.
  • Risk transfer and derivatives — SPEs issue structured notes and catastrophe bonds.

Abuses and reform

SPEs gained notoriety in the Enron scandal (2001), where entities such as Chewco and the Raptors were used to conceal debt and inflate profits, keeping liabilities off Enron's balance sheet. Abuses led to tighter accounting rules, including FASB's FIN 46 and IFRS 10 consolidation standards, which require entities controlled by a sponsor to be consolidated. SPEs also featured prominently in the 2007–2008 financial crisis through structured investment vehicles (SIVs) and off-balance-sheet conduits.
