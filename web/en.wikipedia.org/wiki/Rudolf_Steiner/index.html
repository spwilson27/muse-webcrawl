Rudolf Joseph Lorenz Steiner (27 February 1861 – 30 March 1925) was an Austrian philosopher, esotericist, social reformer, architect, and claimed clairvoyant. He gained initial recognition as a literary critic and editor of the works of Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, and in the early twentieth century founded the spiritual movement anthroposophy, an esoteric philosophy rooted in German idealist philosophy and theosophy.

Steiner's ideas influenced a wide range of practical fields. He founded Waldorf education, the most numerous independent school movement in the world; biodynamic agriculture, a precursor of modern organic farming; anthroposophical medicine; and the expressive movement art of eurythmy. He also designed the Goetheanum in Dornach, Switzerland, a monumental building serving as the headquarters of the Anthroposophical Society.

Biography

Early life and education

Steiner was born in Murakirály (now Donji Kraljevec, Croatia), the son of a telegraph operator and stationmaster in the service of the Southern Austrian Railway. The family moved to Vienna when he was an infant, and Steiner grew up in various small towns in Lower Austria. From 1879 to 1883 he studied mathematics, physics, and philosophy at the Vienna Institute of Technology (Technische Hochschule) on a scholarship, and attended lectures in philosophy and literature at the University of Vienna, including courses by Franz Brentano.

In 1891 Steiner received a doctorate in philosophy from the University of Rostock with a thesis on Fichte's concept of the ego, later expanded into his book Truth and Science (1892). His philosophical work culminated in The Philosophy of Freedom (1894), an exploration of epistemology and ethics that he regarded as the philosophical foundation of all his later work.

Goethe editor and Berlin years

In 1888 Steiner was invited to edit Goethe's scientific writings for the Kürschner edition of Goethe's complete works, and from 1890 to 1897 he worked at the Goethe-Schiller Archive in Weimar on the authoritative Sophien edition of Goethe's works. In 1897 he moved to Berlin, where he edited the literary journal Magazin für Literatur, taught at the Workers' School founded by Wilhelm Liebknecht, and lectured widely.

Theosophy and anthroposophy

Around 1899 Steiner began speaking publicly about spiritual experience. He became general secretary of the German section of the Theosophical Society in 1902, lecturing extensively on esoteric Christianity, Rosicrucianism, and his own "spiritual science." His emphasis on Christian and Western esoteric traditions, and his refusal to subordinate the society to its Eastern-oriented leadership, led to a break: between 1912 and 1913 Steiner and most of the German section separated from the Theosophical Society and founded the Anthroposophical Society.

Practical work

Waldorf education

In 1919 Emil Molt, owner of the Waldorf-Astoria cigarette factory in Stuttgart, asked Steiner to found a school for the children of factory workers. The resulting Free Waldorf School became the prototype of Waldorf education, characterised by a developmental curriculum, emphasis on the arts, and the class teacher remaining with the same class for several years.

Biodynamic agriculture

In 1924, at the request of farmers concerned about declining seed viability and soil quality, Steiner delivered a series of eight lectures at Koberwitz, Silesia, that founded biodynamic agriculture — among the earliest movements of modern organic agriculture, combining farming with astronomical planting calendars and specific compost preparations.

Architecture and the Goetheanum

Steiner designed seventeen buildings, most notably the first and second Goetheanum in Dornach. The first, an elaborate wooden structure completed in 1920, was destroyed by arson on New Year's Eve 1922/23. The second Goetheanum, built of reinforced concrete and completed in 1928 after Steiner's death, is considered a pioneering work of modern architecture.

Death and legacy

Steiner died on 30 March 1925 in Dornach, Switzerland. His collected works comprise some 350 volumes of books, lectures, and artistic works. Anthroposophically inspired institutions — schools, farms, clinics, and Camphill communities for people with developmental disabilities — operate worldwide. Critics have characterised aspects of Steiner's work, including some statements on race, as pseudoscientific or racially problematic; defenders argue these must be read in historical context and note his explicit condemnations of racial hatred.
