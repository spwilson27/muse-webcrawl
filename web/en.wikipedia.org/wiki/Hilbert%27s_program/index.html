Hilbert's program

Hilbert's program was a foundational program in mathematics proposed by the German mathematician David Hilbert in the 1920s. Its goal was to secure all of mathematics on a complete, consistent, and finitary axiomatic basis: to formalize every branch of mathematics and prove, using only uncontroversial finitary methods, that the resulting system could never yield a contradiction.

Origins

Hilbert had called for a consistency proof for arithmetic at the 1900 International Congress of Mathematicians (his second problem). Faced with paradoxes in set theory and intuitionist challenges from L. E. J. Brouwer, he developed proof theory ("metamathematics") with collaborators such as Wilhelm Ackermann and John von Neumann.

Incompleteness

In 1931 Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems showed that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, defeating the program in its original form. Nevertheless it spurred the development of mathematical logic and proof theory, and modified Hilbert-style programs continue in reverse mathematics and ordinal analysis.
