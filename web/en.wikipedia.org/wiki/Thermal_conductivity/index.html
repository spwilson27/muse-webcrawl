Thermal conductivity

The thermal conductivity of a material, usually denoted k (or λ), is a measure of its ability to conduct heat. It is defined through Fourier's law of conduction: in steady state, the heat flux q through a material is proportional to the negative of the temperature gradient, q = −k∇T. Thermal conductivity is expressed in watts per metre-kelvin (W·m−1·K−1) in SI units. Materials of high thermal conductivity — metals such as silver (≈429 W/m·K), copper (≈401) and diamond (over 1000) — are used as heat conductors and heat sinks, while materials of low conductivity, such as plastic foams, mineral wool and aerogels (≈0.015–0.04), are used as thermal insulators.

Definition and measurement

For a slab of thickness L and area A with a temperature difference ΔT across it, the rate of heat flow is Q = kAΔT/L; the reciprocal arrangement defines thermal resistance, which in building science is widely used in the form of R-values. Thermal conductivity is not a strict constant: it varies with temperature, pressure, phase, and (in anisotropic materials such as wood or graphite) direction. Standard measurement methods include the guarded hot plate and heat-flow meter for insulators, and transient techniques such as the laser flash method, which measures thermal diffusivity, for conductors.

Mechanisms

In solids, heat is carried by lattice vibrations (phonons) and, in metals, predominantly by free electrons — hence the strong correlation between electrical and thermal conductivity in metals expressed by the Wiedemann–Franz law. In pure metals conductivity generally decreases with temperature as phonon scattering of electrons increases, while in crystalline insulators it can rise steeply at low temperature before peaking and falling. In fluids, conduction is governed by molecular collisions and is much weaker than convection; gases have conductivities of order 0.02 W/m·K and liquids 0.1–0.6 W/m·K.

Values and applications

Representative room-temperature values: silver 429, copper 401, aluminium 237, iron ≈80, stainless steel ≈15, water 0.6, glass ≈1, brick ≈0.7, wood 0.1–0.2, expanded polystyrene ≈0.033, still air 0.026 W/m·K. Diamond and engineered materials such as graphene and carbon nanotubes exceed all common metals. Engineers select materials by conductivity for heat exchangers, cookware, semiconductor cooling, building insulation, cryogenics and spacecraft thermal control; interfaces introduce an additional contact resistance often handled through thermal interface materials.
