Complement (linguistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A verb phrase with its complements in a phrase structure tree

In linguistics, a complement is a word, phrase, or clause that is necessary to complete the meaning of a given expression. Complements are so named because they are usually required to make a sentence complete and grammatical: removing a true complement often renders the sentence incomplete or changes the meaning of the predicate. Complements contrast with adjuncts, which add optional, circumstantial information and can be freely omitted.

Types of complements

  • Object complements — noun phrases or clauses required by transitive verbs, e.g. the direct object in "She read the book".
  • Subject complements — expressions following a linking verb (copula) that describe or identify the subject, including predicate nominals ("He is a doctor") and predicate adjectives ("The soup tastes good").
  • Prepositional complements — phrases required by a verb or adjective, such as "rely on her" or "afraid of dogs".
  • Clausal complements — subordinate clauses functioning as complements, as in "I think that she left" or "She wants to leave".

Complements and adjuncts

The distinction between complements and adjuncts is a central topic in syntax. Complements are selected (subcategorized) by a head — a verb, for instance, determines how many and what kind of complements it takes — whereas adjuncts are not selected and can usually be stacked freely. Standard diagnostics include optionality, iterability, and the behaviour of the constituent under movement and substitution tests.

In grammatical theory

In phrase structure grammar and X-bar theory, a complement is the sister of a head within a phrase, distinguished from a specifier and from adjuncts, which adjoin to a higher projection. In functional and traditional grammar, the term is used more broadly for any element that completes a predication, including the objects of verbs and the predicative expressions after copular verbs. In some frameworks, such as HPSG and LFG, the notion is captured through argument structure and subcategorization lists.
