Self-affirmation

Self-affirmation theory is a psychological framework, introduced by Claude Steele in 1988, holding that people are motivated to maintain a global sense of self-integrity — a perception of themselves as morally and adaptively adequate. When this self-image is threatened in one domain, people can restore it by affirming values or strengths in another, unrelated domain.

Mechanism

Affirmations in experimental research typically involve writing briefly about a personally important value, such as relationships, creativity or humour. By broadening the working self-concept beyond the threatened domain, affirmation reduces defensiveness: people become more open to threatening health information, more accepting of counter-attitudinal arguments, and less prone to biased assimilation of evidence.

Applications

Brief values-affirmation exercises have been shown to narrow achievement gaps for students belonging to negatively stereotyped groups, improve receptivity to health-risk messages, and buffer the physiological effects of stress. Effects are thought to be recursive: a small early improvement can set off cycles of success that persist over time.

Criticism

Some replication attempts have found smaller or context-dependent effects, and researchers continue to debate which populations and settings reliably benefit, as well as the precise boundary conditions of the theory.
