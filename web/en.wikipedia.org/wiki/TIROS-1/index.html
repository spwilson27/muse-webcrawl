TIROS-1 (Television Infrared Observation Satellite) was the world's first successful dedicated weather satellite, launched by the United States on 1 April 1960. The drum-shaped, spin-stabilised spacecraft carried two small television cameras—one wide-angle and one narrow-angle—that photographed Earth's cloud cover from an orbit about 700 km high, storing images on tape recorders for playback over ground stations.

Mission

During its 78 days of operation, TIROS-1 transmitted roughly 23,000 pictures of clouds and storms, demonstrating that satellites could observe global weather patterns, track storms and even reveal the structure of hurricanes. A magnetic attitude-control system kept the cameras pointed appropriately as the satellite spun. An electrical fault ended transmissions in June 1960, but the concept was proven and nine more TIROS satellites followed by 1965, leading to the operational TIROS-N/NOAA polar-orbiting series and today's JPSS programme.

Legacy

TIROS-1 is regarded as the founding mission of satellite meteorology; modern weather forecasting, storm warnings and climate monitoring all descend from its demonstration that routine imaging of the atmosphere from orbit was feasible and valuable.
