NTSC (from the National Television System Committee) was the first widely adopted analogue television colour encoding system. It was used in most of North America, Japan, South Korea, the Philippines and parts of South America until the transition to digital broadcasting in the early twenty-first century.

History

The committee was convened by the US Federal Communications Commission in 1940 to standardise black-and-white television, resulting in the 525-line, 30-frame standard of 1941. A second committee produced a compatible colour system, approved in December 1953, which added colour information on a subcarrier within the existing channel so that monochrome sets still worked. The first coast-to-coast colour broadcast followed in 1954.

Technical overview

An NTSC picture has 525 scan lines, of which about 480–486 are visible, interlaced into two fields. To fit the colour subcarrier (approximately 3.58 MHz) without interference, the field rate was shifted slightly from 60 Hz to 59.94 Hz, giving the characteristic 29.97 frames per second. Colour is encoded as luminance plus two colour-difference components carried in quadrature on the subcarrier; phase errors in transmission could shift hues, a weakness that earned the system the joking expansion "Never Twice the Same Color" and led European rivals PAL and SECAM to adopt phase-correcting designs.

Decline

Analog NTSC broadcasting ended in the United States in 2009, in Canada and Mexico in the following decade, and in Japan in 2011, replaced by digital standards such as ATSC. The name survives informally to distinguish 480-line, 29.97 fps video formats and the DVD/video-game regions that used them.
