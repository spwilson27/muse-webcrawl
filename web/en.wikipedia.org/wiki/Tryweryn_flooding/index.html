The Tryweryn flooding (Welsh: Boddi Tryweryn) refers to the flooding of the Tryweryn Valley in north Wales in 1965 to create the Llyn Celyn reservoir, which supplies water to the city of Liverpool. The flooding submerged the Welsh-speaking village of Capel Celyn, whose residents were forced to leave. It remains one of the most controversial and symbolically charged events in modern Welsh history and a rallying point for Welsh nationalism.

Background

In 1957 Liverpool Corporation promoted a private bill in Parliament authorising the damming of the Afon Tryweryn. Under the procedure used, the consent of the Welsh local authorities was not required. All but one of the Welsh MPs who voted opposed the bill, but it passed anyway — a fact widely cited in Wales as proof that Welsh interests could be overridden at Westminster.

Protests

The decision provoked years of protest. Plaid Cymru campaigned against the scheme; a campaign of civil disobedience included the bombing of an electricity transformer at the dam site in 1963 by members of Mudiad Amddiffyn Cymru. On 21 October 1965 the valley was flooded and Capel Celyn — its homes, chapel, school, post office and cemetery — was lost beneath the reservoir.

Legacy

The slogan Cofiwch Dryweryn ("Remember Tryweryn"), painted on a wall near Llanrhystud and repeatedly defaced and restored, became an enduring emblem of Welsh national consciousness. In 2005 Liverpool City Council formally apologised for the flooding. The event is widely credited with spurring support for Welsh devolution.
