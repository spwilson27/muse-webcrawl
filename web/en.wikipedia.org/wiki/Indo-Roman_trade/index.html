Indo-Roman trade relations

Indo-Roman trade relations were the extensive commercial exchanges between the Indian subcontinent and the Roman Empire, conducted overland through the Silk Road and, more importantly, by sea across the Indian Ocean. The maritime trade flourished from roughly the 1st century BCE to the 2nd century CE, after the discovery (or popularisation) of the monsoon winds allowed ships to sail directly between the Red Sea ports of Roman Egypt and the western coast of India.

Background

Trade between India and the Mediterranean world long predated Rome. Goods from India reached the Hellenistic kingdoms through Persian and Arabian intermediaries. After Egypt was annexed by Augustus in 30 BCE, Roman merchants gained direct access to Red Sea ports such as Myos Hormos and Berenice, and the volume of trade increased dramatically. The geographer Strabo noted that as many as 120 ships a year sailed from Myos Hormos to India in his time.

Goods traded

Roman exports to India included gold and silver coin, glassware, wine, coral, copper, tin, lead, and fine textiles. India exported luxury goods highly prized in Rome: spices (above all black pepper), silk (re-exported from China), cotton textiles, ivory, gemstones, pearls, tortoiseshell, and exotic animals. Pliny the Elder famously complained that India, Arabia and China together drained the empire of 100 million sesterces a year, calling India "the sink of the world's gold."

Ports and routes

On the Indian side, the principal ports included Barygaza (modern Bharuch) in the north-west, and the Malabar Coast ports of Muziris and Nelcynda in the south-west, as well as Arikamedu on the south-eastern coast. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek merchant's handbook of the mid-1st century CE, describes these ports and their markets in detail.

Evidence

Archaeological evidence includes large hoards of Roman gold and silver coins found in southern India, Roman pottery and amphorae at sites such as Arikamedu, and the Muziris Papyrus, a 2nd-century document recording a loan agreement for a cargo shipped from India to Egypt. Tamil Sangam literature also refers to the Yavanas (westerners) who came in ships bringing gold and wine and returned with pepper.

Decline

The trade declined from the 3rd century CE amid the political and economic troubles of the Roman Empire, though it never ceased entirely and was later carried on by Byzantine, Persian and Arab merchants.
