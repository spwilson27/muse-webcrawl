Latin rights

Communities holding the ius Latii in Roman Italy.
Latin nameius Latii, Latinitas
StatusIntermediate civic status in the Roman world

Latin rights (Latin: ius Latii or Latinitas) were a civic status granted by ancient Rome to certain inhabitants of its territories, originally the peoples of the Latin League and later communities throughout the empire. Holders of Latin rights stood between full Roman citizens and non-citizen peregrini.

Content of the rights

Latin status typically included commercium (the right to trade and hold property under Roman law), conubium (the right to contract legally recognised marriages), and often the right to migrate and acquire Roman citizenship by settling in Rome. Under the empire a common form, the Latium maius, granted citizenship to local magistrates and town councillors. Communities holding Latin rights used Roman-style institutions and magistracies, making the status a stepping stone to full citizenship — a mechanism that helped Romanise the provinces.

History

After the Latin War (340–338 BC) the rights were granted to Latin towns in varying forms. The status spread widely in the western provinces under the emperors: Vespasian famously granted Latin rights to the whole of Hispania around 73–74 AD. Latin rights became largely obsolete after the Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 AD extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire.
