Beriberi

Beriberi is a disease caused by a deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). It damages the peripheral nerves and cardiovascular system and classically occurs where diets consist largely of polished white rice, in which the thiamine-rich husk has been removed. The name is thought to derive from a Sinhalese phrase meaning "weak, weak".

Signs and symptoms

Wet beriberi

Wet beriberi mainly affects the cardiovascular system, causing a rapid heartbeat, vasodilation, high-output heart failure and oedema (fluid accumulation), especially in the legs. The fulminant form, Shoshin beriberi, can be rapidly fatal without treatment.

Dry beriberi

Dry beriberi is a peripheral neuropathy: symmetrical numbness and weakness beginning in the legs, muscle wasting, loss of reflexes and difficulty walking. In the brain, thiamine deficiency causes Wernicke encephalopathy and Korsakoff syndrome, seen especially in people with alcohol use disorder.

History

Beriberi was a scourge in East and Southeast Asia in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The Japanese naval surgeon Takaki Kanehiro reduced it aboard ship in the 1880s by adding meat and barley to the diet. Christiaan Eijkman showed in the 1890s that chickens fed polished rice developed a similar polyneuritis cured by rice bran, and Gerrit Grijns proposed that rice lacked an essential substance. Casimir Funk isolated the "vitamine" concept from this work, and thiamine was synthesised in 1936. Eijkman shared the 1929 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment is thiamine, given intravenously in severe cases, with rapid improvement of cardiac symptoms. Prevention relies on a varied diet, parboiling or enriching rice, and fortification of staple foods.
