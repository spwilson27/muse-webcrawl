Automatic meter reading

Automatic meter reading (AMR) is the technology of automatically collecting consumption, diagnostic and status data from water meter or energy metering devices (gas, electric) and transferring that data to a central database for billing, troubleshooting and analysing. This saves utility providers the expense of periodic trips to each physical location to read a meter. Another advantage is that billing can be based on near real-time consumption rather than on estimates based on previous consumption or on an impromptu reading.

Technologies

AMR systems include handheld, mobile and network technologies based on telephony platforms (both wired and wireless), radio frequency (RF), or powerline transmission, as well as satellite and GPS methods. In touch-based systems a reader contacts a touchpad wired to the meter. In radio systems, the meter module transmits a signal that is collected by a handheld receiver, a vehicle passing in the street ("drive-by"), an aircraft, or a fixed network of collectors. Many early deployments used one-way communication, with data flowing only from meter to reader.

History

Remote reading experiments date to the 1970s, using telephone lines to interrogate meters. Large-scale deployment began in the late 1980s and 1990s in the United States, driven by labour costs and difficult-to-access meters; by the mid-2000s millions of endpoints were installed by water, gas and electric utilities.

Advanced metering infrastructure

AMR differs from advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) primarily in that AMI enables two-way communication with the meter, so the utility can send prices, disconnect commands or firmware updates as well as receive readings. Smart meters deployed under AMI programmes provide interval data for time-of-use billing and outage detection, and AMR is often regarded as the first generation of this technology.

Benefits and concerns

Advantages include accurate bills, reduced operating costs, faster leak and outage detection for water and electricity networks, and data for load forecasting. Concerns raised about radio-based systems include privacy of detailed consumption data and, in public debate, the health effects of low-power radio emissions, which regulators have generally found to be far below exposure limits.
