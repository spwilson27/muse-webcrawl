American flamingo

The American flamingo (Phoenicopterus ruber), also known as the Caribbean flamingo, is a large species of flamingo native to the Caribbean Sea region, the Yucatán Peninsula, the northern coasts of South America and the Galápagos Islands. It is the only flamingo that naturally occurs in North America, where a population exists in southern Florida. Its deep pink-to-reddish plumage — richer in colour than that of the greater flamingo, with which it was once considered conspecific — comes from carotenoid pigments in its food.

Description and feeding

Adults stand 120–145 cm tall with a wingspan of about 140–165 cm. The bill is pink with a black tip and a pronounced downward bend, specialised for filter feeding: the bird holds its head upside down in shallow water and uses its tongue as a piston to pump water through comb-like lamellae, trapping algae, diatoms, small crustaceans and insect larvae. The shrimp and algae it consumes provide the carotenoids responsible for its colour; captive birds fade without dietary supplements.

Breeding and behaviour

American flamingos are highly social, living in flocks that can number in the thousands. They breed in shallow saline lagoons and mudflats — major colonies exist in the Bahamas (Great Inagua), Cuba, the Yucatán (Ría Celestún and Ría Lagartos), Bonaire and the Camargue-like wetlands of Venezuela and Colombia. Both parents build a cone-shaped mud nest and incubate a single chalky-white egg for roughly 27–31 days, feeding the chick on a secretion called crop milk. Group displays of synchronised marching, head-flagging and wing-saluting stimulate breeding.

Florida population

Once hunted nearly to extirpation in Florida, flamingos were long treated as escapees from captivity, but research published in 2018 concluded that Florida's flamingos are a recovering native population, and the species is again seen regularly in the Everglades and Florida Bay.
