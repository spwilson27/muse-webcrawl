Power of attorney

Power of attorney
Legal areaAgency law, private law
PartiesPrincipal (donor/grantor); agent (attorney-in-fact)
TypesGeneral, special/limited, durable, springing, lasting (England & Wales)

A power of attorney (POA) is a legal authorisation by which one person (the principal, donor or grantor) gives another person (the agent or attorney-in-fact) the authority to act on the principal's behalf in legal, financial, business or health matters. The agent need not be a lawyer; "attorney" here is used in its older sense of a person appointed to act for another — contrasted with an "attorney-at-law". The authority may be broad (general) or confined to specified acts (special or limited), and historically the document was executed as a deed under seal.

Executing a power of attorney, typically in writing and witnessed.

Types

  • General power of attorney: authorises the agent in all matters the principal could act in, typically used for defined periods such as absence abroad.
  • Special or limited power: confined to a particular transaction, such as selling a property or operating a bank account.
  • Durable power of attorney: under US state law, survives the principal's later incapacity (a traditional, non-durable power ends on incapacity). A springing power takes effect only on a specified future event, usually certified incapacity.
  • Healthcare power of attorney / proxy: authorises medical decisions when the principal cannot make them.
  • Lasting power of attorney (LPA): in England and Wales, introduced by the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (replacing enduring powers of attorney), covering property and financial affairs or health and welfare, and requiring registration with the Office of the Public Guardian.

Creation and termination

Requirements vary by jurisdiction but typically include writing, signature by the principal while mentally competent, and witnessing or notarisation; some types require registration. The authority ends on revocation by a competent principal, on the principal's death, on the expiry of a fixed term or completion of its purpose, or (for non-durable powers) on the principal's incapacity. The agent owes fiduciary duties of loyalty and care and must act within the granted authority and in the principal's interests.
