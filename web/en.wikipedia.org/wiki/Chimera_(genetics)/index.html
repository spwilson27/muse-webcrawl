Chimera (genetics)

A genetic chimaera (or chimera) is a single organism composed of cells with more than one distinct genotype. In animals, this means an individual derived from two or more zygotes, which can include features such as having blood cells of different blood types, or subtle variations in form. Animal chimaeras are produced by the merger of two embryos. In plant chimaeras, by contrast, the different tissue types may originate from the same zygote, and the difference is often due to mutation during ordinary cell division.

Types

Tetragametic chimaerism arises when two separately fertilised eggs fuse into one embryo, so the individual carries four parental contributions. Microchimaerism occurs when a small population of genetically distinct cells persists in an individual, most commonly fetal cells retained in a mother after pregnancy, or maternal cells in offspring. Chimaerism can also be artificially induced by organ transplantation and bone marrow grafts, and it occurs naturally in many marine invertebrates and in callitrichid primates such as marmosets, which routinely share placental circulation between twins.

Human chimaerism

Human chimaeras were long thought to be extremely rare, but DNA testing has revealed cases in which a person carries two sets of DNA — famously in maternity disputes where standard tests suggested a woman was not the mother of her children because her blood DNA differed from the DNA of other tissues. Some human chimaeras have ambiguous sex characteristics when the fused embryos were of different sexes, though many cases show no outward signs at all.

Research use

Experimentally produced chimaeras, such as mice grown from injected embryonic stem cells, are fundamental tools in developmental biology and genetics, allowing researchers to study gene function in specific tissues. Interspecies chimaeras raise significant ethical debate, particularly where human cells contribute to animal embryos.
