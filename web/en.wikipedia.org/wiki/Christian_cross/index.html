Christian cross


Placeholder image: forms of the Christian cross

The Christian cross, seen as a representation of the instrument of the crucifixion of Jesus, is the best-known symbol of Christianity. It is related to the crucifix (a cross bearing the figure of Christ) and to the family's broader "cross" devices used in heraldry and vexillology.

Forms

  • The Latin cross (crux immissa, †) has an upright post longer than the transverse bar, set above the midpoint; it is the most common form in Western churches.
  • The Greek cross has four arms of equal length and is typical of Eastern Orthodox art.
  • The crux commissa or Tau cross is T-shaped.
  • Other variants include the patriarchal cross (double-barred), the papal cross (triple-barred), the cross pattée, the Celtic cross (with a ring), the Russian Orthodox cross (with a slanted lower bar) and the inverted cross of St Peter.

History

Early Christians used several signs, including the fish (ichthys) and the staurogram; the cross became prominent after the conversion of Constantine, who attributed his victory at the Milvian Bridge (312) to a vision of the cross, and after the dedication of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. The custom of making the sign of the cross is attested by Tertullian around 200 AD. During the Reformation, many Protestant churches removed crucifixes and imagery while retaining the plain cross; some traditions, such as the Quakers and (in public worship) The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, do not use the cross as an emblem.

Use

Crosses mark churches, graves and altars; they are worn as jewellery, carried in processions, and awarded as decorations (for example the Victoria Cross and the Iron Cross, the latter predating its military use as a Christian emblem of the Teutonic Order). The red cross on a white ground is the protective emblem of medical services under the Geneva Conventions.
