Developmental language disorder

Developmental language disorder (DLD) is a neurodevelopmental condition in which a child has persistent difficulties learning, understanding and using spoken (and sometimes written) language that are not explained by another condition such as hearing loss, intellectual disability or autism. Previously known as specific language impairment (SLI), the condition affects roughly 7% of children — about two in every classroom — making it one of the most common developmental disorders, though it remains relatively little known.

Characteristics

Children with DLD may have a limited vocabulary, make frequent grammatical errors (such as omitting tense markers), struggle to follow instructions or understand complex sentences, and find it hard to tell stories or hold conversations. Profiles vary: some children have mainly expressive difficulties, others receptive or mixed difficulties. The problems persist into adolescence and adulthood and are associated with increased risk of literacy difficulties, lower educational attainment, and social, emotional and mental-health problems.

Causes and diagnosis

DLD is strongly heritable and runs in families, but no single gene explains most cases; genes such as FOXP2 account for rare, severe speech disorders rather than typical DLD. Diagnosis is made by speech and language therapists using standardised assessments and clinical history, under criteria consolidated by the CATALISE consortium (2016–2017), which recommended the term "developmental language disorder" and dropped the older requirement of a discrepancy between language and non-verbal ability.

Intervention

There is no cure, but speech and language therapy, targeted teaching of vocabulary and grammar, and classroom support improve outcomes. Awareness campaigns, such as the international RADLD movement, promote earlier identification, since many children with DLD are identified late or misidentified as having behavioural difficulties.
