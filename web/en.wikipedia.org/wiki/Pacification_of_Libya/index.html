The Pacification of Libya (Italian: riconquista della Libia, "reconquest of Libya") was the protracted campaign by Fascist Italy, principally between 1923 and 1932, to subdue Libyan resistance and establish effective colonial control over Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and the Fezzan. The campaign is remembered for its exceptional brutality, including mass deportations, concentration camps, and the use of poison gas, and is described by many historians as involving genocidal violence against the population of Cyrenaica.

Background

Italy had seized Libya from the Ottoman Empire in the Italo-Turkish War of 1911–12, but Italian control never extended much beyond the coastal cities. During the First World War, Libyan resistance — above all the Senussi order in Cyrenaica, supported first by the Ottomans and then operating independently — rolled back Italian positions. Post-war Italian governments negotiated accords with local leaders, but Benito Mussolini's Fascist government, in power from 1922, resolved to reconquer the interior by force.

The campaign

From 1923, Italian forces under commanders including Rodolfo Graziani reoccupied Tripolitania, suppressing tribal resistance district by district. The Fezzan was subdued by 1930. The hardest fighting took place in Cyrenaica, where the Senussi resistance was led in the field by the elderly Qur'an teacher Omar al-Mukhtar, whose guerrilla bands used intimate knowledge of the Jebel Akhdar ("Green Mountain") terrain to ambush Italian columns for nearly a decade.

Counterinsurgency measures

To break the resistance, Graziani, appointed vice-governor of Cyrenaica in 1930, adopted drastic measures. The entire nomadic and semi-nomadic population of the Jebel — roughly 100,000 people — was deported to concentration camps in the desert lowlands, where tens of thousands died of hunger, disease, and neglect; their livestock were confiscated or slaughtered on a massive scale. A barbed-wire barrier some 270 kilometres long was built along the Egyptian frontier to cut off supplies, and Italian aircraft used chemical weapons despite the 1925 Geneva Protocol. Omar al-Mukhtar was captured in September 1931 and hanged before an audience of camp inmates at Suluq on 16 September 1931. Organised resistance collapsed soon after; Governor Pietro Badoglio proclaimed the pacification complete in January 1932.

Consequences

Cyrenaica's population fell sharply — by some estimates the region lost a quarter or more of its inhabitants — and settled Italian colonisation followed, with large state-sponsored settlements of Italian farmers on expropriated land. Libya remained an Italian colony until the Second World War. In 2008 Italy and Libya signed a treaty in which Italy apologised for and agreed to compensate colonial-era suffering, acknowledging the campaign's devastating legacy.
