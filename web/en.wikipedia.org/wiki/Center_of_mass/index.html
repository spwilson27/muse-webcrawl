Center of mass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, the center of mass of a distribution of mass in space (sometimes referred to as the barycenter or balance point) is the unique point at any given time where the weighted relative position of the distributed mass sums to zero. It is the point at which a force may be applied to cause linear acceleration without angular acceleration. Calculations in mechanics are often simplified when formulated with respect to the center of mass.

For a system of particles with masses mi at positions ri, the center of mass R is defined as:

R = ( Σ mi ri ) / ( Σ mi )

For a continuous mass distribution with density ρ(r), the sum is replaced by an integral over the volume of the body.

History

The concept of the center of gravity was first studied by the ancient Greek mathematician and engineer Archimedes of Syracuse, who derived results for triangles, parabolas, and hemispheres. The theory was further developed by Simon Stevin, Galileo, and later by Isaac Newton in the Principia Mathematica.

Properties

The center of mass need not lie within the body itself: for a ring or a boomerang it lies in empty space. In a uniform gravitational field, the center of mass coincides with the center of gravity. The total linear momentum of a system equals the total mass times the velocity of the center of mass, and the motion of the center of mass is governed solely by the net external force.

Applications

The concept is central to aerospace engineering (locating an aircraft's balance point), biomechanics (analyzing human gait), astronomy (orbital barycenters, such as that of the Earth–Moon system), and sports science. Experimental methods to locate it include suspension from two points, reaction-board methods, and segmentation analysis.
