The Lochnagar mine (also called the Lochnagar Crater, French: La Grande Mine) was an underground explosive charge, secretly planted by the British during the First World War, which was detonated on 1 July 1916, the first day of the Battle of the Somme. The mine was dug beneath a German field fortification known as Schwabenhöhe ("Swabian Heights") near the village of La Boisselle in the Somme department of France.

Background

The mine was laid by the 185th Tunnelling Company of the Royal Engineers, assisted by the 179th Tunnelling Company. Named after Lochnagar Street, a trench from which the tunnellers began work, the gallery was driven some 300 metres through chalk beneath the German lines. The chamber was loaded with 60,000 pounds (27 tonnes) of ammonal explosive in two charges.

Detonation

The mine was sprung at 07:28 on 1 July 1916, two minutes before zero hour, by Captain James Young of the 185th Tunnelling Company. The explosion destroyed the German strongpoint, obliterated between 91 and 274 metres (300 and 900 feet) of German dugouts, trenches and tunnels, and created a vast crater some 98 metres (320 feet) in diameter and 21 metres (70 feet) deep. Debris was thrown up to 1,200 metres (4,000 feet) into the air; the blast was reportedly heard in London. It was one of the largest mines ever detonated in warfare up to that time.

Despite the destruction, the British infantry assault in the La Boisselle sector largely failed: the follow-up troops could not exploit the crater effectively, and the neighbouring positions were not taken on the first day.

The crater today

The Lochnagar Crater is the largest man-made mine crater created in the First World War on the Western Front. It was bought in 1978 by Richard Dunning to ensure its preservation, and is now maintained as a memorial. A remembrance ceremony is held at the crater at 07:28 every 1 July. A wooden walkway allows visitors to circle the rim of the crater, and a memorial cross stands nearby.
