Skill (labor)


A machinist exercising skilled manual labor

A skill is the ability to perform an action with determined results often within a given amount of time, energy, or both. In the context of labor, skills distinguish categories of work: skilled labor involves specialized training and knowledge, often acquired through apprenticeship, vocational education, or long experience, whereas unskilled labor requires little specific training.

Skilled labor

Skilled workers — such as electricians, plumbers, machinists, carpenters, and nurses — possess expertise that is scarce and takes time to acquire. Historically, craft guilds and later trade unions regulated entry into skilled trades, and skilled workers typically command higher wages because of the productivity and autonomy their expertise confers.

Hard and soft skills

Modern labor discussions distinguish hard skills (technical, teachable, measurable abilities such as programming or welding) from soft skills (interpersonal abilities such as communication, teamwork, and problem-solving). Employers increasingly screen for both.

Deskilling

The thesis of deskilling, associated with Harry Braverman's Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974), holds that mechanization, scientific management, and automation tend to fragment craft knowledge into routine tasks, transferring skill from workers to machines and management. Critics respond that technological change also creates new skilled occupations, producing ongoing debates about "upskilling" and the future of work.

Skill formation and policy

Governments invest in education and vocational training to raise workforce skill levels; the economics of human capital treats skill acquisition as an investment. Skill shortages — mismatches between available and demanded skills — are a recurring concern in labor-market policy.
