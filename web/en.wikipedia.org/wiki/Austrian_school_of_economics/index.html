Austrian school of economics

The Austrian school is a heterodox school of economic thought that originated in late-19th-century Vienna with the work of Carl Menger. It emphasises the spontaneous, organising power of the price mechanism, holds that economic phenomena result from the motivated actions of individuals (methodological individualism), and stresses the subjective nature of value, the role of time and uncertainty, and the problems of dispersed knowledge in economic calculation.

History

Menger's Principles of Economics (1871) independently launched the marginal utility theory of value alongside Jevons and Walras, and his debate with the German historical school gave the "Austrian" label — originally a slur — to the tradition. The second generation, Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk and Friedrich von Wieser, developed theories of capital and interest. In the 1920s–30s, Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek produced the Austrian theory of the business cycle and argued in the socialist calculation debate that rational allocation is impossible without market prices; Hayek's critique contributed to his 1974 Nobel Memorial Prize. After emigration from Nazi Europe, the school's centre moved to the United States, where Mises taught at NYU and influenced Murray Rothbard and Israel Kirzner.

Methodology and tenets

Mises called the school's deductive method praxeology — the logic of human action — and later Austrians have been sceptical of mathematical modelling and econometric testing in economics, a stance that places the school outside the mainstream. Other characteristic positions include a theory of money's non-neutrality, opposition to central banking and fractional-reserve credit expansion as causes of boom–bust cycles, and a strong defence of free markets and private property.
