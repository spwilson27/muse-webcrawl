A supercluster is a large group of smaller galaxy clusters and groups — among the largest known structures in the universe. Superclusters consist of chains and walls of clusters separated by enormous, nearly empty voids, forming the filamentary "cosmic web" revealed by redshift surveys. The Milky Way belongs to the Virgo Cluster, which is part of the Laniakea Supercluster, a structure some 520 million light-years across identified in 2014 and centred on the gravitational attractor region of the Great Attractor.

Discovery

The existence of second-order clustering was argued by Gérard de Vaucouleurs in the 1950s, who proposed a "Local Supergalaxy" centred on the Virgo Cluster. Systematic redshift surveys from the 1980s onward — notably the CfA survey, which revealed the "Great Wall" — confirmed that galaxies are organised into sheets and filaments on scales of hundreds of millions of light-years. The Shapley Supercluster, discovered by Harlow Shapley in the 1930s as an unusually rich concentration, remains one of the most massive known, with an estimated mass around 1016–1017 solar masses.

Structure and dynamics

Unlike galaxy clusters, most superclusters are not gravitationally bound as wholes: their overall expansion still follows the expansion of the universe, though their densest cores may eventually collapse. Defining superclusters by galaxy-density contours (as with Laniakea, defined by flows of galaxies toward a common basin of attraction) is an active methodological question.

Notable superclusters

  • Laniakea — contains the Local Group; c. 100,000 galaxies.
  • Shapley Supercluster — the most massive local concentration, in Centaurus.
  • Perseus–Pisces — a long chain visible in the autumn sky.
  • Saraswati — identified in 2017, c. 600 million light-years across.
  • South Pole Wall — announced 2020, hidden partly behind the Milky Way's plane.
