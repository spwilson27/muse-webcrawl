Medieval music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medieval music encompasses the sacred and secular music of Western Europe during the Middle Ages, from approximately 500 to 1400. It begins with monophonic chant and develops through the emergence of polyphony to the complex styles of the Ars Nova at the period's end.

Early period: chant

The earliest surviving medieval music is liturgical plainchant, a single unaccompanied melodic line sung in Latin. The repertory associated with the Roman rite is known as Gregorian chant. Notation developed gradually from neumes — memory aids written above the text — to the staff notation credited to Guido d'Arezzo in the 11th century, which made accurate transmission and teaching of melodies possible.

Polyphony

From the 9th century, singers began adding parallel voices (organum) to chant. At Notre Dame in Paris in the 12th and 13th centuries, composers such as Léonin and Pérotin created elaborate polyphony, and rhythm was systematized in the rhythmic modes. The motet became the leading genre of learned composition.

Ars Nova and late medieval music

In the 14th century the Ars Nova, exemplified by Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut — composer of the first complete polyphonic Mass setting by a single composer, the Messe de Nostre Dame — brought new rhythmic and notational complexity. The Ars subtilior pushed this sophistication further in southern France and Italy, where Francesco Landini was a leading figure. Secular song flourished throughout, in the works of troubadours, trouvères, and minnesingers, accompanied by instruments such as the vielle, lute, harp, and shawm.
