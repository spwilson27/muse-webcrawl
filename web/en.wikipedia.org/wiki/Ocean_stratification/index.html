Ocean stratification

Stratification divides the water column into layers of differing density, limiting vertical mixing.

Ocean stratification is the natural separation of the ocean's water into horizontal layers by density, with lighter water lying above denser water. Density differences arise from variations in temperature (forming a thermocline) and salinity (forming a halocline); the combined density gradient is called the pycnocline.

Structure

A typical stratified water column has three layers. The upper mixed layer is stirred by wind and waves and has nearly uniform properties. Below it lies the pycnocline, a zone of rapid density change with depth, and beneath that the deep ocean, which is cold, dense, and relatively uniform. Stratification is strongest in the tropics, where warm surface water overlies cold deep water, and weakest at high latitudes, where surface cooling can trigger convection and deep-water formation.

Effects

Stratification acts as a barrier to vertical exchange. It traps heat, carbon, and oxygen in particular layers and limits the upward supply of nutrients from the deep ocean to sunlit surface waters where phytoplankton grow. The strength of stratification therefore exerts a major control on marine primary productivity, ocean carbon uptake, and the ventilation of the deep sea.

Climate change

Observations indicate that the upper ocean has become more strongly stratified in recent decades as the surface warms faster than the depths. Increased stratification is expected to reduce the ocean's uptake of heat and carbon dioxide, reduce oxygen supply to the interior, and alter nutrient supply to surface ecosystems, providing a positive feedback to global warming.
