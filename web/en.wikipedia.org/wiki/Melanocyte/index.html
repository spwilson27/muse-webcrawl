LocationBasal layer of the epidermis, hair follicles, uvea, inner ear, meninges

Melanocytes are melanin-producing cells derived from the neural crest, found mainly in the basal layer (stratum basale) of the skin's epidermis, as well as in hair follicles, the uvea of the eye, the inner ear, the meninges, the heart and bone. Their pigment, melanin, gives color to skin, hair and eyes and protects underlying tissues from ultraviolet radiation.

Function

Within specialised organelles called melanosomes, the enzyme tyrosinase converts the amino acid tyrosine through DOPA and dopaquinone into melanin — mainly brown-black eumelanin or red-yellow pheomelanin. Mature melanosomes are transferred along the melanocyte's dendritic processes into neighbouring keratinocytes, where they cluster above the nucleus as a protective cap. Each melanocyte supplies pigment to roughly 30–40 keratinocytes, a unit called the epidermal melanin unit.

Skin color

All human populations have roughly similar numbers of melanocytes; differences in skin color arise chiefly from the amount, type and distribution of melanin produced and from melanosome size and packaging, all under genetic control. Exposure to ultraviolet light stimulates melanogenesis — tanning — via signals including α-MSH from keratinocytes, which raises melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) activity.

Development and related disorders

Melanocytes develop from melanoblasts that migrate from the neural crest during embryogenesis; mutations affecting this pathway cause pigmentary disorders such as piebaldism and Waardenburg syndrome. Loss or destruction of melanocytes produces vitiligo and the greying of hair with age, while failure of melanin synthesis causes albinism. Malignant transformation of melanocytes gives rise to melanoma, the most dangerous form of skin cancer.
