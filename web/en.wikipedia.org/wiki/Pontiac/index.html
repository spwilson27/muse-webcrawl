Pontiac

Pontiac was an American automobile brand owned, manufactured, and commercialized by General Motors. Introduced as a companion make for GM's more expensive line of Oakland automobiles, Pontiac overtook Oakland in popularity and replaced the brand entirely by 1933. The brand was known for its performance-oriented image, producing cars such as the GTO, the Firebird, and the Trans Am.

History

The Pontiac name was first used in 1893 by the Pontiac Buggy Company of Pontiac, Michigan, and later by the Pontiac Spring and Wagon Works. General Motors acquired control in 1909, and the first Pontiac car, a five-passenger coach, appeared in 1926 as a companion to the Oakland line. Pontiac sold roughly 77,000 cars in its first year, and by 1931 it had fully displaced Oakland.

During the 1960s Pontiac became famous for its emphasis on performance. Under the leadership of figures such as John DeLorean, the division introduced the GTO in 1964, widely regarded as one of the first true muscle cars. The Firebird pony car followed in 1967, and its Trans Am variant became an icon, aided by the film Smokey and the Bandit (1977).

Discontinuation

Following General Motors' 2009 bankruptcy and restructuring, the company announced that Pontiac would be phased out. The last Pontiac-badged cars, built in 2009, were sold through early 2010, ending an 84-year run for the marque.

Notable models

Notable Pontiac models include the Bonneville, Grand Prix, Grand Am, Sunfire, Fiero, Solstice, Aztek, Vibe, G6, G8, and the GTO.
