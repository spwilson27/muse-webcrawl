Spondylolysis is a defect or stress fracture in the pars interarticularis of the vertebral arch, most often of the fifth lumbar vertebra (L5), and less commonly L4. The pars interarticularis is the segment of bone between the superior and inferior articular processes of a vertebra. The condition occurs in roughly 3–6% of the general population and is a common cause of back pain in adolescent athletes. When the defect is bilateral, the vertebral body may slip forward relative to the one below, a progression called spondylolisthesis.

Causes and mechanism

Most cases result from repetitive stress — particularly repeated hyperextension and rotation of the lumbar spine — producing a fatigue fracture of the pars. Sports with high rates include gymnastics, diving, cricket fast bowling, American football (especially linemen), weightlifting, and wrestling. There is also a hereditary component: the condition is markedly more common in some populations, including Inuit groups, suggesting a congenital predisposition to a slender or dysplastic pars. It is rarely present at birth and typically develops during childhood or adolescence.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with spondylolysis have no symptoms and the defect is an incidental imaging finding. Symptomatic patients usually report aching low back pain aggravated by extension and relieved by rest; pain may radiate into the buttock or posterior thigh. Hamstring tightness is common. The classic physical-examination manoeuvre is the one-legged hyperextension ("stork") test, which may reproduce pain on the affected side.

Diagnosis

Oblique lumbar radiographs classically show a break in the "neck of the Scottie dog" formed by the posterior elements. CT provides the clearest view of the pars defect and fracture healing stage. SPECT bone scanning and MRI can detect early stress reactions before a complete fracture develops, which matters because early lesions have the best chance of healing with rest.

Treatment

Most patients improve with conservative care: temporary cessation of the provoking sport, physiotherapy emphasising core stabilisation and avoiding hyperextension, and sometimes a lumbosacral brace. Young patients with acute, unilateral, or early-stage defects may achieve bony union. Surgery — pars repair or spinal fusion — is reserved for persistent pain despite months of conservative treatment, neurological deficit, or progressive high-grade spondylolisthesis.
