The Treaty of Nystad (Russian: Ништадтский мир; Swedish: Freden i Nystad) was the peace treaty that ended the Great Northern War (1700–1721) between the Tsardom of Russia and the Swedish Empire. Signed on 10 September 1721 at Nystad (present-day Uusikaupunki in Finland), it marked the transfer of Baltic supremacy from Sweden to Russia and is conventionally taken to mark the birth of the Russian Empire.

Terms

Sweden ceded to Russia its Baltic provinces of Livonia, Estonia and Ingria, together with the part of Karelia around Vyborg in southeastern Finland. In return, Russia evacuated the rest of Finland (which its armies had occupied during the "Great Wrath") and paid Sweden two million riksdaler in compensation. Sweden retained limited rights to purchase grain duty-free in Riga, and the treaty guaranteed the privileges of the Baltic German nobility, the Lutheran church and the German language in the ceded provinces — arrangements that long shaped the Baltic provinces' special status within the empire.

Consequences

The treaty completed Peter the Great's drive for a Baltic coastline, where he had founded Saint Petersburg in 1703 in the conquered Ingrian marshes. Two months after the peace, in October 1721, the Russian Senate proclaimed Peter Emperor of All Russia, and Russia took Sweden's place as the great power of north-eastern Europe. Sweden's age as a great power (stormaktstiden) was over.
