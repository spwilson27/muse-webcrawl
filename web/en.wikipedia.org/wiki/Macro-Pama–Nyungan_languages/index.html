Macro-Pama–Nyungan is a proposed family of Australian Aboriginal languages, an extension of the widely accepted Pama–Nyungan family intended to group it with several smaller families of the Gulf of Carpentaria region and Cape York Peninsula. The proposal is associated principally with the linguist Nicholas Evans, who argued in work from the 1990s onward that Pama–Nyungan shares deep structural and lexical features with the Garawan and Tangkic languages of the southern Gulf country — and possibly others — that cannot be explained by borrowing alone.

Background

Pama–Nyungan is the largest family of Aboriginal languages, covering roughly seven-eighths of the Australian mainland with some 200–300 languages at contact, and its internal coherence is accepted by nearly all specialists. The remaining "non-Pama–Nyungan" families — about two dozen small families and isolates concentrated in the far north (Kimberley, Arnhem Land, and the Gulf country) — are far more diverse. Evans's Macro-Pama–Nyungan hypothesis treats some of these northern groups, notably Garawa and the Tangkic family (such as Kayardild and Lardil), as coordinate branches of a deeper family whose proto-language would have carried distinctive verbal morphology and pronominal patterns inherited into Pama–Nyungan.

Reception

The proposal has been cautiously received. Comparative work in Australian linguistics is hampered by the great time depth involved, extensive contact and borrowing, and the loss of many languages before adequate documentation. Some scholars (including in the tradition of R. M. W. Dixon's scepticism about long-range grouping) regard the evidence as suggestive but not conclusive, while others incorporate Macro-Pama–Nyungan as a useful working hypothesis in classifications such as those of Claire Bowern, whose phylogenetic work treats Pama–Nyungan as part of a broader set of relationships across the continent.
