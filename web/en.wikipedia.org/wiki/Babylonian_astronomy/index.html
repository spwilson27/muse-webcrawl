Babylonian Astronomy

Babylonian astronomy was the study and recording of celestial objects during the early history of Mesopotamia. Over more than a thousand years, Babylonian scribes compiled systematic observations of the Moon, planets, and stars on clay tablets, developing first omen-based astrology and later sophisticated mathematical methods for predicting lunar and planetary phenomena.

Origins and observations

The earliest star catalogues and omen series, such as MUL.APIN and Enuma Anu Enlil, date to the second and early first millennia BCE. From at least the reign of Nabonassar (747 BCE) — the epoch later used by Ptolemy — astronomers kept continuous records in the Babylonian astronomical diaries, noting lunar eclipses, planetary positions, solstices, weather, and even market prices. Babylonian astronomers recognized the Saros cycle of eclipse recurrence and timed lunar eclipses that later Greek astronomers relied upon.

Mathematical astronomy

In the last few centuries BCE, Babylonian scholars created arithmetical schemes (the so-called System A and System B) using step functions and zigzag functions in the sexagesimal (base-60) number system to predict the positions of the Moon and planets, the dates of new and full moons, and the lengths of daylight. They divided the ecliptic into the twelve signs of the zodiac of 30 degrees each — a scheme inherited by Greek astronomy and still in use — and their value for the length of the synodic month was exceptionally accurate.

Influence

Babylonian data and methods passed to Greek astronomers such as Hipparchus and Ptolemy, and through them into Islamic and European astronomy. The division of the circle into 360 degrees and of the hour into 60 minutes and the minute into 60 seconds are Babylonian legacies.
