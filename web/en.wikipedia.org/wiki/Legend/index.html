A legend is a genre of folklore that consists of a narrative featuring human actions perceived or believed both by teller and listeners to have taken place within human history. Narratives in this genre may demonstrate human values, and possess certain qualities that give the tale verisimilitude. Legend, for its active and passive participants, includes no happenings that are outside the realm of "possibility", but may include miracles. Legends may be transformed over time to keep them fresh and vital.

The word derives from the Medieval Latin legenda, "things to be read" — originally the stories of saints read aloud in religious services and refectories. Collections such as Jacobus de Voragine's Legenda Aurea (Golden Legend, c. 1260) were among the most popular books of the Middle Ages.

Legend and other genres

Folklorists distinguish the legend from the myth, which is set in a remote, sacred time and concerns gods and the creation of the world, and from the folktale or fairy tale, which is told as fiction and set in an indeterminate time and place ("once upon a time"). A legend, by contrast, is anchored to a named person, place and (roughly) historical period: King Arthur, Robin Hood, El Dorado, the Pied Piper of Hamelin, or the founding of a city. Many legends attach themselves to real historical figures — Alexander the Great, Charlemagne, Joan of Arc — but surround them with marvels accumulated in retelling. The brothers Grimm defined the legend as a folktale with a historical basis.

Types

  • Historical and heroic legends — sagas and epic traditions attached to kings, heroes and battles.
  • Saints' legends (hagiography) — lives and miracles of saints, collected in legendaries and martyrologies.
  • Local legends — stories tied to a particular landscape feature, ruin, treasure or haunting (ätiologische Sagen explain origins).
  • Urban legends — modern legends circulated as true ("a friend of a friend") by word of mouth and media, such as the vanishing hitchhiker or alligators in the sewers.
  • Living legends — by extension, the word is used of celebrated persons whose fame is treated as near-mythical.

Study

Legend scholarship examines how legends are formed, transmitted and believed; how they negotiate the boundary between history and fiction; and how communities use them to express identity and values. Modern legend research, associated with scholars such as Linda Dégh and Jan Harold Brunvand (who popularised the study of urban legends), treats belief, rumour and ostension — acting out a legend — as central topics.
