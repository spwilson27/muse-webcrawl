Dutch auction

A descending auction clock at a Dutch flower auction.

A Dutch auction is a descending-price auction in which the auctioneer begins with a high asking price that is progressively lowered until a participant accepts the current price, or until a reserve price is reached. The first bidder wins and pays the price at which they stopped the clock. The format takes its name from the flower auctions of the Netherlands, where perishable lots must be sold quickly.

Operation

At the great Dutch flower auctions, such as those of Royal FloraHolland at Aalsmeer, an auction clock displays a falling price; buyers press a button to claim a lot, and the transaction completes in seconds so that flowers can be shipped the same day. The format rewards quick judgement: waiting risks losing the lot to a rival, while bidding early guarantees the purchase at a higher price. Game-theoretically, the Dutch auction is strategically equivalent to a first-price sealed-bid auction.

Applications

Beyond flowers and other perishables, Dutch auctions are used to sell treasury securities in some countries and to price certain initial public offerings—most famously Google's 2004 IPO—where all successful bidders pay a single clearing price. The term is also applied in finance to "Dutch auction" share repurchases, in which a company invites tenders within a price range and buys back shares at the lowest price that secures the desired quantity.
