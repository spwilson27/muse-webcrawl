Trevally

Trevally is a common name given to a number of species of marine fish in the jack family, Carangidae, particularly those of the genus Caranx and related genera. Trevallies are fast-swimming predatory fish distributed throughout the tropical and subtropical waters of the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific Oceans. Many species are valued as food fish and as game fish.

Species

Well-known trevallies include the giant trevally (Caranx ignobilis), the bluefin trevally (Caranx melampygus), the bigeye trevally (Caranx sexfasciatus), the crevalle jack (Caranx hippos), and the silver trevally found in Australian waters. The giant trevally, known in Hawaiian as ulua, can exceed 1.7 metres in length and 80 kilograms in weight and is an apex predator of coral reef systems.

Biology and habitat

Trevallies generally have deep, compressed bodies, forked tails, and powerful swimming ability. Juveniles often inhabit estuaries and sheltered bays, while adults range over reefs, lagoons, and open ocean. They feed on smaller fish, crustaceans, and cephalopods, and some species form large schools. The giant trevally is known for hunting cooperatively and for individually recognizable behavior around reefs.

Fisheries

Trevallies support commercial and recreational fisheries across their range. They are taken by trolling, jigging, and netting, and are popular with sport anglers for their strength. In some regions, large trevallies have been associated with ciguatera poisoning, and stocks of some species are considered vulnerable to overfishing because of their slow growth and late maturity.
