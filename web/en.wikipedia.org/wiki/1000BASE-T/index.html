1000BASE-T

1000BASE-T (also known as IEEE 802.3ab) is a standard for Gigabit Ethernet over copper twisted-pair cabling. Standardised in 1999, it provides 1 Gbit/s transmission over unshielded twisted pair (Category 5 or better) for distances up to 100 metres, and became the most widely deployed form of Gigabit Ethernet in local area networks.

StandardIEEE 802.3ab (1999)
Speed1 Gbit/s full duplex
MediaCat 5/5e/6 UTP, 4 pairs
Max. distance100 m
Encoding4D-PAM5 with trellis coding

Technical details

Unlike earlier half-duplex Ethernet over two pairs, 1000BASE-T transmits and receives simultaneously on all four pairs of the cable. Each pair carries 250 Mbit/s in both directions at once, using hybrid circuits and echo cancellation to separate the two directions. Signals use five-level pulse-amplitude modulation (PAM-5) at 125 megabaud per pair, combined with trellis-coded modulation and scrambling, achieving the required spectral efficiency within the 100 MHz bandwidth of Category 5 cable.

Autonegotiation and deployment

1000BASE-T requires autonegotiation, which also resolves the master/slave clock timing relationship between link partners. Interfaces typically support 10BASE-T and 100BASE-TX as well ("10/100/1000"), easing migration. Because it runs over the installed base of Cat 5 cabling, 1000BASE-T rapidly displaced fibre and short-haul copper alternatives (such as 1000BASE-CX) for desktop and server connections, and it underpins later power-over-Ethernet deployments.
