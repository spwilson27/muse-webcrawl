Drug prohibition

Drug prohibition is the policy of restricting or banning the production, distribution and possession of certain psychoactive substances through criminal law. Prohibition is implemented internationally through United Nations treaties, principally the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs of 1961, and nationally through laws such as the United States Controlled Substances Act of 1970.

History

Early drug controls included Britain's Pharmacy Act 1868 and various opium laws aimed at Chinese immigrants in the United States. The first international agreement, the International Opium Convention of 1912, was followed by U.S. alcohol prohibition (1920–1933) under the Eighteenth Amendment. The modern global regime consolidated after the Second World War and intensified with the "war on drugs" declared by President Richard Nixon in 1971.

Arguments

Supporters argue prohibition reduces availability, use and associated harms, and upholds public order and morality. Critics argue it creates lucrative black markets and organized crime, drives mass incarceration with racial disparities, makes drug use more dangerous through unregulated supply, and fails to reduce use overall. Some jurisdictions have moved toward decriminalization (Portugal, 2001) or legal regulation (cannabis in Uruguay, Canada and several U.S. states).
