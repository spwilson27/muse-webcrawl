Jupiter (mythology)

Jupiter (Latin: Iūpiter), also known as Jove, is the king of the gods in Roman mythology and the god of the sky and thunder. He was the chief deity of the Roman state religion, counterpart of the Greek Zeus, and head of the Capitoline Triad with Juno and Minerva.

Origins and cult

Jupiter descends from the Proto-Indo-European sky father *Dyēus. In Rome his great temple on the Capitoline Hill, dedicated in 509 BC, was the religious heart of the Republic and Empire. As Jupiter Optimus Maximus ("Best and Greatest"), he embodied Roman sovereignty; generals celebrated triumphs at his temple and consuls took office under his auspices. His priest, the flamen Dialis, was hedged by elaborate taboos.

Myths

Roman myth largely adapted Greek stories of Zeus: Jupiter overthrew his father Saturn, divided the world with his brothers Neptune and Pluto, and fathered numerous gods and heroes, including Mars, Vulcan, Minerva, Hercules, and the Dioscuri. His symbols are the thunderbolt, the eagle, and the oak.

Legacy

Jupiter gives his name to the largest planet of the solar system and to Thursday in several Romance languages (French jeudi, Spanish jueves). His cult persisted until the Christianization of the Empire in the fourth century AD.
