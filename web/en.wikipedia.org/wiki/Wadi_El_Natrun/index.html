Wadi El Natrun

Wadi El Natrun (Arabic: وادي النطرون, "Valley of Natron"), known in antiquity as Scetis or the Scetic desert, is a valley and depression in northern Egypt, located about 100 kilometres northwest of Cairo and west of the Nile Delta. The valley contains a chain of alkaline lakes rich in natron salts, which were harvested in ancient times for use in mummification, glassmaking, and cleaning.

Christian monasticism

Wadi El Natrun is one of the most important centers of early Christian monasticism. In the 4th century AD, hermits including Saint Macarius the Great settled in the desert, and Scetis became, alongside Nitria and Kellia, one of the three great monastic centers of Egypt described in the Apophthegmata Patrum (Sayings of the Desert Fathers). Four fortified Coptic monasteries survive and remain active today:

  • Monastery of Saint Macarius the Great
  • Monastery of Saint Pishoy
  • Monastery of Saint Mary (Baramos)
  • Monastery of the Syrians (Deir el-Suryani)

The monasteries preserve ancient churches, manuscripts, and wall paintings, and experienced a significant revival of monastic life in the 20th century under figures such as Matta El Meskeen. The Monastery of Saint Pishoy is a residence of the Coptic Pope.

Natron lakes

The valley's lakes, fed by seepage from the Nile aquifer, fluctuate seasonally and precipitate natron — a naturally occurring mixture of sodium carbonate and sodium bicarbonate — used since pharaonic times. The area gave its name to the chemical symbol for sodium (Na, from natrium).
