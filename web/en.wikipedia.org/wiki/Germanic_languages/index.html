The Germanic languages are a branch of the Indo-European language family spoken natively by about 515 million people, mainly in Europe, North America, Oceania and southern Africa. The most widely spoken Germanic language is English, the world's most widely spoken language overall, followed by German and Dutch.

Branches

All Germanic languages descend from Proto-Germanic, spoken in Iron Age Scandinavia and northern Germany. They are conventionally divided into three branches:

  • North Germanic — Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, Icelandic and Faroese, descended from Old Norse;
  • West Germanic — English, German, Dutch, Afrikaans, Frisian, Low German and Yiddish;
  • East Germanic — Gothic, Vandalic and Burgundian, all extinct; Gothic is by far the best attested, largely through the fourth-century Bible translation of Ulfilas.

Characteristics

Proto-Germanic is identified by a characteristic set of sound changes, most notably Grimm's law, which shifted the Indo-European stop consonants, and Verner's law, which accounts for apparent exceptions. Other hallmarks include a fixed stress accent on the first syllable of a word, the distinction between strong and weak verbs, a dental suffix for the past tense of weak verbs, a two-tense verbal system, and the large-scale reduction of the Indo-European case and inflection system in the modern languages. The High German consonant shift later separated High German from the other West Germanic languages.

History

The earliest Germanic texts are runic inscriptions from the second century AD. During the Migration Period Germanic peoples spread across the former Roman Empire, and their languages gave rise to the medieval tongues Old English, Old Saxon, Old High German, Old Dutch and Old Norse. Through the British Empire and later globalisation, English spread worldwide, making the Germanic branch one of the most geographically extensive in the world.
