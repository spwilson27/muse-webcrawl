Heart rate variability

Heart rate variability (HRV) is the physiological phenomenon of variation in the time interval between consecutive heartbeats. It is measured by the variation in the beat-to-beat (RR) interval on an electrocardiogram. HRV reflects the activity of the autonomic nervous system and the balance between its sympathetic and parasympathetic branches: a healthy heart does not beat like a metronome, and greater variability generally indicates flexible, responsive autonomic control.

Measurement

HRV is analysed in the time domain, frequency domain and by non-linear methods. Common time-domain measures include SDNN, the standard deviation of all normal-to-normal intervals, and RMSSD, the root mean square of successive differences, which is particularly sensitive to parasympathetic (vagal) activity. Frequency-domain analysis divides the signal into bands — high frequency (HF, 0.15–0.4 Hz), low frequency (LF, 0.04–0.15 Hz) and very low frequency (VLF) — with HF power linked to respiratory sinus arrhythmia and vagal tone. A 1996 Task Force of the European Society of Cardiology and the North American Society of Pacing and Electrophysiology standardised measurement practice.

Clinical significance

Reduced HRV is associated with worse outcomes after myocardial infarction, with heart failure, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, and increased risk of sudden cardiac death. Depressed HRV also correlates with stress, poor sleep, overtraining in athletes, and systemic inflammation. Conversely, higher resting HRV is generally associated with cardiovascular fitness and effective emotional regulation.

Applications

HRV is used in cardiology risk stratification, fetal monitoring, anaesthesia, sports science (to guide training load and recovery) and biofeedback therapy, in which patients learn to increase HRV through slow breathing at their resonance frequency. Consumer wearables increasingly report HRV-derived "readiness" or "recovery" scores, though consumer optical (PPG) measurements are less precise than ECG and sensitive to artefact.

Limitations

HRV measures are strongly affected by age, sex, fitness, posture, time of day, caffeine, alcohol and breathing pattern, and there are no universal normal values. Artefacts and ectopic beats must be corrected before analysis, and comparisons are most meaningful within the same individual under standardised conditions.
