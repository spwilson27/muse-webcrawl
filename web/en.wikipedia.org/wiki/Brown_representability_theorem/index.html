Brown representability theorem

The Brown representability theorem in algebraic topology, proved by Edgar H. Brown Jr. in 1962, gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a contravariant functor on the homotopy category of pointed connected CW complexes to be representable — that is, to arise as the functor of homotopy classes of maps into a fixed space.

Statement

Let F be a contravariant functor from the homotopy category of pointed connected CW complexes to sets. Then F is representable — there is a CW complex X and a natural isomorphism

F(Y) ≅ [Y, X] for all Y

— if and only if F satisfies the following two axioms:

  • Wedge axiom: F sends coproducts (wedge sums) to products of sets.
  • Mayer–Vietoris axiom: for a space glued from two subspaces, if two elements of F agree on the intersection, they glue to an element over the whole space.

Applications

The theorem guarantees the existence of classifying spaces and representing spaces for cohomology theories: for example, the Eilenberg–MacLane spaces K(G, n) representing ordinary cohomology, and the spectra of generalised cohomology theories. A dual version for covariant functors exists under similar hypotheses. Brown's theorem was later extended, notably by work of Alex Heller and by Józef Przytycki and others, to broader settings, and it underlies the representability results used throughout stable homotopy theory. A counterexample due to later authors shows the connectedness and point-set hypotheses cannot simply be dropped.
