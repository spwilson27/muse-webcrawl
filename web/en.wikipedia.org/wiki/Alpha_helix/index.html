Alpha helix

The alpha helix (α-helix) is a common motif in the secondary structure of proteins: a right-handed helix in which the amino-acid backbone is wound so that each carbonyl group (C=O) forms a hydrogen bond with the amino group (N–H) four residues further along the chain. It is one of the two principal regular secondary structures, alongside the beta sheet.

Geometry

An α-helix has 3.6 residues per turn, a pitch of 5.4 Å, and characteristic backbone dihedral angles (φ ≈ −60°, ψ ≈ −45°). Side chains project outward from the helix axis. Proline, lacking a backbone N–H donor and having a rigid ring, acts as a helix breaker, while amino acids such as alanine favour helix formation.

Discovery

The structure was proposed by Linus Pauling, Robert Corey and Herman Branson in 1951, deduced from bond geometry in advance of direct crystallographic confirmation — a triumph of model building — and was soon found in keratin and myoglobin. Roughly a third of residues in a typical globular protein lie in α-helices; the motif also spans membranes as transmembrane helices and combines into bundles and coiled coils in structural proteins.
