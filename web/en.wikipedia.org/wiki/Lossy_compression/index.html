Lossy compression

In information technology, lossy compression is a class of data compression methods that uses inexact approximations and partial data discarding to represent content, achieving far greater reductions in file size than lossless compression at the cost of permanently discarding some information. It is used above all for multimedia — images, audio, and video — where small perceptible losses are an acceptable trade for much smaller files.

Principles

Lossy codecs exploit the limits of human perception (psychoacoustics and psychovisual models): detail that is hard to see or hear is encoded crudely or omitted. Typical pipelines transform the signal into a frequency domain (for example with the discrete cosine transform in JPEG), quantise the coefficients — the genuinely lossy step — and then apply lossless entropy coding to the result. The decoder reconstructs an approximation, not the original.

Trade-offs and artefacts

Quality is governed by bitrate or a quantisation/quality setting. Too-aggressive compression produces characteristic artefacts: blockiness and ringing in images, warbling and pre-echo in audio, and smearing in video. Repeated re-encoding compounds the losses ("generation loss"), so archival masters are kept lossless. Lossy compression is inappropriate where data must be exact — text, executables, and scientific or medical records — which use lossless methods instead.
