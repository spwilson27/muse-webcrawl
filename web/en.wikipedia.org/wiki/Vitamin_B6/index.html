Vitamin B6

Vitamin B6 is one of the B vitamins, and thus an essential nutrient. The term refers to a group of six chemically similar compounds, or vitamers: pyridoxine, pyridoxal, pyridoxamine, and their respective 5′-phosphate esters. The metabolically active form, pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP), serves as a coenzyme in more than 140 enzyme reactions, most of them in amino-acid metabolism, and also in neurotransmitter synthesis, haem production, and the metabolism of homocysteine and glucose.

Sources and requirements

Vitamin B6 is widely distributed in foods: poultry, fish, meat, potatoes and other starchy vegetables, chickpeas, and fruit such as bananas are good sources. The U.S. Recommended Dietary Allowance for adults is about 1.3–1.7 mg/day depending on age and sex. Because the vitamin is plentiful in food, isolated dietary deficiency is uncommon.

Deficiency

Deficiency causes a scaly dermatitis and cheilitis, glossitis, sideroblastic anaemia, confusion and depression, and, in infants, convulsions; it is most often seen with alcoholism, malabsorption, or treatment with antagonistic drugs such as isoniazid, which is therefore co-prescribed with pyridoxine to prevent peripheral neuropathy.

Uses and toxicity

Pyridoxine is part of first-line treatment (with doxylamine) for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, and is used to prevent or treat drug-induced deficiency. Unlike most water-soluble vitamins, vitamin B6 can be toxic in chronic high doses: prolonged intake of large supplement doses causes a sensory peripheral neuropathy with numbness and difficulty walking, which is the basis for upper intake limits (100 mg/day in the United States; the European Food Safety Authority set a lower limit of 12 mg/day in 2023).
