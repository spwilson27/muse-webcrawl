Roman baths

Roman baths were public bathing complexes (thermae for the large imperial establishments, balneae for smaller ones) central to daily life in ancient Rome and across the Roman Empire, combining bathing, exercise, socialising and business.

A typical sequence moved through the apodyterium (changing room), tepidarium (warm room), caldarium (hot room) and frigidarium (cold room), often with a palaestra for exercise and an open-air pool (natatio). Rooms were heated by the hypocaust system, in which hot air from a furnace circulated beneath raised floors and through hollow walls.

Famous examples

The Baths of Caracalla (c. 216 AD) and Baths of Diocletian (306 AD) in Rome covered many hectares. Well-preserved provincial examples include the Roman Baths in Bath, England, built around natural hot springs. Entrance fees were nominal, and bathing was a daily social ritual for Romans of all classes.
