Dike (geology)

In geology, a dike or dyke is a sheet of rock that is formed in a fracture of a pre-existing rock body. Dikes can be magmatic, composed of igneous rock that solidified from magma that intruded the fracture, or sedimentary, formed when sediment fills a pre-existing crack. Magmatic dikes are discordant intrusions: they cut across the bedding planes or foliation of the host rock, unlike sills, which intrude parallel to bedding.

Formation

When magma rises through the crust, the pressure of the magma can open and wedge apart cracks in the surrounding rock, or magma may fill existing fractures. The magma then cools and crystallises in place, forming a tabular body whose thickness is usually much smaller than its length and height. Because the host rock is typically cooler than the magma, the margins of a dike are often fine-grained or glassy — a chilled margin — while the interior crystallises more coarsely.

Dike swarms

Dikes frequently occur in groups called dike swarms, which may contain hundreds of individual dikes emplaced during a single magmatic episode. Radial swarms fan out from a central volcanic centre, while vast linear swarms can extend for more than a thousand kilometres across a continent. The Mackenzie dike swarm in Canada, associated with a mantle plume about 1.27 billion years ago, is one of the largest known. Dike swarms are important evidence for ancient episodes of crustal extension and continental breakup.

Ring dikes and cone sheets

Above shallow magma chambers, ring-shaped fractures may form as the chamber roof subsides; magma filling these fractures produces ring dikes. Inward-dipping conical fractures filled with magma produce cone sheets. Both forms are associated with caldera-forming volcanism.

Clastic dikes

Sedimentary, or clastic, dikes form when sediment is injected or settles into cracks in rock, sometimes during earthquakes when liquefied sand is forced upward into fissures. They are useful indicators of ancient seismic activity.
