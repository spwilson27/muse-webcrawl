Community garden

Community gardens divide shared urban land into plots tended by local residents.

A community garden is a single piece of land gardened collectively by a group of people, usually in an urban or suburban neighbourhood. Community gardens are typically divided into individual or family plots, sometimes with common areas, and are used mainly to grow vegetables, fruit, herbs, and flowers. They are related to, but distinct from, British allotments, which are individual plots rented from a local authority.

History

Precursors include the "potato patches" of the 1890s depression in the United States, school gardens, and the victory gardens of the two world wars, when governments promoted household and community food production. The modern community gardening movement emerged in the 1970s, when residents of cities such as New York reclaimed vacant lots for gardening; organisations such as the Green Guerrillas and the American Community Gardening Association (founded 1979) supported the spread of gardens across North America. Similar movements exist worldwide, from German Kleingärten to community gardens in Australia and the developing world.

Benefits and challenges

Research and practitioner reports associate community gardens with improved access to fresh food, physical activity, social cohesion, neighbourhood beautification, reduced crime around garden sites, and environmental benefits such as stormwater absorption and urban cooling. Gardens on borrowed or publicly owned land often face insecurity of tenure, as rising land values create pressure for development; many cities have responded with land trusts, zoning protections, and long-term leases. Other challenges include soil contamination, water access, and volunteer burnout.
