Awadh

Awadh (Hindi: अवध), known in British historical texts as Oudh, is a historical and cultural region in the modern Indian state of Uttar Pradesh, centred on the cities of Lucknow, Ayodhya (historically Faizabad) and Kanpur. Under its Nawabs in the 18th and 19th centuries it was one of the richest and most refined courts of India, famed for its composite Hindu–Muslim ("Ganga-Jamuni") culture.

History

The region takes its name from Ayodhya, the legendary capital of Kosala. In 1722 the Mughal emperor appointed Saadat Ali Khan as governor (Nawab) of Awadh; his dynasty became effectively independent as Mughal power waned. After the Battle of Buxar (1764) Awadh became a buffer state allied to, and increasingly subordinated to, the British East India Company. The capital moved from Faizabad to Lucknow under Asaf-ud-Daula in 1775.

In 1819 the Nawab declared himself King (Padshah) of Oudh. The last king, Wajid Ali Shah, was deposed when the British annexed Awadh in 1856 on grounds of alleged misgovernance—a grievance that helped ignite the Indian Rebellion of 1857, in which Awadh was a principal theatre. Afterwards the region was administered within the North-Western Provinces and eventually Uttar Pradesh.

Culture

Awadh is celebrated for Urdu poetry, Kathak dance, kathak's Lucknow gharana, and Awadhi cuisine, including biryani, kebabs and the slow-cooked dum pukht style. Its language, Awadhi, is an Eastern Hindi language with a rich literary tradition.
