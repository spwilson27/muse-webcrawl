AVL tree

TypeTree (self-balancing binary search tree)
Invented1962
Invented byGeorgy Adelson-Velsky, Evgenii Landis
Search / insert / deleteO(log n)

In computer science, an AVL tree (named after inventors Adelson-Velsky and Landis) is a self-balancing binary search tree, and the first such data structure to be invented. In an AVL tree, the heights of the two child subtrees of any node differ by at most one; if they differ by more than one, rebalancing is performed to restore the property. Lookup, insertion, and deletion all take O(log n) time in both the average and worst cases.

Balance factor

Each node stores a balance factor equal to the height of its right subtree minus the height of its left subtree. A node with balance factor 1, 0, or −1 is considered balanced. Insertions and deletions that push a node's factor to 2 or −2 are repaired with single or double rotations — the so-called left-left, left-right, right-right, and right-left cases.

Comparison

AVL trees are more strictly balanced than red–black trees, so they provide faster lookups at the cost of somewhat slower insertion and deletion due to more frequent rotations. The height of an AVL tree with n nodes is bounded by about 1.44 log2(n+2), guaranteeing that the tree never degenerates into a linked list. AVL trees are used where search dominates, and they also appear in teaching as the canonical example of rotation-based balancing.

History

Adelson-Velsky and Landis published the structure in a 1962 Soviet paper, "An algorithm for the organization of information." It predates the red–black tree by a decade and established the paradigm of maintaining a height-balance invariant under updates.
