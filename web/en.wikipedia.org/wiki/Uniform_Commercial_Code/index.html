Uniform Commercial Code


The Uniform Commercial Code, a model act governing commercial transactions in the United States.

The Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), first published in 1952, is one of several uniform acts that have been enacted as law with the goal of harmonizing the laws of sales and other commercial transactions across the United States through adoption by all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and the U.S. territories. The UCC is a model law, not itself law until adopted by a state legislature.

The code was developed jointly by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws (now known as the Uniform Law Commission) and the American Law Institute. Its chief reporter was Karl Llewellyn, a legal realist scholar at Columbia Law School, who sought to bring American commercial law closer to actual mercantile practice.

History

Before the UCC, commercial law in the United States rested on a patchwork of state statutes and common law, including the Uniform Sales Act of 1906. Work on the code began in the 1940s; the first official text appeared in 1952. Pennsylvania was the first state to adopt it, in 1953, and over the following two decades nearly every state followed. Louisiana adopted much of the code but, because of its civil law tradition, did not adopt Article 2 (sales) in full.

Organization

The code is divided into articles, each dealing with a distinct subject:

  • Article 1 — General Provisions
  • Article 2 — Sales of goods
  • Article 2A — Leases of goods
  • Article 3 — Negotiable instruments
  • Article 4 — Bank deposits and collections
  • Article 4A — Funds transfers
  • Article 5 — Letters of credit
  • Article 6 — Bulk sales (revised or repealed in most states)
  • Article 7 — Documents of title
  • Article 8 — Investment securities
  • Article 9 — Secured transactions

Article 2 and the sale of goods

Article 2 governs contracts for the sale of goods, defined as movable things, where the price is $500 or more. It is known for departing from strict common-law doctrines: it relaxes the mirror-image rule for contract formation, permits open price terms, restricts the Statute of Frauds through merchant exceptions, and implies warranties of merchantability and fitness for a particular purpose. Article 2 applies only to goods, not to services or real estate; mixed contracts are analyzed under the predominant-purpose test.

Article 9 and secured transactions

Article 9 governs security interests in personal property. It created a unified framework for attachment, perfection (typically by filing a UCC-1 financing statement in a central state registry), and priority among competing creditors. The 1998 revisions to Article 9, effective in 2001, expanded its scope to include deposit accounts and other new forms of collateral.

Revisions and amendments

The sponsoring organizations periodically revise the code. Significant projects include the 1998 revision of Article 9, amendments to Articles 3 and 4, and a long-contested effort to revise Article 2 that was abandoned in 2011 after states declined to enact it. In 2022 the Uniform Law Commission and the ALI approved amendments addressing digital assets, including a new Article 12 on controllable electronic records.
