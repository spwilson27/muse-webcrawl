Microbiome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The microbiome is the collection of microorganisms — bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses and other microbes — and their genes living in a particular environment, such as the human body, a plant's roots, or the ocean. The term is often used interchangeably with "microbiota" (the organisms themselves).

The human microbiome

The human body harbours roughly as many microbial cells as human cells, concentrated in the gut. The gut microbiome digests otherwise indigestible carbohydrates, produces vitamins and short-chain fatty acids, trains the immune system, and competes against pathogens. Its composition varies with diet, birth mode, antibiotics and geography, and it begins to be established at birth.

Health and research

Disrupted microbiomes (dysbiosis) are associated — though causation is often unproven — with obesity, inflammatory bowel disease, allergies and even mood disorders, the so-called gut–brain axis. Faecal microbiota transplantation is an established, highly effective treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection. Interest in probiotics, prebiotics and microbiome-based drugs is intense, as is caution: the field is young, and most commercial claims outrun the evidence.
