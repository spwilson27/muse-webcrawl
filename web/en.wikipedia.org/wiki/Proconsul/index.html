Proconsul

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A proconsul (pro consule, "in place of the consul") was an official of ancient Rome who acted in the capacity of a consul, usually as governor of a province or commander of an army, without holding the consulship in Rome itself. The office arose when a consul's command (imperium) was extended — prorogued — beyond his year in office, or when the growing number of provinces exceeded the number of serving magistrates.

Republic

During the Roman Republic, proconsuls governed the major provinces, commanded legions, administered justice and collected revenues, attended by lictors and exercising near-monarchical power within their sphere — which created famous opportunities for both conquest and corruption, as in Cicero's prosecution of Verres, the former proconsul of Sicily. Julius Caesar's decade as proconsul of Gaul gave him the army and wealth with which he crossed the Rubicon.

Empire

Under Augustus's settlement of 27 BC, provinces were divided between the emperor and the Senate. Senatorial provinces were governed by proconsuls chosen by lot from ex-consuls (Asia and Africa) or ex-praetors, serving one year, while imperial provinces were run by the emperor's legates. The title survived into late antiquity, notably the proconsul of Africa.

Later usage

By extension, "proconsul" is used informally for powerful overseas administrators — British viceroys, colonial governors and American theatre commanders have all been described as proconsuls.
