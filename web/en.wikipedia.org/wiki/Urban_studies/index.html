Urban studies is the interdisciplinary study of cities and urban life. It examines the development, structure and functioning of urban areas — their economies, societies, politics, built environments and cultures — and the processes of urbanisation that have made cities home to most of humanity. Urban studies emerged as a distinct academic field in the mid-20th century, drawing together geography, sociology, economics, political science, anthropology, history, public health and urban planning.

Scope and themes

Major themes include the causes and forms of urban growth; neighbourhood change, segregation and gentrification; housing and homelessness; urban governance and finance; transport and infrastructure systems; sustainability and resilience to climate change; and the cultural and experiential life of cities. Contemporary scholarship gives increasing attention to cities of the Global South, informal urbanisation, and comparative work beyond the Euro-American cases on which early theory was built.

Intellectual history

Foundations were laid by the Chicago School of sociology in the 1920s (Park, Burgess, Wirth), by urban historians and geographers, and by reformers such as Jane Jacobs, whose The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961) reshaped thinking about urban vitality. Later strands include political-economy approaches associated with Henri Lefebvre, Manuel Castells and David Harvey; "Los Angeles School" postmodern urbanism; and current work on planetary urbanisation, smart cities and urban data science.

Education and practice

Urban studies is taught as undergraduate and graduate degrees distinct from professional urban planning programmes, though the fields overlap heavily. Graduates work in government, nonprofits, consulting, housing, transport and community development as well as academia.
