Second Party System

The Second Party System is a name used by political scientists and historians for the period of intense party competition in the United States from about 1828 to 1854. It featured rapidly rising levels of voter interest and turnout, at the direction of leaders such as Martin Van Buren, and a two-party contest between the Democratic Party, led first by Andrew Jackson, and the Whig Party, assembled by Henry Clay from the National Republicans and other opponents of Jackson.

Characteristics

The system emerged from the split of the Democratic-Republican Party after the election of 1824 and the rise of Jacksonian democracy. It was characterised by mass parties organised in every state and locality, partisan newspapers, torchlight parades and rallies, and voter turnout that regularly exceeded 80 per cent of eligible voters — figures never matched since. Both parties sought a national majority and therefore tended to downplay the polarising issue of slavery.

Issues

The main lines of division were economic: Democrats opposed the national bank, protective tariffs and federally funded internal improvements, preaching limited government, while Whigs favoured a modernising programme of banks, tariffs and infrastructure — Clay's "American System". Cultural and religious identities, including attitudes to immigration and temperance, also shaped party loyalty.

Collapse

The system collapsed in the mid-1850s under the strain of the slavery question. The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 destroyed the Whig Party, whose northern and southern wings could no longer coexist, and gave rise to the new Republican Party, inaugurating the Third Party System.
