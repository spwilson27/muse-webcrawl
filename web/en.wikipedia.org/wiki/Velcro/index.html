Velcro

Velcro is a brand of fasteners — officially "hook-and-loop fasteners" — consisting of two components: typically two linear fabric strips (or, alternatively, round "dots" or "squares") which are attached (sewn or otherwise adhered) to the opposing surfaces to be fastened. The first component features tiny hooks, the second features smaller loops. When the two are pressed together the hooks catch in the loops and the two hold fast; pulling them apart produces a distinctive ripping sound. The name is a portmanteau of the French words velours ("velvet") and crochet ("hook").

Invention

The fastener was invented in 1941 by Swiss electrical engineer George de Mestral. Returning from a hunting trip in the Alps, he noticed burdock burrs clinging to his clothes and his dog's fur; under a microscope he saw that each burr was covered in tiny hooks that caught in loops of fabric and fur. After years of experimentation he reproduced the effect with nylon — one side woven with hooks cut from loops, the other with dense loops — and patented the invention in 1955, commercialising it through his company, Velcro SA.

Uses

NASA's use of the fastener in the Apollo programme — to secure equipment in weightlessness and help astronauts into and out of bulky spacesuits — brought it wide publicity in the 1960s. It is now ubiquitous in clothing and shoes (especially children's), luggage straps, blood-pressure cuffs, cable ties, medical supports, and industrial applications where a strong but readily separable fastening is needed.

Trademark

"Velcro" is a registered trademark of the Velcro Companies; it has become partially genericised in everyday speech, and the company actively campaigns for the generic term "hook-and-loop" to be used for non-brand products.
