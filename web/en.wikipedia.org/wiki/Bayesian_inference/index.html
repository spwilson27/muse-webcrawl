Bayesian inference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A diagram showing a prior distribution updated by data into a posterior distribution]

Bayesian inference is a method of statistical inference in which Bayes' theorem is used to update the probability for a hypothesis as more evidence becomes available. Probability is interpreted as a degree of belief, and prior knowledge is combined with the likelihood of the observed data to give a posterior distribution.

The approach is named after Thomas Bayes, whose theorem was published posthumously in 1763, and was developed by Laplace. Long overshadowed by frequentist statistics in the 20th century, Bayesian methods expanded rapidly from the 1990s as Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms and cheap computing made them practical.

The framework

Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The prior distribution expresses belief before seeing data; the likelihood encodes what the data say; the posterior is the coherent update. Credible intervals from the posterior have the direct 'probability the parameter lies here' interpretation that frequentist confidence intervals lack.

Bayesian model comparison uses Bayes factors and marginal likelihoods, automatically penalising unnecessary complexity (a form of Occam's razor).

Applications

Bayesian methods are used in spam filtering, clinical trials, phylogenetics, machine learning (Bayesian networks, Gaussian processes), A/B testing, and election forecasting. Software such as Stan, BUGS and PyMC implements general Bayesian computation.
