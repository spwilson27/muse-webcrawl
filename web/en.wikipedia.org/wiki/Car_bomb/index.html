Car bomb

Car bomb
TypeImprovised explosive device (vehicle-borne)
First notable use1920 (Wall Street bombing, horse-drawn wagon)
AcronymVBIED

A car bomb, or vehicle-borne improvised explosive device (VBIED), is an improvised bomb concealed in a car or other vehicle and detonated remotely, by timer, or by a suicide driver. The vehicle allows a large explosive payload to be transported and positioned close to a target, and the vehicle's own structure and fuel add to the blast and fragmentation.

History

Early antecedents include the 1920 Wall Street bombing, in which a horse-drawn wagon loaded with dynamite and iron sash weights killed 38 people. Car bombs became a recurring weapon of assassination and insurgency in the mid-20th century — used by the Viet Cong in Saigon, by paramilitary groups in Northern Ireland, by organised crime, and extensively in the Lebanese Civil War, including the devastating 1983 attacks on the US embassy and Marine barracks in Beirut.

Use in modern conflicts

During the Iraq War and Syrian Civil War, car bombs — including armoured suicide vehicles driven by Islamic State fighters — became a signature weapon. Truck bombs have also been used in attacks on government buildings, such as the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing (a rental truck carrying a fertiliser-based explosive), which remains the deadliest domestic terrorist attack in United States history.

Countermeasures

Defences include blast-resistant building design, stand-off distances enforced by barriers and checkpoints, vehicle screening, and intelligence-led disruption of bomb-making networks.
