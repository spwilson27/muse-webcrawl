The Kingdom of Gwynedd (Welsh: Teyrnas Gwynedd) was a post-Roman and medieval kingdom in north-west Wales, covering the mountains of Snowdonia (Eryri), the island of Anglesey (Ynys Môn) and the Llŷn Peninsula. Emerging in the fifth century after the Roman withdrawal from Britain, it became the most powerful Welsh kingdom, the seat of the princes who came closest to uniting Wales, and survived until the Edwardian conquest of 1282–83.

Early kings

Welsh tradition traced the dynasty to Cunedda, said to have come from Manaw Gododdin in southern Scotland to expel Irish settlers. His descendant Maelgwn Gwynedd (d. c. 547), censured by the monk Gildas as a "dragon of the island", was remembered as a dominant overlord. Rhodri Mawr (r. 844–878) united Gwynedd with Powys and Seisyllwg and resisted the Vikings, and his grandson Hywel Dda codified Welsh law — though Gwynedd's kings often resented his line's rule.

High Middle Ages

Gruffudd ap Llywelyn (r. 1039–1063) briefly united all Wales under his rule before being defeated and killed with Harold Godwinson's connivance. Gruffudd ap Cynan (r. 1081–1137) rebuilt the kingdom against Norman incursions, and his son Owain Gwynedd (r. 1137–1170) extended Gwynedd's power and styled himself prince. Owain's great-grandson Llywelyn the Great (r. 1195–1240) dominated Wales, married King John's daughter Joan, and was recognised in the Treaty of Worcester (1218). His grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was recognised as Prince of Wales by Henry III in the Treaty of Montgomery (1267).

Conquest

Edward I's campaigns of 1277 and 1282 stripped away Llywelyn's gains; Llywelyn was killed near Builth Wells in December 1282 and his brother Dafydd captured and executed in 1283. The Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) annexed Gwynedd's lands to the English crown as the Principality of North Wales, divided into the counties of Anglesey, Caernarvonshire and Merionethshire, and ringed by Edward's great castles at Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech and Beaumaris.
