Rotation number

In mathematics, particularly in dynamical systems, the rotation number is an invariant of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of the circle, introduced by Henri Poincaré. It measures the average number of turns per iteration made by points under repeated application of the map, and it classifies circle homeomorphisms up to a large extent: rational rotation numbers correspond to periodic behaviour, while irrational rotation numbers are associated with quasiperiodic dynamics.

Definition

Let f : S1 → S1 be an orientation-preserving homeomorphism of the circle. Choose a lift F : ℝ → ℝ of f to the real line, satisfying F(x + 1) = F(x) + 1. Poincaré showed that the limit

rot(f) = limn→∞ (Fn(x) − x) / n (mod 1)

exists and is independent of both the starting point x and the choice of lift (up to the integer ambiguity removed by working modulo 1). For a rigid rotation of the circle through an angle 2πα, the rotation number is simply α mod 1.

Properties

The rotation number is invariant under topological conjugacy by orientation-preserving homeomorphisms, and it depends continuously on the map. If rot(f) = p/q is rational (in lowest terms), then f has a periodic point, and every periodic point has period q. If rot(f) is irrational, then f has no periodic points; by a theorem of Denjoy, if f is sufficiently smooth (for example a C² diffeomorphism with derivative of bounded variation), then f is topologically conjugate to the rigid irrational rotation, and every orbit is dense in the circle. Denjoy also constructed C¹ counterexamples — Denjoy homeomorphisms — with irrational rotation number but wandering intervals, showing the smoothness hypothesis is essential.

Examples and applications

Rotation numbers arise in the study of circle maps, including the Arnold family, where rational values produce mode locking and "Arnold tongues" in parameter space. They also appear in the theory of flows on the torus (via Poincaré sections), in quasiperiodic motion and KAM theory, and in applications ranging from coupled oscillators and cardiac rhythms to the dynamics of Josephson junctions.
