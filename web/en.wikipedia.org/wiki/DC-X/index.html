McDonnell Douglas DC-X

The McDonnell Douglas DC-X, short for Delta Clipper Experimental, was an uncrewed prototype of a reusable single-stage-to-orbit launch vehicle built by McDonnell Douglas in conjunction with the United States Department of Defense's Strategic Defense Initiative Organization (SDIO) from 1991 to 1993. Starting in 1994, the project was transferred to NASA, which upgraded the vehicle as the DC-XA, the Delta Clipper Experimental Advanced.

Background

The DC-X was conceived to demonstrate vertical takeoff and vertical landing (VTVL), rapid turnaround, and low-cost operations for a future single-stage-to-orbit (SSTO) vehicle. It was one-third scale, about 12 metres tall, conical in shape, and powered by four Pratt & Whitney RL-10A-5 rocket engines burning liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen. Unlike conventional rockets, it was designed to be operated by a small crew with aircraft-like ground handling.

Flight testing

The DC-X first flew on 18 August 1993 at White Sands Missile Range, New Mexico. Over a series of test flights it demonstrated hovering, translation, nose-first ("flip") return manoeuvres, and precision rocket-powered landings — techniques later central to reusable boosters such as SpaceX's Falcon 9. In June 1995 it reached an altitude of 3,140 metres. As the DC-XA, it set a turnaround record of 26 hours between flights in 1996.

Cancellation

On its final flight, 31 July 1996, one of the DC-XA's landing legs failed to deploy; the vehicle tipped over and its hydrogen tank ruptured and burned. NASA, whose priorities had shifted to the X-33 programme, chose not to fund repairs, and the programme ended. The DC-X nonetheless remains a landmark in the history of reusable launch vehicles.
