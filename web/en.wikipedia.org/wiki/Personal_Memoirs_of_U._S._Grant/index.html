The Personal Memoirs of U. S. Grant is an autobiography by Ulysses S. Grant, the 18th president of the United States, covering his early life and military service through the end of the American Civil War. Written in the last year of Grant's life while he was dying of throat cancer, the two-volume work was published by Mark Twain's firm, Charles L. Webster & Co., and became a commercial and critical triumph, widely regarded as one of the finest military memoirs in English.

Background

Grant, ruined financially by the Grant & Ward bank fraud of 1884 and diagnosed with terminal cancer, accepted Twain's unusually generous publishing offer — a 70 percent royalty — and wrote to provide for his family. He completed the manuscript days before his death on 23 July 1885, writing in longhand and dictating as his strength failed.

Content

The memoirs describe Grant's boyhood in Ohio, his education at West Point, and his service in the Mexican–American War, which he judged an unjust war of conquest. The bulk of the work covers the Civil War: Fort Donelson, Shiloh, the Vicksburg campaign, Chattanooga, the Overland Campaign, Petersburg, and Appomattox. Grant pointedly omits his presidency and political career. His judgments of fellow generals are candid; his account of the war's cause is unambiguous — slavery.

Reception

Sold by subscription, the memoirs sold over 300,000 sets and earned Grant's widow, Julia, roughly $450,000 in royalties — one of the largest publishing successes of the century. Critics then and since, from contemporary reviewers to modern historians and writers such as Gore Vidal and Edmund Wilson, have praised the clarity, economy, and directness of Grant's prose, often comparing him to Caesar for his commentaries on his own campaigns.
