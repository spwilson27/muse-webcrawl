The Tzistarakis Mosque (Greek: Τζαμί Τζισταράκη, from Turkish: Cizderiye Camii) is an Ottoman mosque, built in 1759, in Monastiraki Square, central Athens, Greece. It now functions as an annex of the Museum of Greek Folk Art.

History

The mosque was built in 1759 by the Ottoman governor (voivode) of Athens, Mustapha Agha Tzistarakis. According to tradition, Tzistarakis used one of the pillars of the Temple of Olympian Zeus to make lime for the building, an act said to have brought a plague upon the city — and which led to his dismissal the following year, since destroying ancient monuments was considered a sacrilege likely to provoke vengeful spirits.

The mosque was also known as the "Mosque of the Lower Fountain" (Τζαμί του Κάτω Σιντριβανιού) because of its proximity to a nearby fountain. During the Greek Revolution of 1821, the building was used as an assembly hall for the local town elders. After independence, it served various purposes: in 1834 it was used as a ballroom in honour of King Otto, and it later served as a barracks, a prison, and a storehouse.

In 1915 it was partly rebuilt under the supervision of the architect Anastasios Orlandos, and from 1918 it housed the Museum of Greek Handwork (later the Museum of Greek Folk Art) until 1973. In 1966 it was temporarily refitted for worship to provide a prayer place for the deposed King Saud of Saudi Arabia during his stay in Athens. Since 1981 the building has housed the museum's collection of ceramics, including the notable collection donated by Vassilios Kyriazopoulos.

Architecture

The square prayer hall is surmounted by a large central dome resting on pendentives, with a portico on its western side. A tall, slender minaret once stood at the southwestern corner but was demolished in the nineteenth century. The interior preserves fragments of painted and carved decoration characteristic of Ottoman religious architecture of the period.
