Tor Harold Percival Bergeron (15 August 1891 – 13 June 1977) was a Swedish meteorologist who proposed a mechanism for the formation of precipitation in clouds, known as the Bergeron process or Bergeron–Findeisen process. He was a central figure of the Bergen School of Meteorology, which revolutionized weather forecasting in the 1920s.

Life and career

Bergeron was born in England to Swedish parents and studied at Stockholm University, where he came under the influence of Vilhelm Bjerknes. In 1919 he joined Bjerknes's group in Bergen, Norway, alongside Jacob Bjerknes, Carl-Gustaf Rossby and Halvor Solberg. The Bergen school developed the polar front theory of midlatitude cyclones and modern methods of weather analysis based on air masses and fronts.

Bergeron made key contributions to the analysis of weather maps, including the three-dimensional description of frontal structures and a classification of air masses. His 1928 work on three-dimensional analysis and his 1933 paper on the physics of cloud and precipitation introduced the idea that in mixed-phase clouds, ice crystals grow at the expense of supercooled water droplets because the saturation vapour pressure over ice is lower than over liquid water — the physical core of the Bergeron process, later elaborated with Walter Findeisen's laboratory work.

After returning to Sweden, Bergeron taught at Stockholm and Uppsala universities and worked on cloud seeding experiments (Project Cumulus) and on the climatology of precipitation. He received numerous honours, including the Symons Gold Medal of the Royal Meteorological Society (1949) and the International Meteorological Organization Prize.

Legacy

The Bergeron process remains a standard explanation in textbooks for how precipitation forms in cold clouds outside the tropics. Bergeron is remembered as one of the founders of modern synoptic meteorology.
