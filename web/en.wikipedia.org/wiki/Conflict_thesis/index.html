Conflict thesis

The conflict thesis holds that science and religion have been in perpetual conflict.

The conflict thesis is a historiographical approach in the history of science which holds that there is an intrinsic intellectual conflict between religion and science, and that their relationship has inevitably led to hostility. The thesis interprets episodes such as the Galileo affair as instances of a perennial "warfare" between science and theology.

The thesis was popularised in the 19th century by John William Draper's History of the Conflict Between Religion and Science (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom (1896). Draper and White argued that as science advanced, dogmatic religion retreated, and they presented religious institutions as obstacles to scientific progress.

Criticism

Most contemporary historians of science reject the conflict thesis as an oversimplification. They point to long periods of cooperation, religious patronage of learning, and the complexity of episodes such as the trial of Galileo, which involved politics and personal rivalries as much as theology. Alternative frameworks include the "complexity thesis" associated with historian John Hedley Brooke, which stresses that relations between science and religion have varied widely by time, place and subject.
