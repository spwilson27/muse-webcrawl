Clock cycle

Clock cycle
DefinitionOne complete pulse of the clock signal
UnitSeconds (period); reciprocal is clock rate (Hz)
RelatedClock rate, instructions per cycle (IPC), pipelining

In a synchronous digital circuit, especially a central processing unit (CPU), the clock cycle is the smallest unit of time, defined by one complete oscillation of the clock signal that synchronizes the circuit's operations. During each cycle, the CPU can perform a basic step of work such as fetching an instruction, decoding it, or executing part of an operation. The clock cycle's duration is the clock period; its reciprocal is the clock rate (clock speed), measured in hertz — modern processors run at several gigahertz, corresponding to cycle times of fractions of a nanosecond.

Instructions and cycles

Simple processors may take several clock cycles to execute one instruction (cycles per instruction, CPI), while modern pipelined, superscalar designs complete more than one instruction per cycle on average (instructions per cycle, IPC). Performance therefore depends on clock rate, IPC, and the number of instructions executed, not on clock speed alone. The clock signal is distributed across the chip on a clock tree, and timing constraints (setup and hold times, propagation delays such as the critical path) set the maximum achievable frequency. Dynamic voltage and frequency scaling varies the clock rate to trade performance against power consumption, and clock gating switches off idle components to save energy.
