Power outage


Labelled image placeholder: Manhattan during the 2003 Northeast blackout

A power outage, also called a power cut, power blackout, or power failure, is the loss of the electrical power network supply to an end user. Outages range from momentary interruptions affecting a single building to rolling blackouts and cascading failures that darken entire regions.

Causes

Common causes include storms, lightning, ice, and fallen trees damaging lines; equipment failure; short circuits (for example, from animals contacting lines); overload during peak demand; and planned maintenance. Large cascading failures can result when the loss of one component overloads others, as in the Northeast blackout of 2003, which affected an estimated 55 million people in the United States and Canada, and the 2012 India blackouts, the largest in history by population affected.

Types

Utility terminology distinguishes transient faults (momentary interruptions), brownouts (a deliberate or accidental drop in voltage), and blackouts (total loss of power). Rolling blackouts are intentionally imposed, rotating interruptions used to shed load when generation is insufficient, as occurred during the 2000–2001 California electricity crisis.

Protection and restoration

Grids protect themselves with relays, circuit breakers, and automatic reclosers; hospitals and data centres use uninterruptible power supplies and backup generators. Reliability is measured by indices such as SAIDI and SAIFI, the average duration and frequency of interruptions per customer.
