Jazz funeral

[Image: Jazz funeral procession, New Orleans]

A jazz funeral is a funeral procession accompanied by a brass band, a distinctive tradition of New Orleans, Louisiana. Rooted in the city's African American community, the custom combines West African, Haitian, and European military funeral traditions with the music of New Orleans brass bands. It is closely associated with the "second line" parades of the city's social aid and pleasure clubs.

The procession

The funeral begins with a slow, mournful procession from the funeral home or church to the cemetery, led by the band playing dirges and hymns such as "Nearer, My God, to Thee". After the body is interred — the moment known as "cutting the body loose" — the music shifts abruptly to upbeat numbers such as "When the Saints Go Marching In", and mourners dance in celebration of the deceased's life. The main procession is the "first line"; the crowd of dancers and onlookers following behind forms the "second line".

History

The tradition developed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries among Black benevolent societies and social aid and pleasure clubs, which provided burial insurance and organised funerals for their members. Musicians' funerals became celebrated events; notable examples honoured figures such as Danny Barker and many of the city's brass band pioneers. After Hurricane Katrina in 2005, jazz funerals were held for victims of the storm and became symbols of the city's recovery.

Modern practice

Today jazz funerals are held for musicians, community figures, and others, and elements of the tradition have spread beyond New Orleans. The custom remains a central expression of the city's culture and its philosophy of celebrating life in the face of death.
