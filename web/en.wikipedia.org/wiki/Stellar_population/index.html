Stellar population

A globular cluster, a classic Population II system.

In 1944, Walter Baade categorized groups of stars within galaxies into stellar populations, based on his observations of the Andromeda Galaxy and its companions, which he could resolve into individual stars during wartime blackouts in Los Angeles. Baade recognized two classes — Population I and Population II — differing in location, color, and chemical composition, and astronomers later added a hypothetical first generation, Population III.

The populations

PopulationMetallicityTypical location / age
Population IHigh (rich in heavy elements; the Sun, ~1.4% metals)Galactic disk and spiral arms; young to middle-aged
Population IILow (metal-poor; as little as 0.01% of solar)Globular clusters, galactic halo and bulge; old (>10 Gyr)
Population IIIZero (only hydrogen, helium, trace lithium from the Big Bang)The first stars, formed a few hundred million years after the Big Bang; none directly observed

Significance

The population scheme is a fossil record of galactic chemical evolution. Population II stars formed from gas barely enriched by earlier supernovae, while Population I stars condensed from interstellar material enriched by many generations of star formation and death. Population III stars — presumably very massive and short-lived — are thought to have produced the first heavy elements, reionized the universe, and seeded early galaxies; candidate detections with the James Webb Space Telescope and ground-based surveys remain under active study. Modern usage treats "population" more as a continuum of metallicity and age than as two sharply distinct classes.
