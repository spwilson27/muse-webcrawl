TypeFat-soluble vitamin
FunctionCofactor for γ-carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX, X and other proteins

Vitamin K is a family of structurally similar, fat-soluble vitamins found in foods and marketed as dietary supplements. The human body requires vitamin K for post-translational modification of certain proteins required for blood coagulation (clotting) and for the binding of calcium in bones and other tissues. The "K" comes from the German/Danish word koagulation; the vitamin was discovered in 1929 by Danish scientist Henrik Dam, who shared the 1943 Nobel Prize for the work with Edward A. Doisy.

Forms and sources

Vitamin K₁ (phylloquinone) is made by plants and is abundant in leafy green vegetables. Vitamin K₂ (menaquinones, MK-4 through MK-13) is produced by bacteria, occurs in fermented foods — notably Japanese nattō, exceptionally rich in MK-7 — and some animal products; gut bacteria also produce menaquinones. The body can convert K₁ to MK-4. Synthetic K₃ (menadione) is no longer used in human supplements because of toxicity concerns.

Physiology

Vitamin K is the cofactor for the enzyme γ-glutamyl carboxylase, which activates (carboxylates) calcium-binding Gla proteins: the clotting factors II (prothrombin), VII, IX, and X and anticoagulant proteins C and S in the liver, and osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein in bone and vasculature. The anticoagulant drug warfarin works by blocking the recycling of vitamin K (via VKORC1), which is why dietary vitamin K intake interacts with warfarin therapy, and why vitamin K is the antidote to warfarin overdose.

Deficiency and clinical use

Deficiency in adults is uncommon but can follow malabsorption or prolonged antibiotic use; in newborns, whose guts are sterile and whose intake is low, deficiency can cause life-threatening vitamin K deficiency bleeding, which is why a single intramuscular injection of vitamin K at birth is standard practice in many countries since 1961. Vitamin K is also used to treat certain coagulopathies and is monitored in patients on anticoagulants.
