Die casting is a metal casting process that is characterised by forcing molten metal under high pressure into a mould cavity. The mould cavity is created using two hardened tool steel dies which have been machined into shape and work similarly to an injection mould during the process. Most die castings are made from non-ferrous metals, specifically zinc, copper, aluminium, magnesium, lead, pewter, and tin-based alloys.

Process variants

In the hot-chamber process, used for low-melting alloys such as zinc, the injection mechanism is immersed in the molten metal. In the cold-chamber process, used for aluminium and magnesium, metal is ladled into a shot sleeve and driven into the die by a plunger at pressures of tens to over a hundred megapascals. After solidification the die opens and ejector pins push the casting out; the part is then trimmed of its sprue and runners.

Characteristics

Die casting produces parts with excellent dimensional accuracy, smooth surfaces, thin walls and complex detail at very high production rates, making it economical for large volumes — from die-cast toy cars to transmission housings and laptop chassis. Disadvantages include high initial tooling cost and porosity caused by entrapped gas, which generally prevents heat treatment or welding of conventional castings; vacuum and squeeze-casting variants mitigate this.

History

Die casting equipment was invented in the mid-19th century for printing type; the Soss die-casting machine (early 1900s) enabled mass production of small metal parts, and the process expanded greatly with the automotive industry.
