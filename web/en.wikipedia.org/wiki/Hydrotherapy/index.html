Hydrotherapy is the use of water in the treatment of injury, disease and pain. It encompasses aquatic physiotherapy exercises, immersion baths, whirlpools, steam and compresses, and exploits the physical properties of water — buoyancy, hydrostatic pressure, viscosity and heat transfer — for therapeutic effect.

Principles

Buoyancy offloads body weight, reducing stress on joints and allowing movement that would be painful or impossible on land; immersion to the neck can reduce effective weight bearing by around 90%. Hydrostatic pressure assists venous return and can reduce oedema, while the resistance of water provides graded strengthening exercise in all directions of movement. Heated water (typically 33–36 °C in therapy pools) relaxes muscle spasm and eases stiffness.

Applications

Hydrotherapy is widely used in rehabilitation after orthopaedic surgery and injury, in neurological conditions such as stroke and multiple sclerosis, in chronic pain and arthritis management, and in sports rehabilitation. Whirlpool therapy and irrigation are used for wound and burn care, although their role has declined with modern dressings. Contrast water therapy — alternating hot and cold immersion — is popular among athletes for recovery, though evidence for it is mixed.

History

Therapeutic bathing dates to antiquity: Egyptian, Greek and Roman cultures all used baths medicinally, and Roman thermae were social as well as therapeutic institutions. In the 19th century, figures such as Vincenz Priessnitz and Sebastian Kneipp popularised "water cure" establishments in Europe, and spa towns grew around mineral springs. Modern aquatic physiotherapy developed alongside rehabilitation medicine in the 20th century.
