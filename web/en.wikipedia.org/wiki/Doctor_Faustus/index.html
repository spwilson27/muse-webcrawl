The Tragical History of the Life and Death of Doctor Faustus, commonly referred to simply as Doctor Faustus, is an Elizabethan tragedy by Christopher Marlowe, based on German stories about the title character Faust. It was probably written in 1592 or 1593, shortly before Marlowe's death, and first published in 1604.

Faustus summons Mephistopheles.

Plot

Doctor John Faustus, a brilliant but dissatisfied scholar at Wittenberg, rejects logic, medicine, law and divinity and turns to necromancy. He summons Mephistopheles, a servant of Lucifer, and signs a pact in his own blood: twenty-four years of service from Mephistopheles in exchange for his soul. Despite warnings from a Good Angel and an Old Man, Faustus wastes his powers on frivolous tricks — mocking the Pope, conjuring grapes in winter, and summoning the spirit of Helen of Troy, whose appearance prompts the famous line "Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships?" As the final hour strikes, Faustus begs in vain for mercy and is carried off by devils.

Texts

The play survives in two significantly different versions: the shorter A text (1604) and the longer B text (1616), which adds scenes likely written by other hands — Samuel Rowley and William Birde were paid for additions in 1602. Scholars continue to debate which text better represents Marlowe's original.

Themes and legacy

The play explores ambition, the limits of knowledge, sin and redemption, and the conflict between medieval theology and Renaissance humanism. Its soliloquies are among the earliest masterpieces of English blank verse. Doctor Faustus established the Faust legend in English literature, a story later treated by Goethe and many others.
