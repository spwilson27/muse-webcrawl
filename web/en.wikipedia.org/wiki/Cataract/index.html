A cataract is a cloudy area in the lens of the eye that leads to a decrease in vision. Cataracts often develop slowly and can affect one or both eyes. Symptoms may include faded colours, blurry or double vision, halos around light, trouble with bright lights, and trouble seeing at night. Cataracts are the most common cause of blindness worldwide and the most common cause of vision loss in people over 40.

Causes and risk factors

The lens is normally clear; proteins within it break down and clump together with age, clouding the lens. Ageing is the most common cause. Other causes include trauma, exposure to ionizing radiation or ultraviolet light, diabetes mellitus, smoking, alcohol use, corticosteroid medication, and certain congenital conditions such as congenital rubella infection. Cataracts present from birth are called congenital cataracts.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by an eye examination, typically involving a slit-lamp microscope, visual acuity testing, and examination of the retina after pupil dilation. The opacity may be nuclear, cortical, or posterior subcapsular in type.

Treatment

No medication has been shown to reverse cataracts. Early on, symptoms may be managed with glasses, brighter lighting, and magnifying lenses. When vision loss interferes with daily life, cataract surgery is performed: the clouded lens is removed — most commonly by phacoemulsification, using ultrasound to fragment the lens — and replaced with a clear artificial intraocular lens. Cataract surgery is one of the most common and successful surgical procedures worldwide, with tens of millions of operations performed each year, though access remains limited in many low-income countries, leaving cataract the leading cause of treatable blindness.
