American Gothic

American Gothic is a 1930 painting by Grant Wood in the collection of the Art Institute of Chicago. It depicts a farmer standing beside his daughter — often mistakenly described as his wife — in front of a farmhouse built in the Carpenter Gothic style. One of the most famous and most parodied images in American art, it was inspired by the Dibble House in Eldon, Iowa, whose Gothic window Wood noticed on a drive in 1930; his models were his sister Nan Wood Graham and his dentist, Dr. Byron McKeeby.

Creation and early reception

Wood entered the painting in a competition at the Art Institute of Chicago, where it won a bronze medal and a $300 prize and was purchased for the museum's collection. Iowans were initially offended by what they read as a caricature of rural people, but the painting's meaning shifted with the onset of the Great Depression: it came to be celebrated as an image of steadfast, self-reliant American pioneer virtues, though art historians continue to debate whether Wood intended affectionate tribute, gentle satire, or both.

Style

The work reflects Wood's study of Northern Renaissance painters such as Jan van Eyck, with its tight composition, frontal poses, and meticulous detail — the man's overalls and pitchfork, the woman's cameo brooch and printed apron — set against the house with its distinctive upper window resembling a church window.

Cultural impact

American Gothic has been endlessly reproduced and parodied in advertising, cartoons, films, and photographs, and the Eldon house is now a visitor attraction. Along with works such as Christina's World and Nighthawks, it is among the most recognisable American paintings of the 20th century.
