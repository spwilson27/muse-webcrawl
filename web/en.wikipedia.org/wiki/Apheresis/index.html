Apheresis

Apheresis (from Greek aphairesis, "a taking away") is a medical technology in which the blood of a person is passed through an apparatus that separates out one particular constituent and returns the remainder to the circulation. It is used both to collect blood components from donors and as a treatment that removes harmful substances or cells from a patient's blood.

An apheresis machine separates blood components by centrifugation

Methods

Most modern apheresis machines separate blood using centrifugation, exploiting the different densities of plasma, platelets, white blood cells, and red blood cells; membrane filtration is used in some plasma separation systems. An anticoagulant, typically citrate, is added during the procedure, and the unneeded component is diverted to a collection bag while the rest of the blood is returned.

Donor apheresis

Apheresis allows blood centers to collect specific components in larger quantities than whole-blood donation: plateletpheresis collects platelets, plasmapheresis collects plasma (including source plasma for fractionation into medicines), and other procedures collect red cells, granulocytes, or hematopoietic stem cells from peripheral blood.

Therapeutic apheresis

Therapeutic plasma exchange replaces a patient's plasma with albumin or donor plasma and is a standard treatment for conditions including thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis crises, and certain antibody-mediated transplant rejections. Other therapeutic procedures include LDL apheresis for severe familial hypercholesterolemia, leukocytapheresis for dangerously high white cell counts in leukemia, erythrocytapheresis (red cell exchange) in sickle cell disease, and photopheresis for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma and graft-versus-host disease.

Risks

Side effects are usually mild and include tingling from citrate-related lowering of ionized calcium, light-headedness, and bruising at needle sites. More serious complications are uncommon.
