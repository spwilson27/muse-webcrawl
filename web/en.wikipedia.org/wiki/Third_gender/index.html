Third gender

Third gender is a concept in which individuals are categorized, either by themselves or by society, as neither man nor woman. It is also a social category present in many societies that recognize three or more genders. The term third is usually understood to mean "other", though some anthropologists and sociologists have described fourth and fifth genders.

Around the world

Many cultures have long-standing third-gender categories:

  • Hijra in South Asia — one of the best-known third-gender categories; hijras were legally recognized as a third gender by the Supreme Court of India in 2014, and are also officially recognized in Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal.
  • Fa'afafine in Samoa and fakaleiti in Tonga — people assigned male at birth who embody feminine gender traits and occupy an accepted social role.
  • Two-spirit — a modern pan-Indigenous term used by some Native North American communities for people fulfilling mixed-gender roles, which historically existed in many tribes.
  • Muxe in Oaxaca, Mexico; burrnesha (sworn virgins) in the Balkans; kathoey in Thailand; and waria in Indonesia.

Legal recognition

A growing number of countries allow a non-binary or third-gender marker on official documents. Germany introduced a "divers" (diverse) option on civil records in 2018; several other states, including Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Argentina, and some US states, permit an "X" marker on passports or identity documents.

Relation to modern concepts

Anthropologists caution that historical third-gender roles are not identical to the contemporary Western concepts of transgender or non-binary identity, though they are often discussed together. The categories are embedded in specific religious, kinship, and economic contexts; for example, hijras traditionally perform at births and weddings and follow a guru–chela (teacher–disciple) household structure.
