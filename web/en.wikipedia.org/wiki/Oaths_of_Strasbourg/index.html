Oaths of Strasbourg

The Oaths of Strasbourg (French: Serments de Strasbourg; German: Straßburger Eide) were mutual pledges of allegiance sworn on 14 February 842 by Louis the German and Charles the Bald, the younger sons of the emperor Louis the Pious, against their elder brother Lothair I. They are famous as the earliest surviving text in a Romance vernacular — an early form of French — and among the earliest in Old High German.

Background

After the death of Louis the Pious in 840, civil war broke out among his three surviving sons over the division of the Carolingian Empire. Louis the German and Charles the Bald defeated Lothair at the Battle of Fontenoy in 841 and met at Strasbourg the following year to seal their alliance publicly before their assembled armies.

The ceremony and text

So that each army could understand the oath of the allied king, Louis swore in Romance (romana lingua) for Charles's Frankish followers, and Charles swore in Germanic (teudisca lingua) for Louis's men. Their soldiers then swore in their own tongues.

Louis's Romance oath begins:

Pro Deo amur et pro christian poblo et nostro commun salvament, d'ist di in avant, in quant Deus savir et podir me dunat, si salvarai eo cist meon fradre Karlo... ("For the love of God and for Christendom and our common salvation, from this day onward, in so far as God gives me knowledge and power, I shall protect this my brother Charles...")

The account survives in the Historiae of Nithard, a grandson of Charlemagne who fought for Charles, the only contemporary source for the ceremony.

Significance

Philologists prize the Romance text as the first connected document in the emerging French language, showing how far spoken Latin in Gaul had diverged from the written classical language. Politically, the alliance led within a year to the Treaty of Verdun (843), which partitioned the empire into the kingdoms that foreshadowed France and Germany; the oaths are thus often treated as a symbolic founding moment for both nations.
