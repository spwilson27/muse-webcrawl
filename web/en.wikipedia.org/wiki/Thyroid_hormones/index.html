Thyroid hormones

Thyroid hormones are two hormones produced and released by the thyroid gland, namely triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4). They are tyrosine-based hormones that are primarily responsible for regulation of metabolism. T3 and T4 are partially composed of iodine, and a deficiency of iodine leads to decreased production and enlargement of the thyroid gland, known as goitre.

Production

The thyroid gland produces roughly ten times as much T4 as T3. Most T3 is generated in peripheral tissues by deiodination of T4. Production is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis: thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from the pituitary stimulates synthesis and release, and the hormones exert negative feedback on both the pituitary and hypothalamus.

Effects

Thyroid hormones increase the basal metabolic rate, affect protein synthesis, help regulate bone growth and neural maturation, and increase the body's sensitivity to catecholamines. They are essential for proper development and differentiation of all cells of the human body.

Medical use

Levothyroxine, a synthetic form of T4, is widely prescribed to treat hypothyroidism. Excess production, as in Graves' disease, causes hyperthyroidism, whose symptoms include weight loss, heat intolerance, and rapid heartbeat.
