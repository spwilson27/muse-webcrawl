Benefice

A benefice is a reward received in exchange for services rendered, and as a retainer for future services. In historical usage, particularly within the Roman Catholic Church and the Church of England, a benefice is an ecclesiastical office — such as a parish — together with the revenue (from glebe land, tithes or an endowment) attached to it, held by a cleric in return for performing its duties.

History

The term derives from the Latin beneficium, originally a grant of land for life in return for service; in the feudal era it became closely associated with the fief. Ecclesiastical benefices became systematized in medieval canon law: the holder (the incumbent) enjoyed the income for life so long as he discharged the spiritual duties of the office. Abuse of the system — pluralism (holding several benefices at once), absenteeism, and the sale of offices (simony) — was a recurring target of reformers.

In the Church of England

In the Church of England, a benefice is the position of rector or vicar of a parish, carrying the "cure of souls" and historically freehold tenure of the parsonage house and glebe. Modern legislation groups several parishes into united benefices served by a single incumbent, and the traditional freehold has largely been replaced by common tenure.

Canon law

Under the Catholic Church's 1983 Code of Canon Law, the old benefice system was largely superseded: a benefice is defined as a juridical entity consisting of a sacred office and the right of receiving revenues from its endowment, but most offices are now financed otherwise.
