Float glass

Float glass is a sheet of glass made by floating molten glass on a bed of molten metal, typically tin, hence the name tin bath process. This method gives the sheet uniform thickness and very flat surfaces. Since the 1960s the float process — invented by Sir Alastair Pilkington of the British company Pilkington, who conceived it in 1952 and announced it publicly in 1959 — has been the dominant method of manufacturing plate glass worldwide, replacing the costly grinding and polishing of plate glass.

Process

In the float process, raw materials (silica sand, soda ash, limestone, dolomite and cullet) are melted in a furnace at about 1,500 °C. The molten glass, at roughly 1,050 °C, pours continuously onto a bath of molten tin contained in a controlled nitrogen–hydrogen atmosphere that prevents oxidation of the tin. Being less dense, the glass floats and spreads across the tin, forming a ribbon with naturally fire-polished surfaces and an equilibrium thickness of about 6.8 mm; rollers and top rolls stretch or compress the ribbon to produce thicknesses typically from about 0.4 mm to 25 mm. The ribbon is then cooled slowly in an annealing lehr, scored, and cut.

Uses

Float glass is the base material for most architectural glazing, windows, mirrors (after silvering), vehicle windows, and for further processing into tempered (toughened) glass, laminated glass, insulated glazing units, and low-emissivity coated products. One side of the sheet bears a trace of tin — the "tin side" — which can be detected under ultraviolet light and affects some coating and printing processes.
