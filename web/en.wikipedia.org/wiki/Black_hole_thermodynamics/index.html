Black hole thermodynamics

Black hole thermodynamics is the field that applies the laws of thermodynamics to black hole event horizons. In the 1970s Jacob Bekenstein proposed that a black hole has entropy proportional to the area of its horizon, and Stephen Hawking showed that black holes emit thermal radiation — Hawking radiation — at a temperature inversely proportional to their mass. These results link gravity, quantum mechanics, and thermodynamics and underlie the black hole information paradox.

The four laws

Bardeen, Carter, and Hawking formulated four laws of black hole mechanics analogous to those of thermodynamics: the zeroth law (surface gravity is constant over the horizon), the first law (changes in mass relate to changes in area, angular momentum, and charge), the second law (horizon area never decreases — Hawking's area theorem), and the third law (surface gravity cannot be reduced to zero in finite steps).

Entropy and radiation

Bekenstein argued that if black holes had no entropy, dropping hot matter into one would violate the second law. Hawking's 1974 calculation showed black holes radiate like black bodies and slowly evaporate, confirming the identification of area with entropy. The Bekenstein–Hawking entropy of a solar-mass black hole is enormous, far exceeding the entropy of the star that formed it.

Implications

The area scaling of entropy inspired the holographic principle — the idea that the information content of a volume is encoded on its boundary — and AdS/CFT correspondence research. Whether evaporation destroys information, seemingly violating quantum unitarity, remains a central open problem, though work on Page curves and "islands" has shifted many physicists toward information preservation.
