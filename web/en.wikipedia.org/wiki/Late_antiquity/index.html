Late antiquity

Late antiquity is a periodisation of history used by historians to describe the time of transition from classical antiquity to the Middle Ages in Europe and the Near East. It generally spans from the late 3rd century to the 7th or early 8th century AD — from the Crisis of the Third Century and the reforms of Diocletian (from c. 284) through the fall of the Western Roman Empire, the flourishing of the Byzantine Empire, and the early Muslim conquests. The concept emphasises continuity and transformation rather than abrupt decline: the period saw the Christianisation of the Roman world, the formation of rabbinic Judaism, the fall of the Sasanian Empire, and the birth of Islam, even as Roman law, urban life, and classical learning persisted in altered forms.

Late antique art blended classical Roman styles with new Christian iconography.

Terminology and historiography

The German term Spätantike was in use among German-speaking scholars from the early twentieth century; the Burckhardtian notion of a decadent later empire was decisively challenged by historians such as Peter Brown, whose The World of Late Antiquity (1971) presented the period as one of creativity, religious ferment, and cultural change rather than mere decay. The period is now a well-established field of study, with dedicated journals and research centres. Scholars debate its exact boundaries: some extend it to c. 800, encompassing the first two centuries of Islam and the reign of Charlemagne.

Key developments

  • Political: The division of the Roman Empire into eastern and western halves; the deposition of the last western emperor in 476; the survival of the eastern (Byzantine) empire; the Gothic, Vandal, and other successor kingdoms in the West; the last Roman–Persian war (602–628); and the Arab conquests of the 630s–640s that detached Syria, Egypt, and North Africa from Byzantine rule and destroyed the Sasanian Empire.
  • Religious: The toleration (313) and eventual establishment of Christianity; the councils that defined orthodox doctrine; the rise of monasticism; the codification of the Babylonian Talmud; Zoroastrian orthodoxy under the Sasanians; and the preaching of Muhammad and the formation of the early Islamic community.
  • Social and economic: The binding of tenant farmers (coloni) to the land; shifting patterns of urban life; new networks of pilgrimage, trade, and scholarship linking the Mediterranean world with Persia, Arabia, and beyond.
  • Cultural: The patronage of saints and holy men; new artistic forms in mosaics, ivory carving, and illuminated manuscripts; and the preservation and transformation of classical knowledge in both the Byzantine and early Islamic worlds.
