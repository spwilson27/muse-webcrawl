Multidimensional scaling

Multidimensional scaling (MDS) is a means of visualizing the level of similarity of individual cases of a data set. It refers to a set of related ordination techniques used in information visualization for exploring similarities or dissimilarities in data. MDS is a special case of ordination and is used to translate a matrix of distances or dissimilarities between objects into a configuration of points in a low-dimensional space, usually two or three dimensions, so that the distances between the points match the original dissimilarities as closely as possible.

Types

Classical MDS, also known as principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), takes a matrix of inter-item distances and produces coordinates whose Euclidean distances approximate the given ones. It proceeds by double-centering the squared distance matrix and extracting its largest eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Metric MDS generalizes the optimization problem, minimizing a loss function known as stress, which measures the mismatch between distances in the low-dimensional embedding and a function of the original dissimilarities.

Nonmetric MDS, developed by Roger Shepard and Joseph Kruskal, uses only the rank order of the dissimilarities, seeking a monotone transformation of the data together with a point configuration whose distances preserve that order.

Applications

MDS originated in psychometrics, where it was used to model perceptual and preference data, but it is now applied across the sciences: in marketing research (perceptual mapping of brands), in ecology (ordination of communities), in bioinformatics and molecular biology (mapping relationships between sequences or structures), and in machine learning as a nonlinear dimensionality-reduction technique related to methods such as Isomap, t-SNE, and UMAP.

Stress

The quality of an MDS solution is commonly assessed by a stress value: lower stress indicates a better fit between the distances in the reduced space and the original dissimilarities. Rules of thumb originating with Kruskal regard stress below 0.05 as excellent and above 0.2 as poor, though these depend on the number of objects and the dimensionality.
