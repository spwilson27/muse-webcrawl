The Canada Act 1982 (French: Loi de 1982 sur le Canada) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that, at the request of the Parliament of Canada, ended the British Parliament's authority to legislate for Canada and formally transferred the power to amend the Canadian constitution to Canada itself. Its Schedule B is the text of the Constitution Act, 1982, which includes the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and an amending formula.

Background

Although the Statute of Westminster 1931 gave Canada full legislative independence, the British North America Acts — Canada's constitution — remained amendable only by the UK Parliament because Canadians could not agree on a domestic amending formula. Prime Minister Pierre Elliott Trudeau's government renewed the effort to "patriate" the constitution in 1980. After intense federal–provincial negotiations — including the November 1981 "Kitchen Accord" — agreement was reached with nine of ten provinces; Quebec, under René Lévesque, did not assent.

Passage and proclamation

The Act was passed at Westminster and received royal assent on 29 March 1982. Queen Elizabeth II proclaimed the Constitution Act in force at a ceremony on Parliament Hill, Ottawa, on 17 April 1982, signing the proclamation in the rain. Section 2 of the Canada Act provides that no future Act of the UK Parliament extending to Canada shall have effect, and section 3 brought Schedule B into force.

Significance and Quebec

The Act completed Canada's constitutional independence. Its legacy in Quebec remains contested: the Quebec government of the day felt betrayed by the November 1981 agreement reached in its absence, and subsequent attempts to secure Quebec's formal endorsement — the Meech Lake Accord (1987–90) and the Charlottetown Accord (1992) — both failed, though the Supreme Court held in 1982 that Quebec possessed no veto and the patriation was legally valid.
