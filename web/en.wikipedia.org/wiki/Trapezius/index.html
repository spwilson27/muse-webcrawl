Trapezius

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Trapezius
OriginExternal occipital protuberance, nuchal ligament, spinous processes of C7–T12
InsertionClavicle, acromion, spine of scapula
NerveSpinal accessory nerve (CN XI); C3–C4 sensory/proprioceptive
ActionsElevates, retracts, depresses and rotates the scapula; extends the neck

The trapezius is a large paired trapezoid-shaped surface muscle that extends longitudinally from the occipital bone to the lower thoracic vertebrae of the back and laterally to the spine of the scapula. It moves the scapulae and supports the arm. The trapezius has three functional parts: a superior part that supports the weight of the arm, an intermediate part that retracts the scapulae, and an inferior part that medially rotates and depresses the scapulae. The two muscles together form a diamond (trapezoid) shape, from which the name derives.

Structure

The superior fibres arise from the skull and nuchal ligament and run down to the lateral third of the clavicle. The middle fibres arise from the upper thoracic spinous processes and run horizontally to the acromion and scapular spine. The inferior fibres arise from the lower thoracic spines and converge upward onto the scapular spine. Unlike most muscles, which are supplied by nerves of the brachial or cervical plexuses, the trapezius receives its motor supply from the spinal accessory nerve (the eleventh cranial nerve), reflecting its embryological origin in the branchial arches, with contributions from the cervical plexus (C3, C4) for pain and proprioception.

Function

The upper fibres elevate the shoulder girdle — as in shrugging — and, acting with other muscles, help extend and side-flex the neck. The middle fibres retract (adduct) the scapula toward the spine. The lower fibres depress the scapula and, with the upper fibres, form a force couple that upwardly rotates the scapula during arm elevation, an essential part of raising the arm overhead. The muscle also stabilises the scapula as a base for arm movement.

Clinical significance

Damage to the spinal accessory nerve — for example during lymph node biopsy or neck dissection — weakens the trapezius, causing shoulder droop, difficulty elevating the arm, and winging of the scapula. Trapezius pain and spasm are common complaints associated with posture and stress, and the muscle's trigger points can refer pain to the neck, head, and shoulder. It is tested clinically by asking the patient to shrug against resistance and to brace the shoulders back.

Exercise

The trapezius is commonly trained with shrugs, rows, deadlifts, and carries in strength training, where its upper portion is one of the most visible muscles of the upper back and neck.
