Condottiero

A condottiero (plural condottieri) was the leader of a band of mercenaries engaged by Italian city-states and the papacy from the late Middle Ages into the mid-16th century. The word derives from condotta, the contract by which a captain undertook to provide a specified number of troops for a fixed term and payment. Condottieri commanded professional companies — the compagnie di ventura ("free companies") — whose backbone was heavy cavalry of men-at-arms supported by infantry and, later, crossbowmen and handgunners.

Rise

The system arose as wealthy but militarily weak Italian communes, unwilling to rely on citizen militias or feudal levies, hired foreign and then Italian captains. Early companies were largely foreign — Germans, Provençals, and Englishmen such as Sir John Hawkwood, who served Florence in the late 14th century. By the 15th century the great captains were Italian: Muzio Attendolo Sforza and Braccio da Montone founded rival schools of warfare, and their followers dominated the wars of the Italian states. The most successful condottieri became rulers in their own right: Francesco Sforza, son of Muzio, seized the Duchy of Milan in 1450; Federico da Montefeltro made Urbino a court of the Renaissance; and figures like Bartolomeo Colleoni and Erasmo da Narni ("Gattamelata") were immortalized by equestrian statues of Verrocchio and Donatello respectively.

Conduct of war and reputation

Condottieri warfare is traditionally depicted — notably by Machiavelli, its great critic — as cautious, maneuver-oriented, and relatively bloodless, with captains avoiding risky battle and sometimes colluding with opponents; modern historians regard this picture as exaggerated, noting brutal episodes such as the sack of Cesena in 1377, but the economic logic of preserving one's company (the captain's capital) did shape tactics. Machiavelli blamed mercenaries for Italy's subjugation and urged citizen armies in The Prince and the Discourses.

Decline

The Italian Wars after 1494 brought massed national armies of Swiss pikemen, Spanish infantry, and heavy artillery against which the smaller condottieri companies could not compete, and the institution faded by the mid-16th century as territorial states built standing forces.
