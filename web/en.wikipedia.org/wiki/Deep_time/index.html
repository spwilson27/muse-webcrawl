Deep time

Deep time is the concept of geological time — the vast, multimillion- and billion-year history of the Earth — first developed in the 18th century by Scottish geologist James Hutton (1726–1797). The term was popularised in modern usage by writer John McPhee in his book Basin and Range (1981).

Concept

Examining unconformities such as Siccar Point in 1788, Hutton saw that cycles of deposition, uplift and erosion required time spans far beyond the roughly 6,000 years then allowed by biblical chronology — in his famous closing words, "no vestige of a beginning, — no prospect of an end." Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830–33) and uniformitarianism built on the idea, and it provided the timescale Darwin's theory of evolution required. Radiometric dating later quantified the Earth's age at about 4.54 billion years.

Cultural impact

McPhee framed human history against deep time with images such as the human lifespan as a single stroke of a nail file on the fingernail of one arm of the outstretched arms of Earth's history. The concept underlies the modern geologic time scale of eons, eras, periods and epochs, and remains a touchstone in science writing and in projects such as the Long Now Foundation's 10,000-year clock.
