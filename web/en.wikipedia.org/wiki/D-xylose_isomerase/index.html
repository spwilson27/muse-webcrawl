In enzymology, a D-xylose isomerase (EC 5.3.1.5) is an enzyme that catalyzes the chemical reaction converting the aldose sugar D-xylose into the ketose D-xylulose. It belongs to the family of isomerases, specifically intramolecular oxidoreductases interconverting aldoses and ketoses. In industrial practice, the same enzyme is widely — if somewhat inaccurately — called glucose isomerase, because it also catalyzes the isomerization of D-glucose to D-fructose, the key step in the production of high-fructose corn syrup.

Structure of D-xylose isomerase.

Reaction and mechanism

The enzyme catalyzes the reversible interconversion of aldoses and ketoses. Its physiological substrate is D-xylose, which it converts to D-xylulose so the sugar can enter the pentose phosphate pathway in many bacteria. The active site contains two divalent metal ions — typically Mg2+, Mn2+ or Co2+ — that coordinate the sugar and mediate a hydride shift between C-1 and C-2 after ring opening of the substrate.

Structure

D-xylose isomerases are typically tetramers (or dimers) of subunits built around an (α/β)8 TIM barrel fold, with the active site at the C-terminal end of the barrel. The enzyme is notable for its high thermal stability in industrial preparations derived from thermophilic and mesophilic Streptomyces and other actinomycetes.

Industrial use: high-fructose corn syrup

The enzyme's ability to convert glucose to the sweeter fructose underpins the high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS) industry, developed commercially in the late 1960s and 1970s. In the process, starch is liquefied and saccharified to a glucose syrup, which is passed over immobilized glucose isomerase to yield a syrup of roughly 42% fructose; further enrichment produces HFCS-55, used widely in soft drinks. It is one of the largest-scale industrial applications of an immobilized enzyme.
