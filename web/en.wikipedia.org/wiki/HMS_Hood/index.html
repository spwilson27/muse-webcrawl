HMS Hood (pennant number 51) was an Admiral-class battlecruiser of the Royal Navy and, for over twenty years, the largest warship in the world. Built during the First World War but completed after it, she became the most famous ship of the inter-war Royal Navy — the symbol of British sea power on worldwide showing-the-flag cruises. Her destruction by the German battleship Bismarck at the Battle of the Denmark Strait on 24 May 1941, with the loss of all but three of her crew of 1,418, was one of the greatest shocks of the Second World War at sea.

Design and construction

The Admiral class was ordered in 1916 in response to German battlecruiser construction; after the Battle of Jutland revealed the lethal vulnerability of British battlecruisers to plunging shellfire, the design was heavily revised with much thicker armour, and three of the four ships were cancelled, leaving Hood alone. Even so, her deck protection over the magazines remained thin by the standards of the 1930s — a weakness her officers knew and a planned reconstruction in 1941 was meant to remedy.

Launched at Clydebank on 22 August 1918 by Lady Hood, widow of Rear Admiral Sir Horace Hood, who had been killed at Jutland in HMS Invincible, she was commissioned on 15 May 1920. Her name commemorated the Hood family of naval officers, above all Admiral Lord Hood of the Revolutionary Wars and Admiral Sir Samuel Hood of the Napoleonic era.

Inter-war service

Hood became flagship of the Battlecruiser Squadron, based at Scapa Flow, and spent the inter-war decades as the Royal Navy's public face. Her most celebrated deployment was the Empire Cruise of 1923–24, when she led the Special Service Squadron on a ten-month circumnavigation of some 40,000 miles, visiting dozens of ports across the British Empire. Tens of thousands of visitors came aboard at every stop, and the cruise was remembered as a high point of imperial pageantry. She also served with the Mediterranean Fleet, was present during the Spanish Civil War (protecting British interests and evacuating refugees), and by the late 1930s her age and worn machinery were showing; a major modernisation, which would have given her new boilers, a dual-purpose secondary armament and much improved deck armour, was repeatedly postponed and then cancelled by the outbreak of war.

Second World War

Hood patrolled against German raiders in 1939 and joined Force H at Gibraltar in 1940, taking part in the attack on the French fleet at Mers-el-Kébir in July 1940 — a melancholy action against her former allies. After a refit on the Clyde in early 1941, during which she received radar and additional light anti-aircraft guns, she returned to the Home Fleet at Scapa Flow.

Loss at the Denmark Strait

On 21 May 1941 Hood, under Vice Admiral Lancelot Holland, sailed with the new battleship Prince of Wales to intercept the German battleship Bismarck and the heavy cruiser Prinz Eugen, which were breaking out into the Atlantic to raid convoys. The British ships engaged the Germans at dawn on 24 May in the Denmark Strait between Iceland and Greenland. Holland closed the range rapidly to reduce the time his ships were exposed to plunging fire, but at about 06:00, just as Hood began to turn to bring her after turrets to bear, a 15-inch shell from Bismarck's fifth salvo struck her amidships. A pillar of flame erupted near her mainmast, she heeled, split in two and sank within about three minutes.

Of her company of 1,418 officers and men, only three survived: Ordinary Signalman Ted Briggs, Able Seaman Robert Tilburn and Midshipman William Dundas. Prime Minister Winston Churchill, informed of the loss, issued the famous order to "sink the Bismarck" — a hunt that consumed the Royal Navy's energies until Bismarck was destroyed three days later.

The wreck

The wreck of Hood was found in July 2001 by explorer David Mearns, lying in pieces at a depth of about 2,800 metres. Survey evidence confirmed that a magazine explosion destroyed the ship's stern. In 2015 a privately funded expedition recovered the ship's bell, which had been located in 2001; it was conserved and presented to the National Museum of the Royal Navy in Portsmouth, where it serves as a memorial to her crew. The wreck site is protected as a war grave.
