The African Meeting House in Boston, Massachusetts, is the oldest African-American church building in the United States. Built in 1806 in what is now the Beacon Hill neighbourhood, it served the African Baptist Church congregation and became a centre of Boston's free Black community and of the abolitionist movement.

History

The congregation was organized by Thomas Paul, an African-American Baptist minister, after Black worshippers faced discrimination in white churches. Land on Joy Street was purchased in 1805 and the meeting house completed the following year, built largely by Black artisans. The building housed worship services, a school for Black children in its basement, and community gatherings.

During the nineteenth century the meeting house hosted leading abolitionists including Frederick Douglass and William Lloyd Garrison; the New England Anti-Slavery Society was founded there in 1832, and recruiting for the Black regiments of the Civil War, including the 54th Massachusetts, took place in the building. In 1898 the congregation moved to Roxbury and the building was sold, later serving as a Jewish synagogue for much of the twentieth century.

Museum

The Museum of African American History acquired the building in the 1970s and restored it to its 1855 appearance. It is a National Historic Landmark and a site on Boston's Black Heritage Trail.
