Antonie van Leeuwenhoek

Born: 24 October 1632, Delft, Dutch Republic
Died: 26 August 1723, Delft
Known for: Microbiology; discovery of microorganisms

Antonie Philips van Leeuwenhoek (24 October 1632 – 26 August 1723) was a Dutch microbiologist and microscopist in the Golden Age of Dutch science and technology. A largely self-taught man in science, he is commonly known as "the Father of Microbiology", and one of the first microscopists and microbiologists. Van Leeuwenhoek is best known for his pioneering work in microscopy and for his contributions toward the establishment of microbiology as a scientific discipline.

Microscopes and discoveries

Working as a draper in Delft, van Leeuwenhoek learned to grind lenses of exceptional quality and mounted them in small single-lens microscopes capable of magnifications exceeding 250 times — far beyond the compound microscopes of his day. Beginning in the 1670s he examined pond water, pepper infusions, dental plaque, blood, and many other substances, reporting his findings in letters to the Royal Society of London.

In 1676 he observed tiny living creatures in water, which he called dierkens or "animalcules" — the first recorded observations of bacteria and protozoa. He also described red blood cells, spermatozoa (1677), the banded structure of muscle fibres, and capillary circulation. Elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1680, he continued his observations until his death at the age of 90, bequeathing a cabinet of his microscopes to the Society.

Legacy

Van Leeuwenhoek's discoveries opened the microscopic world to science, though the significance of microorganisms in disease was not fully appreciated until the nineteenth century. The journal Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, devoted to microbiology, bears his name, as does the Royal Society's Leeuwenhoek Medal and Lecture.
