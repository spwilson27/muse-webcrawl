Impeachment in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Impeachment in the United States is the process by which a legislature may bring charges against a civil federal officer — including the President — for “Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors” (Article II, Section 4). The House of Representatives impeaches by majority vote (the analogue of indictment); the Senate tries the case, with the Chief Justice presiding at a presidential trial, and convicts by two-thirds, the penalty being removal and possible disqualification from future office. Impeachment is political-legal: the standard is not confined to indictable crimes, a point debated in every modern case.

Three presidents have been impeached: Andrew Johnson (1868; acquitted by one vote), Bill Clinton (1998; acquitted), and Donald Trump (2019 and again in 2021; acquitted both times). Richard Nixon resigned in 1974 facing certain impeachment over Watergate. About twenty federal officials in total have been impeached, most of them judges, of whom eight were convicted. State officials face analogous procedures under state constitutions.
