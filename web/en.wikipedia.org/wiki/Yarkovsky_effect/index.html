Yarkovsky effect

The Yarkovsky effect is a force acting on a rotating body in space caused by the anisotropic emission of thermal photons, which carry momentum. It is usually considered in relation to meteoroids or small asteroids, whose orbits it can measurably alter over long timescales.

Yarkovsky effect
Named after: Ivan Yarkovsky
Proposed: c. 1900
First measured: 2003 (6489 Golevka)
Related: YORP effect

Mechanism

Sunlight heats an asteroid's surface; the warmed afternoon side re-radiates heat with a delay due to thermal inertia. The recoil from this infrared radiation produces a small thrust along or against the orbital direction, depending on the direction of rotation, gradually changing the semi-major axis.

History

Polish-Russian engineer Ivan Osipovich Yarkovsky described the effect around 1900; it was largely forgotten until revived by Öpik and others. It was first directly measured in 2003 for asteroid 6489 Golevka using radar ranging from Arecibo and Goldstone.

Significance

The effect delivers main-belt asteroids into resonances that can send them toward Earth, and it must be accounted for in predicting impacts — notably for asteroid Bennu, visited by OSIRIS-REx.
