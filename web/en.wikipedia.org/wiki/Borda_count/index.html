Borda count

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Borda count is a single-winner (and, in modified form, multi-winner) election method proposed by Jean-Charles de Borda in 1770, in which voters rank candidates and points are assigned by position — in a contest of n candidates, a first preference scores more than a second, and so on — and the candidate with the highest total wins. Borda designed it for elections to the French Academy of Sciences, arguing against plurality's election of candidates most voters reject; Condorcet's rival, pairwise method sparked the founding debate of social choice.

Properties and use

The Borda count tends to elect broadly acceptable consensus candidates and uses all ranking information, but it can fail to elect a majority's clear favourite (even a Condorcet winner), is unusually vulnerable to strategic nomination and "teaming" (running clones to shift points), and punishes polarising candidacies whether or not the election should. Current public uses include Slovenia's ethnic-minority parliamentary seats, Nauru's parliament (a weighted variant), Kiribati's presidential nomination stage, and countless non-governmental settings (prize juries, Eurovision's points era, sports awards, wine competitions) — anywhere the goal is finding the option with widest aggregate acceptance rather than a majority's champion.
