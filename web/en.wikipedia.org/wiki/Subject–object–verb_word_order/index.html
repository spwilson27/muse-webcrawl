Subject–object–verb word order

Subject–object–verb (SOV)
Example languagesJapanese, Korean, Turkish, Hindi, Persian, Latin
Example"She him loves" (gloss)
FrequencyMost common order worldwide by number of languages

In linguistic typology, subject–object–verb (SOV) is a sentence structure in which the subject comes first, the object second, and the verb third: schematically, "I you love" rather than English's subject–verb–object "I love you." SOV is the most widespread word order among the world's languages by count of languages, and is also the order most often hypothesised to be the default or ancestral pattern in language evolution.

Examples

Japanese is a canonical SOV language: in Tarō ga ringo o tabeta ("Taro apple-ACC ate"), the object precedes the verb. Korean, Turkish, Hindi-Urdu, Persian, and classical Latin also favour SOV order, though Latin's rich case system allowed considerable freedom. In many SOV languages, other constituents likewise tend to precede the verb, and adpositions are typically postpositions rather than prepositions — a correlation first systematically described by Joseph Greenberg.

Properties

Typologists have identified statistical tendencies associated with SOV order: modifiers tend to precede the verb, auxiliary verbs follow the main verb, and subordinate clauses commonly precede main clauses. However, these are tendencies rather than absolutes; some SOV languages, such as Persian, diverge from several of the expected correlations.
