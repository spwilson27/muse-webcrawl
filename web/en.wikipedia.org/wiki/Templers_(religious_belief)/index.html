The Templers (German: Tempelgesellschaft, "Temple Society") are members of a Christian religious community founded in the Kingdom of Württemberg in the mid-19th century by the theologian Christoph Hoffmann (1815–1885). The society's name refers not to the medieval Knights Templar, with whom it has no connection, but to the idea of the community itself as a living "temple of God", in accordance with New Testament passages describing believers collectively as God's temple and individually as temples of the Holy Spirit.

The Templers are best known historically for the agricultural colonies they established in Palestine beginning in 1868, including settlements at Haifa, Jaffa, Sarona near Tel Aviv, and Jerusalem. Their well-built stone houses, modern farming methods, and industries made the "German Colonies" conspicuous landmarks in the late Ottoman and British Mandate periods. Most Palestine Templers were deported by the British during and after the Second World War, many of them to Australia, where the society maintains a significant presence today alongside its German branch.

Beliefs and organisation

Hoffmann, a Lutheran theologian and member of the Württemberg state parliament, developed his ideas in reaction against what he regarded as the doctrinal rigidity of the established churches. In works such as Occident und Orient (1875), he argued that salvation depended not on adherence to dogma but on the inner renewal of individuals and society, and that a community organised according to the teachings of Jesus would hasten the coming of the Kingdom of God. The name "Temple" expressed the conviction drawn from Paul's letters — notably 1 Corinthians 3:16 and Ephesians 2:21 — that a community of believers forms a spiritual temple.

The society dispensed with many traditional church structures. It recognised baptism and the Lord's Supper but treated them flexibly, placed strong emphasis on practical community life, education, and mutual aid, and organised itself in congregations led by elders. Services centred on sermons and communal singing rather than liturgy. Because Hoffmann expected the imminent gathering of the "People of God" in the Holy Land as a precondition of the Kingdom, emigration to Palestine became the society's defining project.

History

Foundation in Württemberg

Hoffmann gathered followers at the Kirschenhardthof estate near Marbach from the 1850s. After breaking with the Lutheran state church, he and his brother-in-law Georg David Hardegg formally founded the Temple Society in 1861, also establishing a settlement at Kirschenhardthof. The society's journal, Die Warte des Tempels ("The Watchtower of the Temple"), publicised its plans.

Settlement in Palestine

After an initial fact-finding journey, the first group of settlers arrived in Palestine in 1868, founding a colony at Haifa below Mount Carmel. Further colonies followed: Jaffa (1869), Sarona (1871) on the plain north-east of Jaffa, and the Rephaim Valley colony in Jerusalem (1873). Later foundations included Wilhelma (1902), Bethlehem of Galilee (1906), and Waldheim (1907). The colonies introduced modern crop rotation, mechanised milling, soap and oil production, workshops, hotels, and transport services; Templer neighbourhoods were noted for their straight streets, tiled roofs, and carved biblical inscriptions over doorways.

A rift between Hoffmann and Hardegg in the 1870s led Hardegg and about a third of the settlers to leave the society and join the state church, but the colonies continued to grow, numbering roughly 2,200 people in Palestine on the eve of the First World War. During that war the settlers, as German nationals, were interned by the British in Egypt in 1918 and afterwards permitted to return. In the 1930s a minority of younger Templers joined the Nazi Party's Palestine branch, a fact that contributed to British suspicion of the community as a whole when the Second World War began.

Deportation and dispersal

From 1939 the British interned Palestine Templers as enemy aliens; many were deported to internment camps at Tatura in Victoria, Australia, while others were exchanged for Jewish refugees from Europe. After the establishment of Israel in 1948, the remaining Templers were expelled, and their property passed to the new state, which later paid compensation under a 1962 agreement with the Federal Republic of Germany. The deportees in Australia formed the nucleus of the Temple Society Australia, centred in Melbourne suburbs such as Bayswater and Boronia, while a German branch re-established itself around Stuttgart and Kirschenhardthof.

Legacy

The Templer colonies left a marked imprint on the built environment of Israel. The German Colony in Haifa, the former Sarona colony — restored as a commercial and leisure district in Tel Aviv — and the Jerusalem German Colony remain prominent. Historians have credited the settlers with introducing advanced agricultural techniques, craftsmanship, and services that influenced the region's development, while also situating them within the broader context of European settlement in the late Ottoman Levant.
