Integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect

The integrated Sachs–Wolfe (ISW) effect is a change in the energy of photons of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) as they travel through time-varying gravitational potential wells produced by large-scale structure in the universe. Predicted by Rainer K. Sachs and Arthur M. Wolfe in 1967, the effect arises because photons gain energy falling into a gravitational potential well but lose energy climbing out; if the well deepens or shallows while the photon crosses it, the two do not cancel, leaving a net temperature shift. In a universe dominated by dark energy, potentials decay at late times, so photons crossing superclusters emerge slightly hotter and those crossing supervoids slightly colder.

Distinction from the Sachs–Wolfe effect

The ordinary (non-integrated) Sachs–Wolfe effect refers to temperature fluctuations imprinted at the surface of last scattering, where photons climb out of primordial potential wells. The integrated effect accumulates along the whole line of sight. An "early" ISW contribution arises just after recombination, when radiation still affects the growth of potentials, while the "late" ISW effect is associated with dark energy or spatial curvature and probes the recent acceleration of the universe.

Observation

Because the ISW signal is small and spread over large angular scales, it is usually detected statistically by cross-correlating CMB temperature maps from satellites such as WMAP and Planck with catalogues of galaxies, quasars and clusters that trace the intervening structure. Detections at a significance of a few standard deviations have been reported by multiple groups and are considered independent evidence for dark energy: in a flat, matter-only universe the late-time potentials would be constant and the late ISW effect would vanish. Stacking analyses centred on known superclusters and supervoids have reported temperature imprints consistent with the effect, though some reported supervoid signals (such as around the Eridanus supervoid and the CMB Cold Spot) appear larger than standard predictions and remain debated.

Related effects

Moving gravitational potentials also produce the Rees–Sciama effect, a nonlinear extension important on smaller scales, while lensing and the Sunyaev–Zeldovich effects provide complementary probes of the same structures.
