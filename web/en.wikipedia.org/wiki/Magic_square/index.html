Magic square

In recreational mathematics, a magic square is an arrangement of distinct numbers — usually integers — in a square grid, where the numbers in each row, each column, and both main diagonals all add up to the same total, called the magic constant or magic sum.

History

Magic squares have a history spanning thousands of years. The oldest known example is the Chinese Lo Shu square, a 3×3 square associated with a legend of a turtle emerging from the Luo River, dated in tradition to the time of the mythical Emperor Yu. Magic squares later spread through the Islamic world, India, and eventually Europe, where they were studied by mathematicians and invested with mystical and astrological significance. Albrecht Dürer's 1514 engraving Melencolia I famously contains a 4×4 magic square.

Properties

A normal magic square of order n contains the integers from 1 to n². Its magic constant is given by the formula M = n(n² + 1)/2. For the 3×3 Lo Shu square the constant is 15:

There is no 2×2 normal magic square of distinct integers. The number of essentially distinct magic squares grows rapidly with order: there is 1 of order 3, 880 of order 4, and 275,305,224 of order 5.

Construction methods

Classical algorithms include the Siamese method (de la Loubère's method) for odd orders, and various methods for doubly and singly even orders. Related objects include pandiagonal (most-perfect) squares, bimagic squares (which remain magic when entries are squared), and magic cubes in three dimensions.
