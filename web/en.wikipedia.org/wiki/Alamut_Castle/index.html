Alamut Castle

Alamut Castle (Persian: قلعه الموت) was a mountain fortress in the Alamut region of the Alborz mountains, in what is now Qazvin Province, Iran. Perched on a narrow rock spur some 2,100 metres above sea level, it became famous as the headquarters of the Nizari Ismaili state under Hassan-i Sabbah from 1090 until its destruction by the Mongols in 1256.

History

According to legend the castle's name derives from an eagle that guided a king to the site: Alamut is read as āluh amut, "the eagle's teaching". Hassan-i Sabbah, the Nizari missionary who had converted many local villagers, took the fortress by infiltration in 1090, reportedly paying its commander 3,000 gold dinars for it. From Alamut the Nizaris — whose fedayeen carried out targeted assassinations of Seljuk and Crusader-era leaders, giving rise to the word "assassin" — resisted the Seljuk Empire for over 160 years. The castle housed a renowned library and gardens that later fed legends told by Marco Polo about the "Old Man of the Mountain".

Fall and remains

In 1256 the last lord of Alamut, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, surrendered to the Mongol prince Hülegü; the fortress was demolished and its library burned. A smaller Safavid-era prison castle later occupied the rock. Today only ruins and rock-cut cisterns survive, but Alamut remains a popular hiking destination and a symbol in Ismaili memory.
