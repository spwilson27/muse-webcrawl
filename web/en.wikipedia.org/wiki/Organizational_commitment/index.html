Organizational commitment

Field: Organizational psychology, industrial relations

Key model: Meyer & Allen three-component model (1991)

Outcomes linked: Turnover, absenteeism, performance

In organizational behaviour, organizational commitment is the psychological attachment an individual feels toward the organisation that employs them. Committed employees identify with the organisation's goals and wish to remain members of it; commitment is studied because it predicts turnover, absenteeism and, more weakly, job performance.

Three-component model

John Meyer and Natalie Allen's influential model (1991) distinguishes three forms of commitment that can coexist in different degrees:

Affective commitment — emotional attachment: the employee stays because they want to. It develops from satisfying work experiences, supportive leadership and perceived organisational support.

Continuance commitment — awareness of the costs of leaving: the employee stays because they need to, given pension losses, scarce alternatives or accumulated "side bets" in the organisation.

Normative commitment — a sense of obligation: the employee stays because they feel they ought to, from socialisation or reciprocity for benefits received.

Antecedents and consequences

Meta-analyses associate affective commitment most strongly with attendance, citizenship behaviour and performance, whereas continuance commitment is unrelated or negatively related to performance. Commitment is shaped by job characteristics, fairness of procedures and the handling of organisational change, and is measured with instruments such as the Organizational Commitment Questionnaire of Mowday, Steers and Porter.

Related concepts

Commitment may focus on targets other than the organisation — the work group, supervisor, occupation or union — and is distinct from, though correlated with, job satisfaction and employee engagement.
