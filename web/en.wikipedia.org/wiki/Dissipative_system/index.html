Dissipative system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dissipative system is a thermodynamically open system which is operating out of, and often far from, thermodynamic equilibrium in an environment with which it exchanges energy and matter. In dynamical systems theory, a dissipative system is one in which volumes in phase space contract over time, so that trajectories are drawn towards an attractor of lower dimension than the full phase space. A tornado is a classic example of a dissipative structure maintained by flows of energy and matter.

Thermodynamic dissipative structures

The term dissipative structure was coined by Ilya Prigogine, who received the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work on such systems. Prigogine showed that far-from-equilibrium open systems can spontaneously develop ordered structures — such as Bénard convection cells or the oscillating Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction — sustained by the dissipation of energy. This self-organisation contrasts with equilibrium structures (like crystals), which need no energy flow to persist.

Dissipative dynamical systems

In the mathematical sense, a system is dissipative if there exists a bounded set that all trajectories eventually enter and never leave. Friction in a pendulum is a simple example: energy is continually lost, volumes in phase space shrink, and the motion settles onto a fixed-point attractor. Chaotic dissipative systems, such as the Lorenz system, settle onto strange attractors of fractal dimension.

Examples

  • Living organisms, which maintain internal order by exporting entropy.
  • Hurricanes and convection cells.
  • Lasers, once the pump threshold is exceeded.
  • Chemical oscillators such as the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction.
