Plate girder bridge

A through plate girder railway bridge; the deep plate girders carry the track between them.

A plate girder bridge is a bridge supported by two or more plate girders — deep beams built up from steel plates joined by riveting, bolting or welding, typically with an I-beam cross-section formed from separate top and bottom flange plates and a vertical web plate. Plate girders can carry heavier loads over longer spans than rolled steel beams of standard size, and are commonly used for railway bridges and highway overpasses.

Design

The flanges resist bending (compression in the top flange, tension in the bottom flange over a simple span) while the web resists shear. Vertical stiffeners are welded or riveted to the web to prevent buckling, and cross-bracing connects the girders. Girder depth is often around one fifteenth to one twenty-fifth of the span. In a deck configuration the roadway or track sits on top of the girders; in a through configuration it passes between them, which reduces the construction depth beneath the deck — useful where clearance below is limited, as at railway underpasses.

History and use

Plate girder construction developed in the nineteenth century with wrought iron; Robert Stephenson's Britannia Bridge (1850) used related box-girder principles. Welded plate girders became standard for medium-span bridges of roughly 15 to 100 metres. For longer spans, box girders or trusses are usually more economical. Many older plate girder bridges remain in service on rail networks worldwide, and modern replacements in weathering steel or composite steel-concrete construction follow the same form.
