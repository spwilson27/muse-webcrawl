Paper machine

A paper machine is a large industrial machine that produces paper in a continuous process from pulp stock. The most common design is the Fourdrinier machine, patented in the early nineteenth century and named after the stationers who financed its development from Louis-Nicolas Robert's 1799 invention. Modern machines may be more than 150 metres long, produce a sheet over 10 metres wide, and run at speeds above 1,500 metres per minute.

Sections

  • Wet end / forming section: a dilute suspension of fibres in water (the stock) flows from the headbox onto a moving mesh wire, where most of the water drains away, leaving a wet fibre web.
  • Press section: the web passes between rollers and felts that squeeze out further water and consolidate the sheet.
  • Dryer section: the sheet travels over a long series of steam-heated cylinders that evaporate the remaining water.
  • Finishing: the paper may be size-pressed and calendered between hard rollers for smoothness, then wound onto a large reel at the dry end.

Variations

Cylinder (vat) machines form paper on rotating wire-covered cylinders and are used for board and specialty grades; twin-wire formers sandwich the stock between two wires for better formation; and tissue, paperboard and newsprint machines vary the configuration along the same principles.
