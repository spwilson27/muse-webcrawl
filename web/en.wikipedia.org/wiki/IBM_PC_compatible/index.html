IBM PC compatible

Computers compatible with the original IBM PC architecture

An early PC compatible ("clone") computer

IBM PC compatible computers are similar to the original IBM Personal Computer, launched in 1981, and able to use the same software and expansion cards. Such computers were referred to as PC clones or IBM clones, and the term remains in use informally for the ubiquitous x86-based personal computers descended from that architecture.

Origins

Because IBM built the PC largely from off-the-shelf components and published technical references, other manufacturers could reverse-engineer a clean-room BIOS — Compaq famously did so for its Portable in 1982 — and produce compatible machines that ran MS-DOS software. Companies such as Compaq, Dell, HP and many others built large businesses on compatibles, and IBM gradually lost control of the platform it had created.

Evolution

Over time "IBM PC compatible" gave way to looser phrases such as "Wintel" (Windows on Intel CPUs), though modern PCs increasingly use AMD processors and non-Windows operating systems. The architecture's legacy includes the x86 instruction set, UEFI firmware replacing the BIOS, and standard expansion buses such as PCIe.
