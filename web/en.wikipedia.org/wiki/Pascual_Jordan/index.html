Ernst Pascual Jordan (18 October 1902 – 31 July 1980) was a German theoretical and mathematical physicist who made foundational contributions to quantum mechanics and quantum field theory. Working with Max Born and Werner Heisenberg in Göttingen, he co-authored the 1925 papers that created matrix mechanics, the first complete formulation of quantum mechanics — yet his later association with the Nazi regime obscured his reputation, and he was the only one of the three not to receive the Nobel Prize for that work.

Quantum mechanics

Jordan earned his doctorate at Göttingen in 1924 and became Max Born's assistant. In 1925 he and Born published the paper that systematically formulated Heisenberg's ideas in matrix form, and soon after the three men produced the famous "three-man paper" (Dreimännerarbeit) completing matrix mechanics. With Born, Jordan also introduced the canonical commutation relations and the probabilistic interpretation of the theory. In work with Paul Dirac and others he helped found quantum field theory: the Jordan–Pauli commutation relations for fields and the Jordan–Wigner transformation (1928), which maps spin systems to fermions, remain standard tools.

Jordan algebras

Seeking an algebraic framework for quantum observables, Jordan introduced in 1933 the structures now called Jordan algebras — commutative, non-associative algebras satisfying the Jordan identity. Though they did not displace the standard Hilbert-space formalism, Jordan algebras found lasting applications in mathematics, including in the classification of formally real Jordan algebras (Jordan–von Neumann–Wigner, 1934) and in projective geometry and quantum logic.

Politics and later work

Jordan joined the Nazi Party and its paramilitary SA in 1933 and published wartime writings attempting to reconcile Nazism with modern physics, a stance that, along with his stutter and reserved manner, damaged his postwar standing. After the war he was rehabilitated through denazification, became a professor at Hamburg (1947), and served as a conservative member of the Bundestag (1957–1961) for the CDU. His later scientific work included a scalar–tensor theory of gravity (Jordan–Brans–Dicke theory's predecessor) in which the gravitational constant varies over time, and writings on biology and parapsychology.
