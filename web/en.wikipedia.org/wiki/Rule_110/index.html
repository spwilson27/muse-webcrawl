Rule 110

Rule 110
Type: Elementary cellular automaton
Named by: Stephen Wolfram
Notable property: Turing complete (proved by Matthew Cook)

Rule 110 is an elementary cellular automaton — a one-dimensional automaton with two possible states per cell — notable for being capable of universal computation. It is named after the Wolfram code number describing its transition table.

The rule

In Rule 110, the next state of a cell depends on its current state and those of its two neighbours. The transitions for the eight possible neighbourhoods (111, 110, 101, 100, 011, 010, 001, 000) are 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0 respectively; reading these outputs as a binary number gives 01101110₂ = 110 in decimal, hence the name. Run from random initial conditions, Rule 110 produces a complex mixture of regular background patterns ("ether") and moving structures known as gliders, which interact in complicated ways — behaviour Wolfram classified as "Class IV", on the edge of chaos.

Universality

Stephen Wolfram conjectured in the 1980s–90s that Rule 110 was Turing complete. Matthew Cook, working at Wolfram Research, proved this in the mid-1990s by showing that its gliders could emulate a cyclic tag system, a known universal model of computation; the proof was published in 2004 after a legal dispute over its earlier disclosure. Rule 110 is the simplest known cellular automaton with a proof of universality, a striking demonstration that very simple rules can generate behaviour of unbounded complexity.
