The Federal Council for the Advancement of Aborigines and Torres Strait Islanders (FCAATSI), founded in 1958 as the Federal Council for Aboriginal Advancement (FCAA), was the first national coordinating body for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander rights organisations in Australia. It united state-based advancement leagues and churches, trade unions and supporters, and became the principal vehicle of the campaign that led to the landmark 1967 referendum.

Foundation

The council was formed at a conference in Adelaide in February 1958, bringing together existing state councils and leagues. Its founding platform called for the repeal of discriminatory state and federal legislation, equal pay and industrial rights, the right to own and inherit property, adequate housing, education and health services, preservation of Indigenous culture, and amendment of the Australian Constitution — which then excluded Aboriginal people from being counted in the census and barred the Commonwealth from making laws for them.

The 1967 referendum campaign

From 1958 FCAATSI circulated petitions for constitutional change, eventually gathering around 100,000 signatures. Activists such as Faith Bandler ran the national campaign, while Indigenous leaders including Joe McGinness — elected the council's first Aboriginal president in 1961 — gave the movement growing Indigenous leadership. The referendum of 27 May 1967 passed with over 90 per cent support nationally, the highest "yes" vote for any Australian referendum, empowering the Commonwealth to legislate for Aboriginal people and including them in the census count.

Later years

After 1967 FCAATSI campaigned on land rights, supporting the Gurindji strike at Wave Hill and the Yirrkala bark petitions' legacy, and pressed for a federal land-rights policy. Internal tensions over Indigenous self-determination grew after 1970, when the council adopted rules ensuring Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander control of its executive; some non-Indigenous supporters left to form other bodies. The Whitlam government channelled funding to new organisations, and FCAATSI's influence declined; it wound up in 1978. Its papers and history are an important record of the first era of national Indigenous political organisation.
