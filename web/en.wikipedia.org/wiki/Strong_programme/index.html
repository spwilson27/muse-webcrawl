The strong programme (or strong sociology) is a variety of the sociology of scientific knowledge associated particularly with David Bloor and his colleagues at the Science Studies Unit of the University of Edinburgh. It set out a programme for explaining scientific beliefs—true and false alike—by the same kinds of social causes, and became one of the most influential and controversial approaches in science and technology studies.

The four tenets

In Knowledge and Social Imagery (1976), Bloor stated that a sociology of knowledge should be:

  1. Causal — concerned with the conditions (social and otherwise) that bring about belief or states of knowledge.
  2. Impartial — with respect to truth and falsity, rationality and irrationality, success and failure; both sides of these dichotomies require explanation.
  3. Symmetrical — in its style of explanation: the same types of cause should explain both true and false beliefs.
  4. Reflexive — its patterns of explanation should in principle be applicable to sociology itself.

Background and influence

The strong programme drew on Durkheim's sociology of knowledge, Wittgenstein's philosophy of language, and the history of science of Thomas Kuhn. Together with Barry Barnes, Bloor applied the approach to topics from classification systems to mathematics (the "finitism" developed in Barnes's work). It paved the way for laboratory studies and actor–network theory, though those later approaches moved beyond its framework.

Criticism

Philosophers of science have charged the strong programme with relativism and with underplaying the role of nature and rational inference in shaping scientific belief—the "symmetry" tenet being the chief target in the so-called Science Wars of the 1990s. Defenders reply that the programme is methodological, not a denial that the world constrains belief.
