Self-loading rifle

A self-loading rifle is a rifle that uses energy from the fired cartridge — gas pressure, recoil, or blowback — to eject the spent case and chamber the next round automatically. In common usage the term overlaps with semi-automatic rifle, in which a single shot is fired for each press of the trigger; some self-loading designs are capable of fully automatic fire and are then classed as automatic rifles or, if select-fire, assault rifles.

The M1 Garand (adopted 1936) established the self-loading service rifle.

History

Workable self-loading designs appeared by the end of the nineteenth century; Ferdinand Mannlicher and others patented early mechanisms, and the Mexican Mondragón rifle of 1908 was an early military adoption. Denmark, France (with the RSC M1917), and the Soviet Union (SVT-40) fielded self-loaders, but the decisive step was the United States' adoption of the M1 Garand as its standard infantry rifle before the Second World War, giving American infantry a marked firepower advantage over bolt-action-armed opponents. After the war, designs such as the Soviet SKS, the Belgian FN FAL, the German G3, and the American M14 made self-loading rifles universal in military service.

Mechanisms

Self-loading actions include long- and short-stroke gas piston systems, direct gas impingement, recoil operation (long and short), and simple or delayed blowback (unusual in full-power rifles). Most feed from detachable box magazines of 5–30 rounds or, in earlier designs, from internal magazines loaded with clips.

Civilian use

Semi-automatic rifles are widely owned for hunting, sport shooting, and collecting; their legal status varies substantially between jurisdictions, and they feature prominently in debates over firearms regulation.
