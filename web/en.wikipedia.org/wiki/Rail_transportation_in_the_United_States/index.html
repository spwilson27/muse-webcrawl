Rail transportation in the United States

Rail transportation in the United States consists primarily of freight shipments along a well-integrated network of standard-gauge private freight railroads that also extend into Canada and Mexico. The United States has the largest rail network of any country, at around 140,000 miles (225,000 km) of track, the great majority owned by freight companies.

The freight system is dominated by large "Class I" railroads such as BNSF Railway and Union Pacific, and moves bulk commodities (coal, grain, chemicals) and intermodal containers, accounting for a large share of intercity freight ton-miles. Passenger service, by contrast, is comparatively limited: Amtrak, founded in 1971, operates intercity trains over mostly freight-owned track, with its busiest services in the Northeast Corridor, while commuter rail systems serve major metropolitan areas.

History

American railroads expanded explosively in the 19th century; the first transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869. The industry peaked in mileage around 1916, declined with the rise of automobiles and aviation, and was reshaped by deregulation under the Staggers Rail Act of 1980, which revived freight profitability.
