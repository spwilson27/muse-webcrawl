Improvised explosive device

An improvised explosive device (IED) is a bomb fabricated in an improvised manner incorporating destructive, lethal, noxious, pyrotechnic, or incendiary chemicals, designed to destroy or incapacitate personnel or vehicles. IEDs are used by criminals, insurgents, and terrorists, and became widely known during the Iraq War (2003–2011), where they were the leading cause of coalition casualties. They range from small pipe bombs to enormous vehicle-borne devices capable of destroying buildings.

Components

An IED generally comprises an explosive main charge (which may be military ordnance such as artillery shells, commercial explosives, or homemade explosives made from fertiliser and other precursors), an initiator or detonator, a power source, and a switch or trigger. Additional materials — shrapnel such as nails or ball bearings, fuel, or chemical agents — may be added to increase lethality.

Types

  • Command-initiated: detonated by an operator via wire ("command wire") or radio signal (RCIED), including mobile phones and garage-door openers.
  • Victim-operated: triggered by the target, e.g. pressure plates, tripwires, or infrared sensors (VOIEDs), functioning like mines.
  • Vehicle-borne (VBIED): a car or truck loaded with explosives, sometimes driven by a suicide attacker.
  • Person-borne: explosives carried in a vest or belt, typically in suicide attacks.
  • Explosively formed penetrator (EFP): a self-forging fragment device used in Iraq against armoured vehicles, forming a high-velocity slug capable of penetrating armour.

History of use

Improvised bombs long predate the term: examples include the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, booby traps in the Second World War, extensive Viet Cong devices in Vietnam, and the Provisional IRA's sophisticated campaign during the Troubles in Northern Ireland. The acronym "IED" entered widespread usage during the Iraq War, where roadside bombs, often built from 155 mm artillery shells, caused a majority of coalition combat deaths at several points. In Afghanistan, fertiliser-based devices became the signature weapon of the Taliban insurgency, prompting restrictions on ammonium nitrate fertiliser.

Countermeasures

Responses include armoured vehicles designed to deflect blasts (the MRAP programme), electronic jammers against radio-controlled devices (CREW systems), route-clearance patrols with ground-penetrating radar, explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) teams and robots, surveillance, and intelligence efforts against bomb-making networks — coordinated in the United States by the Joint IED Defeat Organization (JIEDDO), established in 2006. Bomb disposal by hand, pioneered at great cost in Northern Ireland and Iraq, remains among the most dangerous military occupations.
