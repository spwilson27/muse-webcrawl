Color vision

Color vision (also colour vision) is the ability of an organism to distinguish objects based on the wavelengths of the light they reflect, emit or transmit. It arises when the visual system compares signals from photoreceptors with different spectral sensitivities.

The visible spectrum

ReceptorsCone cells (S, M, L in humans)
Human typeTrichromacy
DeficiencyColour blindness

Mechanism

Human retinas contain three cone types sensitive to short (blue), medium (green) and long (red) wavelengths. The Young–Helmholtz trichromatic theory explains colour matching, while Hering's opponent-process theory describes the red–green, blue–yellow and black–white opponent channels formed in retinal and brain circuitry; modern theory combines both stages. The brain also achieves colour constancy, discounting changes in illumination.

Across animals

Most mammals are dichromats; Old World primates evolved trichromacy, aiding detection of ripe fruit and young leaves. Many birds, reptiles and insects are tetrachromats, some seeing ultraviolet; mantis shrimps have a dozen or more spectral channels. Defects in human cone pigments cause red–green colour blindness, affecting about 8% of men.
