Czochralski method

Year1916
Used forSingle-crystal silicon, germanium, gallium arsenide

The Czochralski method, also known as the Czochralski process or crystal pulling, is a method of crystal growth used to obtain single crystals of semiconductors (such as silicon, germanium and gallium arsenide), metals, salts and synthetic gemstones. It is the dominant commercial method for producing the monocrystalline silicon from which silicon wafers for the electronics industry are cut.

History

The method is named after the Polish scientist Jan Czochralski, who invented it in 1916 while investigating the crystallisation rates of metals. According to the well-known account, he discovered the effect accidentally when he dipped his pen into a crucible of molten tin rather than his inkwell and drew out a thin thread of solid metal that proved to be a single crystal.

Process

High-purity polycrystalline semiconductor material is melted in a crucible (usually quartz for silicon) at temperatures just above the melting point — 1,425 °C for silicon. A small seed crystal, mounted on a rotating rod, is lowered until it just touches the surface of the melt. The rod is then slowly withdrawn upward while rotating, pulling a cylindrical single-crystal ingot (boule) from the melt. By precisely controlling the temperature gradient, pulling rate and rotation speeds, ingots of large diameter — 300 mm is standard in modern production, with 450 mm developed — can be grown.

Dopant impurities may be added to the melt to give the crystal the desired electrical properties, such as boron for p-type or phosphorus for n-type silicon, although uniform doping is complicated by the segregation of impurities between the solid and the melt.

Applications and limitations

Czochralski silicon accounts for the great majority of the world's monocrystalline silicon production for integrated circuits, as well as material for solar cells. The process introduces some oxygen contamination from the quartz crucible, which can be beneficial for wafer strength but limits resistivity; for the highest-purity material, the float-zone process is used instead. The method is also used to grow crystals of gallium arsenide (in the related liquid-encapsulated variant), sapphire and various laser and gemstone crystals.
