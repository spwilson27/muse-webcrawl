Enlightenment in Spain

The Enlightenment in Spain (Spanish: Ilustración española) was the intellectual and reform movement that developed in Spain during the eighteenth century, under the Bourbon monarchs who succeeded the Habsburgs after the War of the Spanish Succession. It combined ideas arriving from France and the wider European Enlightenment with a homegrown tradition of reformist critique of Spain's economic and institutional decline.

Reform from above

Unlike in France, the Spanish Enlightenment was largely sponsored by the Crown itself, above all under Ferdinand VI and Charles III (reigned 1759–1788). Ministers such as the Count of Floridablanca, the Count of Aranda, Pedro Rodríguez de Campomanes and Gaspar Melchor de Jovellanos promoted administrative centralisation, agricultural and commercial reform, public works and education. The expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767 removed a powerful conservative influence and freed resources for state-directed reform.

Institutions and ideas

Economic Societies of Friends of the Country (Sociedades Económicas de Amigos del País), founded across Spain and its empire, diffused useful knowledge in agriculture, industry and science. Thinkers such as Benito Jerónimo Feijóo attacked superstition and defended experimental science; Jovellanos's Report on the Agrarian Law (1795) criticised entailed estates and guilds. The Royal Botanical Garden and great scientific expeditions — including Malaspina's around-the-world voyage — marked the era's scientific ambition.

Limits and legacy

Reformism coexisted with the Inquisition (abolished only in the 1830s) and censorship, and the shock of the French Revolution pushed Charles IV's Spain toward reaction. Even so, ilustrado reformers shaped the liberal movements of the nineteenth century, in Spain and in Spanish America.
