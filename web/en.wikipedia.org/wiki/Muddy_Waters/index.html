McKinley Morganfield (April 4, 1913 – April 30, 1983), known professionally as Muddy Waters, was an American blues singer and musician who is often cited as the "father of modern Chicago blues". Raised on the Stovall Plantation near Clarksdale, Mississippi, he carried the Delta blues north in the Great Migration and electrified it, in the process laying the foundations of rock and roll.

Early life and the Delta

Raised by his grandmother Della Grant after his mother's death, Morganfield was nicknamed "Muddy" for playing in the mud and "Waters" later. He learned harmonica and then guitar, imitating Son House and Robert Johnson, and in 1941 was recorded on Stovall Plantation by Alan Lomax for the Library of Congress. In 1943 he moved to Chicago, where he switched to electric guitar to be heard over noisy clubs.

Chicago and Chess Records

After early successes for Aristocrat Records, Waters became the cornerstone of Chess Records. His 1950s band — with Little Walter on harmonica, Jimmy Rogers, Otis Spann and Willie Dixon as songwriter and bassist — produced defining records including "Rollin' Stone", "Hoochie Coochie Man", "Mannish Boy", "Got My Mojo Working" and "Rollin' and Tumblin'". His amplified band format of guitar, harmonica, piano, bass and drums became the template of the blues-rock band.

Later career and influence

Waters's triumphant performance at the 1960 Newport Jazz Festival introduced him to white audiences; British bands such as the Rolling Stones — who took their name from his "Rollin' Stone" — broadcast his influence worldwide. After a lull he returned with the Johnny Winter-produced Grammy-winning albums Hard Again (1977), I'm Ready and King Bee. He won six Grammy Awards overall and was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1987. His songs have been covered by Led Zeppelin, Eric Clapton, the Rolling Stones and countless others; Rolling Stone ranked him among the greatest guitarists and artists of all time.
