Kabah

Kabah (also spelled Kabaah, Kabát, or in older literature Kahbah) is a Maya archaeological site in the Puuc region of Yucatán, Mexico, located south of Mérida on Highway 261 near the town of Santa Elena. It is connected to the larger city of Uxmal, about 18 km to the northwest, by an 18-km raised causeway (sacbé) terminating in a monumental free-standing arch. Kabah is, after Uxmal, the second-largest ruin in the Puuc region.

History

The area was occupied by the mid-Preclassic period, but most standing architecture dates to the Terminal Classic (roughly 7th–11th centuries CE), when Kabah was a flourishing regional centre linked to Uxmal. The site was documented in the 1840s by John Lloyd Stephens and Frederick Catherwood, whose engravings of its ornamented palace introduced it to a wide audience. The name is usually translated from Yucatec Maya as "strong hand" or "the hand that sculpts."

Architecture

Kabah's best-known building is the Palace of the Masks (Codz Poop, "rolled-up mat"), a 46-metre-long structure whose western façade is covered with nearly 300 nearly identical stone masks of the long-nosed rain god Chaac, stacked in a repeating pattern three rows high — among the most exuberant examples of Puuc mosaic stonework. Other structures include the Great Pyramid, the temple platforms of the eastern plaza, and the great triumphal arch (El Arco) at the Uxmal end of the sacbé, a simple vaulted gateway on a low platform that marks the ceremonial entrance to the city.
