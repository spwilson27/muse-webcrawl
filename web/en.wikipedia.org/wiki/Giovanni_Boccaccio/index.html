Giovanni Boccaccio (16 June 1313 – 21 December 1375) was an Italian writer, poet, correspondent of Petrarch, and an important Renaissance humanist. He is best known for The Decameron, a collection of 100 tales told by ten young people fleeing plague-stricken Florence. Together with Petrarch and Dante, he is considered one of the "three crowns" (tre corone) of Italian literature.

Life

Boccaccio was born in Tuscany, possibly the illegitimate son of a Florentine merchant, and spent his youth in Naples at the court of King Robert of Anjou, where he fell in love with a lady he celebrated as "Fiammetta". Recalled to Florence around 1340, he witnessed the Black Death of 1348, which forms the frame of The Decameron (written c. 1348–1353). He later served Florence as a diplomat and envoy, and became a close friend and disciple of Petrarch.

Works

Besides The Decameron, his works include the romances Filocolo and Filostrato (a source for Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde), the Teseida, the pastoral Ameto, and in Latin the encyclopaedic Genealogia deorum gentilium, De casibus virorum illustrium, and De mulieribus claris (On Famous Women), a pioneering collection of biographies of women.

Later years

In his final years Boccaccio lectured publicly on Dante in Florence (1373) and began a commentary on the Divine Comedy. He died at Certaldo on 21 December 1375.
