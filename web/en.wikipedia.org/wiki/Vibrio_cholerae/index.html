Vibrio cholerae

DomainBacteria
FamilyVibrionaceae
ShapeComma-shaped rod, single polar flagellum
DiseaseCholera

Vibrio cholerae is a Gram-negative, comma-shaped bacterium, some strains of which cause the disease cholera in humans. It is a facultative anaerobe with a single polar flagellum, naturally inhabiting brackish and estuarine waters, often in association with copepods and other zooplankton.

Pathogenicity

Pathogenic strains — chiefly of serogroups O1 and O139 — carry the genes for cholera toxin, encoded by a filamentous bacteriophage (CTXφ), and for the toxin-coregulated pilus used to colonise the small intestine. Cholera toxin raises cyclic AMP in intestinal epithelial cells, driving massive secretion of water and electrolytes and producing the profuse watery diarrhoea ("rice-water stool") that can kill by dehydration within hours if untreated. The O1 serogroup is divided into the classical and El Tor biotypes; El Tor has driven the seventh cholera pandemic since 1961, while O139 emerged in 1992 in the Bay of Bengal region.

Discovery and control

The bacterium was described by Filippo Pacini in 1854 and isolated in pure culture by Robert Koch in 1884. Cholera is transmitted mainly through contaminated drinking water and food, so control rests on safe water, sanitation, oral rehydration therapy, antibiotics in severe cases, and oral cholera vaccines.
