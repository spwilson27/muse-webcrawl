British sitcom

A British sitcom or a Britcom is a situation comedy produced in the United Kingdom. British sitcoms typically run for short series of six episodes — a contrast with the long American seasons — and are usually written by one or two writers rather than a large writing room, giving them a distinctive authorial voice and frequently a bleaker, more character-driven humour.

History

The genre grew out of radio comedy such as Hancock's Half Hour (1954), which transferred to television with writers Ray Galton and Alan Simpson. The 1960s and 1970s produced enduring classics including Steptoe and Son, Dad's Army, Porridge, Fawlty Towers (1975–79) — frequently voted the greatest British sitcom of all time — and The Good Life. Alternative comedy reshaped the form in the 1980s with The Young Ones and Blackadder.

Modern era

From the 1990s, single-camera series without laughter tracks — The Royle Family, The Office (2001), Peep Show and Fleabag — redefined the Britcom, emphasising cringe comedy, realism and pathos. Many British sitcoms have been remade for American television, most famously The Office and Steptoe and Son (as Sanford and Son), with varying success.

Characteristics

Recurring themes include class, snobbery, frustrated ambition and the comic trap of a static situation: Basil Fawlty's hotel, Del Boy's market stall or David Brent's office. Failure, rather than success, is usually the permanent condition of the Britcom protagonist.
