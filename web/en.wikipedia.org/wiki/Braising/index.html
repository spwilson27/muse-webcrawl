Braising

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A braised beef dish in a heavy lidded pot, with carrots and onions]

Braising is a combination cooking method that uses both dry and moist heat: food, usually tough cuts of meat, is first seared at high temperature to brown it, then finished in a covered pot with a modest amount of liquid at low temperature for a long time. The slow, gentle cooking dissolves collagen into gelatin, turning chewy cuts fork-tender while the braising liquid becomes a rich sauce. Classic examples include French boeuf bourguignon and coq au vin, Italian osso buco, German sauerbraten and Chinese red-braised pork.

Braising resembles stewing, but uses less liquid — typically one-third to halfway up the food rather than covering it — and the food is usually in larger pieces. The French term braiser derives from braise, live coals, because the pot was traditionally heated above and below.

Method

The meat is seasoned, sometimes dredged in flour, and browned well in fat. Aromatics such as onion, carrot and celery are softened in the same pot, the pan deglazed with wine or stock, and the meat returned with liquid, herbs and a tight lid. Cooking proceeds in a low oven (around 150–160 °C) or at the barest simmer for one to several hours. The sauce may be strained and reduced at the end.

Suitable foods

Braising suits collagen-rich cuts — beef chuck and shin, pork shoulder, lamb shanks, oxtail, chicken thighs — and firm vegetables such as cabbage, fennel, leeks and endives. It is economical, largely unattended, and the flavour of braised dishes often improves when reheated the next day.

Famous braised dishes

Alongside boeuf bourguignon, famous braises include pot roast, brisket, Japanese kakuni, Korean galbi-jjim, and pulled pork (slow-cooked and shredded, sometimes finished on a barbecue).
