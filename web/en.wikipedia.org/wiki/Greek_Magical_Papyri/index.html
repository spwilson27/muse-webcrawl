Greek Magical Papyri

A fragment of a magical papyrus from Roman Egypt

The Greek Magical Papyri (Latin: Papyri Graecae Magicae, abbreviated PGM) are a body of papyri from Graeco-Roman Egypt, written primarily in Ancient Greek (with some texts in Demotic Egyptian and Coptic), which preserve a collection of spells, hymns, rituals and magical recipes. The manuscripts date mainly from the second century BCE to the fifth century CE, with the bulk belonging to the Roman imperial period.

Discovery and publication

Most of the papyri came to light in the nineteenth century, many apparently deriving from a single ancient library or archive found near Thebes and dispersed among European collections through the antiquities trade; the collector Giovanni Anastasi acquired many of the most important pieces. The corpus was edited by Karl Preisendanz in the 1920s and 1930s, and an English translation of the collection was published in 1986 under the editorship of Hans Dieter Betz.

Contents

The papyri contain spells for purposes including love magic, divination, healing, protection, cursing and the summoning of gods and daimones. Their religious outlook is strikingly syncretic: Greek deities such as Hermes, Hecate and Apollo appear alongside Egyptian gods, Jewish divine names such as Iao and Sabaoth, and elements of early Christian language. Characteristic features include long strings of voces magicae — apparently meaningless but powerful words and letter sequences — palindromes, vowel series, and elaborate ritual instructions involving amulets, figurines and offerings.

Significance

The papyri are a principal source for the study of religion and magic in the ancient Mediterranean world. They include texts such as the "Mithras Liturgy", an ascent ritual of disputed connection to Mithraism, and lengthy handbooks such as the Great Magical Papyrus of Paris. Scholars use them to illuminate popular religious practice, the interaction of Greek, Egyptian and Jewish traditions, and the background of early Christianity and Gnosticism.
