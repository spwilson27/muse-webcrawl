Plate girder

A plate girder railway bridge

A plate girder is a deep steel beam built up from separate plates – a vertical web plate and horizontal flange plates – joined by riveting, bolting, or, in modern practice, welding. Because the depth and thickness of each element can be chosen freely, plate girders can be made much deeper and stronger than rolled I-beams of standard size.

Design

In bending, the flanges carry most of the tensile and compressive forces while the web resists shear. Thin webs are liable to buckle, so vertical stiffeners are commonly added at intervals, with heavier bearing stiffeners over supports. Plate girders may be constant in depth, haunched over piers, or curved in profile to follow the bending-moment diagram.

Uses

Plate girders are widely used in bridges – typically in pairs under a deck, or as the main girders of half-through railway bridges – and in buildings for long-span transfer beams and crane girders. Early examples, such as Robert Stephenson's Britannia Bridge (1850), used riveted wrought-iron box and plate construction; welded steel girders became standard in the twentieth century.
