Laïcité

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Laïcité is the French principle of state secularism: the strict separation of churches and state and the neutrality of the state and its agents in religious matters, with freedom of conscience guaranteed. Its legal foundation is the Law of 9 December 1905 on the Separation of the Churches and the State, passed under the Third Republic after decades of conflict between republican governments and the Catholic Church (including the Dreyfus affair and the expulsion of religious congregations); laïcité was constitutionalised in 1946 and 1958 (Article 1 of the Fifth Republic's constitution).

In practice laïcité governs religious dress and symbols for public employees and in public schools (the 2004 law banning conspicuous religious symbols in schools; the 2010 ban on full-face coverings in public spaces, upheld by the European Court of Human Rights in S.A.S. v. France, 2014), religious instruction (absent from public schools except in Alsace-Moselle, which retains the Napoleonic Concordat), and public funding of worship (the state maintains pre-1905 church buildings but does not subsidise religions). Its meaning is contested: defenders see the condition of equal citizenship and emancipation (especially of women); critics, including many Anglo-American liberals, see illiberal restriction falling disproportionately on Muslims; the debate intensified after the terrorist attacks of 2015–16 and the murder of the teacher Samuel Paty (2020).
