Non-Mendelian inheritance


Labelled placeholder: inheritance pattern diagram

Non-Mendelian inheritance is any pattern in which traits do not segregate in accordance with Mendel's laws. These laws describe the inheritance of traits linked to single genes on chromosomes in the nucleus. In Mendelian inheritance, each parent contributes one of two possible alleles for a trait. If the genotypes of both parents in a genetic cross are known, Mendel's laws can be used to determine the distribution of phenotypes expected for the population of offspring. There are several situations in which the proportions of phenotypes observed in the progeny do not match the predicted values.

Non-Mendelian inheritance plays a role in several disease processes.

Types

Extranuclear inheritance

Extranuclear inheritance, also known as cytoplasmic inheritance, is the transmission of genes that occur outside the nucleus. It is found in most eukaryotes and is commonly known to occur in cytoplasmic organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts, or from cellular parasites like viruses or bacteria. Mitochondrial DNA is transmitted almost exclusively from the mother, so mitochondrial disorders show maternal inheritance.

Gene conversion

Gene conversion can be one of the major forms of non-Mendelian inheritance. Gene conversion arises during DNA repair, when damaged DNA is repaired using a homologous sequence as a template, potentially replacing one allele with another and skewing segregation ratios.

Infectious heredity

Another form of non-Mendelian inheritance is known as infectious heredity. Infectious particles such as viruses can infect host cells and continue to reside in the cytoplasm of these cells; if the presence of these particles results in a new phenotype, this phenotype may then be passed on to progeny.

Genomic imprinting

Genomic imprinting is a type of non-Mendelian inheritance that involves DNA methylation. The methylation changes the activity of a DNA segment without changing the sequence, causing some genes to be expressed only when inherited from the mother or only from the father. Examples include Prader–Willi syndrome and Angelman syndrome, associated with imprinting at chromosome 15q11–13.

Mosaicism

Mosaicism denotes the presence of two or more populations of cells with different genotypes in one individual who has developed from a single fertilized egg, resulting from mitotic errors or mutations during development.

Trinucleotide repeat disorders

Trinucleotide repeat disorders, also known as microsatellite diseases, display non-Mendelian inheritance through anticipation: repeats expand across generations, causing earlier onset and greater severity. Examples include Huntington's disease, fragile X syndrome, and myotonic dystrophy.
