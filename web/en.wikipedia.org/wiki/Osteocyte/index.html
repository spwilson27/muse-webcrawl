Osteocyte

An osteocyte is a type of bone cell: a mature osteoblast that has become embedded in the mineralized bone matrix it secreted. Osteocytes are the most abundant cells in mature bone tissue — outnumbering osteoblasts and osteoclasts many times over — and can survive for decades, as long as the bone containing them persists.

Structure

Each osteocyte sits in a small cavity called a lacuna and extends numerous slender cytoplasmic processes through tiny channels, the canaliculi, which connect neighbouring osteocytes by gap junctions and allow exchange of nutrients, signalling molecules and waste. The osteocyte cell body contains a reduced complement of organelles compared with secretory osteoblasts, reflecting its shift from matrix synthesis to maintenance and sensing.

Function

Osteocytes act as the skeleton's mechanosensors: fluid flow through the lacuno-canalicular network in response to mechanical load is detected by the cells, which then orchestrate bone remodelling by signalling to osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Key osteocyte secretions include sclerostin (encoded by the SOST gene), which inhibits bone formation via the Wnt pathway, and RANKL, which promotes osteoclast formation; osteocytes are now regarded as the major source of RANKL in adult bone. Osteocytes also produce FGF23, a hormone regulating phosphate metabolism. When osteocytes die — for example with ageing, microdamage or estrogen withdrawal — the surrounding bone is targeted for removal and renewal.

Development

Osteocytes form when osteoblasts are entrapped in their own matrix during bone deposition, passing through transitional "osteoid osteocyte" stages while reducing their volume and extending dendritic processes before mineralization completes.
