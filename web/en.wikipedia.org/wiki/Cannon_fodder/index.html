Cannon fodder

Cannon fodder is an informal, derogatory term for combatants — typically infantry soldiers — who are regarded by their commanders or by commentators as expendable in the face of enemy fire. The phrase likens soldiers to fodder, food for livestock: material to be consumed by the enemy's cannon. It is frequently used to criticize tactics or strategies that treat soldiers' lives as cheap, and has been extended metaphorically to any people used as expendable resources.

Origin

The concept predates the phrase: in Shakespeare's Henry IV, Part 1 (c. 1597), Falstaff calls his ragged recruits "food for powder" who will "fill a pit as well as better". The French soldier François-René de Chateaubriand is credited with the formulation chair à canon ("flesh for cannon") in an 1814 anti-Napoleonic pamphlet, and the German equivalent Kanonenfutter was in use by the early 19th century; the English term appears in print in the following decades.

Usage

The term became especially associated with the First World War, when mass conscript armies and attritional tactics on the Western Front produced enormous casualties, and it has remained a staple of anti-war rhetoric. It was applied again to mass-infantry assaults in later conflicts, including human-wave attacks in the Korean War and the use of poorly trained recruits in the Iran–Iraq War and the Russo-Ukrainian War.

Beyond war, "cannon fodder" is used figuratively for junior employees, interns, or political foot-soldiers sent into situations where they are expected to absorb losses or blame.

Related concepts

Related notions include attrition warfare, forlorn hope, and penal battalions — units composed of convicts or disciplined soldiers assigned to especially dangerous duties, a practice associated with the Soviet shtrafbat units of the Second World War.
