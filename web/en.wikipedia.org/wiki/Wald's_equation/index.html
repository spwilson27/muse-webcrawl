Wald's equation

Abraham Wald (1902–1950)
FieldProbability theory
Named afterAbraham Wald
ConceptsRandom sum; stopping time; expected value

In probability theory, Wald's equation (also called Wald's identity or Wald's lemma) is a fundamental result giving the expected value of a sum of a random number of independent and identically distributed random variables. It is named after the mathematician Abraham Wald, who derived it in the course of developing sequential analysis during the 1940s.

Statement

Let X1, X2, … be a sequence of independent, identically distributed random variables with finite expectation, and let N be a non-negative integer-valued random variable satisfying appropriate conditions — classically, that N is independent of the future increments, or more precisely that N is a stopping time with finite expectation with respect to the sequence. Then the expected value of the random sum is the product of the expected term count and the expected summand:

E[X1 + ⋯ + XN] = E[N] · E[X1]

In the simplest version, N is independent of the X's; the stopping-time version is the one used most often in applications, including sequential statistical testing, queueing theory, branching processes, and insurance risk theory.

Example

If a fair die is rolled a random number of times N (chosen independently of the rolls, with E[N] = 10), Wald's equation gives the expected sum of the outcomes as E[N] × 3.5 = 35, without needing the full distribution of the sum. More consequentially, in Wald's sequential probability ratio test the equation underlies approximations for expected sample sizes; in renewal theory it yields the expected total cost over a random number of renewal cycles.

Conditions and generalisations

The equation fails if the conditions are dropped: if the number of terms depends on the values of the summands (for example, "stop the first time the running sum exceeds a threshold" in a way not captured by the stopping-time setup, or if E[N] is infinite), the product formula need not hold. Extensions include a second-moment version (related to Blackwell–Girshick identity), versions for martingales, and Wald's equation for processes with dependent increments.
