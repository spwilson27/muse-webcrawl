Procaine

Procaine is a local anesthetic drug of the amino ester group. It is most commonly used in dental procedures to numb the area around a tooth and also in some other minor procedures. It is the prototypical ester-type local anesthetic and was the first synthetic agent of its kind to replace cocaine in medicine.

History

Procaine was first synthesized in 1905 by the German chemist Alfred Einhorn, who gave it the trade name Novocaine (from Latin novus, "new", and -caine, a common suffix for alkaloids used as anesthetics). It was introduced into medical practice by the surgeon Heinrich Braun, who improved it by combining it with epinephrine to prolong its effect. For the first half of the twentieth century procaine was the standard injectable local anesthetic, but it was largely supplanted by amide agents such as lidocaine from the 1940s onward because of its slower onset, shorter duration, and relatively high rate of allergic reactions.

Pharmacology

Like other local anesthetics, procaine acts by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve membranes, preventing the initiation and propagation of action potentials. It is rapidly hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase) to para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), which is responsible for most allergic reactions to ester anesthetics and can antagonise sulfonamide antibiotics. Procaine is also a weak vasodilator, so it is frequently combined with a vasoconstrictor such as epinephrine to slow its absorption and extend its duration of action.
