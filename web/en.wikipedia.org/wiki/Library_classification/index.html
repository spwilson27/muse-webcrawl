Library classification

A library classification is a system of organisation of knowledge by which library resources are arranged and sorted. Library classifications use a notational system that represents the order of topics in the classification and allows items to be stored in that order. A library classification system groups related materials together, typically arranged as a hierarchical tree structure. A different kind of classification system, called a faceted classification, uses an organised set of categories which are combined by the classifier.

Purpose

Classification provides a systematic arrangement of books and other materials on shelves or in catalogues so that users can find works by subject and browse neighbouring items on related topics. The notation assigned to each item forms part of its call number, which fixes its shelf location.

Major systems

Dewey Decimal Classification

Devised by Melvil Dewey in 1876, the Dewey Decimal Classification divides knowledge into ten main classes (000–900), each subdivided decimally. It is the most widely used classification in the world, employed by libraries in more than 130 countries, especially public and school libraries.

Library of Congress Classification

Developed by the Library of Congress beginning in the late nineteenth century, this system uses letters of the alphabet for twenty-one main classes, expanded to meet the needs of large research collections; it predominates in academic libraries in North America.

Other systems

Other notable schemes include the Universal Decimal Classification, the Colon Classification of S. R. Ranganathan (a pioneering faceted system), the Bliss Bibliographic Classification, and various national and subject-specific schemes such as the National Library of Medicine classification.

History

Systematic arrangement of books dates to antiquity, but modern classification emerged in the nineteenth century as library collections grew and open-shelf access spread. The development of enumerative and faceted schemes in the twentieth century reflected continuing debate over how knowledge should be organised and how classification should adapt to new subjects.
