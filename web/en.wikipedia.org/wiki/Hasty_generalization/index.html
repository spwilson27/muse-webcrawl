Hasty generalization

TypeInformal fallacy
Other namesFaulty generalization; jumping to conclusions; secundum quid (related)
FormDrawing a conclusion about a population from a sample that is too small or unrepresentative
OppositeSlothful induction (ignoring a large, strong sample)

A hasty generalization is an informal fallacy in which a conclusion about an entire group or category is drawn from evidence that is insufficient — typically a sample that is too small, biased, or otherwise unrepresentative. It is one of the most common errors in everyday reasoning and is closely related to stereotyping, the anecdotal fallacy and biased sampling.

Structure

The fallacy has the general form:

  • Sample S is drawn from population P.
  • Some (or all) of S has property A.
  • Therefore, all (or most) of P has property A — even though S is too small or skewed to support the inference.

For example: "My two neighbours from that country were rude, so people from that country are rude." The sample (two people, encountered in one context) cannot support a claim about millions.

Related concepts

Hasty generalization underlies the anecdotal fallacy (using a personal story as proof), cherry picking (selecting only confirming cases) and many stereotypes. Statistically, it amounts to ignoring sampling error: small samples vary wildly, so generalizing from them is unreliable even when the sample is honestly drawn. The law of small numbers — the cognitive tendency to treat small samples as representative — is the psychological mechanism often blamed.

Countering the fallacy

The standard remedy is to demand an adequate, representative sample and a margin of error before accepting a population-level claim, and to treat individual cases as hypotheses to be tested rather than as evidence. Not every generalization from limited data is fallacious: where a mechanism is understood (as in much of medicine and physics), a small number of cases can be strong evidence; the fallacy lies in generalizing where no such warrant exists.
