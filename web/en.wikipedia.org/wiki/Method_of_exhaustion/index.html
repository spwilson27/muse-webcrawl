The method of exhaustion (Latin: methodus exauriendi) is a method of finding the area of a shape by inscribing inside it a sequence of polygons whose areas converge to the area of the containing shape. If the sequence is correctly constructed, the difference in area between the n-th polygon and the containing shape will become arbitrarily small as n increases. As this difference becomes arbitrarily small, the possible values for the area of the shape are systematically "exhausted" by the lower bound areas successively established by the sequence members.

History

The method originated in the 5th century BC with Antiphon the Sophist, who reasoned that a polygon inscribed with enough sides would become indistinguishable from the circle. Eudoxus of Cnidus (c. 370 BC) gave the method its rigorous form, based on a lemma (now the Archimedean property) avoiding actual infinitesimals: any magnitude can be made smaller than any given magnitude by repeatedly halving it.

Euclid used the method in Book XII of the Elements, proving, for example, that circles are to one another as the squares on their diameters. Archimedes made the most powerful use of it, computing bounds for π by inscribing and circumscribing polygons of 96 sides, and finding the areas and volumes of parabolas, spheres and cones.

Application

Proofs by exhaustion are typically two-sided: a double proof by contradiction. To show a figure has area A, one shows that assuming an inscribed figure of area greater than A and one of area less than A both lead to contradictions. The method was the principal rigorous tool for such problems until the development of integral calculus in the 17th century, and it is regarded as a forerunner of the limit concept.
