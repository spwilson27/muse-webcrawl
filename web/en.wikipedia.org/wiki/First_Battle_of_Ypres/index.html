First Battle of Ypres

First Battle of Ypres
Date: 19 October – 22 November 1914
Location: Ypres, Belgium
Result: Allied victory
Belligerents: British Empire, France, Belgium — against the German Empire
Strength: c. 5.4 million men engaged over the battle (both sides, rotating)
Casualties: German c. 130,000; French c. 50,000–85,000; British c. 58,000; Belgian c. 21,000

The First Battle of Ypres (19 October – 22 November 1914) was a battle of the First World War, fought on the Western Front around Ypres in West Flanders, Belgium. The battle was part of the First Battle of Flanders, in which German, French, Belgian and British armies fought from Arras in France to the North Sea coast of Belgium. The engagements since the "Race to the Sea" (late September – October 1914) had ended attempts at manoeuvre, and the battle's end marked the beginning of the static trench warfare that would define the Western Front for four years.

Background

After the German advance through Belgium and France was checked at the First Battle of the Marne in September 1914, both sides tried repeatedly to outflank each other northwards. The British Expeditionary Force (BEF) was moved from the Aisne to Flanders to shorten its supply lines to the Channel ports, and took position around Ypres. Germany's chief of staff, Erich von Falkenhayn, sought to break through to Dunkirk and Calais, enveloping the Allied left flank; the German Fourth and Sixth Armies, reinforced with many newly trained reserve corps of young volunteers, attacked from mid-October.

The battle

Fighting began on 19–21 October with German attacks against the Belgian army on the Yser and against the British around Langemarck, where massed infantry assaults by inexperienced reserve units against entrenched riflemen suffered appalling losses — remembered in Germany as the Kindermord bei Ypern ("massacre of the innocents"). At the end of October the German offensive nearly broke through at Gheluvelt, where the line was restored by a counter-attack of the Worcestershire Regiment on 31 October. The final great effort came on 11 November, when the Prussian Guard attacked along the Menin Road toward Ypres and was repulsed by British regulars, cooks, grooms and engineers pressed into the line. Rain, exhaustion, and ammunition shortages ended the battle by 22 November.

Aftermath

The Allies held Ypres, but the town now formed a bulging salient in the line, overlooked by German positions on the surrounding ridges — the stage for four more years of battles, including the Second Battle of Ypres (1915), where poison gas was first used on the Western Front, and the Third Battle (Passchendaele) in 1917. The old regular battalions of the BEF were largely destroyed at First Ypres; as Field Marshal Sir John French's men joked bitterly, the battle was the graveyard of the "Old Contemptibles". Ypres was almost completely destroyed by shelling, and its Cloth Hall and cathedral were rebuilt after the war.
