Magnetic cartridge

A magnetic cartridge (also called a phono cartridge or pickup) is an electromechanical transducer used in the playback of phonograph records. It rests on the record surface via a stylus and converts the mechanical vibrations produced as the stylus traces the record groove into an electrical audio signal, which is then amplified and converted into sound by a loudspeaker system. Magnetic cartridges dominate modern turntable designs because of their low tracking force, good frequency response, and low moving mass compared with the ceramic or crystal cartridges used in cheaper record players.

Operating principle

All magnetic cartridges work on the principle of electromagnetic induction: relative motion between a magnet and a coil changes the magnetic flux through the coil, inducing a voltage proportional to the velocity of the stylus movement. The stylus is attached to a cantilever, which moves in response to the groove modulation of the record. Depending on the type of cartridge, the motion is transmitted to a magnet, to a coil, or to a piece of high-permeability metal in the magnetic circuit.

Types

Moving magnet (MM)

In a moving magnet cartridge, the magnet is attached to the rear of the stylus cantilever and sits between two sets of coils mounted in the cartridge body. As the stylus tracks the groove, the magnet vibrates, inducing a signal voltage in the fixed coils. Moving magnet cartridges have relatively high output, typically 3–6 mV, and can be connected directly to the phono input of a preamplifier. Their styli are usually user-replaceable.

Moving coil (MC)

In a moving coil cartridge, the coils are attached to the cantilever and move within the field of fixed magnets. Because the coils are small and light, the moving mass is lower and the transient response is better, but the output is much lower, typically 0.2–0.5 mV for low-output types, requiring an additional amplification stage or a step-up transformer. The stylus of a moving coil cartridge is generally not user-replaceable.

Moving iron (MI)

In a moving iron design (also called variable reluctance), both the magnet and the coils are fixed; a tiny piece of iron or mu-metal attached to the cantilever moves between them, varying the reluctance of the magnetic circuit and thus the flux through the coils. Moving iron cartridges combine reasonable output with low moving mass.

Performance characteristics

Key specifications include output voltage, frequency response, channel separation, tracking force (typically 1.0–2.5 grams for hi-fi cartridges), compliance of the cantilever suspension, and stylus profile. Stylus shapes include spherical (conical), elliptical, and advanced line-contact profiles such as Shibata and micro-ridge, which trace more of the groove wall and can extract more detail with lower record wear.

History

The magnetic pickup dates to the electrical recording era of the 1920s and 1930s, when heavy magnetic pickups were used with 78 rpm records. The development of the microgroove LP after 1948 drove the design of lighter, more compliant cartridges; companies such as Shure, Ortofon, Audio-Technica, and Grado became leading manufacturers. The moving coil design gained audiophile popularity in the 1970s. Despite the dominance of digital formats after the 1980s, magnetic cartridges remain in production for vinyl playback.
