Laser hair removal

Laser hair removal is the process of removing unwanted hair by means of exposure to pulses of laser light that destroy the hair follicle. It is based on selective photothermolysis: light at a chosen wavelength is absorbed by the melanin pigment in the hair shaft, generating heat that damages the follicle while sparing the surrounding skin. The technique was experimented with for two decades before becoming commercially available and widely used in the mid-to-late 1990s; it is now one of the most commonly performed cosmetic procedures.

Procedure

Before treatment, hair in the area is trimmed and the skin cleaned; protective eyewear is worn because of the eye hazard of the beams. A handpiece delivers pulses of light to the skin, often with a cooling device or gel to protect the epidermis and reduce discomfort, which is often described as feeling like a rubber band snapping against the skin. Sessions are quick for small areas but can take an hour or more for large ones such as the back or legs.

Effectiveness

Because only follicles in the active growth (anagen) phase are susceptible, multiple sessions — typically six or more, spaced weeks apart — are needed for a large reduction. Laser treatment generally produces long-term hair reduction rather than guaranteed permanent removal of every hair. Results are best on light skin with dark coarse hair, since the contrast lets the light target the hair; very light, red, grey, or white hair responds poorly because it lacks melanin. Longer-wavelength lasers such as the Nd:YAG are safer for darker skin tones, which are at greater risk of burns and pigment changes. Common side effects include temporary redness and swelling; rare complications include burns, blistering, scarring, pigment changes, and paradoxical increased hair growth.

Technology and regulation

Common devices use alexandrite (755 nm), diode (about 800–810 nm), Nd:YAG (1064 nm), and ruby (694 nm) lasers; intense pulsed light (IPL) devices, though not true lasers, work on a similar principle. In the United States the Food and Drug Administration permits marketing claims of "permanent hair reduction" but not complete permanent removal, and regulation of who may operate the devices varies by jurisdiction.
