Nonsense verse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nonsense verse is a form of nonsense literature — usually, and often exclusively, verse. The effect of nonsense is often caused by an excess of meaning, rather than a lack of it. Nonsense writers frequently deploy a playful mix of invented words, incongruous imagery, illogical juxtapositions and strict adherence to formal structures such as metre and rhyme, so that the verse sounds meaningful while resisting ordinary interpretation.

Unlike mere gibberish, nonsense verse typically follows the grammar and rhythm of ordinary poetry closely, which creates much of its humour and charm. Many of the best-known pieces are written for children, though the tradition also includes satirical, philosophical and experimental work.

History

The roots of nonsense verse lie in folk traditions: nursery rhymes, counting-out rhymes, riddles and tall tales frequently contain playful absurdities. The genre flowered in 19th-century Britain with Edward Lear, whose A Book of Nonsense (1846) popularised the limerick and invented creatures such as the Dong with the Luminous Nose, and Lewis Carroll, whose poems "Jabberwocky" (1871) and "The Hunting of the Snark" (1876) remain the genre's most famous achievements. Carroll's technique of portmanteau words — "slithy" (lithe + slimy), "galumphing" (gallop + triumph) — was explained by Humpty Dumpty in Through the Looking-Glass.

'Twas brillig, and the slithy toves
Did gyre and gimble in the wabe:
All mimsy were the borogoves,
And the mome raths outgrabe.
— opening of "Jabberwocky" by Lewis Carroll

Practitioners

Later writers associated with nonsense verse include:

  • Edward Lear — limericks, "The Owl and the Pussy-Cat"
  • Lewis Carroll — "Jabberwocky", The Hunting of the Snark
  • Hilaire Belloc — Cautionary Tales for Children (1907)
  • Ogden Nash — whimsical light verse with deliberate misspellings and stretched rhymes
  • Spike Milligan — Silly Verse for Kids (1959)
  • Shel Silverstein — Where the Sidewalk Ends (1974)
  • Dr. Seuss — rhymed picture books with invented creatures and words
  • Christian Morgenstern — the German Galgenlieder ("Gallows Songs", 1905)

Techniques

Common devices in nonsense verse include invented vocabulary, false etymologies, absurd lists, logical non sequiturs, distortions of proverbs, and the treatment of impossible beings — such as Lear's Jumblies or Carroll's Snark — with complete narrative seriousness. Scholars have noted that nonsense verse often parodies the didactic children's poetry of its era, subverting moral lessons with delighted absurdity.
