Charles Le Brun

Charles Le Brun (baptised 24 February 1619 – 12 February 1690) was a French painter, art theorist, and interior decorator, generally regarded as the dominant figure in 17th-century French art. As first painter to Louis XIV and a founder of the Royal Academy of Painting and Sculpture, he shaped the official artistic style of the French monarchy, most famously in the decoration of the Palace of Versailles, including the Hall of Mirrors.

Career

A child prodigy, Le Brun trained under Simon Vouet and studied in Rome from 1642 to 1646, where he absorbed the classicism of Nicolas Poussin. Back in Paris he won the patronage of Cardinal Mazarin and then of Nicolas Fouquet, whose château of Vaux-le-Vicomte he decorated. After Fouquet's fall, Louis XIV took Le Brun into royal service.

Master of the arts under Louis XIV

Le Brun co-founded the Académie royale de peinture et de sculpture in 1648 and became its leading theorist; his lectures on the expression of the passions codified academic doctrine for generations. As director of the Gobelins Manufactory he supervised the production of tapestries, furniture, and objets d'art for the crown. At Versailles he directed the decoration of the state apartments and designed the ceiling of the Hall of Mirrors (begun 1678), celebrating the king's victories in thirty large compositions.

Style and legacy

Le Brun's grand, orderly classicism became synonymous with the image of Louis XIV's reign. His influence waned after the death of his patron Colbert in 1683, but his academic system long dominated French art education.
