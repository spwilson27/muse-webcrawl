Radiator (engine cooling)

Radiators are heat exchangers used for cooling internal combustion engines, mainly in automobiles but also in piston-engined aircraft, railway locomotives, motorcycles, stationary generating plant and other places where heat engines are used (water cooling).

Operation

The engine is cooled by a liquid — water mixed with antifreeze (typically ethylene glycol) — circulated by a pump through passages in the engine block and head. The hot coolant flows into the radiator, which consists of many thin tubes fitted with fins to maximise surface area. Air passing through the fins, driven by the vehicle's motion and an electric or engine-driven fan, carries the heat away; the cooled liquid then returns to the engine. A thermostat regulates flow to keep the engine at its optimum operating temperature, and a pressure cap raises the coolant's boiling point.

Construction

Early radiators were made of copper and brass; most modern car radiators have aluminium cores with plastic end tanks, which are lighter and cheaper. Configurations include down-flow and cross-flow designs. Many radiators incorporate a transmission oil cooler. In electric vehicles, radiators cool batteries, motors and power electronics at lower temperature differentials, often requiring larger frontal areas.

Failure modes

Common problems include external blockage by debris, internal scaling or corrosion from neglected coolant, leaks at seams and tanks, and fan or thermostat failures leading to overheating.
