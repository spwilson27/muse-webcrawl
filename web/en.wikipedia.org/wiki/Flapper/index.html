Flapper

Flappers were a subculture of young Western women prominent after the First World War and throughout the 1920s. They wore short skirts (with hemlines at or just below knee, which was considered short for the time), bobbed their hair, listened to jazz, and flouted social and sexual norms considered acceptable at the time.

Behaviour and image

Flappers were seen as brash for wearing excessive makeup, drinking alcohol, smoking cigarettes in public, driving automobiles, treating sex in a casual manner, and otherwise flouting social and sexual norms. As automobiles became more available, flappers gained a freedom of movement not seen before. The image is strongly associated with the "Roaring Twenties", jazz clubs, speakeasies during Prohibition in the United States, and dances such as the Charleston and the Black Bottom.

Origins and language

The exact origin of the slang word "flapper" is uncertain; it may derive from an earlier British term for a teenage girl, or from the image of a young bird flapping its wings as it learns to fly. The phenomenon grew out of the social changes of the war years, women's suffrage victories, and the liberal period in American and European cities after 1918.

End of the era

The flapper era effectively ended with the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the onset of the Great Depression, when the carefree hedonism of the 1920s became harder to sustain and styles shifted toward more conservative fashions.
