The Pitcairn Islands are a group of four volcanic islands in the remote South Pacific Ocean — Pitcairn, Henderson, Ducie, and Oeno — forming the last British Overseas Territory in the Pacific. Only Pitcairn Island is inhabited, by roughly fifty people, most descended from the mutineers of HMS Bounty and their Tahitian companions.

History

Pitcairn was sighted by Europeans in 1767 and named for midshipman Robert Pitcairn. In 1790, nine Bounty mutineers led by Fletcher Christian settled there with Tahitian men and women, burning the ship. After years of violence, the sole surviving mutineer, John Adams, led a small community rediscovered by an American sealing ship in 1808. The islanders were resettled on Norfolk Island in 1856, but some families returned, and their descendants form today's population.

Geography and life

The island has no harbour or airstrip; supplies arrive by longboat from ships anchored offshore, principally a regular supply vessel from Mangareva in French Polynesia. The capital, Adamstown, contains a church, school, and store. The economy rests on government support, postage stamps, honey, curios, and, increasingly, tourism and a surrounding marine reserve — one of the largest in the world, designated in 2016.

Language

Residents speak English and Pitkern, a creole mixing eighteenth-century English with Tahitian.
