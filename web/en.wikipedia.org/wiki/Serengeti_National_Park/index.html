Serengeti National Park

The great wildebeest migration in the Serengeti.

Serengeti National Park is a large national park in northern Tanzania, covering some 14,750 km² (5,700 sq mi) of grassland plains, savanna, riverine forest, and woodlands. It is famous for the annual migration of over 1.5 million white-bearded wildebeest and hundreds of thousands of zebras and gazelles — widely regarded as one of the greatest wildlife spectacles on Earth — and for its dense populations of lions, leopards, elephants, rhinoceroses, and buffalo. The park forms the core of the wider Serengeti ecosystem, which includes the Ngorongoro Conservation Area and Kenya's Maasai Mara. Its name derives from the Maasai word siringet, "the place where the land runs on forever."

The great migration

Driven by seasonal rains and the search for fresh grazing, the herds move in a vast circular route through the ecosystem. Calving takes place on the short-grass plains of the southern Serengeti in January–March; the herds then move northwest, crossing the Grumeti and Mara rivers — where crocodiles and strong currents take a toll — into the Maasai Mara during the dry season, before returning south as the short rains begin. Predators follow the herds, and the migration sustains one of the largest remaining terrestrial mammal movements in the world.

History

The area was known to the Maasai, who grazed their cattle on the plains for generations. British administration made the Serengeti a game reserve in the 1920s and a national park in 1951 — then Tanganyika's first. The naturalist Bernhard Grzimek and his son Michael campaigned to document and protect the migration; their book and film Serengeti Shall Not Die (1959) won an Academy Award, and Michael Grzimek is buried in the park area after dying in a plane crash during survey work. The establishment of the park entailed the exclusion of the Maasai from their former grazing lands, a grievance with a long legacy. The park was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981.

Wildlife and ecology

Beyond the migratory herds, the park supports large resident populations: an estimated several thousand lions — among the largest concentrations anywhere — as well as leopards, cheetahs, spotted hyenas, African elephants, black rhinoceroses (heavily guarded against poaching), hippopotamuses, giraffes, and more than 500 bird species, including ostriches, secretary birds, and kori bustards. Habitats range from the treeless southern plains to the acacia woodlands of the centre and the hilly, forested Western Corridor and northern reaches toward the Mara River.

Tourism and threats

Tourism is a major revenue source for Tanzania, and the park receives well over a hundred thousand visitors a year. Threats include poaching for bushmeat and ivory, human population growth and settlement at the park's western edge, and proposals such as a commercial highway across the northern Serengeti, shelved after international protest in 2011. The ecosystem's management involves Tanzanian park authorities, local communities, and conservation organizations.
