Music hall

Music hall is a type of British theatrical entertainment that was popular from the early Victorian era, beginning around 1850, through the Great War. It subsequently lessened in popularity as it faced competition from radio and cinema, and effectively died out after 1918 as the halls rebranded their entertainment as variety. Perceptions of a distinction in Britain between bold and scandalous music hall entertainment and subsequent, more respectable variety theatre differ.

A music hall performance in the late 19th century.

Music hall can also refer to the venue itself — a theatre built primarily or used for such entertainment. Examples of music halls in the United Kingdom include the Hackney Empire, the London Palladium (originally a music hall), and the Leeds City Varieties, among the longest-surviving.

Origins

Music hall developed out of the entertainment provided in the new style of public houses of the 1830s, in "free and easies", "song and supper rooms", and saloon bars of large pubs. As these venues grew in popularity, purpose-built halls began to be constructed. The Canterbury Music Hall in Lambeth, opened in 1852 by Charles Morton — often called the "Father of the Halls" — is frequently cited as the first true music hall. Morton's success was followed by halls such as the Oxford Music Hall on Oxford Street (1861) and hundreds of others across Britain.

The performance

A typical music hall bill offered a succession of variety acts: singers of comic and sentimental songs, comedians, dancers, acrobats, jugglers, magicians, minstrels, and speciality acts. The evening was presided over by a chairman, seated at a table on stage, who introduced each act with a flourish. Audiences, seated at tables, could eat, drink, and smoke throughout, and participated enthusiastically, joining in the choruses of popular songs.

Songs were central to the appeal. Famous numbers included "Champagne Charlie", "The Man Who Broke the Bank at Monte Carlo", "My Old Man (Said Follow the Van)", and "Down at the Old Bull and Bush". Publishers sold sheet music of successful songs, and a performer's exclusive rights to sing a song were a valuable commodity.

Star performers

Music hall produced some of Britain's first mass-entertainment stars. Marie Lloyd, the "Queen of Music Hall", was famed for her saucy comic songs and innuendo. Dan Leno was a celebrated pantomime dame and clog dancer; Harry Champion sang rapid-fire cockney numbers; Vesta Tilley was a celebrated male impersonator; and Gus Elen and Albert Chevalier popularised the coster character. Little Tich created the famous "Big Boot Dance".

Decline

From the 1900s, circuit managers consolidated halls into syndicates, and by 1912 the first Royal Variety Performance gave the form establishment approval. However, competition from cinema, radio, and later television eroded audiences. The rise of revue and "variety" — a sanitised form of the same acts — replaced the name "music hall" on theatre bills, and by the end of the First World War the classic music hall era was effectively over.

Legacy

Music hall influenced British comedy, pantomime, and popular song for generations. The BBC television series The Good Old Days (1953–1983), filmed at the Leeds City Varieties, recreated the atmosphere of the halls for modern audiences. Many music hall songs remain well known as traditional popular songs.
