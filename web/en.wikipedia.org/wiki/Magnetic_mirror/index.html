Magnetic mirror

FieldPlasma physics, fusion research
PrincipleConservation of magnetic moment μ in a converging field
Key parameterMirror ratio R = Bmax/Bmin
Natural exampleVan Allen radiation belts

A magnetic mirror is a configuration of magnetic field in which the field strength increases along the field direction, causing charged particles spiralling along the field lines to be reflected. It is one of the oldest schemes for magnetic confinement of plasma in fusion research and also explains the trapping of particles in planetary radiation belts.

Principle

A charged particle in a magnetic field gyrates around a field line with magnetic moment μ = m v⊥² / 2B, which is an adiabatic invariant — approximately conserved when the field varies slowly. As the particle moves into a region of stronger field, conservation of μ and of energy forces its perpendicular velocity to grow at the expense of its parallel velocity; if the field becomes strong enough, the parallel velocity reaches zero and the particle turns back. Two mirrors facing each other therefore form a "magnetic bottle". Particles whose velocity lies within a certain cone of directions — the loss cone — have too much parallel motion and escape through the mirrors, the fundamental weakness of simple mirror confinement.

History and devices

Mirror machines were pursued from the 1950s, notably at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (the 2X series and the large MFTF-B tandem mirror) and in the Soviet Union under Gersh Budker. Tandem mirrors added electrostatic plugging, and the Field-Reversed Configuration and modern high-temperature-superconducting mirrors such as WHAM continue the line. In nature, the Earth's dipole field acts as a giant mirror machine: particles of the Van Allen belts bounce between mirror points in opposite hemispheres, and the same physics operating on Jupiter and in magnetic bottles explains auroral precipitation when collisions scatter particles into the loss cone.
