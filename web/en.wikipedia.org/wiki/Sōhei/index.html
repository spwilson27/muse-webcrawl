Sōhei (僧兵, "monk soldiers") were Buddhist warrior monks of both classical and feudal Japan. At certain points in history they held considerable power, obliging the imperial and military governments to collaborate with them. The sōhei were similar to the mountain ascetics known as yamabushi, but unlike the often solitary yamabushi, sōhei generally operated as organised monastic armies attached to great temples.

History

The warrior monks first appeared during the Heian period, when the great temples of Nara and Kyoto began to maintain armed forces to settle disputes over temple appointments, land rights and precedence. The Tendai temple of Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei and the Fujiwara clan temple of Kōfuku-ji in Nara became notorious for descending on the capital in force, bearing portable shrines (mikoshi) in a practice of protest known as gōso ("forceful appeal"), which secular authorities found difficult to resist because of the sacred objects involved.

During the Genpei War (1180–1185), sōhei fought on both sides; the monks of Mii-dera supported Prince Mochihito's call to arms against the Taira, and the Taira responded by burning Mii-dera and, in 1181, Nara itself — an event recorded with horror in The Tale of the Heike. In the Sengoku period the fortified temple of Ishiyama Hongan-ji, defended by the Ikkō-ikki leagues, resisted Oda Nobunaga for a decade. Nobunaga's destruction of Enryaku-ji in 1571, in which thousands of monks and laypeople were killed, and Hideyoshi's attack on Negoro-ji in 1585, effectively ended the military power of the great temples.

Weapons and dress

The sōhei are most strongly associated with the naginata, a polearm with a curved blade, though they also used the bow, the tachi and other standard weapons of the period. They typically wore monastic robes — often a white or saffron head cowl over kimono-like garments — with armour worn over or under the robes. The most famous individual sōhei is Benkei, the loyal retainer of Minamoto no Yoshitsune, whose exploits are celebrated in Noh and kabuki theatre.
