Tooth decay, also known as dental caries or cavities, is the breakdown of teeth due to acids produced by bacteria. The cavities may be yellow to black in colour. Symptoms may include pain and difficulty eating. Complications may include inflammation of the tissue around the tooth, tooth loss, and infection or abscess formation.

Cause

The cause of decay is acid from bacteria dissolving the hard tissues of the teeth (enamel, dentine and cementum). The acid is produced when bacteria in dental plaque — notably Streptococcus mutans and lactobacilli — ferment food debris, especially sugars, left on the tooth surface. When demineralisation outpaces remineralisation by saliva and fluoride, a lesion develops: at first a subsurface "white spot", then, if untreated, a cavitated hole.

Signs and symptoms

Early decay typically has no symptoms. As the lesion approaches the pulp, sensitivity to sweet, hot or cold stimuli, and then spontaneous toothache, may occur. Untreated decay can lead to pulpitis, periapical abscess, facial swelling and, rarely, serious spreading infection.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by visual and tactile examination, often supplemented by dental radiographs (bitewing X-rays), which reveal lesions between teeth and the depth of decay.

Prevention

Decay is largely preventable. Key measures include brushing twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, cleaning between teeth, limiting the frequency of sugary food and drinks, community water fluoridation, topical fluoride applications, and fissure sealants on molars. Regular dental check-ups allow early lesions to be managed non-operatively where possible.

Treatment

Treatment depends on severity: early lesions may be arrested with fluoride; cavitated lesions are restored with fillings of amalgam or composite resin; extensive damage may require crowns; pulp involvement may need root canal treatment; unrestorable teeth are extracted.

Epidemiology

Tooth decay is one of the most common chronic diseases worldwide, affecting billions of people. Around 2.4 billion people are estimated to have caries in permanent teeth, and over 500 million children in primary teeth. Prevalence has declined in many developed countries with fluoridation but remains high globally.
