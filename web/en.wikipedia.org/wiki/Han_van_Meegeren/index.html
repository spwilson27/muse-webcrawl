Henricus Antonius "Han" van Meegeren (10 October 1889 – 30 December 1947) was a Dutch painter and portraitist, considered one of the most ingenious art forgers of the 20th century. Frustrated by critics who dismissed his own traditional work, he set out to deceive the art world with paintings in the style of Johannes Vermeer, whose oeuvre was small and whose early religious period was poorly documented.

The Vermeer forgeries

His most famous forgery, The Supper at Emmaus (1937), was authenticated by the leading Vermeer scholar Abraham Bredius and praised as a masterpiece, then purchased by the Museum Boijmans in Rotterdam. Van Meegeren perfected techniques for ageing his paintings: he painted on old canvases, used period pigments, and hardened the paint with Bakelite resin so that it would resist solvents and crack like centuries-old oil paint. Over the following years he sold a series of fake Vermeers and works attributed to Pieter de Hooch and others for millions of guilders.

Trial

During the Second World War one of his fake Vermeers, Christ with the Adulteress, was sold to Hermann Göring. After the war Van Meegeren was arrested for collaboration and treason for selling Dutch cultural property to the Nazis. To save himself, he confessed that the painting was his own forgery, and demonstrated by painting another "Vermeer" under guard. He was convicted only of forgery and fraud, sentenced to one year in prison, and died of a heart attack before serving it, having become something of a folk hero for duping Göring.

Legacy

Van Meegeren's forgeries forced a re-examination of connoisseurship and provenance research and remain famous case studies in art crime; expert opinion still ranks The Supper at Emmaus among the most convincing forgeries ever made.
