Laser printer

A laser printer is a type of computer printer that produces high-quality text and graphics by passing a laser beam back and forth over an electrically charged photoreceptor drum, using the electrophotographic process adapted from xerographic photocopiers. Laser printers are known for speed, sharp output, and low cost per page for text, and are standard equipment in offices; colour models are also common.

History

The process began with Chester Carlson's invention of electrophotography (xerography) in 1938. At Xerox PARC in the early 1970s, Gary Starkweather adapted a copier with a laser to create the first laser printers, leading to the Xerox 9700 in 1977. The first mass-market desktop laser printer was the HP LaserJet, launched in 1984 around a Canon engine and priced at about $3,495; together with the Apple LaserWriter (1985) and PostScript page description, it ignited the desktop publishing revolution.

How it works

The printer's processor rasterises the page into a bitmap. A corona wire or charge roller gives the rotating drum a uniform electrostatic charge; the laser (scanned across the drum by a spinning mirror) discharges points where the image should appear, forming an electrostatic latent image. Toner — a fine dry powder of plastic and pigment — adheres to the discharged areas, is transferred to paper given the opposite charge, and is then fused permanently by heated rollers. Colour printers repeat the process with cyan, magenta, yellow, and black toner.

Characteristics

Resolution is typically 600 or 1,200 dots per inch, speeds range from about 20 pages per minute for personal models to well over 100 for production machines, and toner cartridges commonly print thousands of pages. Compared with inkjet printers, laser printers usually have a higher purchase price but lower running costs for text, and their output does not smudge when wet; inkjets retain advantages in photo printing. Some toner particles and ozone are emitted during operation, so ventilation guidance exists in some jurisdictions.
