Ashrama (Sanskrit: āśrama) is a system of stages of life discussed in Hindu texts of the ancient and medieval eras of Indian history. The four ashramas are: Brahmacharya (student life), Grihastha (household life), Vanaprastha (retired forest-dweller life), and Sannyasa (renunciation). The ashrama system is one facet of the dharma concept in Hinduism, traditionally combined with the four aims of life (purushartha) and the varna system.

The four stages

Brahmacharya is the celibate student stage, devoted to study of the Vedas under a guru. Grihastha, the householder stage, is considered the most important socially, since householders support the members of all other stages; it centres on marriage, family, earning a livelihood and the pursuit of dharma, artha and kama. Vanaprastha is a gradual withdrawal from worldly duties, traditionally begun when a householder sees grandchildren, involving retreat to the forest and increased contemplation. Sannyasa is complete renunciation: the sannyasi owns nothing, subsists on alms, and devotes himself entirely to moksha, liberation.

History and practice

The ashrama scheme crystallised in Dharmashastra literature in the centuries around the start of the Common Era, partly as a response to ascetic movements such as Buddhism and Jainism. In classical theory the stages applied to the three upper varnas, with a notional lifespan of 100 years divided into quarters. In modern Hindu life the system is largely an ideal: many observe student and householder stages, few formally enter vanaprastha, and sannyasa — which could historically be entered directly from any stage — remains a respected but minority path.
