George Emil Palade (19 November 1912 – 7 October 2008) was a Romanian-American cell biologist. Described as "the most influential cell biologist ever," he pioneered the use of electron microscopy and cell fractionation to reveal the internal structure of cells, and shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Albert Claude and Christian de Duve.

Career

Palade studied medicine at the Carol Davila University of Medicine and Pharmacy in Bucharest, where he became a professor. After the Second World War he emigrated to the United States and joined the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research in New York, working with Albert Claude. He later held chairs at Yale University and at the University of California, San Diego, where he was founding dean of scientific affairs of the School of Medicine.

Discoveries

Using the electron microscope, Palade described the fine structure of mitochondria and identified the small granular components of the endoplasmic reticulum, which were later shown to be ribosomes and were initially known as "Palade granules." His classic pulse-chase experiments on pancreatic acinar cells traced the pathway of secretory proteins through the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, establishing the modern account of protein secretion. He also identified, with Ewald Weibel, the endothelial storage organelles known as Weibel–Palade bodies.

Honours

In addition to the Nobel Prize, Palade received the Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research (1966) and the National Medal of Science (1986). In 2001 the University of California, San Diego named its cellular and molecular medicine building in his honour, and Romania's George Emil Palade University of Medicine in Târgu Mureș bears his name.
