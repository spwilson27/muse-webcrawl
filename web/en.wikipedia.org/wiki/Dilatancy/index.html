Dilatancy

Dilatancy is the volume change observed in granular materials when these are subjected to shear deformations. The word was introduced by Osborne Reynolds in his 1885 paper "On the Dilatancy of Media Composed of Rigid Particles in Contact", in which he showed that a deformable bag of either dry sand or sand saturated with water changes volume when deformed, and that this effect is caused by the particles rearranging so that they occupy a larger volume. Dilatancy is a characteristic feature of soils; sand and over-consolidated clay show dilatant behaviour, while normally consolidated clays contract (show negative dilatancy) when sheared.

Mechanism

In a dense packing, adjacent grains are interlocked. Under shear, grains must ride up and over one another in order for deformation to proceed, so the bulk volume of the sample increases. In a loose packing the grains instead fall into the available voids and the sample contracts. The magnitude of dilatancy depends on the initial density of the soil and the confining stress: at high confining stresses grain crushing suppresses dilation. The change of volume per unit change of shear strain is commonly expressed through the dilatancy angle ψ, which relates the ratio of volumetric strain increment to shear strain increment.

Significance in geotechnical engineering

Dilatancy strongly affects the strength of soils. Because the peak shear strength of a dense sand includes a component attributable to the work done against the confining stress as the sample dilates, the peak friction angle exceeds the critical-state (constant-volume) friction angle by an amount related to the dilatancy angle. In saturated soils under undrained conditions, the tendency to dilate generates negative excess pore water pressures, which increases the effective stress and hence the undrained shear strength; conversely, contractive loose sands under undrained loading generate positive pore pressures and can lose almost all strength, a phenomenon underlying static liquefaction in flow slides.

Critical state theory

In critical state soil mechanics, dilatancy ceases at the critical state: at sufficiently large shear strains the soil deforms at constant volume and constant effective stress. The stress-dilatancy relation developed by Rowe (1962) links the ratio of principal stresses to the rate of dilation and remains a foundation of modern constitutive models for granular materials.
