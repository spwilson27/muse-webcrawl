Modal logic

Modal logic is a type of formal logic that extends classical logic with operators expressing modality — ways in which statements can be true, principally necessity (□) and possibility (◇). It is used to reason about knowledge, belief, obligation, time and provability, and has applications in philosophy, computer science, linguistics and artificial intelligence.

Language and systems

Modal propositional logic adds □p ("necessarily p") and ◇p ("possibly p"), interdefinable via negation. Systems are distinguished by axioms: K (distribution), T (□p → p), S4 (adding □p → □□p) and S5 (adding ◇p → □◇p); S5 is often taken as the logic of metaphysical necessity. C. I. Lewis developed the first modern systems from 1912 in reaction to material implication paradoxes.

Semantics

Saul Kripke's possible-worlds semantics (1959 onwards) evaluates □p as true at a world if p holds at all accessible worlds, with properties of the accessibility relation (reflexivity, transitivity, symmetry) matching axiom systems. Algebraic and neighbourhood semantics provide alternatives. Completeness and decidability results underpin modal logic's use in verification.

Varieties and applications

Families include alethic, deontic (obligation), epistemic (knowledge), temporal and doxastic logics, plus dynamic logic of programs. Applications range from model checking in computer science to the ontological argument and metaphysics of possible worlds in philosophy. See possible world and Kripke.
