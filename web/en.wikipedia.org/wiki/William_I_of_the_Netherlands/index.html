William I of the Netherlands

Reign16 March 1815 – 7 October 1840
SuccessorWilliam II
Born24 August 1772, The Hague, Dutch Republic
Died12 December 1843 (aged 71), Berlin, Prussia

William I (Dutch: Willem Frederik; 24 August 1772 – 12 December 1843) was King of the Netherlands and Grand Duke of Luxembourg from 1815 until his abdication in 1840. The first Dutch monarch, he united the northern and southern Netherlands into the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, promoted industry, canals and education, and stubbornly refused to accept the secession of Belgium until 1839.

Early life and exile

William was born in The Hague, the eldest son of Stadtholder William V, Prince of Orange, and Wilhelmina of Prussia. When the French invaded the Dutch Republic in 1795, the Orange family fled to England. William later commanded troops in Prussian and Austrian service against Napoleon, and lost his family's German possessions, though he received the principality of Nassau-Orange-Fulda in compensation.

Return and accession

After the collapse of French rule in 1813, William returned to the Netherlands at the invitation of a provisional government and landed at Scheveningen on 30 November 1813. He accepted the title of Sovereign Prince, and following the decisions of the Congress of Vienna, which joined the former Austrian Netherlands and the Prince-Bishopric of Liège to his realm, he proclaimed himself King William I on 16 March 1815.

Rule

William ruled as an energetic but authoritarian "enlightened despot". In the industrial south he encouraged mining and manufacturing, founded the Société Générale bank in 1822, and dug canals such as the Ghent–Terneuzen Canal. In the north he promoted trade and shipping, founding the Netherlands Trading Society (NHM) in 1824. His language and religious policies, intended to unify the kingdom, alienated the largely Catholic, partly French-speaking south.

Belgian Revolution and abdication

The Belgian Revolution broke out in August 1830. William refused for years to accept Belgian independence, launching the unsuccessful Ten Days' Campaign of 1831 and maintaining a costly army mobilisation. Only in 1839 did he accept the Treaty of London, which recognised Belgium and partitioned Limburg and Luxembourg. Bitter and disappointed, William abdicated on 7 October 1840 in favour of his son, William II.

Later life

Taking the title Count of Nassau, William moved to Berlin and married his late wife's former lady-in-waiting, Henrietta d'Oultremont, a marriage that had contributed to the unpopularity preceding his abdication. He died in Berlin in 1843.
