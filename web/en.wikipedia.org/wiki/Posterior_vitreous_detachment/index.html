Posterior vitreous detachment

A posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) is a condition of the eye in which the vitreous membrane separates from the retina. It is a common age-related change: with ageing the vitreous gel liquefies (syneresis) and eventually collapses away from the retinal surface.

Symptoms

Many PVDs are asymptomatic. Others present with a sudden increase in floaters — including a characteristic ring-shaped (Weiss ring) floater — and flashes of light (photopsia) caused by traction on the retina as the gel pulls free. Symptoms usually settle over weeks to months as the brain adapts.

Risks and complications

Risk factors include age over 50, myopia (short-sightedness), cataract surgery, trauma, and inflammation. In a minority of cases the separation tears the retina, causing a retinal tear or retinal detachment, which requires urgent treatment. New floaters or flashes therefore warrant prompt dilated eye examination.

Management

An uncomplicated PVD requires no treatment other than reassurance and observation, with follow-up examination to exclude delayed retinal tears. Patients are advised to seek immediate care if they notice a shower of floaters, persistent flashing lights, or a shadow or curtain in the peripheral vision.
