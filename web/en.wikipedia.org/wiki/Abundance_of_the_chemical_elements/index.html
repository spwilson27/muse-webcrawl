Abundance of the chemical elements

The abundance of the chemical elements describes the relative proportions of the elements present in a given environment — the universe, the Solar System, a planet, or the human body. Abundances are usually expressed as fractions by mass or by number of atoms, or on a logarithmic scale normalised to hydrogen or silicon.

The universe and the Solar System

The cosmos is overwhelmingly hydrogen (about 74% by mass) and helium (about 24%), produced in Big Bang nucleosynthesis; all heavier elements together make up only about 2% and were forged in stars and supernovae. Solar System abundances, measured from the Sun's spectrum and from primitive CI chondrite meteorites, show the characteristic sawtooth pattern: even-numbered nuclei are more abundant than their odd neighbours (Oddo–Harkins rule), there is a pronounced peak at iron, and lithium, beryllium and boron are anomalously scarce because they are fragile in stellar interiors.

The Earth and the human body

Earth's crust is dominated by oxygen (about 46% by mass), silicon (28%), aluminium (8%) and iron (5%), while the bulk Earth is richer in iron and nickel, which sank to the core during differentiation. Seawater is mostly oxygen and hydrogen with chlorine and sodium as the chief dissolved elements. The human body is about 65% oxygen, 18% carbon, 10% hydrogen and 3% nitrogen by mass, with calcium and phosphorus the main mineral constituents and trace elements such as iron, zinc and iodine essential in tiny amounts.
