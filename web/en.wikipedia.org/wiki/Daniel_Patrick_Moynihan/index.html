Daniel Patrick Moynihan

Daniel Patrick Moynihan (March 16, 1927 – March 26, 2003) was an American politician, diplomat and sociologist. A member of the Democratic Party, he represented New York in the United States Senate from 1977 to 2001. He served as Ambassador to India and as Ambassador to the United Nations, and as an adviser to Presidents John F. Kennedy, Lyndon B. Johnson, Richard Nixon and Gerald Ford.

Moynihan authored the controversial 1965 report The Negro Family: The Case for National Action (the "Moynihan Report"), and is often quoted for the remark that "everyone is entitled to his own opinion, but not his own facts."

Early life and education

Moynihan was born in Tulsa, Oklahoma, and raised mainly in New York City. After his father left the family, they lived in relative poverty in Hell's Kitchen. He shined shoes, worked as a longshoreman, and enlisted in the U.S. Navy in 1944. He graduated from the City College of New York in 1948 and earned a PhD in sociology and history from the Fletcher School at Tufts University in 1961, studying in London as a Fulbright scholar.

Early career

Moynihan worked for New York governor W. Averell Harriman and co-authored Beyond the Melting Pot (1963) with Nathan Glazer, an influential study of New York's ethnic groups. In the Kennedy and Johnson administrations he served as Assistant Secretary of Labor, where he wrote the 1965 Moynihan Report on Black families — a document that sparked decades of debate about poverty, family structure and race.

Nixon administration and diplomacy

Under President Nixon, Moynihan served as Counselor to the President for Urban Affairs and championed the Family Assistance Plan, a guaranteed minimum income proposal that failed in Congress. He served as Ambassador to India (1973–1975) and then as Ambassador to the United Nations (1975–1976), where he gained prominence denouncing the UN General Assembly's "Zionism is racism" resolution.

Senate

Elected to the Senate in 1976, Moynihan served four terms, chairing the Finance Committee and the Environment and Public Works Committee. He was known for his erudition, his defence of Social Security, his opposition to the Iraq War authorization, and his successful effort to redevelop Pennsylvania Station in New York City — the station's new train hall, opened in 2021, bears his name. He declined to seek re-election in 2000 and was succeeded by Hillary Clinton.

Death and legacy

Moynihan died in Washington, D.C., on March 26, 2003, of complications from a ruptured appendix. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2000. His books include Maximum Feasible Misunderstanding (1969), The Politics of a Guaranteed Income (1973), Family and Nation (1986) and Pandaemonium (1993).
