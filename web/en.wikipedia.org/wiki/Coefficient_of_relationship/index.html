Coefficient of relationship

Expected proportions of shared genes for common relationships.

The coefficient of relationship is a measure of the degree of biological relatedness between two individuals. It is defined as the expected proportion of alleles shared identical by descent, and equivalently enters the theory of inclusive fitness as the relatedness term in Hamilton's rule (rB > C). The concept was developed by Sewall Wright in the 1920s as part of his work on inbreeding and path coefficients.

Values

RelationshipCoefficient (r)
Identical twin / clone1
Parent–offspring; full siblings1/2
Half-siblings; grandparent–grandchild; aunt/uncle–niece/nephew1/4
First cousins1/8
Second cousins1/32

Each step of meiosis between two relatives through a common ancestor halves the expected shared fraction, so r is computed by summing (1/2)n over all paths through common ancestors. For example, full siblings are connected through two parents with paths of two steps each, giving 2 × (1/2)2 = 1/2.

Relation to inbreeding

The coefficient of relationship between an individual's parents equals (twice) that individual's coefficient of inbreeding. High relatedness in social insects—full sisters in haplodiploid Hymenoptera share r = 3/4—was famously invoked by W. D. Hamilton to help explain the evolution of eusociality through kin selection, though modern treatments emphasize multiple contributing factors.
