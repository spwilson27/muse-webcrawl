Film music

Film music is music written specifically to accompany a film, whether as part of an original score composed for the picture or as pre-existing music selected and licensed for it. Together with dialogue and sound effects, music is one of the principal means by which a film creates mood, signals emotion, marks place and period, and binds scenes together. The original music written for a film is called its film score.

History

Music accompanied films from the first public exhibitions: pianists, organists and small ensembles improvised or compiled accompaniment for silent films, using cue sheets and photoplay music libraries. Camille Saint-Saëns composed one of the first original film scores in 1908, for L'Assassinat du duc de Guise. With synchronised sound after 1927, studios built music departments; Max Steiner's score for King Kong (1933) established the symphonic model — leitmotifs, continuous underscoring and music synchronised to action ("Mickey Mousing").

Golden Age and after

The classical Hollywood era featured composers such as Erich Wolfgang Korngold, Alfred Newman, Bernard Herrmann and Miklós Rózsa. From the 1950s, jazz (Elmer Bernstein, Henry Mancini), popular songs and electronic sounds diversified film music. A symphonic revival in the 1970s, led by John Williams's scores for Jaws, Star Wars and Superman, restored the orchestra to prominence, while composers such as Ennio Morricone, Hans Zimmer and Alexandre Desplat have shaped more recent practice.

Elements and production

The composer typically begins after a "spotting session" with the director decides where music should occur. Themes may be attached to characters (leitmotif), and the score is orchestrated, recorded to picture with click tracks or streamers, and mixed with dialogue and effects. Music that the characters themselves hear is called diegetic or source music.
