Recourse debt

Recourse debt is a debt that is backed by collateral from the borrower. If the borrower defaults and the value of the collateral does not cover the full amount of the debt, the lender can recover the remaining balance — the deficiency — from the borrower's other assets. Recourse debt therefore gives the lender recourse to assets beyond the collateral.

Comparison with non-recourse debt

In a non-recourse loan, the lender's recovery is limited to the collateral itself, so the lender bears more risk and typically charges higher interest or requires lower loan-to-value ratios. Most consumer mortgages in many US states are recourse in principle, though some states restrict deficiency judgments for purchase-money mortgages.

Tax treatment

If recourse debt is forgiven or cancelled, the borrower may realize cancellation-of-debt income; in foreclosure, the transaction is split between a sale of the property and ordinary income from debt discharge, unlike non-recourse debt, where the full balance counts toward the amount realized.
