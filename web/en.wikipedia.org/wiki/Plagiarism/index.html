Plagiarism

EtymologyFrom Latin plagiarius, "kidnapper"
FieldAcademic integrity, publishing ethics
Related conceptsCopyright infringement, ghostwriting, self-plagiarism

Plagiarism is the representation of another person's language, thoughts, ideas, or expressions as one's own original work. Although precise definitions vary, in many cultures and jurisdictions the act is considered a violation of academic integrity and journalistic ethics, as well as social norms, and can provoke sanctions including suspension or expulsion from a school, substantial fines, professional discipline, and even imprisonment in some cases.

History

The word derives from the Latin plagiarius, a kidnapper. The Roman poet Martial used it in the first century AD to complain that another poet had "kidnapped" his verses. The modern notion developed alongside the concept of individual authorship and copyright law in the 18th century; before then, imitation of classical models was widely praised rather than condemned.

Forms

Common forms include copying text verbatim without quotation or citation, paraphrasing another's work too closely, submitting work written by someone else (including paid essay services), and "self-plagiarism" — reusing one's own previously published work while presenting it as new. In academia, plagiarism is typically detected through manual comparison and through text-matching software such as Turnitin.

Consequences

Students found plagiarising may fail assignments or courses or be expelled. Journalists, academics, and public officials have lost positions after plagiarism revelations; several European politicians, including a German defence minister, resigned in the 2010s over plagiarised doctoral theses. Plagiarism is distinct from copyright infringement: plagiarism concerns unattributed use, which can occur even with public-domain material, whereas infringement concerns unauthorized use of protected work regardless of attribution.
