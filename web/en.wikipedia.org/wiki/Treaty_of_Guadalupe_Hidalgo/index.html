The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (Spanish: Tratado de Guadalupe Hidalgo) officially ended the Mexican–American War (1846–1848). It was signed on 2 February 1848 in the town of Guadalupe Hidalgo, now a neighborhood of Mexico City, after United States forces had occupied the Mexican capital. Under the treaty, Mexico ceded a vast territory—often called the Mexican Cession—to the United States and recognized the Rio Grande as the southern border of Texas.

The ceded lands encompassed present-day California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona, and parts of New Mexico, Colorado, and Wyoming, together with the earlier annexation of Texas. In exchange, the United States paid Mexico $15 million and assumed up to $3.25 million in claims by American citizens against the Mexican government.

Background

War broke out in 1846 after the United States annexed Texas, which Mexico still claimed. American forces under Zachary Taylor, Winfield Scott, and Stephen Kearny won a series of campaigns, culminating in the capture of Mexico City in September 1847 after the battles of Molino del Rey and Chapultepec. President James K. Polk appointed Nicholas Trist as his envoy; although recalled by Polk in late 1847, Trist defied the recall and negotiated the treaty.

Terms

The treaty's principal provisions included:

• The Rio Grande was established as the boundary between Texas and Mexico.
• Mexico ceded Alta California and Santa Fe de Nuevo México to the United States.
• The United States paid $15 million and assumed Mexican debts owed to American citizens.
• Mexican citizens living in the ceded territories could choose to relocate to Mexico or remain and become U.S. citizens; the final version deleted Article X, which had guaranteed protection of Mexican land grants.

The U.S. Senate ratified the treaty on 10 March 1848, with some amendments; Mexico's legislature ratified it in May, and ratifications were exchanged at Querétaro on 30 May 1848.

Consequences

The treaty fixed the modern shape of the southwestern United States and set the stage for the Gadsden Purchase of 1854, which completed the present-day continental border. The discovery of gold in California days before the signing triggered the California Gold Rush. For Mexican residents who remained, the loss of the Article X land-grant protections contributed to the widespread loss of communal and ranchero lands in the following decades. The treaty remains a defining document in U.S.–Mexico relations.
