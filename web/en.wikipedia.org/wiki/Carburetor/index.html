Carburetor

A carburetor (also spelled carburettor) is a device that mixes air and fuel for internal combustion engines. It was invented by Hungarian scientists Donát Bánki and János Csonka in 1893. Carburetors were the most common method of fuel delivery for most American-made cars until the late 1980s, when fuel injection became preferred.

Principles of operation

The carburetor works on Bernoulli's principle: as air passes through a constriction called a venturi, its speed increases and its pressure drops. This pressure difference draws fuel from the float chamber through a jet into the airstream, where it atomises. A throttle valve controls the amount of air–fuel mixture delivered to the engine, and hence its power.

Components

Key components include the float chamber, which maintains a constant fuel level; the choke, which enriches the mixture for cold starting; the accelerator pump, which provides extra fuel during rapid throttle opening; and idle and main metering circuits that deliver fuel across the engine's operating range.

Decline

Carburetors were largely replaced because fuel injection offers more precise mixture control, improving fuel economy, emissions, and reliability. They remain common on small engines such as lawn mowers, chainsaws, and older motorcycles.
