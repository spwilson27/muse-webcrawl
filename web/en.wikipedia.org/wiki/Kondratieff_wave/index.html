Kondratieff wave

The Kondratieff wave (also Kondratiev wave, K-wave, or long wave) is a hypothesized cycle in the modern capitalist world economy lasting roughly 40 to 60 years, consisting of alternating phases of high sectoral growth and comparatively slow growth. The concept is named after the Soviet economist Nikolai Kondratiev (1892–1938), who described such long cycles in his 1925 work The Major Economic Cycles.

The theory

Kondratiev, analysing price, interest-rate, wage, and production series for Britain, France, and the United States reaching back to the 1780s, identified two and a half long waves. He associated upswings with the diffusion of major technological and infrastructural innovations, bursts of capital investment, and the incorporation of new regions into the world market, and downswings with the exhaustion of those impulses. Kondratiev himself offered the statistical observation cautiously and did not claim a single mechanical cause; he was later arrested and executed in the Great Purge, and his ideas survived chiefly through Western economists such as Joseph Schumpeter, who named the cycles after him and tied them to clusters of innovation.

Proposed waves

Later writers commonly divide industrial history into successive waves, each launched by a cluster of basic innovations:

WaveApproximate upswingKey technologies
First1780s–1810sSteam engine, cotton textiles
Second1840s–1870sRailways, steel
Third1890s–1920sElectricity, chemicals, internal combustion
Fourth1940s–1970sPetrochemicals, automobiles, aviation
Fifth1980s–2000sComputers, telecommunications, the internet

Criticism

Mainstream economists are divided. Critics argue that the data series are short relative to the claimed period, that dating of the waves is flexible enough to fit almost any narrative, and that apparent cycles can emerge from random shocks filtered in particular ways. Supporters respond that similar long rhythms appear across many price and innovation series and that the framework remains useful in economic history and innovation studies.
