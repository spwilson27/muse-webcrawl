Megasthenes

Megasthenes (Μεγασθένης, c. 350 – c. 290 BC) was an ancient Greek historian, diplomat, ethnographer, and explorer in the Hellenistic period. He served as ambassador of Seleucus I Nicator to the court of Chandragupta Maurya, founder of the Mauryan Empire, at Pataliputra (modern Patna) in northern India, around 302 BC. His lost work Indica is the earliest major Greek account of India and a foundational source for Western knowledge of the subcontinent.

Indica

The Indica does not survive intact, but fragments and summaries are preserved in the works of Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus Siculus, Pliny the Elder, and others. Megasthenes described the geography of India, the Ganges river, the size and administration of Pataliputra (with its wooden palisade and the Mauryan bureaucracy and army), and Indian society. He famously divided Indian society into seven classes (philosophers, farmers, herdsmen, artisans, soldiers, overseers, and councillors) and described customs, religions, fauna, and marvels, mixing careful observation with tales of gold-digging ants and men with ears large enough to sleep in.

Reliability and legacy

Later geographers, notably Strabo, doubted some of Megasthenes' more fabulous reports, but modern scholarship credits much of his administrative and social description as consistent with Indian sources such as the Arthashastra. His account of the absence (as he claimed) of slavery and written laws in India has been much discussed. The standard collection of the fragments was published by E. A. Schwanbeck in 1846 and translated by J. W. McCrindle in the 19th century.
