Fruitlands

Fruitlands was a utopian agrarian commune established in Harvard, Massachusetts, in 1843 by Amos Bronson Alcott and Charles Lane, both associated with the Transcendentalist movement. The experiment lasted only about seven months.


The Fruitlands farmhouse

Founding

Alcott and Lane purchased a 90-acre (36 ha) farm in May 1843 and moved there on 1 June with their families and a small group of followers. Among the residents was Alcott's daughter Louisa May Alcott, then ten years old, who later satirised the experience in her sketch "Transcendental Wild Oats" (1873).

Principles

The founders aimed to live without exploiting animals or other people. Members were vegans before the term existed, eating no meat, dairy or eggs; they avoided animal products such as wool and leather, refused to use animal labour, and would not use manure as fertiliser. They ate mainly fruit, vegetables, grains and water, bathed in cold water, and rose before dawn.

Failure

The community's ideals conflicted with agricultural reality. Planting began late, crops were insufficient, and the members' refusal to use animal labour or manure left them unable to work the land adequately. Disagreements between Alcott and Lane over the role of the family deepened the crisis. Financial difficulties forced the group to disperse in January 1844. The property is now operated as a museum.
