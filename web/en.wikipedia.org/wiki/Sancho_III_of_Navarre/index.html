Sancho Garcés III (c. 992 – 18 October 1035), nicknamed Sancho the Great (Spanish: Sancho el Mayor, Basque: Antso Handia), was the king of Navarre from 1004 until his death in 1035. He also ruled the County of Aragon and, by marriage and conquest, extended his authority over Castile, León, and much of Christian Iberia, becoming the most powerful Christian ruler on the peninsula of his age. He sometimes styled himself rex Hispaniarum — "king of the Spains" — and is associated with fostering the pilgrim route of the Way of St. James.

Reign

Sancho succeeded his father García Sánchez II while still a minor, under a regency council, during the period of devastating raids by the Córdoban commander Almanzor and his sons. As the Caliphate of Córdoba collapsed into civil war, Sancho expanded Navarrese power: he gained control of Sobrarbe and Ribagorza, inherited rights in Castile through his wife Muniadona, daughter of Count Sancho García, and intervened decisively in León, occupying the city in 1034 and taking the imperial title there.

A devout reformer, Sancho promoted Cluniac monastic reform, endowed monasteries such as San Juan de la Peña and San Salvador de Leyre, improved the pilgrim road to Santiago de Compostela, and introduced feudal customs from France into his realms.

Division of the realm and legacy

On his death, Sancho divided his dominions among his four sons: García Sánchez III received Navarre; Ferdinand received Castile, which he raised to a kingdom; Ramiro — Sancho's illegitimate son by a mistress — received Aragon, likewise becoming its first king; and Gonzalo received Sobrarbe and Ribagorza. The partition shaped the political map of medieval Spain for centuries, giving rise to the separate kingdoms of Navarre, Castile, and Aragon. Later chroniclers celebrated him as the unifier of Christian Spain, and Ferdinand, Ramiro, and García were all kings descended from his line.
