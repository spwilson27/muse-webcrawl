Passenger Vessel Services Act of 1886

Enacted: 1886
Codified at: 46 U.S.C. § 55103
Subject: Maritime cabotage law
Related to: Jones Act

The Passenger Vessel Services Act of 1886 (PVSA) is a United States federal law that, together with related cabotage statutes, reserves the transportation of passengers between points in the United States to vessels that are built in the United States, registered under the United States flag, owned by United States citizens, and crewed predominantly by United States citizens.

Provisions

The act prohibits foreign-built or foreign-flagged vessels from carrying passengers directly between two United States ports. A vessel that violates the act by discharging passengers at a United States port different from the port of embarkation without an intervening foreign port call may subject its owner to a monetary penalty, traditionally assessed per passenger (an amount that has risen over time to several hundred dollars).

Effect on cruises

The PVSA is the reason most large cruise ships sailing from United States home ports are registered abroad yet must call at a foreign port during itineraries between United States ports. For example, cruises from California to Hawaii typically call at Ensenada, Mexico, and Alaska cruises from Seattle typically call at Victoria, British Columbia, Canada.

Exceptions and waivers

Limited exceptions exist, including for travel between the United States mainland and Puerto Rico where no qualified United States vessel service is available, and Congress or the executive branch has occasionally granted temporary waivers, for instance during emergencies.

Related law

See also the Jones Act, which imposes similar requirements on the carriage of merchandise, and the general topic of cabotage.
