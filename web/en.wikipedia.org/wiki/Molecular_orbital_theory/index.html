Molecular orbital theory

In chemistry, molecular orbital theory (often abbreviated to MO theory or MOT) is a method for describing the electronic structure of molecules using quantum mechanics. It was proposed early in the 20th century. The method was first developed by Friedrich Hund and Robert S. Mulliken in 1927 and 1928, with significant contributions by John Lennard-Jones, Erich Hückel, and others.

Molecular orbital energy-level diagram showing bonding and antibonding orbitals.

In molecular orbital theory, electrons are not assigned to individual bonds between atoms, but are treated as moving under the influence of the atomic nuclei in the whole molecule. Each electron is described by a wavefunction called a molecular orbital, which may extend over the entire molecule. The spatial and energetic properties of electrons are described by quantum mechanics as molecular orbitals surround more than one atom.

Overview

MO theory uses a linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) to represent molecular orbitals resulting from bonds between atoms. These are divided into three categories: bonding, antibonding, and non-bonding. Bonding orbitals are concentrated in the region between two nuclei and hold the nuclei together. Antibonding orbitals are concentrated in regions behind the nuclei and tend to pull the nuclei apart. Non-bonding orbitals have no contribution from the parent atom's orbitals and are similar to the atomic orbitals of one of the atoms.

As with atomic orbitals, the Pauli exclusion principle allows each molecular orbital to hold at most two electrons with opposite spins, and electrons occupy orbitals of lowest energy first, in accordance with Hund's rule. The bond order of a molecule is calculated as one-half the difference between the number of electrons in bonding orbitals and the number in antibonding orbitals.

History

The theory grew out of attempts to explain the paramagnetism of molecular oxygen (O2), which the simpler valence bond theory could not account for without additional assumptions. Molecular orbital diagrams of O2 correctly predict two unpaired electrons in antibonding π* orbitals, explaining its triplet ground state and its attraction to a magnetic field. This early success helped establish MO theory as a cornerstone of computational and theoretical chemistry.

Comparison with valence bond theory

Valence bond (VB) theory, developed by Walter Heitler, Fritz London, John Slater and Linus Pauling, describes bonds as arising from the overlap of atomic orbitals containing paired electrons localized between two atoms. MO theory, by contrast, delocalizes electrons over the whole molecule. For many purposes the two approaches converge when extended (for example, through configuration interaction), but MO theory lends itself more naturally to spectroscopy, photoelectron spectra, and modern computational methods such as density functional theory and Hartree–Fock calculations.

Applications

  • Predicting bond orders and bond lengths of diatomic molecules such as N2, O2 and F2
  • Explaining aromaticity through Hückel's rule for conjugated π systems
  • Interpreting ultraviolet–visible and photoelectron spectra
  • Understanding frontier orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) in reaction chemistry, formalized in the Woodward–Hoffmann rules for pericyclic reactions
