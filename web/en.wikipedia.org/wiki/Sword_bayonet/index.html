Sword bayonet

A sword bayonet is a long-bladed bayonet designed to function both as an attachment to a rifle, converting it into a spear-like weapon, and as a hand-held short sword or large knife when detached. Sword bayonets typically have blades of 40 cm (16 in) or longer, a full hilt with crossguard and pommel, and often a curved "yatagan" blade copied from Ottoman short swords.

History

Early plug and socket bayonets were spikes or simple blades, but by the mid-nineteenth century armies began issuing long bayonets that doubled as sidearms, particularly for artillerymen, engineers and rifle regiments who were not issued separate swords. The French Chassepot rifle's Model 1866 yatagan bayonet is a classic example; British, German, Japanese and other armies fielded similar weapons.

Sword bayonets remained common through the First World War — the British Pattern 1907 bayonet for the Lee–Enfield rifle had a 17-inch blade — but interwar experience favoured shorter, handier knife bayonets, and sword bayonets largely disappeared from service after the Second World War.

Design considerations

The appeal of the sword bayonet was economy: one weapon served as both bayonet and short sword. Against this, long blades made rifles unwieldy, hindered handling in trenches and close quarters, and added weight. Their effectiveness when actually used as swords was limited because of the offset mounting and the balance required for a dual-purpose design.
