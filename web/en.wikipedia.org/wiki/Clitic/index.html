Clitic

In morphology and syntax, a clitic (from Greek enklitikos, "leaning") is a morpheme that has syntactic characteristics of a word but depends phonologically on another word or phrase. Clitics cannot stand alone in speech — they are pronounced as part of a neighbouring "host" word — yet they behave grammatically like independent words rather than affixes.

[Image: Diagram showing a clitic attached phonologically to its host word]

Types

A proclitic leans on the following word (as the French article in l'homme leans on the noun), while an enclitic leans on the preceding word (as Latin -que, "and", or English 's in "the Queen of England's hat"). A rare mesoclitic appears inside its host, as in some Portuguese verb forms.

Clitics versus affixes

The boundary between clitics and affixes is famously fuzzy. Arnold Zwicky and Geoffrey Pullum proposed diagnostics: clitics typically attach to hosts of many word classes, show low selectivity, and can have scope over coordinated phrases ("the king and queen's crowns"), whereas true affixes attach only to specific stems and interact more with idiosyncratic morphology and phonology. English possessive 's and reduced auxiliaries ('ll, 've) are textbook examples.

Special clitics and Wackernagel's law

In many Indo-European languages, clitics cluster in second position in the clause — a pattern described by Jacob Wackernagel in 1892. Serbo-Croatian, Czech, and Sanskrit all place chains of pronominal and auxiliary clitics after the first word or phrase of the sentence.
