Capital punishment in the United States

Capital punishment, also known as the death penalty, is a legal penalty in the United States at the federal level and in a number of states for certain aggravated murders and other capital crimes. Its use, frequency and public support have declined in recent decades.

Legal statusLegal federally and in about half of states; many states have abolished or imposed moratoria
Main methodLethal injection
Key casesFurman v. Georgia (1972), Gregg v. Georgia (1976)

History

Executions date to colonial times. In Furman v. Georgia (1972) the Supreme Court halted executions as then administered; states rewrote statutes, and Gregg v. Georgia (1976) allowed executions to resume. Numbers peaked in the late 1990s.

Current practice

Texas has carried out the most executions in the modern era. Lethal injection is the primary method, with controversies over drugs, botched procedures and alternatives such as nitrogen hypoxia and firing squad authorised in some states. The federal government resumed executions in 2020–21 after a long hiatus, then returned to a moratorium.

Debate

Arguments concern deterrence, retribution, cost, racial and geographic disparities, wrongful convictions and exonerations from death row. About half of Americans have historically supported the penalty for murder, though support has softened. See Death row.
