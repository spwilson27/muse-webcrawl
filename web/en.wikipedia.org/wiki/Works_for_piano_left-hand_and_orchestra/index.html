Works for piano left-hand and orchestra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A number of works for piano left-hand and orchestra have been written, most famously at the commission of the Austrian pianist Paul Wittgenstein (1887–1961), who lost his right arm in the First World War and devoted his career to performing and commissioning music for the left hand alone. Composers responded with some of the most distinctive concertos of the 20th century.

Paul Wittgenstein and his commissions

Wittgenstein, brother of the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein, was a promising concert pianist when he was wounded on the Russian front in 1914 and his right arm was amputated. Determined to continue performing, he practised intensively and used his family's fortune to commission new works from leading composers. His commissions included concertos and concertante pieces by Maurice Ravel, Sergei Prokofiev, Paul Hindemith, Erich Wolfgang Korngold, Franz Schmidt, Benjamin Britten, and Richard Strauss.

The best known is Ravel's Piano Concerto for the Left Hand in D major (1929–30), a single-movement work of dark, jazz-inflected power that creates the illusion of two hands at the keyboard. Prokofiev's Piano Concerto No. 4 (1931) was never played by Wittgenstein, who reportedly did not understand it; it was premiered only in 1956, by Siegfried Rapp, another one-armed pianist. Hindemith's Klaviermusik mit Orchester, Op. 29 (1923) was likewise rejected and rediscovered only in 2004. Franz Schmidt, who wrote several works for Wittgenstein, produced left-hand concertos and chamber music that the pianist championed widely. Britten's Diversions, Op. 21 (1940) was written after Wittgenstein emigrated to the United States.

Before and after Wittgenstein

Earlier left-hand works were usually solo pieces: Charles-Valentin Alkan, Leopold Godowsky, and Alexander Scriabin (Prelude and Nocturne, Op. 9) wrote studies exploiting the left hand's reach across the keyboard. The 19th-century Hungarian pianist Géza Zichy, who lost his right arm in a hunting accident, composed a left-hand concerto and arranged many works. After the Second World War, pianists who had lost an arm — including Siegfried Rapp and, in a related tradition, Leon Fleisher, who lost the use of his right hand to focal dystonia for decades — inspired further works. Composers such as Ned Rorem, Lukas Foss, William Bolcom, and John Corigliano wrote left-hand concertos for Fleisher. The repertoire continues to grow, with modern pianists sometimes adopting the works for artistic rather than physical reasons.

Technique

Composers for the left hand exploit the thumb as a melody-carrying "fifth finger", rapid arpeggiation and leaps across the keyboard, and pedal resonance to fill harmonies. Effective writing avoids sustained two-voice counterpoint that a single hand cannot articulate, instead suggesting fullness through figuration.
