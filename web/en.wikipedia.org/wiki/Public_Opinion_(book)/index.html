Public Opinion (book)

Author: Walter Lippmann
Published: 1922
Publisher: Harcourt, Brace and Company
Subject: Media, democracy, public opinion

Public Opinion is a book by the American journalist Walter Lippmann, published in 1922. It is widely regarded as a foundational work in media studies, political science and the study of propaganda, and is famous for introducing the modern sense of the word "stereotype" into social thought. Lippmann argued that modern democracy rests on a flawed foundation, because citizens cannot have direct knowledge of the complex world they are asked to govern.

Summary

Lippmann begins with the concept of the "pseudo-environment": between people and the world stands the mediated picture they form from news, reports and images. Because the real environment is too big and fleeting for direct acquaintance, people act on "the pictures in our heads", which are shaped by stereotypes — simplified, culturally inherited preconceptions through which facts are filtered before they are seen. He examined the press of his day and concluded that newspapers could not reliably supply the accurate, complete information democratic theory assumed, owing to censorship, limited resources, the compression of events into brief dispatches, and the commercial structure of news.

Later chapters analyse the "manufacture of consent" — a phrase later popularised by Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky — the role of symbols and leadership in mobilising publics, and the limits of self-governing publics. Lippmann proposed that organised intelligence bureaus of experts should supply decision-makers with reliable knowledge, a position that drew criticism from the philosopher John Dewey, whose response in The Public and Its Problems (1927) defended democratic participation; their exchange is known as the Lippmann–Dewey debate.

Reception and influence

Public Opinion shaped twentieth-century thinking about propaganda, agenda-setting and the social responsibility of the press, and remains a standard text in journalism and communication courses. Its pessimism about citizens' capacities continues to be contested by democratic theorists.
