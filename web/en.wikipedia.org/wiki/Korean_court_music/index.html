Korean court music

Korean name (Hangul)궁중음악
Principal categoriesAak, tangak, hyangak
Key institutionNational Gugak Center

Korean court music comprises the music and dance performed at the court of Korean monarchs, especially during the Joseon dynasty (1392–1910). Traditionally the repertoire is divided into three categories according to origin: aak, Confucian ritual music derived from Chinese yayue; tangak, music of Chinese origin adapted at court; and hyangak, native Korean court music.

Categories

Aak was performed at ceremonies such as the royal ancestral rites at Jongmyo shrine and the Munmyo Confucian shrine, with large ensembles of stone chimes (pyeongyeong), bell chimes (pyeonjong), flutes, and string instruments, accompanied by stately ritual dances (ilmu). The Jongmyo ritual music was inscribed by UNESCO on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Tangak, literally "music from Tang (China)," includes pieces such as Boheoja and Nakyangchun that entered Korea during the Goryeo period; Nakyangchun later became known in the West through composer Lou Harrison's arrangements. Hyangak encompasses indigenous court pieces such as Yeominrak and the orchestral suite Yongsan hoesang, and uses characteristic Korean instruments including the geomungo and gayageum zithers, the daegeum flute, and the haegeum fiddle.

Institutions and preservation

During the Joseon dynasty court music was administered by the Jangagwon, the royal music bureau. Today the tradition is preserved and performed by the National Gugak Center in Seoul, founded in 1951 as the successor to the royal music institute, which trains court musicians (many designated holders of Important Intangible Cultural Properties) and performs the surviving repertoire.
