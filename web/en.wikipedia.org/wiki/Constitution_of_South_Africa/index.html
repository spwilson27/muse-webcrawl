Constitution of South Africa

The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996 is the supreme law of South Africa. It provides the legal foundation for the existence of the republic, sets out the rights and duties of its citizens, and defines the structure of the government. It was enacted by the Constitutional Assembly and promulgated by President Nelson Mandela on 18 December 1996, taking effect on 4 February 1997. It replaced the Interim Constitution of 1993, under which the first post-apartheid elections were held.

History

The constitution was the product of negotiations between the apartheid government and liberation movements in the early 1990s, followed by drafting in the Constitutional Assembly elected in 1994. The Constitutional Court was required to certify that the text complied with 34 agreed constitutional principles; it initially refused certification in September 1996, and after amendments certified the text in December 1996.

Contents

The constitution has a preamble and 14 chapters containing 244 sections. Chapter 1 sets out founding provisions: human dignity, equality, human rights, non-racialism and non-sexism, and universal adult suffrage. Chapter 2 is the Bill of Rights, among the most expansive in the world, covering civil and political rights as well as socio-economic rights such as housing, health care, food, water, and social security. Other chapters establish Parliament, the President and executive, the courts including the Constitutional Court, provinces, and independent institutions supporting democracy.

Amendments

The constitution has been amended numerous times, generally requiring a two-thirds majority in the National Assembly; amendments affecting the Bill of Rights or provincial matters have additional requirements. Notable amendments adjusted floor-crossing rules (later repealed) and provincial boundaries.
