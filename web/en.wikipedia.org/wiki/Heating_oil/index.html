Heating oil

Heating oil (known generally as fuel oil in the United States and Canada, and as gas oil in the United Kingdom for one grade) is a low-viscosity liquid petroleum product used as a fuel oil for furnaces and boilers in buildings. It is similar to diesel fuel and is classified as a distillate fuel oil; the most common grade for home heating in North America is No. 2 fuel oil, while the UK uses 28-second kerosene ("heating oil") and 35-second gas oil for domestic and commercial boilers respectively. In the United States heating oil is often dyed red to distinguish it from taxed on-road diesel.

Use

Heating oil is used mainly in the northeastern United States, Atlantic Canada and parts of Europe (Ireland is heavily dependent on kerosene heating). It is delivered by truck to storage tanks and burned in oil-fired boilers and furnaces, producing roughly 138,500 BTU per U.S. gallon. Use has been declining for decades as households convert to natural gas, electricity and heat pumps, driven by cost, convenience and decarbonisation policy.

Market

Prices follow crude oil and diesel markets and are strongly seasonal, peaking in winter. The NYMEX heating oil futures contract (now traded as ULSD heating oil) is a benchmark: since 2012–2020 regional rules in the U.S. Northeast phased in ultra-low-sulfur heating oil (15 ppm), aligning the product closely with ultra-low-sulfur diesel and reducing emissions and burner maintenance.

Environmental issues

Burning heating oil emits carbon dioxide, particulates, nitrogen oxides and (for higher-sulfur grades) sulfur dioxide; leaking domestic tanks are a common source of soil and groundwater contamination. Several jurisdictions plan phase-outs or boiler standards to reduce oil heating as part of climate policy.
