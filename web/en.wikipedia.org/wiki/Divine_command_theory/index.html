Divine command theory

The giving of the Ten Commandments is a classic image of divinely commanded morality.

Divine command theory (also known as theological voluntarism) is a meta-ethical theory which proposes that an action's status as morally good is equivalent to whether it is commanded by God. The theory holds that moral obligations arise from God's commands, so that what is right is right because God wills it. Variants of the view have been defended in Jewish, Christian and Islamic philosophy.

The Euthyphro dilemma

The most famous objection derives from Plato's dialogue Euthyphro: is an action good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? If the former, morality appears arbitrary — God could in principle have commanded cruelty. If the latter, morality is independent of God, and the theory fails. Defenders such as Robert Merrihew Adams have proposed modified versions, identifying moral obligation with the commands of a loving God, to blunt the dilemma.

Other criticisms and defences

Critics raise the epistemological problem of knowing God's commands, the problem of conflicting scriptural injunctions, and the difficulty of applying the theory for non-believers. Supporters reply that divine command theory provides an objective foundation for morality grounded in the nature and will of God, and note its deep roots in the Abrahamic traditions.
