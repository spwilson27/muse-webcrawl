Swagman

A swagman (also called a swaggie, sundowner, or tussocker) was an itinerant worker in Australia and New Zealand who travelled on foot between farms and stations carrying his belongings in a swag — a bedroll slung over his shoulder, often with a billy can. Swagmen were especially common during the economic depressions of the 1890s and 1930s, when unemployed men took to the road seeking casual work such as shearing, fencing, or harvesting.

Culture

The swagman is a romanticised figure in Australian folklore, celebrated in bush ballads and the literature of Henry Lawson and Banjo Paterson. The most famous depiction is the jolly swagman of Paterson's 1895 song "Waltzing Matilda", Australia's unofficial anthem, who camps by a billabong under a coolibah tree. Customs of the road included the expectation that a station would provide a "sundowner" meal and shelter in return for little or no work, and the etiquette of the swagman's call at homesteads.

Decline

The lifestyle declined with post-war prosperity, mechanisation of rural work, motor transport, and the provision of unemployment relief in towns. The word survives in expressions such as the "swag" still used for a bedroll in camping, and the swagman remains an emblem of Australian egalitarianism and the bush tradition.
