Celluloid

Celluloid objects, including imitation-ivory billiard balls.

Celluloid is a class of materials produced from nitrocellulose and camphor, generally regarded as the first thermoplastic. Developed in the 1860s and 1870s—notably by John Wesley Hyatt in the United States—it was the first successful synthetic plastic and was widely used before being largely replaced by safer, more stable polymers.

History

Alexander Parkes had earlier exhibited "Parkesine," a nitrocellulose material, in 1862. Hyatt, seeking a substitute for ivory in billiard balls amid a $10,000 prize offer, patented his process in 1870 and founded the Celluloid Manufacturing Company. Celluloid could be coloured, moulded, and machined to imitate ivory, tortoiseshell, amber, and mother-of-pearl.

Uses

Celluloid was used for combs, brushes, knife handles, collars and cuffs, toys, table-tennis balls, and guitar picks. Its most consequential use was as a flexible film base for photography and motion pictures from the 1890s onward, which is why "celluloid" became a synonym for cinema itself.

Flammability and decline

Nitrocellulose is highly flammable and decomposes over time, making celluloid film archives hazardous and subject to decay; nitrate film fires were a serious danger in cinemas and storage vaults. Cellulose acetate "safety film" and later plastics displaced celluloid for most purposes during the 20th century, though it persists in a few niche products such as table-tennis balls (largely phased out in the 2010s) and fountain pens.
