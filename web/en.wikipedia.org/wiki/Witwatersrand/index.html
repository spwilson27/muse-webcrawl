Witwatersrand

The Witwatersrand (Afrikaans for "ridge of white waters") is a 56-kilometre-long (35 mi) ridge of high ground in South Africa, famous as the centre of the world's largest known gold reserves. Located mainly in Gauteng province, it is the site of Johannesburg, which was founded there in 1886 after the discovery of gold.

Geology

The Witwatersrand basin consists of ancient sedimentary rocks, chiefly conglomerates known as "reefs", deposited some 2.7 to 3 billion years ago in the Archaean era. Gold occurs in thin layers within these conglomerates, along with uranium. The gold is thought to have been concentrated by ancient river systems, making the deposit a fossil placer. The Vredefort impact structure, formed by a meteorite strike about 2 billion years ago, lies at the basin's southwestern edge and helped preserve the gold-bearing strata.

Gold rush and mining

Gold was discovered on the Witwatersrand in 1886, triggering a gold rush and the founding of Johannesburg. The mines of the region have produced an estimated 40% of all the gold ever mined in human history — more than 1.5 billion ounces. Mining moved progressively deeper as surface deposits were exhausted, eventually reaching depths of nearly 4 km at mines such as TauTona and Mponeng, among the deepest mines on Earth.

Economy and legacy

The Witwatersrand goldfields made South Africa the world's leading gold producer for much of the 20th century and shaped the country's economy and politics, including the system of migrant labour that underpinned apartheid-era mining. Although gold production has declined since its 1970 peak of about 1,000 tonnes per year, the region remains the economic heart of South Africa, and the conurbation is commonly called the "Wits" or "Rand".
