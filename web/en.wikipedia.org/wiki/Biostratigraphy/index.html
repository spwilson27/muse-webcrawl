Biostratigraphy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biostratigraphy is the branch of stratigraphy which focuses on correlating and assigning relative ages of rock strata by using the fossils contained within them. Its primary objective is the correlation of strata in different sections by demonstrating that the fossils in a particular interval follow the same succession of evolutionary change, a principle sometimes called the law of faunal succession.

Principles

The method rests on the observation, made systematically by William Smith in the 1790s while surveying canal routes in England, that sedimentary strata contain characteristic fossil assemblages, and that these assemblages succeed one another vertically in a regular, predictable order that can be recognised across wide areas. Because biological evolution is (in the relevant sense) unrepeatable, a fossil assemblage identifies the time interval in which it was deposited.

Biozones

The fundamental unit of biostratigraphy is the biozone, a body of strata defined by its fossil content. Common types include range zones (defined by the total range of a taxon), assemblage zones (defined by a characteristic association of fossils), and abundance zones. Particularly useful are index fossils — organisms that were geographically widespread but lived for only a short interval, such as graptolites, ammonites, foraminifera and conodonts. The boundaries of the geological timescale are largely defined by biostratigraphic events, the first and last appearances of key species.

Applications

Biostratigraphy is a standard tool in petroleum exploration, where microfossils in drill cuttings are used to correlate well sections, and in academic geology for refining the geologic time scale, complementing radiometric dating and magnetostratigraphy.
