Archimedean property

In abstract algebra and analysis, the Archimedean property, named after the ancient Greek mathematician Archimedes of Syracuse, is a property held by some algebraic structures, such as ordered or normed groups, fields, and vector spaces. Roughly speaking, a structure is Archimedean if it contains no infinitely large or infinitely small elements: for any two positive elements x and y, some multiple of x exceeds y. Structures lacking the property are called non-Archimedean.

Definition for ordered fields

An ordered field F has the Archimedean property if for every element x in F there exists a natural number n such that n > x, regarding n as the sum of n ones. Equivalently, the set of natural numbers is unbounded above in F, and there are no infinitesimals: if ε > 0 is smaller than 1/n for every natural number n, then no such nonzero ε exists.

History

The property appears as the definition in Book V of Euclid's Elements, where magnitudes are said to "have a ratio" when a multiple of each can exceed the other; the formulation is attributed to Eudoxus of Cnidus. Archimedes employed the axiom prominently in his method of exhaustion, though he credited Eudoxus. Otto Stolz coined the term "Archimedean axiom" in the 1880s.

Examples and counterexamples

The rational numbers and the real numbers are Archimedean: any real number is exceeded by some integer, a fact that underlies analysis proofs such as the density of the rationals in the reals. By contrast, non-Archimedean structures include the field of rational functions with a suitable ordering, formal Laurent series fields, hyperreal number systems used in non-standard analysis, and valued fields with respect to a non-Archimedean absolute value, where the strong triangle inequality |x + y| ≤ max(|x|, |y|) replaces the usual one. Every Archimedean ordered field can be embedded in the real numbers, making the reals the largest Archimedean field in a precise sense.
