Jump cut

Jump cut
TypeFilm editing technique / transition
Related conceptsContinuity editing; match cut; smash cut
Key exampleBreathless (Jean-Luc Godard, 1960)

A jump cut is a cut in film editing in which a single continuous shot is broken into two parts, with a piece of footage removed, so that the subject's position within the frame appears to jump abruptly. Because the camera position and framing change little or not at all (typically by less than 30 degrees), the cut breaks the rules of classical continuity editing and produces a visible, jarring discontinuity in time and space.

History

The jump cut existed from cinema's earliest days as a technical accident or a trick effect — Georges Méliès used stop-trick substitutions that produced jump-like discontinuities. Within the mature Hollywood continuity system, however, it was regarded as an error to be avoided, typically corrected by the 30-degree rule: consecutive shots of the same subject should differ in angle by at least 30 degrees to read as a deliberate new view.

The technique was transformed into an expressive device by the French New Wave. Jean-Luc Godard's Breathless (À bout de souffle, 1960) famously cut stretches from within continuous shots — famously in Patricia's car ride — reportedly to shorten the film, producing a nervous, elliptical energy that became a signature of modernist cinema. What distributors first took for incompetence became a deliberate aesthetic of fragmentation and immediacy.

Uses and effects

Jump cuts can compress time, create agitation or psychological unease, emphasise a character's instability, or draw attention to the film's construction in a Brechtian spirit. They appear prominently in the work of Godard and other New Wave directors, and in later films as diverse as Snatch (2000) and Whiplash (2014). The technique migrated to television and became the dominant vernacular of online video: vloggers routinely cut out pauses mid-sentence, so that a speaker jumps slightly within a static frame — a once-avant-garde rupture now so familiar as to be nearly invisible.

Related techniques

A jump cut should be distinguished from the match cut, which joins two shots on a visual similarity; the smash cut, an abrupt transition to a sharply contrasting scene; and the cutaway, which temporarily leaves the main action. Continuity editors also use "jump cuts" loosely for any violation of screen direction or the 180-degree rule, though strictly the term refers to temporal removal within a shot.
