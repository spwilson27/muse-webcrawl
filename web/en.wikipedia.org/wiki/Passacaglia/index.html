Passacaglia

A passacaglia (Italian, from Spanish pasacalle, "to pass along the street") is a musical form of continuous variation over a repeating bass line or harmonic progression, usually in triple metre and a minor key, and of a serious, stately character. It is closely related to the chaconne, and the boundary between the two has been debated by musicologists for centuries.

History

The form began in early-17th-century Spain as a guitar ritornello between verses of a song, and was cultivated in Italy by Frescobaldi. Its Baroque summit is German: Buxtehude's organ passacaglias and above all J. S. Bach's Passacaglia and Fugue in C minor, BWV 582, whose eight-bar ostinato supports twenty variations and a fugue built from the theme's first half.

Later examples

Composers revived the form in the 19th and 20th centuries: the finale of Brahms's Fourth Symphony (often classed as a chaconne), the passacaglia movements in Webern's Op. 1, Shostakovich's orchestral and chamber works, Britten's operas, and the use of repeating bass patterns in rock and electronic music, where the technique merges with the loop.
