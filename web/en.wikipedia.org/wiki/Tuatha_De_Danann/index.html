The Tuatha Dé Danann (Irish: [ˈt̪ˠuə(hə) dʲeː ˈd̪ˠan̪ˠən̪ˠ], meaning "the peoples of the goddess Danu"), also known by the earlier name Tuath Dé ("tribe of the gods" or "divine tribe"), are a supernatural race in Irish mythology. Many of them are thought to represent deities of pre-Christian Gaelic Ireland.

The Tuath Dé are often depicted as ancient kings and heroes. In the Lebor Gabála Érenn ("The Book of the Taking of Ireland"), a pseudo-history compiled in the Middle Ages, they are presented as the fifth group to settle Ireland, arriving in dark clouds of mist and defeating the Fir Bolg at the First Battle of Mag Tuired. They were later defeated by the Milesians, the ancestors of the Irish people, and withdrew into the Otherworld, becoming the Aos Sí, the people of the mounds.

Legendary history

According to the Lebor Gabála, the Tuatha Dé Danann descended from Nemed, leader of a previous wave of settlers in Ireland. They came from four northern cities — Falias, Gorias, Murias and Finias — where they acquired their occult skills and their four legendary treasures.

They arrived in Ireland on the first of May, shrouded in a mist that hid them for three days. Their king, Nuada, lost an arm in the First Battle of Mag Tuired and was fitted with a silver arm by the physician Dian Cécht, earning him the name Nuada Airgetlám ("Nuada of the Silver Hand"). At the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, they defeated the Fomorians, led by the tyrant Balor, who was slain by his own grandson Lugh.

The four treasures

The Tuatha Dé Danann brought four magical treasures from their four cities:

  • The Spear of Lugh, which guaranteed victory to its wielder;
  • The Sword of Nuada, from which no one could escape once it was drawn;
  • The Cauldron of the Dagda, from which no company ever went away unsatisfied;
  • The Lia Fáil (Stone of Destiny), which cried out under the true king of Ireland.

Notable members

Prominent members of the Tuath Dé include Nuada, king of the gods; Lugh, the many-skilled hero; the Dagda, a father-figure and chief; the Morrígan, goddess of war and fate; Brigid, goddess of poetry and smithcraft; Manannán mac Lir, god of the sea; Dian Cécht, god of healing; and Ogma, inventor of the Ogham script.

Defeat and the Otherworld

When the Milesians invaded from the south, the Tuatha Dé Danann were defeated and, after a consultation with the Milesian poet Amergin, agreed to divide Ireland: the Milesians would rule the land above ground, while the Tuath Dé withdrew below, into the hollow hills and burial mounds known as sídhe. In later folklore they became the Aos Sí, the fairy people.
