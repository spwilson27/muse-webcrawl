Attenuation coefficient

Attenuation coefficient
Symbolμ
SI unitReciprocal metre (m−1)
GovernsBeer–Lambert law

The attenuation coefficient (also called the linear attenuation coefficient, symbol μ) is a quantity that characterises how readily a beam of radiation — light, X-rays, gamma rays, sound or particles — is absorbed or scattered as it passes through a material. It is defined through the Beer–Lambert law: the intensity decreases exponentially with distance, I = I0 e−μx, where x is the path length in the medium.

Definitions and variants

  • Mass attenuation coefficient (μ/ρ) – the linear coefficient divided by density, useful because it is nearly independent of the material's physical state; widely tabulated for X-ray and gamma-ray energies.
  • Absorption coefficient – counts only energy actually absorbed; the attenuation coefficient also includes scattering out of the beam.
  • Molar attenuation (extinction) coefficient – used in spectroscopy for solutions, relating absorbance to concentration.

Applications

Attenuation coefficients are tabulated by standards bodies such as NIST for all elements and many compounds, and are essential in radiography and CT scanning (where Hounsfield units derive from them), radiation shielding design, optical-fibre engineering (expressed in decibels per kilometre) and atmospheric optics.
