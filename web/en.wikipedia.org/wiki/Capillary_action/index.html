Capillary action

Capillary action
Also calledCapillarity, capillary motion, capillary effect, wicking
CausesSurface tension, adhesion, cohesion
Governing relationJurin's law
ApplicationsPlants (xylem), chromatography, ink, lamps, microfluidics

Capillary action (sometimes called capillarity, capillary motion, capillary rise, capillary effect, or wicking) is the process by which a liquid flows, sometimes against gravity, in a narrow space without the assistance of, or even in opposition to, external forces like gravity. The effect can be seen in the drawing up of liquids between the hairs of a paint-brush, in a thin tube, in porous materials such as paper and plaster, in some non-porous materials such as sand and liquefied carbon fibre, or in a biological cell. It occurs because of intermolecular forces between the liquid and surrounding solid surfaces.

If the diameter of the tube is sufficiently small, then the combination of surface tension (which is caused by cohesion within the liquid) and adhesive forces between the liquid and container wall act to propel the liquid.

Explanation

Capillary action results from the interplay of three phenomena:

  • Surface tension — the tendency of a liquid surface to contract, caused by cohesive forces between molecules.
  • Adhesion — the attraction between the liquid and the solid wall. If adhesion to the wall is stronger than cohesion within the liquid, the liquid wets the wall and its edge (the meniscus) is pulled upward.
  • Curved meniscus and pressure difference — the curved surface creates a pressure difference across it (described by the Young–Laplace equation) that draws the liquid column upward until it is balanced by the weight of the column.

Whether a liquid rises or is depressed depends on the contact angle: water in clean glass has a contact angle near 0° and rises; mercury, which does not wet glass (contact angle about 140°), is depressed in a capillary tube.

Jurin's law

The height h to which a liquid will rise (or fall) in a capillary tube is given by Jurin's law:

h = (2γ cos θ) / (ρ g r)

where γ is the liquid–air surface tension, θ the contact angle, ρ the density of the liquid, g the acceleration due to gravity, and r the radius of the tube. The rise is thus inversely proportional to the tube's radius: the narrower the tube, the higher the liquid climbs. For water in a tube of 1 mm radius the rise is about 1.5 cm; in a tube of 0.1 mm it is about 15 cm, and in the micrometre-scale conduits of soil and plant tissue it can be metres.

Examples and applications

  • Everyday wicking: Paper towels, sponges, candle and oil-lamp wicks, fountain-pen feeds and the action of blotting paper all rely on capillary action.
  • Plants: Capillarity within the microscopic xylem vessels contributes to the movement of water from roots to leaves, together with transpiration pull and root pressure — though capillarity alone cannot lift water to the tops of tall trees.
  • Soil and buildings: Groundwater rises through soil pores by capillarity; "rising damp" in masonry walls is capillary action and is countered by damp-proof courses.
  • Medicine and chemistry: Capillary tubes draw blood samples (as in a finger-prick test); thin-layer and paper chromatography separate mixtures as solvent climbs a stationary phase; lateral-flow tests (such as home pregnancy and COVID-19 tests) move samples along a membrane by capillary flow.
  • Microfluidics: Lab-on-a-chip devices pump fluids through hair-fine channels using capillary forces, without moving parts.

History

The phenomenon was observed by Leonardo da Vinci, and the first recorded observation of capillary action is often attributed to Niccolò Cabeo (1646). Francis Hauksbee and James Jurin carried out experiments in the early eighteenth century; Jurin's law bears his name. Thomas Young (1805) and Pierre-Simon Laplace developed the theory of surface tension that explains it, and Albert Einstein's first published paper (1901) was on capillarity.
