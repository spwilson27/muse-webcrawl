Motor unit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A motor unit: one motor neuron together with all the muscle fibers it supplies.

A motor unit is made up of a motor neuron and all of the skeletal muscle fibers it innervates. Groups of motor units often work together as a motor pool to co-ordinate the contractions of a single muscle. The concept was proposed by Charles Scott Sherrington.

When a motor neuron is activated, all of the muscle fibers innervated by that motor unit are stimulated and contract. The number of muscle fibers within each unit varies: muscles controlling fine movements, such as those of the eye and fingers, have few fibers per unit (as few as three to ten), allowing precise control, whereas large muscles of the limbs and trunk may have a thousand or more fibers per unit.

Recruitment

The nervous system grades the force of a muscle contraction by recruiting additional motor units. According to Henneman's size principle, motor units are recruited in order from smallest to largest: slow-twitch, fatigue-resistant units first, and large, fast-twitch, powerful units last. Increasing the firing frequency of active units (rate coding) also raises force.

Types

Motor units are conventionally classified as slow (S), fast fatigue-resistant (FR), fast fatigable (FF), and fast intermediate (FI), based on the contractile speed and fatigability of their fibers. The motor neuron's properties match those of the fibers it supplies. Activity of motor units is recorded clinically by electromyography (EMG); enlargement or loss of motor units is a diagnostic sign in diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and poliomyelitis.
