Vice president

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A vice president is an officer in government or business who is below a president in rank, in government typically the designated successor and understudy of the head of state or government. The archetype is the Vice President of the United States — an office the Constitution equips with two functions (succeeding the president, and presiding over the Senate with a tie-breaking vote) and which John Adams called “the most insignificant office that ever the invention of man contrived”, though modern occupants, from the late twentieth century, have become governing partners with staffs, portfolios and policy roles (the Mondale model of 1976 onward).

Vice-presidential and deputy offices recur wherever a single-person executive needs continuity insurance: presidential systems across the Americas, Africa and Asia elect vice presidents (usually on a joint ticket), parliamentary systems use deputy prime ministers (informal, without succession rights), and federations and parties replicate the pattern. The office's paradox is structural: chosen for ticket balance or factional peace, its holder is simultaneously heir apparent and political irrelevance — until the succession moment (nine US vice presidents have succeeded on death or resignation) makes it the most important office in the state.
