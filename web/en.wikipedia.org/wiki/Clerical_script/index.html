Clerical script

The clerical script (Chinese: 隸書; pinyin: lìshū) is an archaic style of Chinese calligraphy which evolved from the seal script during the Han dynasty. It was the dominant script of the Han period and remained in use, especially for formal and decorative purposes, long after it was superseded for everyday writing by the regular script.

History

Clerical script developed from the writing of clerks and scribes in the Qin dynasty, who simplified the laborious seal script for administrative efficiency. Tradition credits the Qin official Cheng Miao with its invention. It became the official script after the Han dynasty adopted it, and its characteristic "silkworm head and swallow tail" horizontal strokes, with a pronounced wave and flared ends, defined the look of Han inscriptions on stone steles.

Characteristics

Characters in clerical script are typically wider than they are tall, with compressed vertical extent and heavy horizontal strokes. The transition from seal to clerical script — known as the libian or "clerical change" — was a turning point in the history of Chinese writing, replacing the pictographic, curvilinear forms of seal script with the straight-line, component-based structure of all later Chinese scripts.
