Peer pressure

Peer pressure is a direct or indirect influence on peers, i.e., members of a social group with similar interests and experiences. Peers are more likely to influence a person's beliefs or behaviour because individuals typically wish to belong to or be accepted by the group. Peer pressure can be positive — encouraging study, exercise, or prosocial behaviour — but the term is most often used for negative influence, such as pressure to use alcohol or drugs, engage in risky behaviour, or conform to harmful norms.

Types

Direct peer pressure is explicit: a person is asked, teased, or dared to do something. Indirect peer pressure is unspoken: a person changes behaviour to fit in with what they observe peers doing, such as dressing a certain way. Self-imposed pressure arises when an individual imagines group expectations that peers may not actually hold.

Adolescence

Susceptibility to peer pressure is generally considered to peak in adolescence. Research in developmental psychology, including the classic conformity experiments of Solomon Asch adapted for peer groups, shows that adolescents are more sensitive than adults to the presence and opinions of peers, partly because the brain systems involved in reward processing mature earlier than those involved in impulse control. Peer influence in this period is linked to risk-taking behaviours including smoking, drinking, and dangerous driving, especially when peers are present.

Resistance and positive effects

Factors that reduce harmful peer influence include supportive parenting, self-esteem, and membership of groups with constructive norms. Peer pressure can also be deliberately harnessed for good, for example in peer-led health education and anti-bullying programmes.
