Aluminium sulfate is a chemical compound with the formula Al₂(SO₄)₃. It is soluble in water and is mainly used as a coagulating and flocculating agent in the purification of drinking water and in wastewater treatment, and in paper manufacturing, where it was historically the "alum" of the sizing process. It typically occurs as a hydrate, most commonly Al₂(SO₄)₃·18H₂O.

Production

Aluminium sulfate is produced industrially by reacting aluminium hydroxide, bauxite, or alumina-rich clays with sulfuric acid, and can be prepared in the laboratory by dissolving aluminium metal in sulfuric acid. It also occurs naturally as the rare mineral alunogen, formed in oxidising pyrite deposits and volcanic environments.

Uses

  • Water treatment: its principal use. In water, Al³⁺ hydrolyses to form gelatinous aluminium hydroxide flocs that sweep up suspended particles and colour, which are then removed by sedimentation and filtration.
  • Paper: traditionally combined with rosin to size paper, though acidic alum sizing degrades paper permanence and has largely given way to neutral/alkaline processes.
  • Textiles and leather: as a mordant in dyeing and in tanning.
  • Gardening: to acidify soil and turn hydrangea flowers blue.
  • Other: in fire-extinguishing foams, antiperspirant formulations (related aluminium chlorohydrate is more common), styptic preparations, and as the food additive E520 in some pickling and baking applications.

Chemistry and safety

Aqueous solutions are acidic owing to hydrolysis of the hydrated Al³⁺ ion. On strong heating the salt decomposes to alumina and sulfur trioxide. Aluminium sulfate has low acute toxicity but is an eye and skin irritant in concentrated form; its large-scale use in water treatment is controlled because residual aluminium in finished water is limited by drinking-water standards.

1988 Camelford incident

In July 1988, twenty tonnes of aluminium sulfate were accidentally delivered into the wrong tank at the Lowermoor water treatment works supplying Camelford, Cornwall, England, contaminating the drinking water of some 20,000 people. The incident — one of Britain's worst water pollution events — caused immediate illness and a long public controversy over possible long-term health effects and the authorities' handling of the emergency.
