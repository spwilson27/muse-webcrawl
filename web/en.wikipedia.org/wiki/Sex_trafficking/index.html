Sex trafficking

Sex trafficking is a form of human trafficking in which people are recruited, transported, harboured, or obtained by force, fraud, or coercion for the purpose of commercial sexual exploitation. Under the United Nations Palermo Protocol (2000) and national laws such as the United States Trafficking Victims Protection Act (2000), any commercial sex act involving a minor is classified as trafficking regardless of whether force or coercion can be shown.

Prevalence and victims

The International Labour Organization has estimated that forced commercial sexual exploitation accounts for a substantial share of the roughly 28 million people in forced labour worldwide, and generates the largest profits of any form of forced labour. Victims are disproportionately women and girls, though men and boys are also trafficked. Recruitment commonly occurs through false job offers, romantic manipulation ("loverboy" methods), abduction, or the exploitation of poverty, addiction, homelessness, migration status, or prior abuse. Trafficking may be domestic as well as cross-border; movement across a border is not required by most modern definitions.

Methods and settings

Victims may be exploited in brothels, escort services, massage businesses, pornography production, strip clubs, street prostitution, and arrangements advertised online. Traffickers commonly control victims through debt bondage, confiscation of documents, threats, violence, isolation, and psychological coercion.

Responses

International and national responses are conventionally framed around prevention, prosecution of traffickers, and protection of victims (the "3P" paradigm), supplemented in some frameworks by partnership. Measures include specialised police units and task forces, victim identification training for front-line workers, shelters and legal aid, hotlines such as the US National Human Trafficking Hotline, demand-side offences criminalising the purchase of sexual services from trafficking victims, and platform-liability laws such as FOSTA-SESTA (2018) in the United States. Critics debate the effectiveness and side effects of some approaches, including conflation of trafficking with consensual sex work, the reliability of prevalence statistics, and the adequacy of victim services.
