The Battle of Pinkie Cleugh, sometimes known as the Battle of Musselburgh, was fought on 10 September 1547 on the banks of the River Esk near Musselburgh, Scotland. The last pitched battle between Scotland and England as independent states before the Union of the Crowns, it was a catastrophic defeat for the Scots, remembered as "Black Saturday".

Background

The battle was the climax of the "Rough Wooing", England's attempt to force a marriage between the infant Mary, Queen of Scots and Edward VI, and thereby an Anglo-Scottish union. After the Scots repudiated the Treaty of Greenwich, the English Lord Protector, the Duke of Somerset, invaded Scotland with a large army supported by a fleet in the Firth of Forth.

The battle

The Scottish army under the Regent Arran occupied a strong position west of the Esk. Drawn out of position by an exchange with English cavalry and by naval gunfire, the Scots advanced in their large pike formations. Scottish pikemen thrown into disorder crossing marshy ground were struck by English cavalry, then subjected to combined fire from arquebusiers, archers, artillery and the guns of the fleet offshore. The formations collapsed and the retreat became a slaughter, with thousands of Scots killed in the pursuit toward Edinburgh and Dalkeith. English losses were comparatively light.

Aftermath

Despite the victory, Somerset could not hold Scotland: English garrisons were established at places such as Haddington, but the Scots turned to France, and Mary was sent to safety there in 1548, betrothed to the Dauphin. Pinkie thus failed in its political object, though it remains notable as an early example of combined-arms warfare integrating land and naval firepower.
