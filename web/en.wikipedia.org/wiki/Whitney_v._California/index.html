Whitney v. California
Citation: 274 U.S. 357 (1927)
Court: United States Supreme Court
Decided: May 16, 1927
Holding: Upheld conviction under California's Criminal Syndicalism Act
Overruled by: Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969)

Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357 (1927), was a United States Supreme Court decision upholding the conviction of Charlotte Anita Whitney, a wealthy socialist activist, under California's 1919 Criminal Syndicalism Act for helping to establish the Communist Labor Party of America. The Court held that a state could punish speech advocating the violent overthrow of government when the speech posed a danger to public peace and security.

Background

Whitney, a member of a prominent California family, was arrested after speaking at a convention in Oakland in 1919 that organised a state branch of the Communist Labor Party. She was convicted of criminal syndicalism. On appeal she argued the Act violated her rights to freedom of speech and assembly under the Fourteenth Amendment.

Decision and concurrence

Justice Sanford's majority opinion deferred to the legislature's judgment that such advocacy was dangerous. The case is best remembered for Justice Louis Brandeis's concurrence (joined by Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.), one of the most celebrated defences of free speech in American law. Brandeis argued that the remedy for harmful speech "is more speech, not enforced silence," and that only an emergency — serious evil, imminent and probable — could justify suppression. Because Whitney's counsel had not properly raised a "clear and present danger" argument, Brandeis concurred in the judgment itself.

Legacy

Whitney was pardoned by California's governor shortly after the decision. The Court's unanimous framework endured until Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), which expressly overruled Whitney and adopted a stricter test protecting advocacy unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it.
