Overview

Navigation is the process of planning, recording and controlling the movement of a craft from one place to another — determining position, course and distance travelled, and avoiding dangers. It blends geography, astronomy, mathematics and seamanship, and its history is largely the history of finding latitude and longitude ever more reliably.

The oldest methods never disappeared: piloting by landmarks and depth soundings near coasts, and dead reckoning — advancing a known position by compass course and speed through the water — which filled the gaps between fixes.

Celestial and modern navigation

Celestial navigation fixes position from the measured altitudes of the Sun, Moon, planets and stars above the horizon, taken with a sextant and interpreted with an almanac and a chronometer. For two centuries it was the mariner's and later the aviator's global positioning system. The twentieth century added radio direction finding, radar, inertial navigation and the hyperbolic systems such as LORAN, before satellite navigation — above all GPS, which itself works in the geographic coordinates of geodesy — made continuous, automatic, metre-level positioning universal. Celestial methods are still taught as an independent backup that no jamming can disable.
