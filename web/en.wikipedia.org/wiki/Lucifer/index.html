Lucifer

Lucifer is the Latin name for the planet Venus in its appearance as the morning star ("light-bringer"). The word entered Christian tradition through the Latin Vulgate translation of Isaiah 14:12, where the Hebrew Helel ben Shahar ("shining one, son of the dawn") — a taunt addressed to a king of Babylon — was rendered Lucifer. In later tradition, the passage was reinterpreted as describing the fall of Satan, and "Lucifer" became a proper name for the Devil before his fall.

Etymology and scripture

Latin Lucifer combines lux (light) and ferre (to bear). In classical usage it simply denoted the morning star, and the New Testament even applies related imagery to Christ ("the bright morning star", Revelation 22:16); 2 Peter 1:19 uses the Greek phōsphoros, "light-bringer". Jerome's Vulgate used Lucifer in Isaiah 14:12 and a few other passages. Because Isaiah's oracle against Babylon was read alongside Ezekiel 28 and Luke 10:18 ("I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven"), church fathers such as Tertullian and Origen linked the text to Satan's fall, and the name stuck.

In literature and theology

Dante's Inferno places Lucifer frozen at the centre of the Earth, a gigantic three-faced figure chewing the great traitors. Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) made Lucifer/Satan a tragic, rebellious protagonist whose fall follows his refusal to submit after the creation of humankind. In modern usage, "Lucifer" is used variously by theologians as a synonym for Satan, by occult and Luciferian movements as a symbol of enlightenment and independence, and in popular culture — novels, television, and music — as a character ranging from tempter to sympathetic antihero.

Other uses

The name has also been borne by a 3rd-century bishop of Cagliari known for his rigorism ("Luciferianism"), by a genus of shrimp (Lucifer), and in astronomy as a historical name for Venus; the asteroid 1930 Lucifer commemorates the Latin word.
