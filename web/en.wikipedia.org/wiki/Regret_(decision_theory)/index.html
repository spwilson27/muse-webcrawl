Regret (decision theory)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In decision theory, regret is the difference between the payoff of the best action that could have been taken in hindsight and the payoff of the action actually taken, once the state of the world is known. A decision maker who anticipates this feeling may choose actions so as to minimise expected regret, an approach formalised in the minimax regret criterion proposed by Leonard J. Savage in 1951.

Minimax regret

The minimax regret approach to decision making under uncertainty constructs a regret matrix: for each state of nature, the regret of an action is the difference between the best payoff available in that state and the payoff the action yields. The decision maker then selects the action whose maximum regret across all states is smallest. Unlike Wald's maximin criterion, which considers only worst-case payoffs, minimax regret takes account of the opportunity loss of each choice.

Regret theory

In economics, regret theory, developed by Graham Loomes and Robert Sugden and separately by David Bell in the early 1980s, models choice under risk by assuming that people compare the outcome of their chosen option with the outcomes they would have obtained from rejected options, experiencing regret or rejoicing accordingly. The theory can explain certain violations of expected utility theory, such as simultaneous gambling and insurance purchasing.

Online learning

In machine learning and online optimisation, regret measures the cumulative difference between the loss of an algorithm's choices and the loss of the best fixed action in hindsight. Algorithms with sublinear regret, such as multiplicative weights and follow-the-regularised-leader methods, are guaranteed to perform nearly as well as the best fixed decision over time.
