Master shot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A master shot (or simply a master) is a film recording of an entire dramatised scene, usually a wide or long shot, from start to finish. In classical continuity editing the master establishes the geography of a scene and provides footage the editor can always cut back to; the scene is then "covered" by complementary setups — medium shots, close-ups, over-the-shoulder angles, inserts and reaction shots — filmed separately, whose action is matched to the master.

Production practice

Traditionally the master is shot first, once the director and cinematographer have blocked and lit the whole scene, because lighting and set dressing are arranged for the widest view and the closer setups are lit to match it. Actors are expected to repeat their performances consistently so that the coverage matches the master at the intended edit points. Having a complete master also guarantees that, whatever happens to the coverage, an editor can assemble the whole scene.

Some directors depart from the method: filming only the shots they intend to use ("cutting in the camera"), or designing long, choreographed masters that become the finished scene themselves. Variations include the two-shot (two characters in frame) and the group master used in multi-camera television, where several cameras record simultaneously and the master may be a continuous wide shot underpinning closer angles.

History

The practice grew up with the studio system in the 1910s–1920s as directors such as D. W. Griffith standardised analytical editing. The term "master shot" is also occasionally used for the principal, unbroken take of a special-effects or stunt sequence.
