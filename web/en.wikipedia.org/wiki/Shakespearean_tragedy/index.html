Shakespearean tragedy is the designation given to the plays of William Shakespeare that fall into the genre of tragedy. Unlike the histories, which draw on English chronicles, the tragedies typically centre on a high-status protagonist whose downfall and death result from a combination of character flaw (hamartia), fate, and circumstance. Shakespeare wrote approximately ten tragedies, among them Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, Macbeth, Antony and Cleopatra, Coriolanus, Julius Caesar, Romeo and Juliet, Timon of Athens, and Titus Andronicus.

Characteristics

Shakespearean tragedies share structural features inherited from Senecan and medieval drama — ghosts, revenge, madness, soliloquies, and a final scene strewn with bodies — but Shakespeare deepened the psychological interiority of his heroes. The classic critical account is A. C. Bradley's Shakespearean Tragedy (1904), which analysed the four great tragedies in terms of character: Othello's jealousy, Lear's pride, Macbeth's ambition, and Hamlet's indecision contribute to their falls, though Bradley stressed that catastrophe also requires a world of accident and evil beyond the hero's control.

Sources and performance

Many tragedies draw on Plutarch's Lives (in Thomas North's translation), Holinshed's Chronicles, and earlier plays and tales. The tragedies were written mainly in the decade after 1600, for the King's Men at the Globe and Blackfriars theatres. They remain among the most performed and studied works in world literature.
