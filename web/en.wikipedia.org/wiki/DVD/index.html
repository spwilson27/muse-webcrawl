The DVD (common initialism for Digital Versatile Disc or Digital Video Disc) is a digital optical disc data storage format invented and developed in 1995 and released in late 1996 in Japan and 1997 in the United States. It offers greater storage capacity than compact discs while having the same dimensions, and became the dominant medium for home video distribution and a common format for computer data and software.

Format

A standard DVD holds 4.7 GB on a single layer, or 8.5 GB in dual-layer form, using a red laser (650 nm) with tighter track pitch and more efficient error correction than CD. Variants include recordable DVD±R, rewritable DVD±RW and DVD-RAM, as well as 80 mm "mini DVD" discs. DVDs were read in DVD-ROM drives and standalone DVD players, and the format was extended by the high-definition successors HD DVD and Blu-ray, of which Blu-ray prevailed after a format war in the 2000s.

Applications

DVD-Video discs largely displaced VHS for film distribution by the mid-2000s, and DVDs were widely used for video games, software distribution and data backup. Sales declined from the 2010s as streaming video and downloads grew, though the format retains a significant library and collector market.
