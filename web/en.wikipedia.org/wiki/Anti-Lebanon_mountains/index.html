The Anti-Lebanon mountains (Arabic: جبال الشرقية, Jabal ash-Sharqi, "the Eastern Mountains") are a south-west–north-east trending mountain range that forms most of the border between Syria and Lebanon. The range runs roughly parallel to the Mount Lebanon range to its west, from which it is separated by the Beqaa Valley.

The name "Anti-Lebanon" comes from the Greek Anti-Libanus, "opposite Lebanon", reflecting its position facing the Lebanon range across the valley. Most of the range lies in Syria, where its eastern slopes descend toward Damascus; the border between the two countries follows the watershed crest for much of its length.

Geography

The mountains extend for about 150 kilometres, from the vicinity of Homs in the north to the Golan Heights in the south. The highest point is Mount Hermon (Jabal ash-Shaykh), at 2,814 metres, at the southern end of the range, where the borders of Lebanon, Syria and the Israeli-occupied Golan Heights meet. Other notable summits exceed 2,600 metres, and the range is snow-capped in winter.

Climate and environment

The Anti-Lebanon lies in the rain shadow of the Mount Lebanon range and is markedly drier, with steppe and semi-desert vegetation on its eastern slopes. Its western slopes receive more precipitation and support juniper and wild pistachio woodland. The range's snowmelt feeds springs that supply Damascus and the Beqaa; the Barada River, which waters the Syrian capital, rises in the Anti-Lebanon foothills.

History

The mountains have long formed a natural frontier. Ancient trade routes crossed them at passes linking Damascus with the Mediterranean coast. The slopes contain Roman temples and quarries, and the region figured in the Lebanese Civil War and the Syrian civil war, during which the border highlands around Qalamoun were contested by government forces, Hezbollah and rebel groups.
