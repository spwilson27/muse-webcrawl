Foo

The terms foobar (/ˈfuːbɑːr/), foo, bar, baz, qux, quux, and others are used as metasyntactic variables and placeholder names in computer programming and computer-related documentation. They have been used to name entities such as variables, functions, and commands whose exact identity is unimportant and serve only to demonstrate a concept.

History and usage

The term foobar is a common term used by engineers, and the terms can be traced to the 1930s and 1940s. In computer programming, the terms appear in the works of many computing facilities, and in the teaching of computer science, for example as example names for files, functions, and variables. The foo/bar pairing is documented in the Jargon File, the lexicon of hacker slang maintained since the 1970s.

Etymology

The origins of the terms are not definitively known. One hypothesis derives "foo" from the military slang acronym FUBAR ("fucked up beyond all recognition"), current during the Second World War, possibly itself influenced by the German word furchtbar ("terrible"). Other suggested sources include the Chinese word fu (福, "good fortune") and the comic strip Smokey Stover, whose title character drove a "foo mobile".
