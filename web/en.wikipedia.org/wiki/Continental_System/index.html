Continental System

The Continental System (French: Blocus continental) was the foreign economic policy of Napoleon during the Napoleonic Wars: a large-scale embargo against British trade, proclaimed by the Berlin Decree of 21 November 1806, intended to ruin Britain's economy and force it to make peace after the Battle of Trafalgar had ruled out invasion. It closed the ports of the French Empire and its allies and satellites to British goods — and, by requiring every European state to police smuggling and sacrifice its own commerce, it steadily corroded Napoleon's alliances.

Operation

The Berlin and Milan decrees declared the British Isles in a state of blockade (a "paper blockade" Napoleon lacked the navy to enforce directly), banned trade in British goods, and made British cargoes lawful prize. Britain's Orders in Council answered by requiring neutral shipping to call at British ports — a squeeze that contributed to the Anglo-American War of 1812. Enforcement consumed the empire: customs cordons, licences sold by Napoleon himself, the annexation of Holland and the Hanseatic coast in 1810 when they proved leaky, and above all the invasion of Portugal (1807) and then Spain, when Portugal refused to close its ports — the road to the Peninsular War.

Failure

British exports were diverted to smuggling, colonial re-export and new markets in the Americas and the Levant, while continental consumers and manufacturers — deprived of colonial goods, cotton and markets — resented a system that served French industry. Alexander I's Russia, its grain and timber exports crippled, reopened trade with neutrals in 1810; Napoleon's determination to compel Russian compliance led directly to the invasion of 1812 and the empire's destruction. Economic historians still debate the System's effects, but as grand strategy it is generally judged a self-defeating overreach.
