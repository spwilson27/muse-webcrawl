Utraquism

Utraquism (from the Latin sub utraque specie, meaning "under both kinds") or Calixtinism (from Latin calix, chalice) was a principal dogma of the Hussites: the belief that the Eucharist should be administered to the laity under both kinds — both the bread and the wine — rather than the bread alone, as was then the practice of the Roman Catholic Church.

Origins

The practice was introduced in Prague around 1414 by Jakoubek of Stříbro, a follower of Jan Hus, who argued from Scripture that Christ had instituted communion in both kinds. The chalice became the banner symbol of the Hussite movement, whose demands were set out in the Four Articles of Prague (1420).

The Hussite Wars and the Compactata

The moderate Utraquists, drawn largely from the Prague burghers and nobility, sought reconciliation with the Church. After the Hussite Wars, the Council of Basel conceded communion in both kinds to Bohemia in the Compactata of 1436 — a unique concession in medieval Latin Christianity. The radical Taborites rejected the compromise and were defeated at the Battle of Lipany (1434).

Later history

An Utraquist church, with its own consistory in Prague, existed in Bohemia for nearly two centuries, and the Utraquist George of Poděbrady became king of Bohemia in 1458. After the Battle of White Mountain (1620) Utraquism was suppressed in the Habsburg lands. The modern Czechoslovak Hussite Church (founded 1920) claims the Utraquist heritage.
