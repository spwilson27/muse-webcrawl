Hawaiian honeycreepers are a group of small passerine birds endemic to the Hawaiian Islands. They are now classified as a subfamily (Drepanidinae, within Carduelinae) of the finch family Fringillidae, having evolved from a single ancestor — probably a rosefinch-like finch — that reached Hawaii several million years ago. Their extraordinary diversification of bills, songs and feeding habits makes them a classic example of adaptive radiation, comparable to Darwin's finches of the Galápagos.

Diversity

More than fifty species are known, living and extinct. Surviving species include the scarlet ʻiʻiwi, with a long decurved bill for sipping nectar from ʻōhiʻa blossoms; the crimson ʻapapane; the seed-eating palila, dependent on māmane trees; the ʻakiapōlāʻau, whose straight lower bill and curved upper bill work like a woodpecker's; and the insect-gleaning Hawaiʻi creeper and ʻakekeʻe. Many honeycreepers are brightly coloured in red, yellow or green; their feathers were prized in Native Hawaiian culture for capes and helmets (ʻahu ʻula and mahiole).

Decline and conservation

Hawaiian honeycreepers have suffered one of the worst extinction crises of any bird group. Habitat destruction, introduced predators such as rats and mongooses, and above all introduced avian malaria and avian pox — spread by introduced mosquitoes — have wiped out species and driven survivors into high-elevation refuges too cold for mosquitoes. Climate warming is shrinking those refuges, and many remaining species, such as the kiwikiu and ʻakikiki, are critically endangered; conservation efforts include mosquito control using incompatible-insect techniques, fencing, and captive breeding.
