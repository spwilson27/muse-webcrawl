The Punisher (Frank Castle) is an antihero appearing in American comic books published by Marvel Comics. Created by writer Gerry Conway and artists Ross Andru and John Romita Sr., the character first appeared in The Amazing Spider-Man #129 (1974), initially as an antagonist hired to kill Spider-Man. Unlike most Marvel heroes, the Punisher is a vigilante who wages a one-man war on crime using lethal force — murder, torture, extortion and kidnapping — and his willingness to kill sets him apart from, and frequently against, the rest of the Marvel Universe.

Fictional biography

Frank Castle is a former U.S. Marine and Vietnam War veteran. While picnicking with his wife Maria and their two children in New York's Central Park, the family witnessed a Mafia execution; to eliminate witnesses, the mobsters killed Castle's family and left him for dead. Castle survived and, finding the legal system unable or unwilling to bring the killers to justice, embarked on a permanent vendetta against criminals of all kinds. He wears a distinctive black uniform emblazoned with a large white skull, and operates from safe houses stocked with military-grade weaponry, keeping a "war journal" of his campaigns.

Publication history

The character became one of Marvel's most popular of the late 1980s and early 1990s, headlining multiple simultaneous ongoing series — The Punisher, The Punisher War Journal and The Punisher War Zone. The darker adult-oriented Marvel MAX imprint run written by Garth Ennis (2004–2008) is widely regarded as definitive, presenting an ageing, remorseless Castle. Ennis had earlier written the blackly comic miniseries "Welcome Back, Frank" (2000) with artist Steve Dillon.

In other media

The Punisher has been portrayed in film by Dolph Lundgren (1989), Thomas Jane (2004) and Ray Stevenson (Punisher: War Zone, 2008). Jon Bernthal played Castle in the Marvel Cinematic Universe, debuting in the second season of Netflix's Daredevil (2016) and headlining the spin-off series The Punisher (2017–2019). The character's skull logo has also been widely adopted outside comics, including by some military and police personnel and various political movements — a usage that has attracted controversy and that Marvel and the character's creators have publicly debated.
