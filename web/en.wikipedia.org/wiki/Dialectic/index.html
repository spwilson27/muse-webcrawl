Dialectic

Dialectic (Greek: διαλεκτική, dialektikḗ), also known as the dialectical method, is a discourse between two or more people holding different points of view about a subject but wishing to establish the truth through reasoned argumentation.

Dialectic resembles debate, but the concept excludes the emotional elements typically present in debate and the rhetorical elements present in sophistry. It has developed through three successive stages in Western philosophy: the dialectics of Plato, Kant, and Hegel.

Classical dialectic

In Plato's dialogues, Socrates uses questioning (the elenchus) to expose contradictions in an interlocutor's beliefs. Aristotle treated dialectic as reasoning from reputable opinions (endoxa). It became one of the three liberal arts of the medieval trivium, alongside grammar and rhetoric.

Kant and Hegel

Kant used "dialectic" for the illusions generated when reason seeks knowledge beyond experience. For Hegel, dialectic is the method by which thought — and reality itself — develops through contradiction: a thesis gives rise to its antithesis, and the tension is resolved in a synthesis that preserves elements of both (often described as Aufhebung, sublation).

Marxist dialectics

Marx and Engels recast Hegel's idealist dialectic in materialist terms: dialectical materialism holds that contradictions in material and social conditions drive historical change. This framework became the official philosophy of several 20th-century states and a major current in critical theory.
