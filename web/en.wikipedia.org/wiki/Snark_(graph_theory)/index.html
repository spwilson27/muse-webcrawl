Snark (graph theory)

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a snark is an undirected graph, with vertices of degree three, that has edges which cannot be coloured with only three colours. That is, it is a cubic bridgeless graph that is not 3-edge-colourable; by the four colour theorem's equivalence to edge-colouring of planar cubic graphs, snarks must be nonplanar and have girth at least five in the usual strengthened definitions.

History

The smallest snark is the Petersen graph, with 10 vertices. Snarks were named by the American mathematician Martin Gardner in 1976 after the mysterious, elusive creature in Lewis Carroll's poem The Hunting of the Snark. W. T. Tutte conjectured that snarks are exactly the cubic bridgeless graphs with no Petersen-graph minor; this was a key motivation in the theory leading to the Robertson–Seymour theorem and was announced as proved in 2001.

Examples

Known families include the Petersen graph, the Blanuša snarks (18 vertices), the flower snarks, the Descartes snark (210 vertices), the Szekeres snark, and the Goldberg snarks. Snarks are connected with famous conjectures including the cycle double cover conjecture: if every bridgeless graph has a cycle double cover then every snark does.
