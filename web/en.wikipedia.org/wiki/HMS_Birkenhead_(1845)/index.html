The wreck of the Birkenhead, 26 February 1852

HMS Birkenhead, launched in 1845, was one of the first iron-hulled ships of the Royal Navy. Built by John Laird at Birkenhead as a steam frigate, she was converted to a troopship before entering service. She is remembered for her wreck in 1852, which became famous for the discipline of the soldiers aboard and the resulting maritime tradition of "women and children first".

The wreck

Carrying troops of several regiments to the Eighth Xhosa War, plus some women and children — about 643 people in all — Birkenhead left Simon's Town on 25 February 1852. At about 2 a.m. on 26 February she struck an uncharted rock off Danger Point, near Gansbaai, some 180 km from Cape Town. The inrush of water flooded the lower troop deck, drowning many men in their hammocks; the ship's paddle wheels and falling rigging hampered the launching of her few boats.

"Birkenhead drill"

Under Lieutenant-Colonel Alexander Seton of the 74th Regiment of Foot, the soldiers were mustered on deck and stood fast, silently, while the women and children were placed in the boats — the origin of the phrase "Birkenhead drill". When the ship broke in two and sank, the men remained in ranks. Only 193 of those aboard survived. The wreck was commemorated in verse by Rudyard Kipling ("Soldier an' Sailor Too") and in Francis Davis Millet's painting; the discipline shown became a byword for self-sacrifice. The wreck site, in about 28 metres of water, is a protected heritage site in South Africa.
