The Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) was a United States federal law of the New Deal era designed to raise agricultural prices by reducing surpluses. Enacted on 12 May 1933 during the First Hundred Days, it paid farmers subsidies, financed by a tax on food processors, to take land out of production and reduce output of basic commodities such as wheat, cotton, corn, hogs, rice, tobacco and milk.

Background

American farmers had suffered low prices since the early 1920s as wartime demand collapsed and productivity rose. The Act sought to restore parity — the purchasing power farm products had enjoyed in the prosperous period 1909–1914. The Agricultural Adjustment Administration, headed by George Peek and then Chester Davis, under Secretary of Agriculture Henry A. Wallace, administered the programme. Because the 1933 cotton crop was already planted, the government paid farmers to plough under some ten million acres of cotton and to slaughter millions of piglets and sows — measures that raised prices but drew public criticism while millions went hungry.

Effects and legal fate

Farm prices and incomes rose substantially: gross farm income increased by more than half between 1932 and 1935, though drought also played a role. Critics, including Roosevelt's populist rivals Huey Long and Francis Townsend's supporters, attacked the distribution of benefits, since payments to landowners often displaced tenant farmers and sharecroppers, particularly Black farmers in the South. In United States v. Butler (1936) the Supreme Court struck down the Act's processing tax and production controls as exceeding federal power. Congress responded with the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act of 1936 and a second, durable Agricultural Adjustment Act in 1938, which preserved the framework of production controls, marketing quotas and parity supports that shaped US farm policy for decades.
