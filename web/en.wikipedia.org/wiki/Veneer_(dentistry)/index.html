Veneer (dentistry)

In dentistry, a veneer is a layer of material placed over the front surface of a tooth, primarily to improve its appearance or to protect a damaged surface. They are a central technique in cosmetic dentistry.

Materials

Veneers are most commonly made of porcelain (feldspathic porcelain or pressed ceramic such as lithium disilicate) or of a resin composite material. Porcelain veneers are fabricated in a dental laboratory from an impression or digital scan, then bonded to the tooth with resin cement. Composite veneers may be built up directly in the mouth or fabricated indirectly.

Indications

Veneers are used to correct discoloured teeth that do not respond to whitening, worn or chipped teeth, misaligned or irregularly shaped teeth, and unwanted gaps between teeth (diastemata). Because only a thin layer — often 0.3 to 0.5 mm — of enamel is removed, or in "no-prep" designs none at all, the procedure is more conservative than a full crown.

History

The technique is often credited to California dentist Charles Pincus in 1928, who used temporary veneers for actors in film shoots. The advent of enamel etching by Michael Buonocore in 1955 and later silane coupling agents made permanent bonding practical.
