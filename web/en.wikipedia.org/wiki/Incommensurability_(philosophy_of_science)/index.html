Incommensurability (philosophy of science)

In the philosophy of science, incommensurability is the idea that competing scientific theories or paradigms cannot be fully compared or translated into one another because they use different concepts, standards and vocabularies, and may even describe the world in incompatible ways. The term was introduced, in slightly different forms, by Thomas Kuhn in The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) and by Paul Feyerabend.

Kuhn's account

Kuhn argued that scientists working in different paradigms use terms such as "mass" with different meanings — Newtonian mass is conserved, while relativistic mass is convertible with energy — so there is no neutral language into which both theories can be fully translated. He also held that paradigms differ in the problems they deem important and the standards for acceptable solutions.

Criticism and development

Critics such as Donald Davidson argued that total untranslatability is incoherent. Kuhn later softened the thesis to "local incommensurability": only a small cluster of interrelated terms resists translation, and comparison and rational choice between theories remain possible.
