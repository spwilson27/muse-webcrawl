Welfare

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Welfare, or commonly social welfare, is the provision of a minimal level of well-being and social support for citizens and residents of a country, without the stigma of charity. In most developed countries, welfare is mainly provided by the government from tax revenue, together with contributions from non-governmental organisations, charities and informal social groups.

Forms of welfare

Welfare can take a variety of forms: monetary payments, subsidies and vouchers, or public services such as healthcare and education. Programmes are typically means-tested, directed at people whose income or assets fall below a threshold, or universal, available to all citizens. Common examples include unemployment benefits, child benefits, public pensions, food assistance and housing support.

History

Organised poor relief has roots in ancient almsgiving and in measures such as the English Poor Laws. The modern welfare state emerged in the late 19th and 20th centuries, notably with Bismarck's social insurance laws in Germany, the American New Deal of the 1930s and Britain's post-1945 Beveridge settlement, which created the National Health Service.

Effects and debates

Studies generally find that welfare programmes reduce poverty and improve health and educational outcomes, particularly for children. Critics argue some programmes create dependency or disincentives to work, prompting reforms such as the 1996 American welfare reform, which replaced AFDC with the block-granted TANF programme and added work requirements and time limits.
