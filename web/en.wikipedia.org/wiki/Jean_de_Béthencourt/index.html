Jean de Béthencourt (1362–1425) was a French Norman nobleman and explorer who, with Gadifer de la Salle, undertook the conquest of the Canary Islands beginning in 1402 — one of the earliest episodes of European overseas colonization, preceding the voyages of Columbus by ninety years. He took the title "King of the Canary Islands," while acknowledging the suzerainty of Henry III of Castile.

The expedition

Béthencourt, a chamberlain of Charles VI of France, set out from La Rochelle on May 1, 1402, with Gadifer de la Salle and a company of soldiers and settlers, financed in part by mortgaging his estates. Sailing via Cádiz, the expedition reached Lanzarote, whose native Guanche inhabitants, under King Guadarfía, were induced to submit, and Béthencourt established a settlement and the castle of Rubicón. He returned to Castile for reinforcements, where Henry III recognized his conquest and granted him lordship of the islands, making the Canaries effectively a Castilian enterprise.

Conquest and rivalry

Fuerteventura was conquered in 1404–1405 with the baptism of its kings, and settlements or footholds were established on La Gomera and El Hierro. Relations between Béthencourt and Gadifer deteriorated badly, however; the two men quarreled over leadership and rewards, and Gadifer returned to France to press his claims, unsuccessfully, at court. Béthencourt brought over Norman settlers and organized the administration of the islands, founding Betancuria on Fuerteventura as his capital.

Later life and legacy

In 1412 Béthencourt returned to Normandy for good, leaving the islands in the hands of his nephew Maciot de Béthencourt, whose sale of the lordship set off the long disputes that eventually brought the Canaries under direct Castilian control. He died in 1425 at his birthplace, Grainville-la-Teinturière. The expedition is chronicled in Le Canarien, compiled by the expedition's chaplains, one of the key sources for the early history of the conquest and for Guanche society before its destruction. The surname, in the form Betancur/Betancourt, is still common in the Canaries and in Latin America.
