Hugh Everett III (11 November 1930 – 19 July 1982) was an American physicist who, in his 1957 Princeton Ph.D. thesis, first proposed the relative state formulation of quantum mechanics — later popularised as the many-worlds interpretation. After an indifferent reception from the physics establishment, Everett left academic physics for defence analysis and operations research, where he also made notable contributions.

The relative state formulation

Supervised by John Archibald Wheeler, Everett's thesis argued that the universal wavefunction evolves deterministically and never collapses; measurement outcomes appear to split because the observer becomes entangled with the measured system, so that each branch of the wavefunction contains a version of the observer who has recorded one particular result. His thesis was published in shortened form in the Reviews of Modern Physics in 1957, alongside a supportive paper by Wheeler. Niels Bohr and the Copenhagen school were unreceptive, and the work was largely ignored until Bryce DeWitt revived it in the late 1960s, coining the name "many-worlds interpretation". Today it is one of the major interpretations of quantum mechanics and underlies the idea of quantum computing suggested by David Deutsch.

Later work

Everett joined the Pentagon's Weapons Systems Evaluation Group in 1956 and worked on nuclear weapons strategy and game theory, including a recursive algorithm known as Everett's theorem for optimising military resource allocation. He later co-founded the defence consultancy DBS Corporation and the firm Lambda Corporation. His son is Mark Oliver Everett, frontman of the rock band Eels, whose documentary work helped bring renewed attention to his father's life. Everett died of a heart attack at 51; his ashes were, by his own request as a lifelong atheist who believed consciousness ended at death, disposed of with the household trash — a detail frequently recounted in profiles of his life.
