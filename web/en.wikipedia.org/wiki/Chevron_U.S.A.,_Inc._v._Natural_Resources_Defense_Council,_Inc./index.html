Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984), was a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that set forth the legal test for determining whether to grant deference to a government agency's interpretation of a statute that it administers. The decision articulated the doctrine known as Chevron deference, which governed judicial review of agency action for forty years until the Court overruled it in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024).

Background

The case arose from amendments to the Clean Air Act. The Environmental Protection Agency under President Ronald Reagan adopted a regulation permitting states to treat all pollution-emitting devices within an industrial plant as if encased in a single "bubble", allowing plants to modify some equipment without triggering stringent permit requirements so long as total plant emissions did not increase. The Natural Resources Defense Council challenged the rule; the D.C. Circuit set it aside, and the Supreme Court granted review.

The two-step framework

Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice John Paul Stevens set out a two-step analysis. First, has Congress directly spoken to the precise question at issue? If the intent of Congress is clear, that is the end of the matter. Second, if the statute is silent or ambiguous, the court asks whether the agency's interpretation is based on a permissible construction of the statute; if so, the court defers to it. The rationale was that agencies, accountable through the executive and expert in their fields, are better placed than courts to resolve policy-laden ambiguities in their governing statutes.

Legacy and overruling

Chevron became one of the most cited administrative-law decisions in American history and a foundation of the modern regulatory state. Critics argued over time that it improperly transferred interpretive power from courts to agencies. On 28 June 2024, in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, the Supreme Court held that the Administrative Procedure Act requires courts to exercise independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority, expressly overruling Chevron.
