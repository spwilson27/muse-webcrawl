Heine–Cantor theorem

In mathematics, the Heine–Cantor theorem, named after Eduard Heine and Georg Cantor, states that if f : M → N is a continuous function between two metric spaces, and M is compact, then f is uniformly continuous. In particular, if a function is continuous on a closed and bounded interval of the real line, it is uniformly continuous on that interval.

On a compact domain, continuity automatically strengthens to uniform continuity.

Motivation

Ordinary continuity allows the choice of δ (for a given ε) to depend on the point x: near steep parts of a graph, smaller intervals are needed. Uniform continuity demands a single δ that works everywhere in the domain. On non-compact domains this can fail — 1/x is continuous but not uniformly continuous on (0, 1), and x² is not uniformly continuous on the whole real line. The theorem identifies compactness as the property that removes this obstruction.

Proof outline

The standard proof proceeds by contradiction. Suppose f is continuous but not uniformly continuous on a compact space M. Then there exist ε₀ > 0 and sequences of points xn, yn in M whose distance tends to zero while the distance between f(xn) and f(yn) stays at least ε₀. By compactness (the Bolzano–Weierstrass property), the sequence xn has a convergent subsequence with limit x; then yn converges to the same x, and continuity of f at x forces both image sequences to converge to f(x), contradicting their separation. Hence f must be uniformly continuous.

History

Heine first proved the result for functions on a closed interval in 1872, in the course of work that required uniform continuity; Cantor's generalisation to compact metric spaces followed. The theorem is a standard consequence used throughout real analysis, for example in proving that continuous functions on [a, b] are Riemann integrable.

Generalizations

The theorem extends to uniform spaces: a continuous function from a compact uniform space to any uniform space is uniformly continuous. Compactness cannot in general be weakened to completeness or boundedness alone.
