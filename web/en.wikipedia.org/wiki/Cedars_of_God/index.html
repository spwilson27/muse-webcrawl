Cedars of God

The Cedars of God (Arabic: أرز الربّ Arz ar-Rabb, "Cedars of the Lord") is a remnant grove of Cedrus libani (Lebanon cedar) trees located in the mountains near Bsharri in northern Lebanon. It is one of the last surviving forests of the cedars that once blanketed Mount Lebanon and were prized throughout the ancient world. Together with the Qadisha Valley, it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998.

History

Cedar wood from Lebanon was exported to Egypt, Mesopotamia, and later Phoenician, Greek, and Roman markets for shipbuilding, temples, and palaces. Cedar resin was used in Egyptian mummification. The grove at Bsharri was protected at various times by imperial decree — including a protective wall attributed to the emperor Hadrian — and in the 19th century Queen Victoria financed a wall to shield the young trees from grazing goats. In 1876 the grove was formally protected, and in the 20th century reforestation efforts began on the surrounding slopes.

The forest

The reserve covers a high mountain bowl at roughly 1,900–2,000 metres above sea level. It contains several hundred cedar trees, including some of the oldest and largest in Lebanon, with a number estimated to be many centuries old. The oldest specimens are gnarled, multi-trunked trees of great girth. The cedar is the national emblem of Lebanon and appears on its flag.

Conservation

The forest is managed by the Committee of the Friends of the Cedar Forest. Threats include climate change, insect pests such as the cedar web-spinning sawfly (Cephalcia tannourinensis), and reduced snowfall. Extensive planting of young cedars on the denuded slopes around Bsharri aims to reconnect the ancient grove with restored forest.
