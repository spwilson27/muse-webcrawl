Tooth enamel

Tooth enamel is one of the four major tissues that make up the tooth in humans and many other animals. It is the hard, white outer covering of the crown of the tooth, and the hardest substance in the human body.

Enamel is composed of about 96% mineral, mainly hydroxyapatite, a crystalline calcium phosphate, with the remainder water and organic material. It is formed by cells called ameloblasts before the tooth erupts; once formed, it contains no living cells and cannot be regenerated by the body, which is why enamel damaged by decay or erosion does not heal.

Structure and care

Enamel is arranged in tightly packed rods, or prisms, that run from the surface to the underlying dentin, giving it great strength against the forces of chewing. Acids from bacterial plaque and from food and drink can dissolve its minerals (demineralisation), leading to cavities; fluoride helps because it converts hydroxyapatite to the more acid-resistant fluorapatite and promotes remineralisation. Enamel thickness and colour vary between people and teeth.
