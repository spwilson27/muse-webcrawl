The American system of manufacturing was a set of manufacturing methods that advanced in the United States in the first half of the 19th century. Its core was the use of interchangeable parts — components made to such precise, standardized tolerances that any part of a given type would fit any assembly of the same type — combined with mechanized production, specialized machine tools, and a division of labour that reduced dependence on skilled craftsmen.

Development

The system developed largely in the federal armories at Springfield, Massachusetts, and Harpers Ferry, Virginia, and among private arms contractors in the Connecticut River valley. Eli Whitney popularized the idea of interchangeable manufacture in his 1798 musket contract, though truly interchangeable production was achieved gradually over decades; John H. Hall's breech-loading rifle works at Harpers Ferry and Simeon North's pistol factory were landmark implementations. The methods spread from firearms into clocks, locks, sewing machines, bicycles, and eventually automobiles.

Significance

British observers at the Crystal Palace Exhibition of 1851 and in parliamentary reports of 1854–55 coined the term "American system" after being impressed by American exhibits of guns, locks, and woodworking machinery. The system laid the groundwork for mass production and the assembly line in the 20th century, and reshaped repair and logistics by allowing broken products to be fixed by simply substituting a standard part.
