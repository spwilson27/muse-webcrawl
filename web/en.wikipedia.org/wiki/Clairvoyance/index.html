Clairvoyance

Clairvoyance (from French clair, "clear", and voyance, "seeing") is the alleged ability to gain information about an object, person, location, or physical event through extrasensory perception, without the use of the known senses. A person said to have the ability of clairvoyance is referred to as a clairvoyant ("one who sees clearly"). Claims for clairvoyance are investigated within parapsychology, but the scientific consensus is that there is no reliable evidence that the ability exists.

History

Beliefs in second sight and prophetic visions appear in many religions and folk traditions, from the oracles of ancient Greece to Scottish and Irish traditions of an da shealladh ("the two sights"). In the 19th century, clairvoyance became a fixture of Spiritualist séances. The French occultist Gérard Encausse and others developed formal terminology distinguishing clairvoyance in space (remote viewing) from clairvoyance in time (precognition and retrocognition).

Scientific assessment

Clairvoyance has been tested in laboratory settings since the work of J. B. Rhine at Duke University in the 1930s, and later in the Ganzfeld experiments and the United States government's remote-viewing research (the Stargate Project, terminated in 1995). Meta-analyses remain disputed, and mainstream science attributes positive results to methodological flaws, sensory leakage, and publication bias; no replicable evidence for clairvoyance has been accepted by the scientific community.
