Electrical telegraph

The electrical telegraph was the first practical system of long-distance communication using electric signals transmitted over wires. Developed in the 1830s and 1840s by, among others, Samuel Morse in the United States and William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone in Britain, it revolutionised commerce, journalism, warfare and personal communication by separating the speed of a message from the speed of physical transport for the first time in history.

Development

Experiments with electricity and signalling went back decades, including Pavel Schilling's needle telegraph (1832) and Carl Friedrich Gauss and Wilhelm Weber's installation in Göttingen (1833). Cooke and Wheatstone patented their needle telegraph in 1837 and installed it along British railways. Morse, working with Alfred Vail and Leonard Gale, demonstrated his single-wire recording telegraph and the code that bears his name; in 1844 the line between Washington, D.C. and Baltimore opened with the famous message "WHAT HATH GOD WROUGHT".

Expansion

Telegraph networks spread rapidly along railway lines and roads. Companies such as Western Union (founded 1851) consolidated the American network; in Britain the system was nationalised into the Post Office in 1870. Submarine cables linked Britain to France (1851), and after failures in 1858 and 1865, a durable transatlantic cable was completed in 1866, cutting communication between Europe and America from days to minutes. By the early 20th century a global network of cables — the "Victorian Internet" — girdled the world.

Operation

Messages (telegrams) were handed in at an office, transmitted in Morse code by trained operators using a key, and received by sounder or paper-tape recorder, then written out and delivered by messenger. Later developments included duplex and quadruplex working, the printing telegraph and teletypewriter, and automatic high-speed transmission. The telegraph also created new professions and practices: press agencies pooled telegraphed news, stock tickers distributed prices, and standard time was coordinated by telegraphed time signals.

Decline

The telephone, telex and eventually fax and email displaced the telegram. Western Union sent its last telegram in 2006 and India's state service, the world's largest remaining network, closed in 2013. The telegraph nonetheless established the template for every later electrical communications network, from its codes and operating procedures to its social effects.
