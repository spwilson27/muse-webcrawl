Felix Mendelssohn

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Felix Mendelssohn in 1839, from a miniature portrait: the young composer-conductor in a dark coat with a high collar]

Jakob Ludwig Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy (3 February 1809 – 4 November 1847), widely known as Felix Mendelssohn, was a German composer, pianist, organist and conductor of the early Romantic period. A prodigy ranked in his teens with Mozart, he wrote the Octet at 16 and the Midsummer Night's Dream overture at 17, revived Bach's St Matthew Passion in 1829 and thereby the Bach tradition itself, and directed the Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra into one of Europe's finest.

His works include five symphonies (the Scottish and Italian), the Violin Concerto in E minor (1844), the oratorios St Paul (1836) and Elijah (1846), the incidental music to A Midsummer Night's Dream (with the famous Wedding March), the Songs Without Words for piano, and the Hebrides Overture (Fingal's Cave).

Prodigy and revivalist

Grandson of the philosopher Moses Mendelssohn and son of a banker, Felix and his sister Fanny grew up in Berlin in a salon of the intellectually eminent; Goethe admired the boy's playing. His 1829 performance of the St Matthew Passion — a century after its premiere — is conventionally the start of the Bach revival and of historically minded conducting. As Gewandhaus Kapellmeister from 1835 he programmed classics systematically and championed Schumann and the young Wagner's rivals alike.

Death and reputation

Overwork and grief at Fanny's sudden death in May 1847 broke him; he died in Leipzig that November, aged 38. Victorian England adored him (Elijah was written for Birmingham); Nazi Germany banned the Jewish-born composer's music and toppled his Leipzig statue. Post-war scholarship restored both the music's darker depths and Fanny Hensel's share in the family genius.
