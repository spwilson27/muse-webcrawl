Selective photothermolysis

Laser energy absorbed by a chosen chromophore destroys the target while sparing surrounding tissue

Selective photothermolysis is a principle of laser medicine, formulated by R. Rox Anderson and John A. Parrish in a landmark 1983 paper in Science, explaining how localised, microscopic tissue damage can be produced by selectively absorbed pulsed radiation. It provides the theoretical basis for most modern laser treatments in dermatology and ophthalmology.

Principles

The theory rests on three conditions. First, choose a wavelength preferentially absorbed by the intended target (a chromophore such as melanin, haemoglobin or tattoo ink) relative to surrounding tissue. Second, deliver the energy in a pulse shorter than the target's thermal relaxation time — the time it takes the target to lose most of its heat to its surroundings — so that heat stays confined to the target. Third, use sufficient fluence to raise the target to a destructive temperature. Together these allow precise destruction of structures such as individual blood vessels or pigment particles while sparing adjacent skin.

Applications

Selective photothermolysis underlies laser treatment of port-wine stains and other vascular lesions (targeting haemoglobin), laser hair removal (targeting melanin in the follicle), tattoo removal (using nanosecond and picosecond pulses to shatter ink particles), and treatment of pigmented lesions and some eye conditions.

Extensions

Later work extended the theory to targets indirectly heated through nearby absorbers, and to fractional photothermolysis, in which microscopic columns of tissue are treated in a grid pattern — the basis of fractional resurfacing lasers.
