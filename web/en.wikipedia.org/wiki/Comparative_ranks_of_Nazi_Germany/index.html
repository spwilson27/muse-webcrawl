Comparative ranks of Nazi Germany

The comparative ranks of Nazi Germany were a loose system of military, paramilitary, and political ranks used by the armed forces and party organisations of Germany between 1933 and 1945. The system compared the rank structures of the Wehrmacht (Heer, Kriegsmarine, and Luftwaffe), the Waffen-SS, the police, and party formations such as the SA, NSKK, and Hitler Youth.

Organisation

Nazi Germany never had a single unified rank table for all its uniformed organisations; equivalence between services was approximate and shifted over time. The Wehrmacht retained largely traditional German rank titles, while the SS developed its own hierarchy derived from the SA. Police ranks were progressively aligned with SS ranks after 1936, when Heinrich Himmler became Chief of the German Police, and police generals commonly held equivalent SS rank.

Below officer level, non-commissioned officers and enlisted men likewise followed broadly parallel ladders: for example, the army Feldwebel corresponded approximately to the SS-Oberscharführer, and the army Gefreiter to the SS-Rottenführer, though exact correspondence varied by branch and year.

Party and paramilitary ranks

The SA (Sturmabteilung), NSKK, NSFK, Hitler Youth, and the NSDAP political leadership corps each maintained their own rank systems, graded from simple member/shooter up to supreme titles such as Stabschef (SA) and Reichsführer-SS. The Waffen-SS ranks were formally aligned with Wehrmacht equivalents for pay and precedence purposes, particularly after 1940 as the Waffen-SS expanded into a major field force.
