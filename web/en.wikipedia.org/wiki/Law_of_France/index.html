French law is the legal system of the French Republic. It is a civil law system, founded largely on written codified statutes rather than judicial precedent, and it has been highly influential worldwide: the Napoleonic Code of 1804 served as a model for civil codes across Europe, Latin America, and beyond.

History

Before the Revolution, France was divided between the pays de droit écrit of the south, influenced by Roman law, and the pays de droit coutumier of the north, governed by customary law. The French Revolution and Napoleon Bonaparte unified and rationalised the law through codification, most famously the Civil Code of 1804 (Code Napoléon), followed by codes of civil and criminal procedure, commerce, and criminal law.

Structure

French law divides public law from private law, and correspondingly maintains two separate hierarchies of courts: the judicial order (ordre judiciaire), headed by the Cour de cassation, and the administrative order (ordre administratif), headed by the Conseil d'État. The Conseil constitutionnel reviews the constitutionality of legislation. Since 2008, litigants may raise a question prioritaire de constitutionnalité (QPC) challenging a statute's conformity with constitutional rights.

Sources of law

The hierarchy of norms places the Constitution of the Fifth Republic (1958) at the top, together with the texts it incorporates, such as the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789. Below it rank international treaties, statutes (lois) voted by Parliament, and regulations (règlements) issued by the executive. Although France is formally a codified system in which case law is not a binding source, the decisions of the highest courts are in practice highly authoritative.

Branches

  • Civil law — governed chiefly by the Civil Code: persons, property, obligations, contracts, and family law.
  • Criminal law — the Penal Code of 1994 replaced the code of 1810.
  • Administrative law — developed largely through the jurisprudence of the Conseil d'État.
  • Commercial law — the Commercial Code governs merchants and companies.
