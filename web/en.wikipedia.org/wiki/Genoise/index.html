Genoise

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A genoise (French: génoise [ʒe.nwaz]), also known as Genoese cake or Genovese cake, is a sponge cake named after the city of Genoa and closely associated with French and Italian cuisine. It is a whole-egg foam cake: unlike many sponge cakes, it uses whole eggs rather than separated yolks and whites, and contains no chemical leavening — all of its rise comes from air beaten into the eggs during whipping.

Preparation

Whole eggs are whisked with sugar, traditionally over a bain-marie until the mixture reaches about 43 °C (110 °F) and leaves a ribbon trail when it falls from the whisk — sugar stabilises the foam and warmth increases its volume. Sifted flour (often with a little cornflour) is then folded in gently to preserve the air, followed by a small amount of melted butter for flavour and moisture. The batter is baked until springy; the result is a fine, even, golden crumb that is somewhat dry on its own.

Use

Because of its neutral flavour and stable structure, genoise is a foundational building block of French pâtisserie. Layers are usually moistened with flavoured syrup and filled with buttercream, ganache, or fruit preparations. It forms the basis of many classic cakes and petit fours, and is used for the jelly-roll style biscuit roulé, for ladyfinger-like biscuits à la cuillère piped from similar batters, and as the sponge element in desserts such as fraisier. A chocolate variant replaces part of the flour with cocoa powder.

The cake's dryness is deliberate: it drinks in syrup without collapsing, which is why it displaced other sponges as the standard layer cake base in professional French baking, a status codified in works such as Escoffier's and still assumed in modern pastry training.
