The Kingdom of Hejaz (Arabic: مملكة الحجاز) was a state in the Hejaz region of western Arabia, proclaimed in 1916 by Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca, following the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire. It was the first independent Hashemite kingdom of the modern Arab world, and it endured until its conquest by Ibn Saud's forces from Nejd in 1924–1925.

History

Hussein, appointed Sharif and Emir of Mecca in 1908, negotiated with the British through the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence (1915–16), in which Britain promised support for Arab independence. In June 1916 he proclaimed the Arab Revolt; his sons Abdullah and Faisal led forces—advised by, among others, T. E. Lawrence—that captured Aqaba and operated alongside British armies in Palestine and Syria. In October 1916 Hussein was proclaimed King of the Hejaz, recognised by Britain, France, and Russia.

The kingdom included the holy cities of Mecca and Medina and the port of Jeddah. In March 1924, after the abolition of the Ottoman caliphate, Hussein proclaimed himself Caliph, a claim rejected by most of the Muslim world and a provocation to Ibn Saud, whose Wahhabi followers had long-standing grievances against Hashemite control of the pilgrimage.

Saudi conquest

In 1924 Saudi forces invaded, taking Taif and Mecca; Hussein abdicated in favour of his son Ali and went into exile. Ali held Jeddah and Medina through 1925 but, with no British support, surrendered in December 1925. The Hejaz was merged with Nejd, becoming in 1932 part of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Hussein's other sons became kings of Transjordan (Abdullah) and briefly Syria and then Iraq (Faisal).

Legacy

Though short-lived, the kingdom embodied the Arab nationalist hopes of the First World War era, and its flag—black, white, green, and red—became the template for the flags of many later Arab states.
