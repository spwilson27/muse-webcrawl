Protectionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Protectionism, sometimes referred to as trade protectionism, is the economic policy of restricting imports from other countries through methods such as tariffs on imported goods, import quotas, and a variety of other government regulations. Proponents argue that protection shields domestic industries and jobs, nurtures infant industries until competitive, secures national defence capacity and raises revenue; critics answer that it raises consumer prices, invites retaliation, shelters inefficiency and, in aggregate, shrinks the economic pie that trade enlarges.

History

Mercantilist states practised protection systematically (Navigation Acts, Colbert's tariffs); the United States industrialised behind high 19th-century tariffs while Britain preached free trade — the pattern Friedrich List theorised (protection as the ladder late developers climb, then "kick away"). The Smoot–Hawley Tariff (1930) became the cautionary tale of protection in a depression, retaliated against worldwide. Post-war embedded liberalism paired openness with safeguards; import-substitution industrialisation dominated Latin America until the debt crises; and since 2018 US–China tariffs, pandemic supply shocks and green industrial subsidies have normalised "strategic" protection in new vocabulary (friend-shoring, economic security, de-risking) — a shift the WTO system struggles to accommodate.
