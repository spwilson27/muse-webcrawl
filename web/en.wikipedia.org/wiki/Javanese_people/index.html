The Javanese (Javanese: ꦮꦺꦴꦁꦗꦮ, Wong Jowo) are an Austronesian ethnic group native to the central and eastern part of the Indonesian island of Java. Numbering more than 100 million people, they form the largest ethnic group in Indonesia and one of the largest in Southeast Asia.

History

Javanese civilisation developed over two millennia on the fertile volcanic plains of the island. Powerful kingdoms — including Mataram, Kediri, Singhasari, and the Majapahit empire — ruled much of maritime Southeast Asia in the medieval period, building monumental temples such as Borobudur and Prambanan. Islam spread through trade networks from the fifteenth century and gradually displaced the earlier Hindu-Buddhist culture, while the Dutch colonial era, beginning in the seventeenth century, reshaped Javanese society. Javanese figures such as Sukarno and Suharto led Indonesia after independence in 1945.

Language and culture

The Javanese language, written historically in its own script and now usually in Latin letters, has an elaborate system of speech levels — ngoko, madya, and krama — reflecting social hierarchy and politeness. Javanese culture is renowned for wayang shadow puppetry, gamelan music, batik textiles, and elaborate court dances, the latter centred on the royal courts (kraton) of Yogyakarta and Surakarta.

Religion

The great majority of Javanese are Muslims, most identifying with the traditionalist Nahdlatul Ulama movement. A syncretic tradition known as Kejawèn blends Islamic, Hindu, Buddhist, and animist elements. Significant Christian, Hindu, and Buddhist minorities also exist, particularly in East Java and the Tengger highlands.

Diaspora

Under Dutch rule, Javanese labourers were transported to Suriname, New Caledonia, and Malaysia, where their descendants still speak Javanese today. Internal migration has also spread Javanese communities throughout Indonesia.
