Ultramarathon

An ultramarathon, also called ultra distance or ultra running, is any footrace longer than the traditional marathon length of 42.195 kilometres (26.2 miles). Races range from 50 kilometres to multi-day events covering thousands of kilometres, and are held on roads, trails, mountains and tracks.

Distances and formats

The most common standard distances are 50 kilometres, 100 kilometres, 50 miles and 100 miles. Timed events of 6, 12 and 24 hours, and multi-day races of up to six days, are also popular. The International Association of Ultrarunners (IAU), working with World Athletics, holds world championships over 50 km, 100 km, 24 hours and trail distances. Trail ultras, typically run over mountainous terrain with large elevation gain, have grown rapidly since the 2000s.

Notable events

Famous ultramarathons include the Comrades Marathon in South Africa (about 89 km, founded 1921), the Western States Endurance Run (100 miles through California's Sierra Nevada, first run as a race in 1977), the Ultra-Trail du Mont-Blanc (about 171 km around Mont Blanc), the Marathon des Sables (a six-day, roughly 250 km stage race in the Sahara), and the Spartathlon (246 km from Athens to Sparta, commemorating Pheidippides). Extreme endurance is pushed furthest by events such as the Self-Transcendence 3100 Mile Race in New York, the world's longest certified footrace.

History

Ultra-distance pedestrianism was a popular spectator sport in the nineteenth century, when professional "pedestrians" raced over six days in indoor arenas in Britain and America. The modern sport revived in the twentieth century with Comrades and the London to Brighton race, and expanded dramatically with the trail-running boom of the 2000s and 2010s, drawing large amateur fields.

Physiology

Ultramarathon running stresses energy metabolism, thermoregulation and musculoskeletal tissues well beyond marathon demands. Successful performance depends heavily on fat oxidation, economical running form, gastrointestinal tolerance of eating during exercise, and pacing; injuries and exercise-associated hyponatraemia are recognised risks.
