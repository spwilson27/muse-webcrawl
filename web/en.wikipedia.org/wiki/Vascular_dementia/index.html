Vascular dementia

SpecialtyNeurology, psychiatry
CausesStroke, small-vessel disease, cerebral hypoperfusion
Risk factorsHypertension, diabetes, smoking, atrial fibrillation

Vascular dementia is dementia caused by problems in the blood supply to the brain, typically from a series of small strokes or damage to small cerebral blood vessels. It is generally considered the second most common cause of dementia after Alzheimer's disease, and the two often occur together ("mixed dementia").

Signs and causes

Symptoms depend on which brain regions are damaged and may include slowed thinking, problems with planning and organisation (executive dysfunction), memory loss, difficulty walking, and mood changes. The condition may progress in steps after each stroke, or gradually in small-vessel disease (subcortical vascular dementia, including Binswanger disease). Risk factors are those of cardiovascular disease: high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, and atrial fibrillation.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture, cognitive testing, and brain imaging (MRI or CT) showing infarcts or extensive white-matter disease. There is no medicine that reverses vascular dementia; management focuses on preventing further strokes with antiplatelet drugs, statins, blood-pressure and diabetes control, smoking cessation, and exercise. Cholinesterase inhibitors, developed for Alzheimer's disease, may give modest benefit in some patients.
