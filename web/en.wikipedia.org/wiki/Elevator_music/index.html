Elevator music

Elevators were among the first venues for programmed background music

Elevator music
Other namesMuzak, background music, piped music
OriginUnited States, 1930s–1950s
Typical instrumentsStrings, piano, soft orchestra

Elevator music (also known as Muzak, background music, or piped music) is a type of easy-listening music that is played in public places such as elevators, shopping malls, hotels, airports and offices. It consists of instrumental arrangements of popular songs, performed unobtrusively so as to create a calm atmosphere without demanding the listener's attention.

History

The term derives from the Muzak Corporation, founded in the 1930s, which piped pre-recorded music into commercial premises and, notably, into elevators — partly to reassure passengers who were anxious about riding in the new elevator technology. In the postwar decades, programmed background music became ubiquitous in retail and office environments, with providers offering "stimulus progression" schedules intended to raise productivity by gradually increasing the tempo of the music through the working day.

Characteristics

Elevator music recordings typically feature lush string orchestras playing simplified arrangements of well-known melodies. Dynamic range is compressed, tempos are moderate, and vocals, if present at all, are soft and wordless. The music is engineered to sit below the threshold of conscious attention.

Criticism and legacy

The genre has often been criticised as bland or manipulative, and "elevator music" is used pejoratively for any insipid music. Campaigns such as the British society Pipedown have lobbied against involuntary exposure to background music. At the same time, the style has been reappraised as a precursor to ambient music, and deliberately generic background music remains a fixture of commercial spaces.
