Phonology

Phonology is the branch of linguistics that studies how languages systematically organise their sounds (or, for sign languages, the equivalent physical parameters of sign). Phonology was traditionally concerned in particular with the study of the systems of phonemes in particular languages, but it may also cover any linguistic analysis either at a level beneath the word (including syllable, onset and rhyme, consonant clusters, consonants, vowels, and tonal or intonational structures) or at all levels of language where sound or sign is considered to be structured for conveying linguistic meaning.

Phonemes and allophones

The central unit of classical phonology is the phoneme: an abstract category of sounds that can distinguish meaning. Sounds that are phonemes in one language may be mere variants (allophones) in another: English /p/ is aspirated in pin but not in spin, yet both are the same phoneme, whereas in Thai the contrast is phonemic. Pairs of words differing in one sound, like pat and bat, are minimal pairs that establish phonemic contrasts.

Phonetics vs. phonology

Phonetics studies the physical production, transmission, and perception of speech sounds in general; phonology studies how those sounds function within the system of a particular language—what contrasts exist, how sounds pattern and alternate, and what sequences are permitted (phonotactics).

Features and processes

Phonemes can be decomposed into distinctive features such as [±voice], [±nasal], and [±continuant], which explain natural classes of sounds that behave alike in rules. Phonological processes include assimilation (a sound becoming more like a neighbour), deletion, insertion, neutralization, and metathesis. Alternations—such as the English plural appearing as /s/, /z/, or /ɪz/—are classic evidence for phonological rules operating on underlying forms.

Suprasegmentals

Phonology also covers structure above the segment: the syllable, stress, tone (as in Mandarin's four tones), and intonation. In tone languages, pitch differences are phonemic; in stress languages, prominence patterns are part of the phonological system.

Theories

Major frameworks include Prague School structuralism, Chomsky and Halle's generative phonology (The Sound Pattern of English, 1968), autosegmental and metrical phonology, lexical phonology, and Optimality Theory (Prince and Smolensky, 1993), which replaces ordered rules with ranked, violable constraints.
