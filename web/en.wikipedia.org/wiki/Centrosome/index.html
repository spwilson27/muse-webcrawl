Centrosome

In cell biology, the centrosome is an organelle that serves as the main microtubule organizing center (MTOC) of the animal cell, as well as a regulator of cell-cycle progression. The centrosome was discovered by Edouard Van Beneden in 1883, and was described and named in 1888 by Theodor Boveri. It is thought to have evolved only in the metazoan lineage of eukaryotic cells.

Composition

The centrosome is made up of two orthogonal cylindrical structures, known as centrioles, surrounded by an amorphous mass of dense protein called the pericentriolar material (PCM). The PCM contains proteins responsible for microtubule nucleation and anchoring, including γ-tubulin, pericentrin and ninein. Each centriole is typically composed of nine triplet microtubules in a cartwheel arrangement.

Functions

During the interphase of the cell cycle, the centrosome nucleates an array of microtubules that maintains cell shape, polarity and motility. In mitosis, the duplicated centrosomes migrate to opposite poles of the cell and organize the mitotic spindle, ensuring faithful segregation of chromosomes into the two daughter cells.

Centrosome duplication

Centriole duplication occurs once per cell cycle, beginning in S phase. Each daughter centriole grows adjacent to an existing mother centriole, giving rise to two centrosomes by the time the cell enters mitosis. Tight control of this process is essential: numerical abnormalities of centrosomes are frequently observed in cancer cells and are thought to contribute to chromosomal instability.

History

Theodor Boveri proposed in the early twentieth century that centrosome abnormalities could drive abnormal mitoses and malignant growth, a hypothesis that anticipated modern research on centrosome amplification in tumours by roughly a century.
