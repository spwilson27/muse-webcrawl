Anorexia nervosa (AN), often referred to simply as anorexia, is an eating disorder characterised by persistent restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, an intense fear of gaining weight, and a disturbance in how body weight or shape is experienced. It typically begins in adolescence or young adulthood and affects women more often than men, though it occurs in all genders.

Signs and symptoms

Features include rigid dietary rules, avoidance of "fattening" foods, excessive exercise, preoccupation with food and body checking, and denial of the seriousness of low weight. Physical consequences of starvation include bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, loss of bone density, amenorrhoea, hair and skin changes, gastrointestinal problems and electrolyte disturbances; refeeding after severe malnutrition carries its own risk (refeeding syndrome). Depression, anxiety and obsessive-compulsive traits are common comorbidities.

Causes

No single cause is known. Twin studies show substantial heritability; neurobiological, temperamental (perfectionism, harm avoidance) and sociocultural factors — including media ideals of thinness — all contribute. The disorder is not simply vanity or a lifestyle choice.

Diagnosis and treatment

The DSM-5 criteria require restriction leading to significantly low weight, intense fear of weight gain, and body-image disturbance; the earlier requirement of amenorrhoea was dropped. Two subtypes are described: restricting type, and binge-eating/purging type. Treatment centres on restoring nutrition and weight, with psychotherapy — family-based treatment (Maudsley approach) for adolescents and enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-E) for adults being leading approaches. Severe cases require hospitalisation for medical stabilisation. Recovery is often slow; anorexia has one of the highest mortality rates among psychiatric illnesses, from medical complications and suicide.
