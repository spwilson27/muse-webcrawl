Acetic acid, systematically ethanoic acid, is an organic compound with the chemical formula CH3COOH (also written as CH3CO2H or C2H4O2). It is a colourless liquid that when undiluted is called glacial acetic acid, because its freezing point of 16.6 °C means it readily solidifies into ice-like crystals in a cool laboratory. Acetic acid is the second simplest carboxylic acid (after formic acid) and is best known as the main component of vinegar apart from water, giving vinegar its characteristic sour taste and pungent smell. It is a major industrial chemical, produced on a scale of millions of tonnes per year.

Properties

Acetic acid is a weak acid: it partially dissociates in water (pKa = 4.76) to give the acetate anion, CH3COO−. Its structure consists of a methyl group attached to a carboxyl group; the acidic hydrogen is the one on the carboxyl group. The liquid is a protic, polar solvent, miscible with water and many organic compounds, and in the pure state and in the vapour it forms hydrogen-bonded dimers. Concentrated acetic acid is corrosive to skin and metals and has a sharp, penetrating odour; typical household vinegar contains about 4–8% acetic acid by volume.

Production

Acetic acid is produced both synthetically and by bacterial fermentation. Most industrial production today is by methanol carbonylation — the reaction of methanol with carbon monoxide — using rhodium catalysts (the Monsanto process) or iridium catalysts (the Cativa process), which together account for the great majority of world output, roughly 5 million tonnes per year or more. Older routes include the oxidation of acetaldehyde and of hydrocarbons such as butane. Fermentation by acetic acid bacteria of the genus Acetobacter, which oxidise ethanol in wine, cider or malt wort, produces the acetic acid in culinary vinegar; this biological route supplies only a small fraction of total demand.

Uses

The largest single use of acetic acid is the production of vinyl acetate monomer, the precursor of polyvinyl acetate and other polymers used in paints, adhesives and films. It is also converted into acetic anhydride (for cellulose acetate, used in photographic film and cigarette filters), into ester solvents such as ethyl acetate and butyl acetate, and into purified terephthalic acid processes as a solvent. In the food industry it is used as an acidity regulator (E260) and condiment; dilute solutions are used as a descaler and household cleaner. In medicine, dilute acetic acid has been used as an antiseptic and in cervical cancer screening (visual inspection with acetic acid). In the laboratory it is a common reagent and solvent, and its salts and esters — the acetates — are widely used as buffers, mordants and pharmaceuticals (for example, aspirin is synthesised using acetic anhydride).

History and biochemistry

Vinegar was known to the earliest civilisations; Babylonian records from around 5000 BC mention it, and Hippocrates used it medicinally. The German alchemist Andreas Libavius described the preparation of glacial acetic acid in the sixteenth century, and in 1847 Hermann Kolbe achieved the first synthesis of an organic compound (acetic acid) from inorganic starting materials, an important step in the overthrow of vitalism. In metabolism, acetic acid in activated form — acetyl-CoA — stands at the centre of carbohydrate and fat metabolism in nearly all organisms, and acetate is an important intermediate in the biosynthesis of fatty acids.
