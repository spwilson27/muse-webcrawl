First Stadtholderless Period

The First Stadtholderless Period (Dutch: Eerste Stadhouderloze Tijdperk) is the era in Dutch history from 1650 to 1672 in which the office of stadtholder was vacant in five of the seven provinces of the Dutch Republic, including the dominant province of Holland. During this period the Republic was governed by the regent oligarchy under the leadership of Holland's Grand Pensionary Johan de Witt, and reached the height of its commercial and naval power during the Dutch Golden Age. The period ended catastrophically in the Rampjaar ("disaster year") of 1672.

Background

Stadtholder William II, Prince of Orange, died suddenly of smallpox in November 1650, a week before the birth of his son, the future William III. William II had been locked in conflict with the Holland regents over the army and had attempted a coup against Amsterdam earlier that year. The provinces of Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland and Overijssel now resolved to leave the stadtholderate vacant; only Friesland and Groningen kept a stadtholder from the Nassau-Dietz line. The Great Assembly of 1651 confirmed the arrangement, asserting provincial sovereignty.

The regime of Johan de Witt

From 1653 the Grand Pensionary Johan de Witt, together with his brother Cornelis, directed the Republic's affairs under the doctrine of the "True Freedom" (Ware Vrijheid), which held that the Republic flourished best without a quasi-monarchical stadtholder. De Witt rebuilt the navy under admirals such as Michiel de Ruyter, fought the First and Second Anglo-Dutch Wars, and secured the humiliating Treaty of Breda after the Raid on the Medway in 1667. The States party also sought to bar the young Prince of Orange permanently: the Act of Seclusion (1654) secretly promised Oliver Cromwell that William would never become stadtholder, and the Perpetual Edict of 1667 abolished the office in Holland altogether.

The Rampjaar and the end of the period

In 1672 France, England, Münster and Cologne attacked the Republic simultaneously. The French army overran the eastern provinces and advanced on Holland, which was saved only by opening the sluices of the Dutch Waterline. Popular panic turned against the regents: William III was appointed stadtholder in July 1672, and on 20 August Johan and Cornelis de Witt were lynched by a mob in The Hague — bringing the First Stadtholderless Period to a violent end. A Second Stadtholderless Period followed from 1702 to 1747.
