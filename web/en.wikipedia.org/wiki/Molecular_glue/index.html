Molecular glue

A molecular glue is a small molecule that stabilizes the interaction between two proteins, or induces a new protein–protein interaction that would not otherwise occur. By binding at an interface between proteins, a molecular glue can bring an enzyme into contact with a new substrate or hold a complex together. The concept became prominent in drug discovery after it was recognized that several important drugs work by this mechanism, notably the immunomodulatory imide drugs (IMiDs) such as lenalidomide and pomalidomide, and the natural products cyclosporine, FK506, and rapamycin.

Mechanism

Molecular glues act through a single binding event: the molecule binds one protein (often an E3 ubiquitin ligase such as cereblon) and remodels its surface so that a second protein — a "neosubstrate" — is recruited into a ternary complex. When the first protein is an E3 ligase, the recruited substrate is ubiquitinated and destroyed by the proteasome, making molecular glues a class of targeted protein degraders. Unlike PROTACs, which are large bivalent molecules linking two binding ligands, molecular glues are typically small, drug-like monovalent compounds that exploit or create a protein interface.

Examples

Cyclosporine and FK506 glue cyclophilin and FKBP12, respectively, to the phosphatase calcineurin, inhibiting T-cell activation and enabling organ transplantation. Rapamycin glues FKBP12 to the mTOR kinase. The plant hormone auxin is a natural molecular glue, bridging the TIR1 receptor to Aux/IAA repressor proteins. The IMiDs glue the E3 ligase cereblon to zinc-finger transcription factors such as IKZF1 and IKZF3, whose degradation underlies their efficacy in multiple myeloma. The sulfonamide indisulam similarly recruits RBM39 to the DCAF15 ligase.

Discovery and drug development

Most known molecular glues were discovered retrospectively — their mechanism was understood only years after clinical use — because rational design of interface binders is difficult. Screening approaches, including phenotypic screens, chemoproteomics, and computational modelling of protein surfaces, are now used to find new glues deliberately, and several biotechnology companies focus on molecular glue degraders as a therapeutic platform.
