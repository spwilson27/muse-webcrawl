In category theory, a branch of mathematics, a Grothendieck topology is a structure on a category C that makes the objects of C act like the open sets of a topological space. A category together with a choice of Grothendieck topology is called a site.

Grothendieck topologies and their associated sheaves were introduced by Alexander Grothendieck in the 1960s to provide a framework for étale cohomology and other cohomology theories in algebraic geometry, in which the classical Zariski topology on a scheme is too coarse to support the constructions Grothendieck needed.

Motivation

The classical definition of a sheaf depends on the open subsets of a topological space. Grothendieck observed that many constructions in algebraic geometry require "open sets" that are not subsets at all: for the étale topology, the role of open sets is played by étale morphisms into a scheme. By axiomatising what it means for a family of morphisms to "cover" an object, one can define sheaves, sheaf cohomology, and descent on categories that carry no ordinary topology.

Definition

A Grothendieck topology on a category C (with finite limits usually assumed) assigns to each object U a collection of families of morphisms {Ui → U} called covering families, satisfying three axioms: the identity morphism is a covering; coverings are stable under base change (pullback); and coverings are stable under composition (transitivity). Equivalently, the topology may be specified by a Grothendieck pretopology, or by sieves in the sense of Giraud.

Examples

  • The trivial (discrete) topology, in which only the maximal sieve covers.
  • The canonical topology, the finest topology for which all representable presheaves are sheaves.
  • The étale topology on the category of schemes, whose coverings are jointly surjective families of étale morphisms.
  • The fppf and fpqc topologies, defined using flat morphisms, standard in the theory of algebraic stacks and descent.
  • The topology on a small category arising from an ordinary topological space, where coverings are the jointly surjective families of open immersions.

Sheaves on a site

A sheaf on a site is a functor satisfying a gluing (descent) condition with respect to the covering families, generalising the usual sheaf axioms. The category of sheaves on a site is a Grothendieck topos, and much of topos theory grew out of this construction.
