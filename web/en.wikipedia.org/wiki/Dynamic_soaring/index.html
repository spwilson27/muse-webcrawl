Dynamic soaring

Dynamic soaring is a flying technique used to gain energy by repeatedly crossing the boundary between air masses of different velocities. Such zones of wind gradient are generally found close to obstacles and close to the surface, so the technique is mainly of use to birds and operators of radio-controlled gliders, but glider pilots have occasionally been able to dynamic soar in meteorological wind shear at higher altitudes.

Dynamic soaring is sometimes confused with slope soaring, which uses the rising air on the windward side of a slope. Dynamic soaring instead extracts energy from the horizontal difference in wind speed itself.

Basic mechanism

A bird or glider flying in a circle across a wind gradient — for example, from calm air sheltered behind a ridge into fast-moving air above it, and back — can increase its airspeed on each crossing. When the aircraft crosses the shear layer into faster air while heading into the wind, its ground speed initially stays the same while its airspeed rises; after turning and crossing back with the wind, the reverse happens. If the gains outweigh the drag losses during the turns, the flyer emerges with more energy than it started with, and can climb or sustain flight indefinitely without an engine or ridge lift.

Birds

Albatrosses are the most famous practitioners of dynamic soaring, using the strong wind gradients above the waves of the Southern Ocean to travel thousands of kilometres with barely a wingbeat. Lord Rayleigh first described the principle in 1883 after observing albatross flight. Petrels, shearwaters, and gulls use the technique on a smaller scale, exploiting the gradient just above the sea surface and wave faces.

Radio-controlled gliders

In the late 1990s, radio-controlled gliding awoke to dynamic soaring, in which pilots fly the leeward side of ridges and repeatedly punch through the shear layer between the separated flow behind the hill and the free stream. RC dynamic soaring has produced extraordinary speeds: dedicated carbon-fibre gliders have exceeded 800 km/h (about 500 mph), with records ratified progressively higher — Spencer Lisenby's flights pushed the mark past 500 mph in 2021. The structural loads involved — accelerations approaching 100 g in the turns — demand extremely strong purpose-built airframes.

Full-size gliders

For full-size sailplanes, dynamic soaring opportunities are limited because the gradient near the ground is dangerous and high-altitude gradients are weaker, but pilots have demonstrated measurable energy gains in wave and jet-stream shear conditions.
