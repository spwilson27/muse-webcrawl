Alcázar of Seville

The Patio de las Doncellas in the Royal Alcázar

The Royal Alcázars of Seville (Spanish: Reales Alcázares de Sevilla), historically known as al-Qasr al-Muriq, are a palace complex in Seville, Spain. Originally an Islamic citadel, the palace is renowned as one of the most beautiful in Spain and is regarded as one of the most outstanding examples of Mudéjar architecture — Islamic-style craftsmanship produced under Christian rule. The upper storeys are still an official residence of the Spanish royal family, making it the oldest royal palace in Europe still in regular use. In 1987 it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site together with Seville Cathedral and the Archivo de Indias.

History

The site began as a fortress residence for Seville's Muslim rulers in the early 10th century under the Umayyad caliphate of Córdoba, and was embellished by the Abbadid kings in the 11th century. After Ferdinand III took Seville in 1248, Christian kings rebuilt parts of the complex, and between 1364 and 1366 Pedro I of Castile created the palace's masterpiece, the Palacio de Don Pedro, with the help of Muslim craftsmen from Granada and Toledo — its Arabic inscriptions proclaim Pedro "sultan" in a striking fusion of cultures.

Highlights

The palace's celebrated spaces include the Patio de las Doncellas (Courtyard of the Maidens), with its sunken garden and lobed arches; the Salón de Embajadores (Hall of Ambassadors), crowned by a golden cedar dome of 1427; the Patio de las Muñecas (Courtyard of the Dolls); and extensive gardens with pavilions, pools and the Mercury Pond. Charles V added Renaissance elements, including the Cenador de la Alcoba and rich tapestries and tilework, for his wedding to Isabella of Portugal in 1526.
