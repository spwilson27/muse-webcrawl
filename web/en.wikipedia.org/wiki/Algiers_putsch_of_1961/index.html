The Algiers putsch (French: Putsch des généraux, "Generals' putsch") was a failed coup d'état in April 1961 by four retired French generals — Maurice Challe, Raoul Salan, Edmond Jouhaud and André Zeller — who seized control of Algiers in an attempt to prevent President Charles de Gaulle from negotiating Algerian independence and abandoning French Algeria.

Background

The Algerian War had raged since 1954. De Gaulle, returned to power in 1958 partly on the promise of keeping Algeria French, gradually moved toward self-determination, announcing in January 1961 a referendum approving negotiations with the FLN. Hardline officers and European settlers (pieds-noirs) saw this as betrayal.

The putsch

On the night of 21–22 April 1961, elite units including the 1st Foreign Parachute Regiment seized key buildings in Algiers. Challe proclaimed that the army would not abandon Algeria. In metropolitan France, de Gaulle invoked Article 16 of the constitution, assuming emergency powers, and broadcast a celebrated appeal on 23 April ordering soldiers not to obey the putschists. Conscripts in Algeria, listening on transistor radios, largely refused to cooperate, and the coup collapsed within four days. Challe and Zeller surrendered; Salan and Jouhaud went underground and later led the terrorist Organisation armée secrète (OAS).

Aftermath

The putschists were tried by a special military tribunal; Challe received fifteen years' imprisonment. The failed coup weakened the "Algeria is France" cause and accelerated the path to the Évian Accords of March 1962 and Algerian independence that July.
